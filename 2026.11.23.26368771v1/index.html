Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Effect Sizes in t-tests and F-tests works

The Effect Size module checks if effect sizes are correctly reported in t-tests and F-tests.

The Effect Size check searches for regular expressions that match typical ways in which effect sizes are reported. It subsequently checks different ways in which Cohen’s d, g, ηp2, and ωp2 can be computed against the reported value. If effects are missing, or might be incorrect, you the module provides a warning. The module was validated on APA reported statistical tests, and might miss effect sizes that were reported in other reporting styles. It was validated by the Metacheck team on papers published in Psychological Science.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Abstract

We evaluated automated classification of 6,829 de-identified free-text EEG reports from 33 Polish hospitals, independently rated by three experts. Three NLP models-fine-tuned PolBERT, a TF-IDF support vector classifier (SVC), and CatBoost-were assessed across seven binary and three-class tasks. Reference labels were derived from mean expert scores, majority voting or the Dawid-Skene (D-S) model, with different treatments of uncertain cases. Inter-rater agreement was moderate (Krippendorff's α = 0.585) and below intra-rater agreement (Cohen's κ = 0.731-0.769); D-S skill rankings differed from unweighted intra-rater κ rankings. PolBERT led on six of seven tasks (binary accuracy 0.876-0.952), although its advantage over CatBoost on threeclass tasks was not significant. On Task 2A, all models exceeded all individual experts (0.762-0.774 vs 0.689-0.726). Binary accuracy held at two isolated hospitals; on Task 2A, SVC fell from 0.762 to 0.616, while PolBERT reached 0.797. Errors clustered in disputed reports (accuracy 0.663-0.771 in intermediate strata). Exploratory zero-shot Qwen3.8-27B achieved binary accuracy of 0.829-0.929, below supervised models, and agreed with experts similarly to their mutual agreement (κ = 0.53-0.61 vs 0.47-0.63), but favoured pathological labels. Automated classification of Polish clinical EEG reports is feasible across centres, with performance comparable to individual experts. Model probabilities were well calibrated (ECE ≤ 0.035), supporting threshold-based triage. Accuracy depends on expert disagreement and the method used to construct reference labels; label aggregation should therefore be part of the task definition.

1. INTRODUCTION

EEG is widely used to diagnose and monitor neurological disorders. Its clinical findings are usually documented in free-text reports, despite structured systems such as SCORE [1, 2].

Differences in terminology, reporting style, and diagnostic wording across clinicians and hospitals complicate automated classification and cross-centre generalisation.

Agreement on raw EEG signal interpretation varies across readers [3], including for binary normal/pathological judgements [4], and is lowest for ambiguous patterns [5, 6].

Automated classification of raw EEG has advanced with the Temple University Hospital Abnormal EEG Corpus (TUAB) [7]. Earlier studies suggested accuracy could plateau near 90% because of label disagreement [8] and described saturating gains with data and model scale [9].

However, transformer models reached 95.7-96.1% on consensus-labelled TUAB data [10]. In TUAB, explicit conclusions in reports reproduce normal/abnormal labels with 99.9% accuracy using four phrases [11]. In our dataset, as is common in free-text EEG reporting, most reports describe the findings without a standardised summary verdict (unlike ACNS-style "Impression" sections or SCORE), so the overall conclusion must be inferred from the description. Label quality has also constrained multi-site classification of Polish EEG signals [12].

EEG-report NLP has mainly supplied labels for signal classifiers [10, 11, 13], rather than being evaluated as a clinical classification task. Direct report studies remain mostly single-centre and English-language, and more often extract particular findings rather than an overall normal/pathological conclusion [14] [15] [16] [17].

Two gaps remain. BERT-based models [18] perform well in clinical text applications [19, 20], but transformer-based normal/abnormal classification of EEG reports has been developed at single centres with at most one external site [15], and has not, to our knowledge, been evaluated across many centres. PolBERT enables this analysis in Polish [21]. Moreover, single-rater labels and majority voting offer limited treatment of ambiguous cases. The Dawid-Skene (D-S) model [22] estimates rater-specific reliability and probabilistic consensus labels.

Large language models (LLMs) can also classify text zero-shot from instructions alone [23].

Locally hosted open-weight models avoid sending clinical reports to external services, but their performance and agreement with experts on Polish EEG reports are unknown. Using 6,829 reports from 33 Polish hospitals rated independently by three experts, we examined (1) intra-and inter-rater agreement and D-S estimates; (2) PolBERT, TF-IDF SVC and CatBoost across seven task definitions; (3) performance relative to individual experts; and (4) transfer to two hospitals excluded from development. We also explored zero-shot classification with a locally hosted open-weight LLM.

2. METHODS

>METHODS

2.1. Data

We analysed 6,829 de-identified free-text EEG reports from ELM19 [12], covering 33 hospitals (at least 20 reports per hospital) and including patient age and sex. Identifiers were removed by the provider. Patients were 55.16% female, with mean age 46.91 years (range 16-98); reports had a median of 61 words (IQR 47-76).

Hospital median report lengths ranged from 15 (H_14) to 107 words (H_13). Most reports lacked an explicit normal/abnormal verdict, requiring interpretation of the findings. Hospital-level demographics and report statistics are in Supplementary Table A1.

Three EEG experts (each with ≥ 10 years' diagnostic experience) independently assigned each report to epilepsy with additional pathology, epilepsy alone, normal, other pathology, or unclassifiable. These were mapped to pathological (1; all three pathology categories), normal (0), or uncertain (0.5; unclassifiable).

For intra-rater reliability, each expert re-scored 473 randomly selected reports (6.9%). Repeats were randomly interspersed over several weeks and were not identified to the experts.

Lemmatisation with spaCy [24], stop-word removal, lowercasing, and punctuation removal did not significantly improve results and were omitted.

2.2. Dataset Partitioning

Using anonymised hospital IDs, we created two isolated transfer hospitals, one fixed development fold and five cross-validation folds. H_32 (228 reports) and H_33 (236) were selected before modelling for adequate site-level sample sizes and contrasting mean expert scores (0.39 and 0.69; Supplementary Table A1). Both hospitals were excluded from training, hyperparameter selection, and cross-validation.

The other 6,365 reports from 31 hospitals were stratified into six folds, preserving hospital proportions. One fold (n = 1,061) was used only for hyperparameter development and joined the training data in every iteration; the remaining five (n = 5,304) served as cross-validation test folds. For transfer testing, models were retrained on all six folds and evaluated once at H_32 and H_33.

2.4. Intra-rater and Inter-rater Agreement Estimation

Intra-rater agreement was measured by percent agreement, Cohen's κ and linearly weighted κ (κω) for ordinal ratings.

Inter-rater agreement was measured by ordinal Krippendorff's α (95% CI from 1,000 bootstrap samples), the two-way mixed-effects consistency ICC(3,1) and pairwise Cohen's κ.

2.5. Dawid-Skene Expert Characterisation

We fitted the Dawid-Skene model [22] with crowd-kit v1.4 [25]. It jointly estimates each rater's errors and posterior report labels by expectation-maximisation, treating the three categories as nominal. As no independent gold standard exists, its latent labels represent a rater-weighted panel consensus.

The primary One-Coin D-S model estimates one skill parameter s (0, 1) per rater, assuming errors are equally distributed between the other two categories. As a sensitivity analysis, the Full D-S model estimates a 3 × 3 confusion matrix for each rater, allowing asymmetric over-or under-calling of pathology.

We compared D-S P(pathological) with mean expert scores using Spearman correlation and examined reports scoring exactly 0.50. One-Coin skill rankings were compared with intra-rater rankings to distinguish agreement with the panel from self-consistency.

2.6. Models

We tested three classifiers on each task. PolBERT [21] used a dropout/linear classification head on pooled [CLS] embeddings, trained first with the encoder frozen (learning rate 1e-5), then jointly fine-tuned (1e-6); both stages used early stopping (patience 20 and 5, respectively) on 200 validation reports. SVC used TF-IDF features and an RBF kernel for binary tasks or a degreefour polynomial kernel for three-class tasks, with within-fold Platt calibration. CatBoost [26] used native text features (bag-of-words, BM25 and Naïve Bayes), generated within each fold, and shallower trees for binary than three-class tasks. Hyperopt Bayesian optimisation (Treestructured Parzen Estimator) selected SVC and CatBoost hyperparameters by development-fold accuracy (Supplementary Table A3). Five-fold test performance was assessed by accuracy and macro-F1. PolBERT learning curves are shown in Supplementary Figure A1.

2.7. Zero-shot LLM Classification (Exploratory Analysis)

As an exploratory zero-shot classifier, we ran open-weight Qwen3.8-27B [27] (4-bit NVFP4 quantisation) through NInfer [28] on one local NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU; no reports left the institution. Each report was processed once in reasoning mode (32,768-token budget), using the recommended sampling settings (Supplementary Text S1). Qwen3.8-27B is the highest-ranked open-weight model in the 4-160B class on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index v4.3.2 (34 points; ranked 1 of 227; accessed 20 October 2026) [29]; see Supplementary Text S4.

Qwen received Polish instructions and only the EEG description as input. Separate prompts produced binary labels (normal, patho; Tasks 1A-1D) and three-class labels (normal, not_sure, patho; Tasks 2A-2C), with each prediction reused across tasks of its type. Predefined rules addressed normal variants, physiological and drug effects, definite abnormalities, borderline wording, contradictions, fragmentary text, and artefacts. An EEG researcher who was not a clinical electroencephalographer drafted the prompts, consulting colleagues and LLMs; the prompts were not revised using evaluation results (Supplementary Text S1).

Experts received no operational definition of 'unclassifiable'. The three-class prompt defined 'not_sure' as borderline or non-specific findings without definite abnormality, or reports too incomplete, contradictory, or artefact-dominated to interpret. The binary prompt always required a normal/pathological decision.

An invalid response triggered up to three corrective prompts (needed once). The LLM returned labels only, so calibration was not assessed.

3.10. Zero-shot LLM Reference (Exploratory Analysis)

Zero-shot Qwen achieved accuracy of 0.829-0.929 on binary tasks and 0.655-0.705 on threeclass tasks (Table 7), below every supervised model. After Holm correction, 20/21 pairwise comparisons were significant (Supplementary Table A6); CatBoost on Task 1D was the exception. Gaps to the best supervised model were 0.023-0.047 (binary) and 0.119-0.153 (threeclass). Three-class macro-F1 was only 0.572-0.629, reflecting weak uncertain-class performance. Contextual language representations are therefore valuable, but not universally preferable to simpler approaches.

Two factors are particularly important for interpreting this performance: the presence of difficult cases and systematic differences between expert raters reflected in the reference labels.

First, model performance was closely related to expert agreement. Accuracy depended much more on how borderline reports were handled than on the choice of model; the three models differed by at most 0.022 within any task (Table 3). The intermediate strata, in which experts disagreed or were undecided, accounted for more than two-thirds of all Task 1B errors for each of the three models. Thus, most model errors occurred in the same cases that were difficult for physicians to classify.

This refines the interpretation of the proposed accuracy ceiling in EEG classification [8, 9]. The ceiling is not a fixed value: overall accuracy depends on the proportion of clear and ambiguous cases and on how borderline cases are defined, which complements the argument by Zhu and Western that high performance can be achieved when evaluation labels are based on expert panel consensus [10]. It also shows where label uncertainty arises. In TUAB, the verdict is stated explicitly by the reporting physician, and the label inherits that physician's threshold [11]. In our data, three experienced readers of the same reports agreed only moderately (α = 0.585), consistent with previous EEG studies [3, 4]. Report-derived labels can therefore carry two layers of individual judgement: that of the physician who wrote the report and that of the physician who reads it. Our design makes the second layer visible.

Second, disagreement between experts was not purely random. Intra-rater agreement was higher than inter-rater agreement for every expert, as also reported by Grant et al. [3], and the Dawid-Skene analysis identified stable differences in how individual physicians used the diagnostic categories. Expert B had the highest unweighted intra-rater κ but the lowest Dawid-Skene skill estimate and the lowest accuracy against the reference label on every task (Table 6), and overcalled pathology, whereas Expert A tended to under-call it. This pattern is consistent with the "similar skill, individualised thresholds" model, in which experts interpret similar evidence but apply different decision thresholds [6].

Intra-rater consistency alone therefore does not demonstrate that an annotator provides unbiased labels: a physician may be highly consistent while consistently differing from the group. Because the latent classes are estimated from the same three raters, this difference is measured relative to the panel consensus, not to an external truth; Dawid-Skene analysis nevertheless separates selfconsistency from alignment with the group consensus, which intra-rater agreement alone cannot do.

This may have implications for training clinical machine-learning models, although it was not tested here. A model trained on one physician's labels may learn that physician's diagnostic threshold together with the clinical content of the report, and reproduce over-or under-calling with high confidence; confidence-based filtering of automatically generated labels, as used in EEG pseudo-labelling pipelines [10, 11], could not by itself remove such a bias. Our models were trained on labels aggregated across three experts, so this remains a hypothesis.

Multi-rater annotation and probabilistic aggregation offer a potential way to reduce this problem.

In our data, Dawid-Skene produced near-binary posteriors for many reports with intermediate mean scores (Figure 2), showing that skill-weighted aggregation is not simply a rescaling of the arithmetic mean; the One-Coin model, however, does not capture asymmetric errors and therefore cannot correct a directional threshold. Whether Dawid-Skene-derived labels provide better training targets than majority voting or confidence filtering should be addressed in future work.

Because the differences in accuracy between model families were small, the higher computational cost of transformer models for training and inference should be weighed against marginal gains [30]. Model transparency matters in high-stakes clinical applications [31], but neither classical model used here is inherently interpretable (the SVC used non-linear kernels and CatBoost is a tree ensemble), so this advantage would apply only to linear models, which were not evaluated.

Binary-task performance was maintained at both fully held-out hospitals, in contrast to the crosssite degradation reported in parallel Polish multi-centre EEG signal classification [12]. On Task 2A at H_32, PolBERT (0.772) remained accurate where SVC fell to 0.474, possibly because contextual embeddings may be more robust when local terminology differs from the training data. However, the lower three-class F1 at H_32 was observed for all models, and H_32 had a much lower proportion of pathological reports than H_33; because macro-averaged F1 is sensitive to small classes, this probably reflects, at least partly, a site-level shift in class distribution rather than failure of a specific architecture. More held-out hospitals are needed before concluding that transformer models transfer systematically better.

The finding that all models exceeded individual experts on Task 2A also requires careful interpretation. Models were trained on the mean-score consensus against which each expert was evaluated, so an expert whose threshold differed systematically from the group was penalised even if their ratings were internally consistent. Higher model accuracy in this setting reflects the ability to reproduce the group-derived target, not superhuman diagnostic performance. Similarly, good calibration within cross-validation supports probability-based triage, but calibration at external sites remains to be established, and a model can be well calibrated to labels that themselves contain systematic physician bias.

The exploratory zero-shot analysis adds a different perspective. Without any training on our data, Local inference avoids transferring clinical text to external services but requires far more computation than the supervised models. The results apply to one model, one quantised build, one prompt and a single stochastic run, and the model produced labels rather than probabilities; they are a practical reference point for a locally deployable general-purpose model, not an estimate of the performance attainable with larger models or optimised prompts.

Several limitations should be noted. Experts evaluated report text without access to the raw EEG signal, so some disagreement may reflect incomplete or ambiguous reporting rather than diagnostic uncertainty. The three-level scale also collapses clinically important distinctions between different types of EEG abnormalities, which could be targets for more detailed NLP classification [14, 16]. Only three annotators were available, limiting the precision of Dawid-Skene skill estimates. The same experts labelled the held-out hospitals, so transfer was tested across reporting sites but not across annotators. Because tasks share reports and folds, the statistical comparisons between models are descriptive. Finally, the study included Polish-language reports from one healthcare system and only two fully isolated hospitals; prospective validation across more centres, languages, and clinical settings is required.

5. CONCLUSION

NLP models can classify free-text EEG reports in a large, multi-centre, non-English dataset with accuracy comparable to individual experts. PolBERT showed the strongest overall performance, but its advantage was small and not consistent across tasks and sites, so computational cost should be considered alongside accuracy. A general-purpose LLM run locally without any training reached somewhat lower accuracy, with a systematic shift towards pathology, and is best regarded as an additional rater whose threshold is set by its prompt.

Achievable performance depends on the labels as much as on the models. Model accuracy falls in the same cases in which physicians disagree, so it depends strongly on case mix and on how ambiguous cases are handled, and the method of label aggregation should be reported as part of the task definition. Physician-generated labels can also carry stable individual thresholds, so they should not automatically be treated as unbiased ground truth; multi-rater annotation and methods such as Dawid-Skene can help distinguish genuine case ambiguity from systematic rater differences.
