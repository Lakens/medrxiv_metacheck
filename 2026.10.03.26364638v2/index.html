To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 6 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Aberrant basal ganglia activity is a key feature of the pathophysiology of dystonia, but evidence is predominantly based on adult cohorts. Neurophysiological signatures during development remain poorly characterised. Local field potentials (LFPs) recorded by bidirectional neurostimulators provide a direct measure of globus pallidus internus (GPi) activity. In a cross-sectional paediatric cohort, we investigated whether pallidal oscillatory, aperiodic and connectivity characteristics are associated with dystonia severity and whether these differ between genetic and acquired dystonias.

Methods: We recruited 30 children and young adults (5-20 years) with medically refractory dystonia (genetic n = 10; acquired n = 20) who underwent bilateral GPi Deep Brain Stimulation (DBS) implantation. Ten minutes of pallidal LFPs were recorded using Medtronic Percept™ RC neurostimulators with stimulation off, before DBS activation and, where available, at 12 months. Spectral power, aperiodic exponent and interhemispheric pallidopallidal coherence were quantified across delta (1-4 Hz), theta (4-8 Hz), alpha (8-13 Hz), beta (13-30 Hz), and low-gamma (30-45 Hz) frequency bands. Linear mixed-effects models were implemented to establish whether LFP markers were predictive of dystonia severity, as measured by the Burke-Fahn-Marsden Dystonia Rating Scale (BFMDRS-Movement). Firth's Bias-Reduced Logistic Regression assessed whether LFP markers were predictive of dystonia aetiology.

Results: Spectral peaks were most consistently detected in the delta, beta, and lowgamma bands. Delta and low-gamma peak power significantly predicted dystonia severity. This association was independent of medication burden, structural GPi involvement, and dystonia aetiology. Theta and alpha peaks were detected less consistently, and their power was not associated with dystonia severity. Pallidopallidal coherence significantly predicted dystonia severity in interaction with medication load in all frequency bands. Low-gamma peak power was significantly lower in acquired than genetic dystonia, but the potentially confounding influence of dystonia severity could not be excluded. The aperiodic exponent was not associated with severity or aetiology.

Conclusion: Pallidal LFP markers are associated with dystonia severity in children, with delta peak power, low-gamma peak power and pallidopallidal coherence reflecting clinically relevant features of disease expression. These findings highlight the potential of pallidal LFPs to characterise dystonia across the lifespan and motivate longitudinal studies of their developmental and clinical trajectories.

Introduction

Dystonia is movement disorder characterised by sustained or intermittent abnormal movements, postures, or both 1 often leading to severe motor disability. It can arise from diverse genetic or acquired causes, 1 with dystonic/dyskinetic cerebral palsy (CP) representing a frequent cause of acquired dystonia in childhood. 2 Although current models implicate dysfunction within a distributed network including the basal ganglia, cerebellum, thalamus and sensorimotor cortex, the pathophysiology of dystonia is not fully understood. [3] [4] [5] Bilateral deep brain stimulation (DBS) of the globus pallidus internus (GPi) is an established treatment for medically refractory dystonia, but clinical outcomes vary considerably, with people with genetic dystonias generally demonstrating greater benefit than those with acquired aetiology. [6] [7] [8] The pathophysiological basis for this heterogeneity in DBS response remains unclear. 6 Local field potential (LFP) recordings obtained from temporarily externalised DBS leads in the peri-operative period and, more recently, from bidirectional neurostimulators provide a direct measure of the electrophysiological activity of subcortical structures. 9 Previous studies have described abnormalities across a range of oscillatory frequencies in GPi LFPs recorded from people with dystonia. 10, 11 Among these, low-frequency (4-10 Hz) power spectral peaks 10 and low-frequency coherence between different nodes of the dystonic network 11 have been reported most consistently.

Multiple studies have also demonstrated associations between low-frequency GPi activity and dystonia severity, including correlations with clinical rating scales, [12] [13] [14] involuntary muscle activity measured by electromyography, [15] [16] [17] [18] and symptom fluctuations. 19, 20 These observations, together with evidence of exaggerated lowfrequency drive to muscles, [21] [22] [23] suggest that low-frequency oscillations may represent an important feature of the pathophysiology of dystonia, with the hypothesis that DBS might overwrite or regularise these pathological activity patterns. 18 Several studies have reported similar low-frequency GPi activity across different dystonia aetiologies such as genetic and acquired, 10 and across groups with different dystonia distributions including generalised, segmental and focal. 11 Additionally, similar findings in patients with other hyperkinetic presentations, such as Parkinson's Disease with levodopa-induced dyskinesia 24 and Tourette's syndrome 25 have led to the suggestion that excessive low frequency power might not be disease-specific, but rather reflective of hyperkinetic symptomatology. 5 These observations suggest that GPi oscillations may primarily reflect the expression and severity of dystonic symptoms rather than the underlying mechanism of disease.

However, evidence supporting this interpretation is limited by small sample sizes, a predominance of adults with idiopathic or rare genetic dystonias in the LFP literature, and a lack of direct comparisons across aetiologies. 10, 11 This interpretation also remains difficult to reconcile with the observed differences in DBS responsiveness between genetic and acquired dystonias, 6 highlighting the need to ascertain whether GPi LFP patterns represent shared electrophysiological signatures across aetiologies. Clarifying this relationship would be critical to informing aetiology-specific treatment strategies with potential for improved clinical outcomes. This question is particularly important in the context of the developing brain, where early brain injury and maturation shape the organisation and plasticity of the network underlying dystonia, and where activity in critical neurodevelopmental windows has enduring effects which may only manifest later in life. However, the paediatric DBS literature has been limited to small retrospective cohorts, 6, 7, 26 with larger studies just recently emerging. 8 Paediatric LFP studies are also extremely limited so far, with only a small number of reports to date, each including no more than ten patients. 19, 20, [27] [28] [29] Defining whether electrophysiological signatures identified in adult cohorts apply to the developing brain and across dystonia aetiologies is essential to understand the generalisability of the proposed physiomarkers and their potential clinical application across the lifespan. This study examines GPi LFP recordings from a single-centre paediatric dystonia cohort, including a substantial group with dystonic CP. Our objectives were to assess i) whether the association between low-frequency LFP activity and symptom severity in adults can be replicated in children and young people with dystonia, and ii) whether different aetiologies exhibit distinct electrophysiological signatures despite a shared clinical phenotype.

Materials and methods

Participants and surgical procedure

We recruited a consecutive sample of 30 children and young adults (age between five and 20; n = 3 over 18 years) who underwent bilateral pallidal DBS implantation (Medtronic Percept TM RC neurostimulator) at Evelina London Children's Hospital (ELCH), Guy's and St Thomas' NHS Foundation Trust (Jan 2024-Jan 2026) for management of dystonia symptoms. Suitability for DBS was determined following multi-disciplinary clinical assessment at ELCH; the decision to implant followed established clinical pathways, as previously reported. 30 Implantation was performed under general anaesthesia using direct MRI-guided targeting and the Neuromate (Renishaw, UK) robot. Lead placement was confirmed with the O-arm intra-operative imaging system. The Implantable Pulse Generator (IPG) was buried in a subcutaneous pocket at right abdominal (n = 27) or chest level (n = 3).

Clinical data collection

Demographic and clinical data were extracted from clinical notes, including the Burke-Fahn-Marsden Dystonia Rating Scale (BFMDRS) 31 -Movement and Disability scores, which assess dystonia severity and associated disability at baseline (pre-DBS) and 12months post-DBS; the five-level Gross Motor Function Classification System (GMFCS) 32 and the Manual Ability Classification System (MACS), 33 assessing mobility/gross motor skills, and object handling, respectively; the Canadian Occupational Performance Measure (COPM) 34 -Performance and Satisfaction scales, capturing patients' and/or caregivers' perspectives on self-care, productivity and leisure (when two raters were present, the average was used); the Caregiver Priorities and Child Health Index of Life with Disabilities (CPCHILD), 35 measuring caregivers' perspectives on health-related quality of life. In addition, the Clinical Global Impression -Severity (CGI-S) 36 scale rated dystonia severity from one to seven (Supplementary Section 1). CGI ratings were independently completed at each time point by the same consultant paediatric neurologist and specialist nurse, with the mean score reported.

Adapted from McClelland et al., 37 brain MRI findings were categorised from pre-DBS neuroradiology reports as (1) normal, (2) evidence of white matter injury (including periventricular leukomalacia, ventricular enlargement, white matter signal change, reduced white matter volume), (3) term Hypoxic Ischaemic Encephalopathy (HIE), (4) know metabolic pattern associated with clinically diagnosed metabolic disorders, (5) focal infarct, (6) malformations of cortical development (e.g., focal cortical dysplasia), and (7) other structural abnormalities.

Information on the presence and normality of motor evoked potentials (MEPs), central motor conduction times (CMCT), and somatosensory evoked potentials (SEPs) was extracted from neurophysiology reports.

LFP data acquisition

Pallidal LFP recordings were acquired as part of routine clinical practice prior to DBS activation (either three to five days, n = 25, or one-month postoperatively, n = 5), with additional recordings at 12 months post-implantation in 14 participants who completed clinical follow-up. Recordings comprised an average of 10.0 min (SD: 4.2; range: 2.1-13.5) of Indefinite Streaming data acquired simultaneously from both hemispheres (four level-wise contacts per lead, excluding segments, giving six bipolar recordings per hemisphere; Fig. 1) during spontaneous activity. Participants were seated or lying and awake during recordings -either resting or during spontaneous activity -except for two recordings obtained during sleep. Stimulation is automatically turned off during Indefinite Streaming. During recordings, BrainSense automatically applies two low pass filters (100Hz), and two high pass filters (1Hz). No additional offline filtering was applied.

Offline LFP preprocessing

Line noise at 50 and 100 Hz was removed using the cleanLineNoise MATLAB function, with 4s taper windows (1s slide), 2Hz search bandwidth, and default iterative and statistical settings. 38 If present, cardiac artifacts were corrected using a custom template subtraction pipeline based on Hammer et al. 39 In-detail description of this method is provided in Supplementary Material; the resulting MATLAB function is publicly available at [link to repository will be included once the manuscript is accepted]. Cardiac artifacts were infrequent, detected and subsequently removed from 26 LFP channels from seven Indefinite Streaming recordings only. Data were then visually inspected and non-stereotyped artifacts manually rejected.

LFP-derived markers

Peak power and aperiodic exponent

Preprocessed LFP data were segmented into 20 s continuous epochs, and the specparam algorithm was applied via the MATLAB function eeg_htpCalcFooof (vhtp toolbox). 40 Specparam models aperiodic 1/f components and periodic gaussians (oscillatory peaks) for each epoch's power spectrum, previously computed between 1 and 45 Hz using a Short-Time Fourier Transform (spectrogram) with 1-s Hamming window and 50% overlap, and time-wise averaging of the time-resolved spectra. Parameters included peak width between 0.5 and 12 Hz, maximum number of peaks equal to five, peak threshold of 2 SD, and modelling the aperiodic knee, i.e., the point marking a change in the aperiodic spectral slope. The "best LFP channel" was then selected as follows. For each LFP channel, specparam-modelled linearised peaks in the theta band (4-8 Hz) were extracted (with only the highest peak considered in case of multiple peaks), and a weighted average (x̄) of peak power across epochs for each channel i was calculated as where xi,j is the peak power in epoch j for channel i, ni is the number of epochs where a peak was detected, and N is the total number of epochs.

This operation scales each channel's peak power mean by the proportion of epochs in which a peak was detected, the latter serving as a "peak reliability factor" that reduces the influence of channels with infrequent theta peaks. As a result, channel selection reflects a balanced consideration of both magnitude and prevalence of theta peaks. The LFP channel with the highest weighted average was selected for further processing (Fig. 1). A small-sample-biasadjusted 41 coefficient of variation was calculated to quantify variability in weighted average between LFP channels.

For the selected channel, average peak power across epochs at the specparam-identified peak frequency was extracted from the original power spectrum following subtraction of the aperiodic component (including the knee).

Analyses were repeated using peak frequency search windows in the delta (1-4 Hz), alpha (8-13 Hz), beta (13-30 Hz), and low-gamma (30-45 Hz) ranges.

The epoch-wise average specparam-modelled aperiodic exponent was computed and averaged across channels (Fig. 1); since the model included a knee, the exponent is a measure of the aperiodic component past the knee frequency; the median (IQR) knee frequency is reported.

Pallidopallidal coherence

As homotopic interhemispheric connectivity occurs with zero-or near-zero phase delay, a zero-phase inclusive method was selected. 42, 43 Pallidopallidal coherence (Cxy), which quantifies how consistently two LFP signals x and y maintain a linear relationship (in both amplitude and phase) at a given frequency f, was therefore calculated as: where Sxy represents the cross-spectrum, Syy and Sxx the auto-spectra.

This was computed using Fieldtrip ft_connectivityanalysis from complex Fourier coefficients obtained via multitaper spectral analysis (all available 20 s segments split into nonoverlapping 2 s epochs and analysed using the mtmfft method with DPSS (Slepian) tapers and 2Hz frequency smoothing window), with cross-spectral and auto-spectral estimates averaged across epochs.

As subtle variations between the positioning of left and right GPi electrodes may occur, all interhemispheric combinations were extracted, and the LFP pair displaying maximum coherence in each frequency band was used for subsequent analyses (Fig. 1), consistent with previous work. 12

Statistical analyses

To test whether LFP-derived measures predicted dystonia severity, linear mixed-effects models 44 with Restricted Maximum Likelihood estimator were implemented. These allow increasing power by leveraging repeated measures (n = 14 with 12-months assessment), handling missing follow-up data (n = 16), and modelling between-patient variability as a random intercept. Multiple models were fitted with theta peak power, aperiodic exponent, or theta pallidopallidal coherence as fixed effects and BFMDRS-Movement score as predicted variable, with a random intercept for participants.

Confidence intervals and P-values for fixed-effects were obtained using parametric bootstrapping (5,000 iterations). For natural spline terms, statistical inference was based on likelihood ratio tests 45 comparing models with and without the spline term, as individual spline-basis-functions coefficients are not independently interpretable.

Competing models were compared using likelihood ratio tests 45 assessing improvement in model fit. Model assumptions were evaluated using simulation-based residual diagnostics ('DHARMa' package 46 V0.4.7, 2024-10-16) and Levene's test for homogeneity of variance. 47 To test whether LFP-derived measures predicted dystonia aetiology, Firth's Bias-Reduced Logistic Regression with penalised log-likelihood estimator was implemented. 48 The Firth's method produces nearly unbiased maximum likelihood estimates in small samples (n = 30), with unbalanced outcome classes (genetic dystonia n = 10; acquired n = 20) and low number of observations in cross-tabulation with the outcome (n < 5 for one covariate). 49 As aetiology is stable over time, LFP-derived measures were averaged across timepoints (for n = 14 with repeated measures) to improve the stability of trait-level markers. The resulting theta peak power, aperiodic exponent, and theta pallidopallidal coherence estimates were entered as predictors in separate models, with aetiology as the predicted variable. Genetic dystonia was the reference category. Odds Ratios (ORs) and Confidence Intervals were derived to estimate the magnitude of the association.

With n = 30 participants (or n = 44 observations in mixed-effect models), statistical considerations allow a limited number of predictors (three to four). 50 For all models, an a priori decision was made to include covariates with the highest potential to confound LFP estimates, namely number of psychoactive medications and pallidal involvement on structural MRI. Unadjusted analyses quantifying the association between LFPderived markers and BFMDRS-M scores without any model covariates are reported.

Whilst our primary hypothesis was focused on the theta frequency band, all analyses were repeated using peak power and pallidopallidal coherence extracted from delta, alpha, beta, and low-gamma; for each predictor type, False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction 51 was applied across bands.

Data were analysed using R statistical software V4. 5.3 (2026-03-11) with the 'logistf' package 52 V1.26.1 (2025-04-16) and the 'lme4' package 53 V.2.0.1 (2026-03-05). If present, the impact of covariates' heteroscedasticity was assessed through a sensitivity analysis using a Gaussian model with a dispersion term allowing residual variance to vary by the covariate's levels ('glmmTMB' package V 1.1.14, 2026-01-15). 45

Control analyses

Control analyses included computing mean power in a 3Hz narrow band surrounding the peak, 12 and computing average peak power and pallidopallidal coherence across all LFP channels to exclude potential channel selection bias (Fig. 1).

To control for potential confounding between the two outcome variables, significant mixed-effects models predicting dystonia severity were re-estimated with aetiology as an additional covariate. Conversely, logistic regression models predicting dystonia aetiology were re-estimated with dystonia severity as an additional covariate.

For significant models, sensitivity analyses were run excluding two recordings acquired during sleep and two (from the same participant) with detected epileptiform discharges.

Note that as epileptiform abnormalities were removed during preprocessing, none were present in any of the analysed data.

Population descriptive statistics

Descriptive statistics are reported for the whole group and stratifying by aetiology.

Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. 54 For continuous normally distributed variables, Welch's t-test 55 estimated the significance of differences between aetiology groups; Mann-Whitney U test 56 assessed other distributions. For categorical variables, Fisher's exact test 57 was used to account for low cell count (n < 5).

Dystonia characteristics

Participants with genetic dystonia (n = 10) included autosomal dominant (DYT1/TOR1A, DYT6/THAP1, DYT11/SGCE, KMT2B, EIF2AK2), autosomal recessive (Glutaric aciduria type 1, GA-I/GCDH, HARP/PANK2), and mitochondrial (Leigh syndrome) aetiologies. The acquired subgroup (n = 20) consisted of people with dystonic/dyskinetic Cerebral Palsy due to prematurity (n = 13), kernicterus (n = 6; of which n = 3 secondary to underlying metabolic diagnoses), or term HIE (n = 1). For all participants, the clinical phenotype was characterised by persistent, generalised dystonia with bilateral involvement; three showed predominantly asymmetric distribution. The disorder was progressive in almost all participants with genetic dystonia and static in all with acquired dystonia (Table 1). Age at onset differed between genetic and acquired aetiologies, whereas dystonia duration at surgery was comparable between groups (Table 1). 73% of participants had isolated dystonia, with proportions consistent across aetiology groups (Table 1); concurrent motor features in the remainder included chorea (n = 3), athetosis (n = 4), spasticity (n = 1) and myoclonus (n = 3). Non-motor neurological comorbidities are in Supplementary Material Section 2.

Demographic characteristics

Demographic characteristics were comparable between genetic and acquired groups, except the acquired group included more people with undetermined handedness and more people attending special schools (Table 1).

Pre-DBS investigation profiles

MRI abnormalities were present in all but two participants, with bilateral involvement in 86.7% (n = 26), unilateral in 6.7% (n = 2). The most common findings were white matter injury (36.7%, n = 11) followed by known metabolic patterns (GA-I, Pantothenate kinase-associated neurodegeneration, or Leigh syndrome, 10.0%, n = 3), malformations (6.7%, n = 2), and term HIE (3.3%, n = 1); 36.7% (n = 11) had other structural abnormalities. Pallidal abnormalities were present in 56.7% (n = 17), with no significant differences between aetiology groups (Table 1).

Neurophysiological assessment demonstrated largely preserved pathways; MEPs were present and normal in all tested (n = 21), with normal CMCTs; SEPs were present in all tested (n = 24), normal in 87.5% (n = 21).

The sample had a median BFMDRS-Movement score of 99.00 (IQR 35.00) at baseline, with the acquired aetiology group scoring significantly higher (Table 1; Supplementary Fig. 1). The acquired group also scored significantly higher on the BFMDRS-Disability, CGI-S, GMFCS and MACS levels; COPM performance and satisfaction scores were comparable across aetiologies (Supplementary Table 1, Supplementary Fig. 1).

Surgery and medications

All participants underwent de novo DBS implantation except two who had revisions or replacements; all received SenSight electrodes except one (Legacy). Lead placement was satisfactory in all cases, with minor impedance abnormalities in three requiring no intervention.

At baseline, participants were receiving a median of three medications (IQR: 3.75, range: 0-6), with a significantly higher medication burden in those with acquired dystonia (Table 1). Medication classes included mainly GABAergic, glutamatergic, adrenergic and cholinergic agents.

LFP data characteristics

Spectral peaks were most frequently identified in the low-gamma band, with at least one peak identified in 86.4% of the analysed 20 s segments, followed by beta (77.7%) and delta (71.9%); theta and alpha peaks were less consistently detected (43.7% and 21% respectively; Fig. 2; Supplementary Table 2). Further details on the variability in peak power between channels and the longitudinal consistency of the best channel selected are in Supplementary Table 3.

Dystonia severity

Peak power and aperiodic exponent

Theta peak power did not significantly predict dystonia severity, as measured by BFMDRS-Movement scores (P = 0.47; Table 2 and Supplementary Table 6). Analyses of peak power extracted from the alpha and beta bands also revealed no association with . dystonia severity (Supplementary Table 6). However, both delta and low-gamma peak power significantly predicted BFMDRS-Movement scores; for delta, lower power was associated with higher severity (FDR-corrected P = 0.045; Supplementary Table 6; Fig. 3). Low-gamma peak power showed a non-linear association with severity, with intermediate values associated with lower dystonia severity (FDR-corrected P = 0.040; Supplementary Table 6; Fig. 3).

Details on model specifications, diagnostics, and sensitivity analyses are in Supplementary Section 3.1.2. Both these and unadjusted analyses (Table 2, Supplementary Table 4) supported the conclusions of the primary analyses. Across participants and recordings, the median knee frequency of the aperiodic exponent was 1.13 Hz (IQR = 1.40). The aperiodic exponent was not a significant predictor of dystonia severity (P = 0.79; Table 2; Supplementary Table 10). Unadjusted (Table 2; Supplementary Table 8) and sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Section 3.2.2) did not change these conclusions.

Consistently across all adjusted models, both covariates were significantly associated with the outcome, with pallidal involvement on MRI and higher number of medications associated with greater dystonia severity (Supplementary Tables 6 and 10; Supplementary Fig. 2).

Pallidopallidal coherence

Theta pallidopallidal coherence did not significantly predict BFMDRS-Movement scores (FDR-corrected P = 0.20; Table 3; Supplementary Tables 12 and 14). However, residual diagnostics demonstrated deviations from the expected distribution and suggested the presence of an interaction with medication number. Including an interaction term improved residual diagnostics and model fit, with a significant coherence × medication number interaction (χ²(3) = 17.02, P < 0.001; Table 3; Supplementary Table 16; Fig. 3).

Pallidopallidal coherence extracted from all other frequency bands revealed similar findings (Supplementary Table 16; Supplementary Fig. 3).

Dystonia aetiology

Peak power and aperiodic exponent

There was no significant association between theta peak power and the odds of having acquired rather than genetic aetiology (FDR-corrected p = 0.65; Table 4). The unadjusted analyses revealed similar findings (Table 4). No association with dystonia aetiology was found for peak power extracted from the delta, alpha, and beta bands (Supplementary Table 28).

In contrast, the logistic regression for low-gamma peak power failed to converge because of quasi-complete separation, precluding reliable estimation of regression coefficients. To quantify the significance of this separation, low-gamma peak power was binarised (threshold of 0.01), and Fisher's exact test 57 demonstrated a significant association with aetiology (P <0.001; Supplementary Table 28; Fig. 4).

Dystonia aetiology was significantly associated with the aperiodic exponent when this was modelled independently (P = 0.006; Table 4). However, this association was no longer significant after adjustment for medication exposure as a binary variable (P = 0.056; Table 4). More granular models including medication number as a covariate were unstable due to quasi-complete separation.

Pallidopallidal coherence

There was no significant association between pallidopallidal coherence in any frequency band and the odds of having acquired rather than genetic aetiology (theta: FDRcorrected P = 0.71; Table 4; other frequency bands: Supplementary Table 29).

Conclusions remained unchanged in unadjusted analyses (Table 4; Supplementary Table 29).

Consistently across all adjusted models, medication number was significantly associated with dystonia aetiology but pallidal involvement on MRI was not (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Control analyses

Control analyses using power extracted from a 3-Hz narrow band centered on the spectral peak continued to demonstrate significant associations between delta and lowgamma power and dystonia severity; however, neither remained significant after FDR correction (Supplementary Table 18).

Analyses using average peak power across all LFP channels continued to support significant associations between severity and both delta peak power and pallidopallidal coherence across all frequency bands, but not low-gamma peak power (Supplementary Tables 20, 22).

Low-gamma power remained significantly associated with dystonia aetiology when this was extracted from a 3-Hz band or averaged across all channels (Supplementary Tables 30 31 32).

Delta peak power, low-gamma peak power and coherence in all bands remained significant predictors of severity after adjustment for dystonia aetiology (Supplementary Table 24).

Control models including dystonia severity as an additional covariate when predicting aetiology from low-gamma peak power were not identifiable due to quasi-complete separation, precluding estimation of the independent association with aetiology beyond the potentially confounding effect of severity.

Sensitivity analyses excluding four recordings (two acquired during sleep, two from a participant with detected epileptiform discharges) yielded results consistent with the primary analyses (Supplementary Table 26).

Discussion

Bidirectional DBS systems offer the opportunity to identify neurophysiological biomarkers and work towards developing adaptive, closed-loop DBS paradigms for neurological and neuropsychiatric disorders. In Parkinson's disease, sub-thalamic beta oscillations are already being used to guide adaptive DBS. 58 In dystonia, the identification of equivalent physiomarkers remains an ongoing challenge, complicated by the fact that the clinical effects of DBS programming often take days to weeks to manifest. Whilst adult studies have highlighted the importance of pallidal low-frequency oscillations in dystonia, their specificity, reliability, and generalisability across age groups and dystonia aetiologies remain poorly characterised. These represent important considerations in the development of robust, objective LFP-guided stimulation protocols.

To our knowledge, this study reports the largest dataset to date of pallidal LFP recordings in children and young people with refractory dystonia of both genetic and acquired origin. Results indicate that pallidal LFP markers (delta peak power, lowgamma peak power and pallidopallidal coherence) are associated with dystonia severity in children. These show different characteristics from those reported in adults.

Delta and low-gamma peak power are significant predictors of dystonia severity

We observed frequent detectable spectral peaks in the delta, beta, and low-gamma frequency bands. Delta and low-gamma peak power were significant predictors of dystonia severity, as measured by the BFMDRS-M. This association was independent of medication burden, structural GPi involvement on MRI, and dystonia aetiology.

Conversely, theta and alpha peaks were only present during a minority of the recording time, and their power was not associated with dystonia severity.

Our results broadly align with previous literature in supporting the relevance of lowfrequency oscillations in dystonia, but we specifically identify the 1-4 Hz (delta) band, rather than the more commonly reported 4-10 Hz in adults. 10 Previous reports of pallidal LFPs in paediatric patients, limited to a few case-studies and small series, have shown inconsistent findings regarding the most prominent peak frequencies and associations with symptom severity: some implicate the delta band; 20, 27 others report alpha 19, 29 or beta. 28, 29 In this study, lower delta power was associated with higher severity suggesting that during development, the neurophysiological substrate of dystonia severity may be characterised by a distinct pallidal spectral profile.

Although animal studies indicate that basal ganglia activity undergoes substantial changes in spontaneous activity during development, the longitudinal profile of healthy subcortical oscillatory physiology in humans remains largely unknown. [59] [60] [61] [62] In the cortex, low-frequency activity is prominent during childhood and becomes progressively less dominant with age. 63, 64 Given that the cortex is a major driver of activity within basal ganglia nuclei, subcortical oscillatory activity may follow a similar trajectory. The observed association between greater delta power and lower dystonia severity may indicate that preserved developmentally typical low-frequency dynamics support the organisation of motor networks during maturation, contributing to a healthier functional state. Increased delta activity is also observed during GABAergic anaesthesia, 65, 66 raising the possibility that greater delta power may reflect a relatively more inhibited network state associated with less severe dystonia. Longitudinal LFP studies are needed to understand the developmental trajectories of pathological oscillatory activity in the basal ganglia.

We also observed a significant association between low-gamma (30-45 Hz) peak power and dystonia severity, with an optimal, intermediate range of activity associated with lower severity and both low and high gamma levels predictive of greater severity.

Gamma activity, which is related to the time constants of GABAA-mediated signalling 67 -abundant in the GPi -is the most prominent resting basal ganglia rhythm in healthy mammals 68, 69 and emerges following restoration of dopaminergic drive in people with Parkinson's disease, 70 supporting its role as a physiologically healthy feature of resting basal ganglia circuits. Additionally, transient basal ganglia gamma changes (>30 Hz) occur during movement execution and inhibition and sustained, finely tuned oscillations (60-90 Hz) have been observed in prokinetic clinical states. [54] [55] [56] In children with active dystonia, a high prevalence of gamma peaks might reflect preserved physiological recruitment of pallidal circuits, with intermediate low-gamma activity potentially reflecting an optimal level of network engagement supporting appropriately timed pallidal inhibitory output to downstream structures. This would be consistent with the idea that an optimal temporal pattern of activity creates the conditions for balanced and flexible excitatory and inhibitory dynamics supporting healthy movement 72, 73 and with computational studies postulating that gamma can emerge when the basal ganglia operate within an appropriate dynamic regimen, with reduced or excessive gamma reflecting pathological network states. 74 The association between low-gamma and dystonia severity also raises the possibility that gamma is not only subserving communication but also plasticity-driven motor consolidation processes during development. Gamma activity has been shown to align neuronal firing within the critical temporal windows relevant for spike-timingdependent plasticity, 20, 67, 75, 76 a process shaped by GABAergic signalling. 67, 77 This is relevant to dystonia, where maladaptive plasticity has been proposed to contribute to consolidation of abnormal motor representations. 73, 78 Given the wider role of the basal ganglia in reinforcement-based learning and action-sequence acquisition, 79 and the predominantly GABAergic nature of GPi output, an optimal range of low-gamma activity could potentially support the development and maintenance of healthier motor representations during development, whereas either reduced or excessive low-gamma activity might disrupt this balance. However, direct evidence linking 30-45 Hz activity to Hebbian plasticity within the GPi is currently lacking, so this interpretation remains speculative.

Recordings were acquired during clinical appointments, typically during rest, or during spontaneous activities such as using a tablet, eating, etc. Therefore, we cannot completely exclude the possibility that the findings represent transient physiological activity associated with voluntary or involuntary movement [54] [55] [56] or alertness levels. [80] [81] [82] However, previous studies have observed voluntary movement-related gamma eventrelated synchronisation in the contralateral GPi of dystonia patients in the 60-80 Hz range, 83, 84 and independently of dystonic symptoms, 84 which is higher than the 30-45 Hz range here. Changes in alertness are also unlikely to solely explain our findings, as sensitivity analyses excluding sleeping patients did not alter results.

Collectively, these findings suggest that peak power signatures identified in adult cohorts (i.e. 4-10 Hz oscillations) may not fully generalise to the developing brain, where the disruption of delta and low-gamma operating regimes appears to be more closely related to symptom severity.

Pallidopallidal coherence predicts dystonia severity in interaction with medication load

Across frequency bands, pallidopallidal coherence also significantly predicted dystonia severity in interaction with medication load. In unmedicated patients and those receiving monotherapy, lower coherence was associated with greater severity; this association no longer applied in people on polytherapy (two or more medications). Although these findings should be considered exploratory due to the number of model terms included, an interaction was strongly supported and the effect was consistent across frequency bands, suggesting a potentially meaningful finding.

Functional homotopy, the spontaneous synchronisation of homologous regions across hemispheres, is a fundamental characteristic of brain architecture, with robust connectivity in the basal ganglia, somatosensory, motor and premotor cortices. 85 This reflects both structural and functional connectivity and is thought to support integrated network activity. [85] [86] [87] [88] [89] [90] Impaired homotopic connectivity has been described in several disorders. 85, 90, 91 Evidence for pallidopallidal coherence in dystonia is scarce, 11 with a single study examining its relationship with symptom severity and reporting an opposite association 12 ; in contrast, reduced homotopic connectivity at the cortical level has been reported in focal dystonias. 11, [92] [93] [94] [95] In our cohort, lower pallidopallidal coherence consistently reflected greater severity.

Although the limited literature precludes a clear mechanistic interpretation, this may indicate adoption of alternative, maladaptive signalling pathways within the motor network. The variability in severity by medication load despite similarly high coherence levels may be due to differences in the underlying disease state prompting treatment; alternatively, medication load may act as a stratification factor for disorder complexity or symptom control. The monotherapy association was not attributable to a single medication or medication class.

Low-gamma peak power is a significant predictor of aetiology, but the potential confounding effect of severity cannot be excluded.

Previous studies have suggested that pallidal neuronal firing patterns 96 and brain glucose metabolism 97 may differ according to dystonia aetiology. However, the extent to which LFP features vary across aetiologies is relatively unexplored, with previous studies limited by small sample sizes and inclusion of few individuals with acquired dystonias. 10, 11 In this study, direct statistical comparison demonstrated broadly comparable LFP characteristics across genetic and acquired dystonias, with no group differences in delta, theta, alpha, and beta peak power, aperiodic exponent and pallidopallidal coherence. We nevertheless observed significantly lower low-gamma peak power in the acquired group. Unfortunately, statistical considerations prevented us from determining whether this association was independent of dystonia severity. This is relevant given that severity was significantly higher in the acquired group, which likely reflects selection bias inherent to our tertiary service, where DBS is considered for genetic dystonias across a broad range of severities but is typically indicated for more severe, treatment-refractory acquired cases. Replication in larger, independently recruited cohorts with different sampling strategies is required to establish whether lowgamma power is indeed associated with dystonia aetiology. Taken together, our findings reinforce previous observations that pallidal physiomarkers may primarily reflect disease expression rather than the underlying mechanisms driving dystonia pathogenesis, 5, 10, 11 with the isolated low-gamma difference requiring cautious interpretation given potential confounding by dystonia severity.

Additional findings and methodological considerations

Our primary analyses examined peak power extracted from each individual's "best channel" (i.e., the LFP pair displaying the highest and most frequent peaks). A series of control analyses confirmed that the observed associations persisted, but in an attenuated form, when power was averaged over a 3-Hz narrow band around the peak, closely reproducing results from a previous study 12 and supporting the use of power at the peak centre as the most sensitive measure. For delta, the association with severity also remained significant when considering the average peak power across all 12 LFP pairs, whereas the low-gamma association did not. This suggests that the low-gamma physiomarker may reflect a more locally restricted process, consistent with the functional segregation of basal ganglia territories and the spatial specificity of pallidosubthalamic connections associated with optimal DBS outcomes. 98 For delta, the persistence of the association across LFP pairs may reflect the spatially broader neuronal assemblies engaged in delta oscillations 99 or greater consistency of delta activity across territories. Multi-site (e.g., EEG and LFP) recordings would be needed to determine whether different oscillation frequencies reflect communication at different spatial scales within the dystonia network. 99, 100 Although beta peaks were frequently present, these were not associated with severity or aetiology, warranting further investigation of their clinical correlates, including the potential effect of medications. 10, 101 In unadjusted models, the aperiodic exponent was significantly associated with dystonia aetiology, but this association was no longer significant after adjusting for medication load. This effect is biologically plausible, given evidence that the aperiodic exponent tends to be modulated by medications in general 102 and interventions affecting basal ganglia activity in movement disorders in particular. [103] [104] [105] These findings highlight the importance of parameterising the power spectrum to disentangle oscillatory and nonoscillatory activity and controlling for medication effects.

Regarding confounders, the presence of structural GPi abnormalities on MRI was associated with greater dystonia severity, providing evidence that the structural integrity of pallidal tissue contributes to dystonia's clinical expression. Structural GPi involvement occurred in similar proportion across aetiological groups in our cohort.

Limitations and future directions

Despite representing one of the largest reported cohorts of LFP data in dystonia, limitations include the relatively small sample size, inherent to invasive recordings; this limits statistical power and the number of covariates that can be reliably studied. Both our genetic and acquired groups were heterogeneous; although representative of the clinical population, this precluded direct comparison between specific genetic aetiologies. This should be investigated in larger studies. One-year follow-up data was only available for 14 patients; however, this was not considered a limitation as the follow-up data increased power for the severity analyses by allowing changes in this state-level marker, while averaging across timepoints in the aetiology analysis mitigated state-related variability to facilitate identification of this stable, trait-level marker. In our pallidopallidal coherence analyses, the presence of non-linear relationships and interactions inevitably increased model parameters over recommended numbers; results should be treated as exploratory, with replication in larger cohorts recommended.

Recordings were obtained during clinical appointments without systematic assessment of voluntary or involuntary movement or arousal levels beyond awake/asleep state; future studies should incorporate objective monitoring. Comparative studies including other hyperkinetic disorders will be essential to establish the specificity of these physiomarkers to dystonia. The cross-sectional nature of the study precludes determining whether the observed LFP patterns represent a cause or an epiphenomenon of dystonia. Longitudinal studies defining developmental trajectories of cortico-basal ganglia LFP activity may provide further insights. This study focused on associations with the BFMDRS as a severity measure. It remains to be established whether any longterm treatment-related changes in BFMDRS correlate with changes in LFP markers. For the development of future adaptive DBS systems, it will be important to determine whether LFP changes relate to more rapid fluctuations in severity and which behavioural or physiological measures are most sensitive to these changes.

Conclusion

Pallidal LFP markers are associated with dystonia severity in children but show different characteristics from previously-reported adult cohorts. These findings support the potential of pallidal LFPs to characterise dystonia across the lifespan and highlight the need for longitudinal studies of their developmental and clinical trajectories.
