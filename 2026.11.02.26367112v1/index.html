Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Text Sentence
p=0.067 The level change was not significant (p=0.067).
p=.239 In regard to the percent of 15-minute blocks with bed variance of 20 or more, there was no evidence of a statistically significant difference in the pre-DCC and postimplementation trends (p=.239); (see Figure 4).

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Hospitals globally are under increasing strain due to rising demand and constrained capacity. This interrupted-time-series study evaluates the impact of a digital coordination centre, implemented on 28 August 2023, where a multidisciplinary operational team (comprising data scientists, patient flow managers, facilities managers, patient transport and nursing staff, for example) use real-time data dashboards showing measures of emergency department (ED) crowding and inpatient utilisation to optimise patient flow in an Australian metropolitan tertiary hospital. Analysis of 195,385 emergency department arrivals admitted to acute care and 184,454 acute inpatient episodes between July 2020 and June 2025 showed substantial improvements in measures of patient flow: 33% decrease in ED length of stay, 73% increase in hospital admissions from the ED within four hours of arrival, 8% decrease in inpatient length of stay, and 5% increase in discharges completed before noon. These changes coincided with a broader programme of organisational and operational reform, of which the DCC was a central integrating component. Hence, the findings support digitally enabled coordination centres as an effective contribution to the overall strategy for improving hospital operational efficiency.

Introduction

Health systems internationally are under increasing pressures from rising demand, workforce shortages, and constrained hospital capacity; challenges that have been exacerbated by population ageing, patient complexity, and the COVID-19 pandemic. [1, 2] Under these conditions, impaired patient flow (the movement of patients from emergency presentation through admission, inpatient care, and discharge) leads to emergency department (ED) crowding, ED boarding (where patients are held in the ED because no inpatient beds are free), prolonged length of stay, and poorer patient outcomes. [1, [3] [4] [5] countries (including England, Canada, Ireland, New Zealand and Australia) have set targets to encourage timely admission and discharge or are considering operational strategies and funding that aim to reduce ED boarding (United States). [6, 7] Nonetheless, many hospitals continue to experience persistent bottlenecks driven by delays in inpatient capacity and discharge processes.

Given limited opportunities to increase capacity, addressing these challenges by improving internal coordination among departments and services has become a critical operational strategy for hospitals. Optimal patient flow depends on coordinated action across multiple domains, including bed management, diagnostics, discharge planning, and transport services. [8] Fragmentation and poor communication among those services limit a hospital's ability to respond dynamically and in real time to changing demand; by contrast, timely and accurate sharing of information, supported by digital systems, can enable effective coordination and adaptive decision-making. [9] [10] [11] [12] To address this need, digital command and coordination centres have emerged as a model to integrate data, teams, and decision-making to improve operational awareness and patient flow. Such centres are typically centralised operational hubs in which co-located, multidisciplinary teams use real-time data from hospital systems including electronic medical records in order to provide a whole-ofhospital view of capacity and demand. Structured huddles, escalation protocols, and decision-making authority to convert shared situational awareness into coordinated action across the hospital enable teams to proactively manage patient flow, [13] often within a wider program of organisational reform. By co-locating operational functions such as bed management and patient-flow coordination with shared dashboards and interdisciplinary teams, coordination centres aim to improve situational awareness, reduce fragmentation, and accelerate coordinated responses to environmental pressures.

Early evidence suggests that coordination centres can improve operational efficiency and patient flow, including reductions in length of stay and improvements in discharge coordination. [13] [14] [15] [16] Importantly, these centres support a shift from reactive to proactive system management by enabling real-time monitoring and predictive planning of demand and capacity. [18] However, there have been few attempts to quantify the contribution of these centres on operational efficiency. Given the investment required for these interventions, robust evaluations are needed to determine their effectiveness and inform future decision-making by policy makers and hospital management teams. The present study addresses this gap by evaluating the implementation of a Digital Coordination Centre (DCC) at the Royal Melbourne Hospital (RMH). We conducted an interrupted-timeseries study to estimate changes in key ED, inpatient, and escalation metrics after DCC implementation. We used 'seasonal autoregressive integrated moving average time-series analyses with an exogenous variable' (SARIMAX) estimators to adjust for pre-existing trends, seasonality and autocorrelation.

The level change using the linear model also differed significantly from the end of the pre-DCC period to the start of the post-implementation period, with an immediate increase of 0.90 percentage points in discharges prior to 12pm (95% CI: 0.28, 1.51; p=0.004). The change in rate suggests there were 690 additional timely discharges by 12pm per year in the post-implementation period, a 5% improvement compared to the projected number.

Discussion

This study evaluated the contribution of implementing a Digital Coordination Centre (DCC) at a large tertiary hospital using interrupted-time-series methods. DCC implementation was associated with significant operational improvements in several patient-flow outcomes, including reduced ED length of stay for admitted patients, increased proportion of ED admissions occurring within four hours, reduced acute inpatient length of stay, and increased discharges before noon. When translated into operational units, these changes corresponded to substantial annualised capacity gains: approximately 120,000 fewer ED hours, 9,100 additional timely ED admissions, 14,000 fewer acute inpatient bed-days, and 700 additional discharges before noon per year. As noted in the design and setting, the DCC was implemented alongside, and helped operationalise, a broader package of organisational changes, including a centralised Patient Flow Coordinator team, a purpose-built transit lounge with real-time digital workflows, and general medicine bed and model-of-care reforms. Hence, the DCC represents an integrating mechanism for these changes, rather than standing as an isolated intervention. Indeed, the DCC may function as a visible manifestation of a broader shift in organisational priorities rather than the sole causal mechanism.

The escalation metrics provide a more nuanced interpretation. Moderate operational-strain thresholds improved for both bed variance and the combined Ambulance Victoria / waiting room queue. In contrast, the most severe thresholds did not consistently improve. This pattern of improvement at moderate thresholds, and no improvement at the most severe threshold, is consistent with a coordination centre optimising the use of latent capacity rather than creating new capacity, for example, accelerating bed allocation, prioritising discharges, coordinating cross-functional action. At extreme demand, when structural constraints dominate, real-time coordination is unlikely to be sufficient. These findings suggest that DCCs should be understood as capacity-optimising interventions, not substitutes for adequate beds, staffing, and downstream care capacity.

A slight level increase in the higher bed variance threshold was observed after DCC implementation, though a declining trend continued. The slight increase may represent some reversion to the mean, or it may be due to improved recording fidelity after DCC implementation. That is, the DCC itself may introduce measurement bias by improving recording fidelity, especially given the improved tracking of escalation metrics in response to DCC implementation. Improved recording fidelity is itself an operational improvement (e.g., a bed marked available sooner enables earlier cleaning and admission).

The RMH Digital Coordination Centre was conceived and implemented in the context of burgeoning strain on hospital systems in Australia and internationally, with record high urgent presentations and ED boarding. In this context, the observed improvements are operationally and clinically meaningful because ED crowding and prolonged boarding are associated with delayed care, worse outcomes, and increased length of stay. [19] [20] [21] Previous studies have reported improvements in operational metrics such as ED throughput, bed utilisation, and discharge coordination following implementation of similar models. However, previous literature has relied on descriptive analyses, [9, 22, 23] has not taken the time-series structure or seasonality into account, [14] have included data during the COVID-19 pandemic, [11, 15] or have focused on the ICU, [14]-limiting their ability to draw conclusions about the hospital-wide contribution of a command-centre intervention. Moreover, differences in hospital organisation and processes within and between countries makes comparison of outcomes difficult across studies. [24] The results align with broader systems-engineering perspectives on healthcare op-erations. [9] Hospitals function as complex sociotechnical systems in which delays or inefficiencies in one area can propagate throughout the system. Command or coordination centres attempt to address this complexity by integrating operational data, facilitating multidisciplinary collaboration, and enabling proactive management of system pressures. [18] The results from this study support the hypothesis that digitally enabled coordination may contribute to improvements in hospital flow when dashboards, co-located expertise, escalation protocols, and executive decision-making are integrated into daily operations.

The present findings are broadly consistent with reports from previous hospital command-centre evaluations, which have described improvements in measures including bed allocation, transfers, emergency department boarding, and other aspects of patient flow. A scoping review of hospital capacity command centres found that all included studies showed improvement in at least some performance measures, although considerable heterogeneity in command-centre design and evidence quality limits the generalisability of these findings. [25] For example, implementation of the Johns Hopkins Capacity Command Center was associated with reduced patient-flow delays despite an increase in hospital occupancy. [9] However, not all evaluations have demonstrated consistent improvements. An interrupted time-series evaluation at Bradford Royal Infirmary in the UK found modest improvements in some stages of emergency department flow but no significant or consistent improvement in overall length of stay or several other flow measures. [11] Differences in command-centre functions, baseline digital maturity, concurrent improvement initiatives, healthcare-system context, outcome definitions, and study designs substantially limit direct comparison between reported effect sizes. The findings of the present study should therefore be interpreted within their particular organisational and health-system context rather than assumed to generalise to all command-centre implementations.

This study has several strengths that support the credibility of its findings. First, the analysis used a long observation period spanning more than three years prior to implementation and over one year following the establishment of the DCC. This extended time frame allowed underlying temporal trends to be identified and statistically adjusted in the modelling process, making it possible to compare observed post-implementation outcomes with counterfactual projections.

Second, interrupted time series analysis is a strong study design within the limitations of this kind of observational data. In the presence of steep pre-intervention trends, a crude comparison of means before and after an intervention can produce a paradoxical result where the difference in means suggests a change in one direction but the shift in level and trend suggest the opposite, as with the AV waiting room queues and bed variance escalation triggers, underlining the importance of modelling the trends over time. Furthermore, the study applied SARIMAX time-series models to account for seasonality, autocorrelation, and other statistical properties commonly present in health system data. Failing to account for these temporal features can lead to biased statistical inferences, including underestimated or inflated standard errors, incorrect confidence in-tervals, and inaccurate p-values, thereby increasing the risk of false-positive or false-negative findings. [26, 27] In addition, unmodelled seasonality or serial dependence may result in intervention effects being incorrectly attributed to the intervention itself when they instead reflect underlying temporal patterns in the data. [28, 29] Diagnostic testing indicated that the models adequately captured the temporal structure of the data, supporting the validity of the estimated effects. This use of time-series evaluation aligns with best-practice recommendations when randomisation is not feasible for hospital-wide interventions and data on a non-intervention comparison group are not available. [27] Third, the evaluation examined multiple complementary outcomes across both ED and inpatient domains. Improvements observed across several related metrics strengthen confidence that the intervention affected system-level patient flow rather than producing isolated or artefactual changes in individual measures. The inclusion of escalation-trigger analyses is a particular point of novelty because they link outcomes to operational "strain" indicators that plausibly mediate patient flow and are conceptually consistent with the real-time threshold logic embedded in DCC or command-centre interventions.

Finally, the analysis translated statistical findings into operationally meaningful estimates, such as total hours saved in the ED and total inpatient days avoided. These estimates are important for executive decision-making, because they connect statistical findings to capacity, service delivery, and potential return on investment.

Several limitations should also be considered when interpreting these findings. First, it cannot be definitively concluded that the observed improvements are solely attributable to the DCC. Other concurrent organisational initiatives, changes in staffing, or broader health-system trends may also have contributed to the observed changes. This is partly a limitation of conducting a single-site study. As noted in design and setting, multiple organisational initiatives focused on improving inpatient access were implemented during the study period. These initiatives may also account for the improvements in metrics observed throughout the study. Nonetheless, the DCC functions as an integrating hub that aligns, coordinates and amplifies the impact of these initiatives, making it a critical enabling mechanism underpinning system-wide improvements in patient access and flow.

Second, though our results were generally robust to different modelling choices in overall direction, some choices inevitably affect the size of the estimates. While the study excluded the initial months of the COVID-19 pandemic with the most drastic differences in activity observed in the data, Victoria continued to experience restrictions and altered patterns of hospital use into 2021. To the degree that restrictions suppressed emergency department presentations and hospitalisations, inclusion of this period may have exaggerated pre-implementation trends, though the volatility of the ED and inpatient metrics measured in this study had reduced substantially by 6 July 2020, when data analysis began for this study (see the average weekly ED and inpatient arrivals from 2020 to 2026 in the Supplementary materials, S3). The annualised percentage-change estimates are calculated under the assumption that the counterfactual is a continuation of the pre-implementation trend. While modelling the counterfactual on observed data is a defensible assumption, assuming a flat trend for the counterfactual would have produced more conservative percentage-change estimates.

Second, the analysis focused on operational performance metrics and did not examine patient-centred outcomes such as patient experience, safety outcomes, or clinical quality indicators. Although improved patient flow is expected to have downstream benefits for patients and providers [3] [4] [5], further research is needed to confirm these effects. Moreover, while reducing unnecessary time in hospital is beneficial, hospitals must take care not to reduce necessary stays. Inclusion of patient-centred outcomes would examine this.

Third, some operational metrics, particularly escalation indicators, may be influenced by factors external to hospital control, such as community demand, ambulance availability, or seasonal variations in illness patterns. Although the time-series models adjusted for temporal patterns, these external factors remain important contextual considerations; command-centre evaluations are conducted within complex and dynamic settings where attribution must be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Finally, the observed effects likely reflect the combined influence of data infrastructure, dashboard design, co-located operational teams, governance routines, escalation protocols, and cultural change. This study was not designed to isolate the marginal contribution of each component. Moreover, the particular design and implementation of coordination centres within hospitals are necessarily specific to their organisational context and operational setting. Hence, providing evidence that this particular implementation was successful may not generalise to the implementation of coordination centres elsewhere.

Despite these limitations, the findings have important implications for hospital management and health system policy. Digital coordination centres may improve patient flow by integrating real-time data with multidisciplinary operational teams, supporting a shift from reactive crisis management toward proactive system coordination. [18] However, technological infrastructure alone is unlikely to be sufficient. Improvements depend on embedding dashboards within governance structures, escalation processes, clearly defined responsibilities, and models of care that translate information into timely, coordinated action. [30] Digital coordination centres should therefore be understood as sociotechnical and organisational interventions rather than simply digital dashboards.

Future research should examine whether these findings are replicable across different hospital settings using stronger designs, including controlled interrupted time series, stepped-wedge approaches, or, where feasible, site-level randomisation. Evaluations should also incorporate measures of case mix, acuity, comorbidity, diagnostic complexity, and service demand to better account for time-varying confounding and determine whether improvements persist across differing levels of system pressure [31, 32]. This is particularly important given the mixed command-centre evidence base and heterogeneity in implementation and study design. [25] Further work should investigate the mechanisms through which coordination centres affect hospital operations, including communication, decision-making, workflows, and organisational culture. Mixed-methods studies involving staff and patients could support development of a theoretical framework linking DCC processes to operational and patient outcomes [25, 33]. Evaluations should include patient-centred measures such as re-presentations, readmissions, adverse events, mortality, and PROMs/PREMs to ensure improvements in flow do not occur at the expense of care quality. [34, 35] Predictive analytics may provide an additional opportunity to strengthen DCC function. Models using historical and real-time hospital data can forecast demand, admission risk, prolonged length of stay, and bed availability, potentially enabling earlier intervention before bottlenecks develop. [36] [37] [38] 41? ? ] However, their value will similarly depend on governance and workflows that translate predictions into operational action.

Finally, economic evaluation is needed to determine the DCC's return on investment and broader health-system value. Future studies should estimate savings associated with reduced length of stay, improved bed turnover, and reduced ED boarding, alongside cost-utility analyses incorporating quality-adjusted life years where appropriate. Such evidence would assist health-system leaders and policymakers in determining whether digital coordination centres warrant wider implementation.

In summary, the implementation of a Digital Coordination Centre at a large tertiary hospital was associated with significant improvements in several key patient-flow metrics, including emergency department throughput, inpatient length of stay, and discharge timing. These improvements suggest that digitally enabled coordination models may contribute to more efficient hospital operations in increasingly pressured healthcare systems. While further research is needed to confirm these findings in other settings and to understand the mechanisms underlying these improvements, the results contribute valuable evidence to the emerging literature on hospital command and coordination centres.

Methods

Design & setting

This study is a single-site, retrospective, segmented interrupted-time-series study of the Digital Coordination Centre (DCC), a hospital-wide operational hub designed to improve patient flow, capacity management, and coordination of care across the Royal Melbourne Hospital (RMH), a public, tertiary referral teaching hospital in Victoria, Australia. The RMH is a large public tertiary/quaternary referral and teaching hospital providing both general and highly specialised services, including major trauma, cardiac, neuroscience, oncology, intensive care, and infectious diseases services. It receives referrals from metropolitan, regional and rural Victoria, as well as interstate, managing a complex case mix and substantial demand for specialist and acute inpatient capacity. The hospital operates within Victoria's publicly funded health system, in which hospital activity and capacity must be managed within government funding and resource allocations. This context differs from predominantly private or insurance-funded hospital systems, such as that of the United States, and places considerable emphasis on optimising the use of finite public hospital beds, workforce, and other resources.

The DCC integrates real-time data from electronic medical records and hospital information systems into digital dashboards that provide a whole-of-hospital view of operational status, including emergency department activity, inpatient bed availability, predicted discharges, escalation triggers, and patient-flow pressures. This digital context is also relevant to the generalisability of the intervention. Adoption and maturity of electronic medical records and interoperable clinical information systems remain variable across Australian hospitals, meaning that not all health services have equivalent access to the integrated, near-realtime data infrastructure required to reproduce the RMH model. Consequently, implementation of a comparable coordination centre elsewhere may require substantial preceding investment in digital infrastructure, data integration, and data governance.

Importantly, the DCC is not solely a technological intervention. Operational teams responsible for functions such as bed management, patient-flow coordination, nursing allocation, environmental services, facilities management, and transport coordination are co-located within the centre. Bringing these functions together provides a common operational picture and facilitates rapid communication and collaborative decision-making across services that may otherwise operate separately. The DCC was implemented as part of a broader transformation strategy intended to shift hospital operations from reactive crisis management toward proactive, system-wide coordination. By combining integrated digital dashboards with multidisciplinary operational governance, the DCC aims to improve situational awareness, identify bottlenecks earlier, and facilitate timely interventions to optimise patient movement through the hospital. The model draws on command-centre approaches used in industries such as aviation, logistics, and emergency management, adapting these principles to the healthcare setting to support more efficient allocation of beds, staffing, and other operational resources. [42] As this was a real-world study conducted in a non-laboratory setting, several initiatives were implemented during the study period, and some in preparation for it. For example, a Transit Lounge model-of-care was launched early in 2023 (about six months prior to DCC implementation in August 2023) that includes digital workflows and real-time dashboards, which were subsequently actively monitored by the DCC. Around the same time, a Patient Flow Co-ordinator team commenced, which subsequently operated within the DCC. As these initiatives commenced during the pre-DCC period, against which the post-DCC period is compared, the contribution of DCC implementation to patient outcomes may be attenuated. Hence, this intervention could not be isolated from several other initiatives, some of which were implemented during the study period and some in preparation for it.

On the other hand, the 'fast track and rapid stay' model of care, operationalised in the ED in January 2024, was focused on flow of non-admitted patients and is therefore unlikely to have had a strong effect on metrics reported in this analysis, as these focus on admitted patients (see 'Participants and eligibility criteria' below). Similarly, the Victorian Virtual ED (an initiative external to RMH) focused on non-admitted patients, and began during the pre-DCC period, so will likely have similar impacts upon the pre-DCC and post-implementation periods.

A retrospective longitudinal time-series evaluation using 'seasonal autoregressive integrated moving average models with exogenous intervention terms' (SARI-MAX; described further in 'Statistical analyses') was undertaken as part of a mixed-methods evaluation of the impact of introducing the DCC at the Royal Melbourne Hospital. The study was approved by the hospital's human research ethics committee (protocol no. QA2023089). This paper reports the impact of the DCC introduction on patient flow metrics, constituting the quantitative component of the multi-methods evaluation. [42, 43]

Data and observation periods

Data were extracted from the hospital's data warehouse on 3 July 2025. Bed variance and escalation-metrics data were available from 17 August 2020. However, escalation metric values were relatively low for the first couple of weeks of data collection for bed variance and until half-way through November for the Ambulance Victoria and wait room metrics (see Figure S1 and S2 in Supplementary Material S1 to see an illustration of these values in the time series). These low values appear to be the result of operational factors where hospital staff took some time to regularly report escalation metrics. In order to ensure that the impact of the DCC intervention is not over estimated, the pre-DCC period was set to start on 31 August 2020 for bed variance and 16 November 2020 for the Ambulance Victoria and waiting room queues.

The remaining metrics were available from 2017 onwards. However, because early COVID-19 pandemic restrictions in Victoria suppressed emergency department presentations and hospital activity, only data after 6 July 2020 were used in analyses; [44] while COVID-19 lockdowns continued in Victoria beyond this time, examination of the metrics in time-series shows a reduction in marked volatility of the data during the COVID-19 period by July, 2020 (see Supplementary materials, S2). Measures were aggregated as weekly averages to address weekly fluctuation patterns and day-to-day noise while preserving sufficient temporal resolution for interrupted-time-series modelling.

Weeks were assigned to a pre-implementation period (from 6 July 2020 to 27 August 2023) and a post-implementation period (from Monday, 27 November 2023 to Monday, 30 June 2025). A 13-week transition period (from 28 August 2023 to 26 November 2023), when the DCC was being implemented and staff were being trained, was excluded from analyses.

Note, however, that as some aspects of the DCC implementation were implemented in early 2023 (specifically, a Patient Flow Co-ordinator team was introduced, a purpose-built transit lounge was opened, and digital workflows and real-time dashboards were implemented) a sensitivity analysis was conducted whereby the DCC implementation period was set to Monday, 13 February 2023, instead of 28 August 2023.

Outcomes

Two emergency department (ED) outcomes were analysed for presentations leading to acute hospital admissions: length of stay in the ED (the time in hours a patient spent in the ED from arrival to inpatient admission) and the proportion of ED patients admitted to an inpatient ward within four hours of presentation. The latter aligns with the four-hour National Emergency Access Target (NEAT) used in Australian public hospital performance reporting. [45] For inpatient metrics, length of stay and percent of discharges completed prior to 12pm were included. Greater proportion of inpatient discharges completed before midday may increase bed availability and reduce ED boarding, though recent evidence is mixed. [10, 46] Two metrics related to ED boarding and representing sufficient bed capacity to meet demand were examined. When the measures reach certain thresholdsinformed by historical data and developed over an 18-month period through consultation with informatics and clinical operations teams prior to the DCC implementation -they create 'escalation triggers'. These triggers prompt the operations teams to form decision-making huddles and consider actions such as opening of closed and transit lounge beds, bringing in additional staff, and prioritising (safe) discharges.

The first escalation trigger, 'bed variance,' is the anticipated number of required admissions calculated from historical averages, minus unallocated beds (according to a midnight census) and discharges over the course of the day. The second escalation trigger, Ambulance Victoria (AV) / waiting room queue, is the number of patients being attended to by ambulance crews in corridors plus patients in the waiting room queue waiting to be brought into an acute treatment area in the ED (further details regarding the capacity management levels associated with escalation triggers are provided in the Supplementary materials, S2). The AV/waiting room queue is a measure of ED boarding that results in a delay to definitive emergency care and can affect ambulance response times and community safety. [47] Both metrics were recorded in 15-minute increments and displayed in the DCC dashboards as threshold indicators: for bed variance, escalation thresholds were 10 to 19 (amber) and 20 or more (red) (Hospital) deficit of beds (i.e. bed variance); and for ambulance/waiting room queues, 6 to 19 (amber) and 20 or more (red) (Hospital) patients waiting for a treatment space. These categories were selected because they correspond to operationally meaningful escalation thresholds used and displayed separately in the Digital Coordination Centre (DCC) dashboards. The analysis examined the average weekly proportion of 15-minute increments where these thresholds were exceeded. Each category was analysed as a separate outcome, rather than using cumulative thresholds (�10 and �20) or mean bed variance, to preserve the operational distinction between moderate and more severe capacity pressures.

Participants and eligibility criteria

Emergency department

Only ED presentations linked to an inpatient admission were included in the analysis. Mental-health related, rapid-stay mental health and emergency behaviouralassessment presentations and admissions were excluded (n = 217,820) from analysis of 423,642 ED presentations, as mental-health bed access and control are managed separately from general hospital patient flow, leaving 205,822. After removing 10,437 ED arrivals during the transition period, 195,385 arrivals remained across 244 weeks. Data for these arrivals were summarised to provide weekly averages, leaving 244 data points for the ED length-of-stay and dischargebefore-noon metrics (164 in the pre-DCC period and 79 in the post-transition period).

Inpatient measures

Inpatient length-of-stay (LoS) and the percentage of inpatients discharged prior to 12pm were examined. These were provided for each patient admission, from which weekly averages were calculated. Only acute admissions (n=542,758) were included as the DCC is less likely to impact other types of admissions (e.g., mental health, palliative, or rehabilitative care); 22,932 non-acute admissions were excluded. Same day admissions (i.e., patients discharged on the same day they were admitted) were excluded (n = 339,528) as the DCC aims to target more complex presentations with barriers to discharge. Inpatient stays from the 'Hospital in the Home' program were also excluded (n = 5,965), as the DCC does not manage these patients.

The DCC was not expected to impact unusually long stays as barriers to discharge are usually due to external, social factors outside of the DCC's purview, such as waiting to appoint a guardian, or for disability housing to become available. Hence, episodes exceeding 35 days (n = 1,832) were excluded. As length of stay is logged at discharge, longer stays would not yet be recorded for the final five weeks (equivalent to excluding episodes beyond 35 days), artificially lowering average LoS at this time; hence, the final five weeks were omitted from LoS calculations (n = 515 excluded). Finally, 10,464 admissions during the transition period were excluded. This left 184,454 admissions. Weekly averages were calculated from these admissions, leaving 240 data points for each metric (164 in the pre-DCC period and 75 in the post-transition period for length-of-stay, but 79 for discharge data).

Statistical analyses

Interrupted time-series analyses were conducted to compare the level and rate of change in each metric during each period. Specifically, seasonal autoregressive integrated moving average time-series analyses with an exogenous variable (SARI-MAX) were implemented. The treatment variable is an indicator representing the post-implementation period.

SARIMAX modelling was used to account for the temporal structure of the data, including autocorrelation and seasonality, with differencing incorporated where necessary to address non-stationarity. Model adequacy was assessed through residual diagnostics, including checks for remaining serial correlation. Seasonality was assessed by comparing yearly (52-week), quarterly (13-week), and non-seasonal candidate models. Seasonal specifications were retained only when seasonal autoregressive or moving-average components were present and model fit was superior, or within 2 Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) units of the best-fitting model; otherwise, non-seasonal models were selected on grounds of parsimony. Model diagnostics and assumption tests are provided in the Supplementary Materials,S4.

The impact of the DCC is reported as two measures for each outcome. The first impact measure is on the trend difference: a comparison of the pre-DCC and post-implementation slopes to test whether implementation of the DCC had a statistically significant impact on the trend over time. The second impact measure is the level difference: the change in outcome from the end of the pre-DCC to the beginning of the post-implementation period, estimated from the linear model. Effect estimates were presented with 95% confidence intervals and p-values.

For hypothesis testing and 95% confidence intervals, following SARIMAX adjustment, a segmented joint linear regression model was fitted to the model-adjusted values to estimate the pre-DCC and post-implementation slopes, the difference between slopes, and the level change at the intervention boundary from the end of the pre-intervention to the start of the post-intervention periods. Heteroscedasticity-consistent (HC3) robust standard errors were calculated for the joint linear regression model. These robust standard errors were used to derive 95% confidence intervals and p-values for the estimated slopes, differences in slopes and level changes. Thus, SARIMAX modelling addressed the temporal dependence structure of the data, whereas HC3 robust standard errors were applied at the subsequent linear regression stage to account for potential heteroscedasticity. Model adequacy was evaluated using residual diagnostics, including tests of autocorrelation, normality and heteroscedasticity. All analyses were conducted in R version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

To interpret the magnitude of the intervention's effect, we estimated the cumulative, annualised effect of the DCC during the observed post-implementation period. To do so, we imputed the model's predicted average outcome per episode of care (e.g., bed-days per admission) for each week in the post-transition period with the DCC and without the DCC (the counterfactual projection assuming the pre-DCC trajectory had continued without the intervention) using the linear model fitted to the SARIMAX-adjusted values. Weekly differences were multiplied by the relevant weekly activity denominator (e.g., number of admissions), summed across the post-implementation period, annualised (e.g., bed-days per year), and expressed as both absolute units saved or gained, and relative percentage difference using the counterfactual as the denominator. The annualised figures thus represent the estimated, cumulative real-world difference integrated across all post-implementation weeks.
