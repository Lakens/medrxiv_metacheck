Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Services that rely on people to recognise their own weight and set their own goals may miss those who do not see themselves as overweight. We examined how adults perceive their weight, whether genetic risk of obesity, measured body size or early weight history explains who under-perceives it, and how perception relates to the weight-management goal they set, in a direct-to-consumer genetics cohort that includes people who have not sought care for their weight (FitnessGenes; N = 20,617). Although 61% of participants were overweight or obese by BMI, only 37% described themselves as such; among those overweight or obese by BMI, 64.0% placed themselves in a lighter category than their BMI indicated. Under-perception was more common in men than in women (73.5% vs 51.6%), in younger adults and at lower BMI, and a sex difference in the same direction, though smaller, was present when non-recognition of overweight was assessed against waist-to-height ratio rather than BMI. FTO rs9939609 behaved as an obesity risk variant (+0.46 kg/m² BMI and OR 1.18 for recalled childhood obesity per risk allele) but did not predict under-perception at a given BMI (per-allele OR 0.97, 95% CI 0.92-1.03), and neither did MC4R rs17782313 nor a combined score. A recalled history of childhood obesity, by contrast, was associated with heavier self-perception at the same adult BMI (+10.0 percentage points). At the same BMI, participants who under-perceived their weight were about half as likely to set a weight-loss goal (26.8% vs 47.5%; OR 0.52, 95% CI 0.48-0.56); among those obese by BMI, 49% who recognised it chose weight loss, against 18% who did not. Genotype did not predict goalsetting beyond its effect on BMI. Men were less likely both to recognise overweight and, having recognised it, to choose weight loss. Genetic risk of obesity therefore identifies who is likely to be heavier but not who will recognise it, and self-perception marks a group, mainly men and those at lower BMI, whom self-directed weight-management services are likely to under-reach.

1 Introduction

Direct-to-consumer (DTC) genetic testing has produced large cohorts in which genotype, body size and self-reported health behaviour are recorded for the same individuals. Unlike clinical samples, these cohorts include people who have not sought care for their weight and may not regard it as a concern.

They therefore show how people judge their own health, and how that judgement relates to what they intend to do about it, at a scale clinical datasets rarely reach.

Whether a person acts on their weight depends in part on recognition: whether they perceive themselves as overweight. Many adults who are overweight by clinical criteria do not describe themselves as such, and those who do not recognise their overweight are less likely to attempt weight management (Duncan et al., 2011; Gruszka et al., 2022). The size of the shortfall varies with setting, sex and the measure used: among 540 adult women in Erbil, Iraq, 79.1% classified their weight status correctly and only 13.3% underestimated it, although underestimation still outnumbered overestimation (Shabu et al., 2024). As overweight has become more prevalent, the reference against which people judge their own size appears to have shifted upward with it (Robinson, 2017). Self-perception also influences whether people engage with structured weight-management programmes, including digital ones (Sharpe et al., 2017). If perception rather than measured size determines intention, services that depend on self-referral or self-set goals will reach people according to how they see themselves rather than according to need. Who under-perceives, and whether under-perception relates to the goals people set, are therefore practical questions for such services.

Under-perception may also have determinants beyond current body size. One candidate is genetic. Body weight is partly heritable, and common genetic variants confer measurable risk of obesity (Loos and Yeo, 2022). The most robustly replicated of these lies at the FTO locus: each copy of the rs9939609 A allele is associated with higher body weight and adiposity, across ancestries and from childhood onwards (Frayling et al., 2007; Yang et al., 2012; Loos and Yeo, 2022), largely through increased appetite and energy intake (Cecil et al., 2008; Speakman, 2015). FTO has also been linked to eating behaviour and affect beyond its effect on BMI (Harbron et al., 2014; Koike et al., 2018), and in an exploratory study of thirty healthy young men the risk allele was associated with higher emotional eating scores (Brown et al., 2026). None of this work examined body perception directly, but it makes an influence on how carriers appraise their own bodies plausible enough to test. Some FTO associations, such as those with type 2 diabetes and related metabolic traits, are explained entirely by its effect on adiposity (Frayling et al., 2007; Freathy et al., 2008); others, such as that with depression, persist after adjustment for BMI (Samaan et al., 2013). Whether genetic risk of obesity shapes self-perception independently of body size has not, to our knowledge, been tested. If it did, genotype could help identify people likely to underperceive their weight; if it does not, genetic risk information alone cannot.

A second candidate is developmental. Higher childhood BMI prospectively predicts later body dissatisfaction (Blundell et al., 2024), and an early history of obesity may set the reference against which adults judge their own size.

We used a DTC genetics cohort in which genotype, self-reported height and weight, self-perceived body type, recalled childhood obesity and a stated weight-management goal were recorded for the same individuals. We asked four questions. First, how closely self-perceived body type agrees with BMI category, and who under-perceives. Second, whether genetic risk of obesity, at FTO rs9939609 and independently MC4R rs17782313, is associated with under-perception at a given BMI, having first confirmed that these variants confer the expected higher BMI and childhood obesity in this cohort.

Third, whether a recalled history of childhood obesity is associated with under-perception at a given BMI. Fourth, whether under-perception is associated with the weight-management goal a person sets, and whether genotype adds to measured size in predicting it.

2 Materials and Methods

2.1 Study design and participants

We performed a cross-sectional analysis of the FitnessGenes direct-to-consumer genetics cohort.

Participants were adult customers at registration who had genetic testing and completed an onboarding questionnaire capturing self-reported anthropometry, self-perceived body type, and childhood obesity history. Association models required non-missing FTO rs9939609 risk and self-perceived body type (N = 20,695). Analyses that additionally required a BMI category -the sample description in Table 1 and the concordance analyses of Section 3.3 -were restricted to the 20,617 participants with all three (BMI category was missing for 78 otherwise-eligible participants). Thirty-two records (0.15%) carry an implausible recorded age -21 below 18 years and 11 above 100, including five near 950 years, consistent with placeholder birth dates. Age enters the models only as a covariate and excluding these records changes no estimate materially (for example, the per-allele FTO association with self-perception moves from +2.43 to +2.39 percentage points), so they were retained; Table 1 reports the age distribution both with and without them. Every analytic sample used in the paper is listed with its inclusion criteria in Supplementary Table S3. The mediation sample additionally required recalled childhood obesity status (N = 10,637; the high-versus-low-risk contrast used for mediation comprised 5,626). Registration and questionnaire dates were not retained in the de-identified extract, so the accrual window cannot be specified. Customer data were used for research under the consent recorded at registration.

2.2 Ethics statement

The study was reviewed by the FitnessGenes internal Ethics Committee (reference FGEC/2026/012), which confirmed valid opt-in research consent, compliance with UK GDPR, and anonymisation of the dataset. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the local legislation and institutional requirements. Participants provided separate, opt-in electronic informed consent for the research use of their data at registration, before genetic testing, and could withdraw consent at any time. The research team had no access to personal identifying information.

2.3 Measures

Genotyping. FTO rs9939609 genotype was recorded as TT / TA / AA and mapped to a three-level risk grouping (TT = low, TA = moderate, AA = high; risk allele = A); records with an absent or ambiguous call (n = 364, of which 15 were ambiguous) were treated as missing throughout. The primary contrast was high (AA) versus low (TT) risk; a per-risk-allele (0/1/2) coding was used for dose-response and sexstratified models. DNA was obtained from saliva collected by the Oragene self-collection kit (DNA Genotek Inc., Ottawa, ON, Canada) and returned by post, and extracted by LGC Genomics (Hertfordshire, UK), following the sample-collection and extraction procedure described previously for a separate, smaller sample of the same provider's customers (West et al., 2018). Genotypes in the present extract came from two sources. For 67.9% of the analytic sample, they were called from a genome-wide Illumina Infinium Global Screening Array (versions 1, 2 and 3); for the remaining 32.1%, they were taken from genotype files that customers had uploaded from third-party consumer testing services. Call rates for FTO rs9939609 were 99.9% in the array subset and 95.1% in the uploaded subset, and for MC4R rs17782313 99.8% and 88.5%, respectively (Supplementary Table S4). Uploaded genotypes were accepted as supplied and were not recalled from raw intensity data, so their platform and qualitycontrol provenance cannot be verified. Genotypes were tested for Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium by goodness-of-fit χ² overall and by genotyping source. FTO rs9939609 is the primary exposure. MC4R rs17782313 is analysed separately as an independent check, because the two variants act through partly different biology. Its own association with BMI in this cohort is weak, however, so a matching null for MC4R is supportive rather than a true internal replication. A two-variant risk-allele score (range 0-4) is secondary, computed both unweighted and weighted by the per-allele BMI effects estimated in this cohort. Each variant and score is reported for every key outcome in Supplementary Table S2, with genotype frequencies, call rates and Hardy-Weinberg tests by genotyping source in Supplementary Table S4. The extract retained only the selected variants rather than genome-wide data, so a BMI polygenic score could not be constructed.

Anthropometry and BMI category.

Height and weight were self-reported. BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared and categorised using standard World Health Organization thresholds (World Health Organization, 2000): underweight (< 18.5 kg/m²), normal weight (18.5-24.9 kg/m²), overweight (25.0-29.9 kg/m²), obese class I (30.0-34.9 kg/m²), obese class II (35.0-39.9 kg/m²) and obese class III (≥ 40.0 kg/m²). Where a binary or three-level classification was required the three obese classes were combined, and "overweight or obese by BMI" denotes BMI ≥ 25.0 kg/m². BMI was used throughout as the reference against which self-perception was judged because it was available for all participants, whereas waist measurements were recorded for about one in five; waistto-height ratio, a better marker of central adiposity (Ashwell et al., 2012; Gibson and Ashwell, 2020), served as a sensitivity check. In a subset, waist circumference was self-reported in centimetres, and waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) was calculated as waist circumference divided by height. Physical activity was self-reported on a single five-level ordinal item ("How active you are, ranging from low to very active") with the response options none, light, moderate, active and very active; in the primary analytic sample (N = 20,617) these were chosen by 385, 3,183, 6,410, 7,513 and 3,126 participants respectively, and the item was modelled as an equally spaced ordinal score from 0 (none) to 4 (very active).

Self-perceived body type.

Participants selected one of five ordered categories: underweight, lean/fit, average, overweight, or obese. A binary overweight/obese self-perception (overweight or obese vs. others) was the outcome for the genotype-association models; under-classification, defined below, was the outcome for analyses of who under-perceives and of goal-setting. Two distinct quantities are reported and are not interchangeable. Under-classification denotes perceiving oneself in a lighter category than one's BMI category, so a participant with class II obesity who describes themselves as overweight is under-classifying; failure to recognise overweight denotes not describing oneself as overweight or obese at all. Among participants who were overweight or obese by BMI, 64.0% underclassified while 41.5% did not describe themselves as overweight or obese. Each quantity is named explicitly wherever it is used. For the descriptive concordance analysis, perceived categories were mapped onto clinical BMI bins (primary mapping: lean/fit and average → normal); a more tolerant mapping, in which "average" was treated as concordant with either normal or overweight BMI, was reported as a sensitivity analysis.

Recalled childhood obesity. A self-reported binary indicator of childhood obesity (yes/no), retrospectively recalled and available for 10,827 participants overall, of whom 10,609 fall in the primary analytic sample. The item asked whether the participant was obese during childhood, defined in the questionnaire as before the age of 16 years, with response options yes and no. Because the item records whether obesity was present in childhood rather than an age at onset, "recalled childhood obesity" here denotes a history of childhood-onset obesity.

Weight-management goal. At onboarding, participants selected one of four goals: get fit, lose weight, gain lean muscle, or body maintenance. A binary weight-loss goal (lose weight versus any other goal) was the outcome for the analyses in Section 3.6. The item was recorded for 19,221 participants.

2.4 Statistical analysis

Sample characteristics were summarised as mean (SD) or percentage. We described concordance between self-perceived body type and BMI category using row-normalised cross-tabulation and classified it as concordant, under-classifying, or over-classifying. Associations between FTO risk and binary overweight/obese self-perception were estimated with linear-probability (ordinary least squares) models, giving effects directly in percentage points. Primary models were adjusted for age and sex. These covariates are conventional rather than necessary here: genotype is fixed at conception and so is not confounded by lifestyle, and the per-allele FTO estimate is the same with and without them (+2.42 percentage points unadjusted, +2.43 adjusted). Population stratification does remain a potential confounder, and is addressed below. Models additionally adjusted for genotyping source were also examined. Heteroskedasticity-robust (HC3) standard errors were used for all linear-probability models.

As a confounding sensitivity analysis, we additionally adjusted for self-reported ethnicity in the subset with ethnicity recorded. As a secondary analysis addressing a distinct question, we adjusted for BMI to ask whether any association persists independently of actual body size; because BMI lies on the causal pathway, this model is not part of the primary inference, and conditioning on a mediator can introduce collider bias if unmeasured factors influence both BMI and self-perception (VanderWeele, 2016).

Mediation of the FTO-self-perception association by recalled childhood obesity was estimated with linear-probability models: the total effect (c) from a model of the outcome on exposure; the direct effect (c ) and mediator→outcome path (b) from a model additionally including the mediator; and the ′ exposure→mediator path (a) from a linear-probability model of the mediator on exposure. The indirect effect was estimated as a × b, which in linear models equals c -c , and the proportion mediated as ′ indirect/total (Preacher and Hayes, 2008; VanderWeele, 2016). Indirect effects and the proportion mediated were bootstrapped with 3,000 resamples (percentile 95% confidence intervals); the mediation decomposition is reported in Supplementary Figure S1 as a secondary analysis. To characterise the mediator, the association of per-allele FTO risk with recalled childhood obesity was also estimated by logistic regression, and the association of childhood onset with adult BMI by linear regression adjusted for age and sex. As a positive control, the per-allele association of FTO rs9939609 with BMI itself was estimated (+0.46 kg/m², 95% CI 0.33-0.59, p = 6 × 10⁻¹², n = 20,616), confirming that the genotype data behave as expected in this cohort. Sex-stratified per-allele effects and a sex-by-genotype interaction were tested, and an exploratory FTO × physical-activity interaction was examined. In a further sensitivity analysis we substituted waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) for BMI, in the subset with waist measurements. As additional robustness checks we refitted the primary association with logistic regression, restricted it to the largest ancestry group, and substituted an aggregate FTO+MC4R risk score for the single variant.

Secondary and sensitivity analyses were not corrected for multiple testing and are interpreted as supportive or exploratory. Associations with weight-loss goal-setting (Section 3.6) were estimated with the same linear-probability approach, entering self-perceived body type and BMI together so that each is estimated holding the other constant; a multinomial logistic model across all four goals with 'get fit' as the reference category, an ordered five-level perception predictor, restriction to the largest ancestry group, and adjustment for physical activity were examined as sensitivity analyses. Under-perception was additionally assessed against central adiposity (waist-to-height ratio ≥ 0.5) in the subset with waist measurements, and under-classification was recomputed excluding participants who described themselves as lean/fit, because that option describes fitness as well as size. Because self-perceived body type and the stated goal were reported in the same questionnaire, these associations are interpreted as recognition tracking intention rather than causing it; the composition of the four goal categories across perceived body types was examined to assess how far the association is definitional. Analyses were performed in Python 3.11.17 using pandas 2.3.3, numpy 2.4.6, scipy 1.17.1, statsmodels 0.15.0 and matplotlib 3.11.2. The study is reported in accordance with the STROBE guideline for observational studies, with the STREGA extensions for the genetic analyses.

3 Results

3.1 Sample characteristics

The primary analytic sample comprised 20,617 adults (Table 1). Mean age was 45.0 years (SD 12.5, excluding 32 implausible recorded ages; Section 2.1), 48.1% were women, and mean BMI was 27.7 kg/m² (SD 6.5); 61.2% were overweight or obese by BMI. FTO rs9939609 genotype frequencies were TT 37.5%, TA 46.8% and AA 15.7% (risk-allele frequency 0.391). Across the 20,929 records with a called genotype, genotype proportions departed mildly from those expected under Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (p = 0.009). The departure was detected in the array-genotyped subset (p = 0.011, n = 14,265) but not in the smaller uploaded-data subset (p = 0.36, n = 6,664), which has less power to detect it; the two subsets therefore cannot be said to differ. Population structure in a multi-ancestry cohort and a genotyping artefact in the array subset both remain possible. Strand misalignment, to which this A/T variant is vulnerable, does not: the A-allele frequency is 0.391 in both subsets, whereas a systematic flip in the uploaded data would give approximately 0.61 (Supplementary Table S4). Equal allele frequencies do not, however, exclude miscalling of heterozygotes, which would also produce the observed heterozygote deficit. Recalled childhood obesity was available for 10,609 participants in the primary analytic sample (yes, 1,473; no, 9,136); models of this item that do not require a called genotype include up to 10,797 participants.

Participants with and without recalled childhood-obesity data had similar BMI (27.5 vs 27.9 kg/m²; standardised difference -0.07) and similar rates of overweight/obese self-perception (36.9% vs 37.4%; -0.01). They differed in questionnaire completeness. Throughout, completing the extended questionnaire module means having answered the childhood-obesity item. The item belongs to a module that also carried the waist, ethnicity, diabetes, family-history and emotional-eating questions: 98.8% of participants who answered it also reported ethnicity, against 10.5% of those who did not, and waist measurements were recorded only within the module. Participants who answered it also stated a weight-loss goal more often (32.3% vs 14.0%). Full comparisons are given in Supplementary Table S1.

3.2 Genetic risk of obesity confers higher BMI and childhood obesity

FTO rs9939609 behaved as an obesity risk variant in this cohort. Each additional A allele was associated with a 0.46 kg/m² higher BMI (95% CI 0.33-0.59, p = 6 × 10⁻¹², n = 20,616). The effect was approximately twice as large in women (+0.62 kg/m² per allele, 95% CI 0.41 to 0.82) as in men (+0.30, 95% CI 0.14 to 0.47; sex-by-genotype interaction +0.31 kg/m² per allele, 95% CI 0.04 to 0.57, p = 0.023). MC4R rs17782313 was only weakly associated with BMI (+0.14 kg/m² per allele, 95% CI -0.01 to +0.29, p = 0.07).

Associations of both variants with waist-to-height ratio follow the same pattern and attenuate fully on adjustment for BMI, indicating no effect on fat distribution independent of overall adiposity; they are reported in Supplementary Table S5.

Genetic risk was also associated with obesity in childhood. Each additional FTO risk allele increased the odds of recalled childhood obesity (OR 1.16, 95% CI 1.07-1.26, p = 2 × 10⁻⁴; adjusted for age and sex, OR 1.18, 95% CI 1.09-1.28, p = 3 × 10⁻⁵). MC4R showed an association of similar size (OR 1.16 per allele, p = 0.001), as did the combined FTO+MC4R risk-allele score (OR 1.17 per allele, 95% CI 1.10-1.24, p = 4 × 10⁻⁷). Childhood obesity in turn marked a heavier adult trajectory. Adults who reported it had a higher BMI (mean 31.6 vs 26.8 kg/m²; +4.7 kg/m² adjusted for age and sex; +5.8 in women and +3.9 in men), a higher prevalence of severe obesity (BMI ≥ 40 kg/m²: 13.3% vs 2.6%) and greater central adiposity (waist-to-height ratio +0.053, p = 8 × 10⁻³⁷; mean 0.545 vs 0.495; n = 4,271). Genetic risk therefore carried through to body size in childhood and adulthood.

3.3 Self-perception follows BMI but systematically understates it

Self-perceived body type rose steadily with measured BMI (Spearman ρ = 0.72), but from the 'average' label upward each category corresponded to a BMI about one WHO band heavier than the label implies.

Mean BMI was 21.3, 24.6, 25.4, 30.7 and 38.8 kg/m² across the five perceived categories from underweight to obese, so participants who described themselves as 'average' were on average already in the overweight band, and those who described themselves as 'overweight' were on average in obese class I. Although 61% of the sample were overweight or obese by BMI, only 37% described themselves as overweight or obese; this comparison does not depend on how perceived categories are matched to BMI categories. When each perceived category was mapped to a clinical BMI category, 53.2% of participants were concordant, 44.8% placed themselves in a lighter category than their BMI indicated (under-classification) and 2.0% placed themselves in a heavier one (over-classification) (Figure 1).

Concordance depended on how the ambiguous 'average' label was mapped: treating 'average' as concordant with either normal or overweight BMI raised concordance to 67.8% and reduced underclassification to 30.2%, with over-classification unchanged at 2.0%. Under either mapping, misperception was almost entirely towards under-classification. Of participants who described themselves as 'lean/fit', 38% were overweight or obese by BMI, as were 49% of those who described themselves as 'average' (Figure 1).

3.4 Who under-perceives their weight

Under-perception was concentrated in men, younger adults and those at lower BMI within the overweight and obese range. Of the 12,840 participants who were overweight or obese by BMI and reported a body type, 64.0% under-classified (this analysis does not require a called genotype and therefore exceeds the 12,610 overweight or obese participants in the primary analytic sample). Men under-classified more often than women (73.5% vs 51.6%; adjusted OR for women vs men 0.41, 95% CI 0.38-0.44, p < 10⁻¹²⁰). Excluding the 1,713 participants who described themselves as lean/fit (13.3% of this group, 88.0% of them men), under-classification fell to 58.4% and the male excess narrowed without disappearing (66.6% of men vs 49.7% of women; adjusted OR 0.52, 95% CI 0.48-0.56). Underclassification decreased with increasing BMI (adjusted OR 0.77 per 5 kg/m², p < 10⁻⁶⁴), so participants further into the obese range recognised their status more often. It also decreased with age, from approximately 66-68% below 40 years to 51.4% at 70 years and above (adjusted OR 0.89 per decade, 95% CI 0.87-0.92, p = 3 × 10⁻¹³), and the age gradient was steeper in men than in women (interaction p = 0.018). Estimates are mutually adjusted for age, sex and BMI as applicable. Across self-reported ethnic groups, under-classification ranged from 53.8% in Asian participants (n = 459) to 67.4% in participants of mixed ethnicity (n = 129).

Because BMI does not distinguish fat from lean mass, we repeated the comparison using waist-to-height ratio (WHtR ≥ 0.5) in the subset with waist measurements (n = 4,270). The sex difference persisted but was smaller: 40.7% of men and 21.7% of women with WHtR ≥ 0.5 did not perceive themselves as overweight or obese, compared with 56.4% and 26.3% of those overweight or obese by BMI in the same subset. Among participants overweight or obese by BMI who did not describe themselves as either, 68.6% of those in the overweight band had WHtR below 0.5, whereas in obese class I only 36.8% did; BMI may therefore overstate misperception in the overweight band, but not in the obese bands (Supplementary Table S6).

3.5 Genetic risk does not predict under-perception; recalled childhood obesity does

Overweight/obese self-perception increased with FTO risk genotype: 35.1% of low-risk (TT), 37.8% of moderate-risk (TA) and 39.9% of high-risk (AA) participants described themselves as overweight or obese (Figure 2A). Adjusted for age and sex, the high-versus low-risk difference was 4.7 percentage points (95% CI 2.7-6.7), and each risk allele added 2.4 percentage points. The estimate was essentially unchanged by adjustment for genotyping source, and it persisted after adjustment for self-reported ethnicity (+3.9 percentage points, 95% CI 1.2-6.6; high-versus low-risk contrast, n = 6,130, among the 11,571 participants who reported ethnicity), in logistic models (high versus low risk OR 1.22, 95% CI 1.12-1.33; per-allele OR 1.11) and in White participants alone (+3.7 percentage points, 95% CI 0.7-6.6, p = 0.015). The combined FTO+MC4R score showed a similar gradient, from 34.8% at a score of 0 to 43.9% at a score of 4 (+1.7 percentage points per risk allele, 95% CI 1.0-2.4, p = 4 × 10⁻⁶). MC4R alone was not associated with self-perception (+0.9 percentage points per allele, 95% CI -0.2 to +2.0, p = 0.11), so the smaller per-allele estimate for the score reflects dilution of the FTO signal rather than a contribution from MC4R (Supplementary Table S2).

These associations were fully explained by body size. Adjusted for BMI, the FTO high-versus low-risk difference fell to 0.6 percentage points (95% CI -1.0 to +2.2), and the score association was abolished (p = 0.25). To test directly whether genotype biases perception at a given body size, we modelled underclassification among participants with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m², adjusted for BMI, age and sex. Neither variant was associated with under-classification: FTO per-allele OR 0.97 (95% CI 0.92-1.03, p = 0.31); FTO high versus low risk OR 0.91 (95% CI 0.82-1.02, p = 0.10); MC4R per-allele OR 0.99 (95% CI 0.93-1.05, p = 0.71); FTO+MC4R score OR 0.98 (p = 0.33). The FTO estimate was null in men and women separately (sex interaction p = 0.63). The analysis had 80% power to detect per-allele effects larger than approximately 8% on the odds scale. At a given BMI, therefore, any effect of either variant on selfperception is small: the 95% confidence interval for the per-allele FTO estimate runs from 8% lower to 3% higher odds.

The per-allele association of FTO with self-perception was larger in women (+3.2 percentage points, 95% CI 1.8-4.6) than in men (+1.6, 95% CI 0.3-2.9), in parallel with its larger effect on BMI in women (Section 3.2), but the sex-by-genotype interaction was not significant (p = 0.12). An exploratory interaction between FTO genotype and the ordinal physical-activity score was also not significant (-0.26 percentage points per allele per activity level, p = 0.53; n = 20,691). Substituting WHtR for BMI in the waist subset (n = 4,171) gave the same pattern, although the subset is underpowered for FTO: the age-and sex-adjusted per-allele association was +1.75 percentage points (95% CI -0.36 to +3.86) and was removed by adjustment for WHtR (+0.11, 95% CI -1.64 to +1.85), as it was by adjustment for BMI (-0.68, 95% CI -2.28 to +0.91) (Supplementary Note S2).

In contrast to genotype, a recalled history of childhood obesity was associated with self-perception independently of current body size. Participants who reported childhood obesity described themselves as overweight or obese more often than those who did not (62.9% vs 32.7%; +31.5 percentage points adjusted for age and sex; Figure 2B), a difference 6.7 times the size of the high-versus-low genotype contrast (+4.7 percentage points). After adjustment for current BMI, age and sex, childhood obesity remained associated with a 10.0-percentage-point higher probability of overweight/obese selfperception (95% CI 7.4-12.5, p = 4 × 10⁻¹⁴; n = 10,797) and, among participants overweight or obese by BMI, with less under-classification (OR 0.64, 95% CI 0.56-0.74; n = 6,595).

An exploratory mediation analysis in the subset with recalled childhood obesity (n = 5,626) confirmed that the route from genotype to self-perception through childhood obesity also runs mainly through current body size: after adjustment for current BMI, the component of the genotype association passing through childhood obesity was +0.4 percentage points, 13% of the total. Supplementary Figure S1 shows the decomposition before current BMI is taken into account, in which the indirect component is +1.3 percentage points, approximately 40% of the total but with a confidence interval too wide to interpret.

3.6 Under-perception is associated with fewer weight-loss goals

Goals were recorded for 19,221 participants: get fit (46.0%), lose weight (25.6%), gain lean muscle (15.1%) and body maintenance (13.3%); 19,156 also had a self-perceived body type and a BMI category.

Within every BMI category, participants who perceived themselves as overweight or obese set a weightloss goal more often than those who did not (Figure 3A): 39.4% vs 10.7% at normal BMI, 42.2% vs 15.8% at overweight BMI and 49.4% vs 18.0% at obese BMI -gaps of 29, 26 and 31 percentage points within the same BMI category. Across the five perceived categories, the proportion setting a weight-loss goal rose from 2.9% (underweight) and 5.8% (lean/fit) to 18.7% (average), 43.8% (overweight) and 54.9% (obese).

Under-classification was associated with fewer weight-loss goals. Among the 11,797 participants who were overweight or obese by BMI and had recorded both a body type and a goal, 26.8% of those who under-classified set a weight-loss goal, against 47.5% of those whose perception matched their BMI category (OR 0.52, 95% CI 0.48-0.56, adjusted for BMI, age and sex). The association was present in men and women separately and in every age band.

With perception and BMI entered together and adjusted for age and sex, perceiving oneself as overweight or obese was associated with a 26.5-percentage-point higher probability of a weight-loss goal (95% CI 24.7 to 28.2), whereas each additional unit of BMI added 0.7 percentage points (95% CI 0.6 to 0.9). Adding perception to the model reduced the BMI coefficient from 2.0 to 0.7 percentage points per kg/m², a reduction of approximately 63%. Because a binary perception contrast and a per-unit BMI slope are not on a common scale, we also expressed both per standard deviation of the predictor (BMI 6.5 kg/m²; perception 0.49). Entered separately, the two were of similar strength: +15.8 percentage points per SD for perception (95% CI 15.2 to 16.4) and +12.7 for BMI (95% CI 12.0 to 13.4). Entered together, perception retained +12.9 percentage points per SD (95% CI 12.0 to 13.7) while BMI fell to +4.7 (95% CI 3.8 to 5.5). The difference between the two predictors therefore lies in how each survives adjustment for the other rather than in their separate predictive strength. This is not a mediation estimate: self-reported BMI is measured with error, which attenuates its coefficient, and perception may partly capture adiposity that BMI misses. A second adiposity measure, however, changed the perception estimate little. In the subset with waist measurements, the perception estimate was +24.1 percentage points adjusted for BMI, +23.4 adjusted for BMI and WHtR jointly, and +28.6 adjusted for WHtR alone.

Logistic models gave the same pattern (perception OR 4.29, 95% CI 3.93-4.69; BMI OR 1.03 per kg/m²).

The perception-goal association was robust to model specification. In a multinomial model across all four goals, taking 'get fit' as the reference category, perceiving oneself as overweight or obese raised the odds of a weight-loss goal 3.34-fold (95% CI 3.04-3.66) and lowered the odds of gaining lean muscle (OR 0.57) and of body maintenance (OR 0.36), each relative to 'get fit'. Treating perception as a fivelevel ordered variable gave +13.2 percentage points per category (95% CI 12.5 to 13.9), and adjustment for physical activity left the binary estimate at +23.4 percentage points. An unmeasured confounder would require a risk-ratio association of at least 5.43 with both perception and goal-setting to account for the association (E-value; lower confidence limit 5.06).

Because perception and goal were reported in the same questionnaire, part of their association could be definitional. Three observations argue against a purely definitional link. First, goals were substituted rather than relabelled (Figure 3B). From self-described underweight to obese, the share choosing to get fit varied little (50.9%, 45.1%, 50.2%, 42.5%, 39.5%), whereas the rise in weight-loss goals (2.9% to 54.9%) was matched by a monotonic fall in body maintenance (33.4% to 1.5%) and, from the 'average' category onwards, in gaining lean muscle (19.7% to 4.1%); a semantic restatement would not produce that particular exchange between goals. Second, recognition did not determine the goal: of the 7,279 participants who perceived themselves as overweight or obese, 46.7% set a weight-loss goal and 53.3% chose another, 78.3% of whom chose to get fit. Third, 12.7% of participants who did not perceive themselves as overweight or obese set a weight-loss goal, and broadening the outcome to any weightor fitness-framed goal left a substantial association (+20.6 percentage points).

The size of the association depended on questionnaire completeness. It was +34.7 percentage points (95% CI 32.3 to 37.1) among participants who completed the extended questionnaire module and +14.2 (95% CI 11.9 to 16.6) among those who did not (difference +20.5, 95% CI 17.1 to 23.8, p = 7 × 10⁻³³).

Module completion explains this difference; ancestry does not, and the larger estimate in White participants alone (+34.4 percentage points) follows from how many of them completed the module.

Participants who did not complete the module also set weight-loss goals less often (16.7% vs 32.8%).

That lower rate, together with the much sparser questionnaire records in this group, may reflect a less deliberate or partly default onboarding response rather than a different relationship between perception and intention. Because the outcome was also less common in this group, part of the difference on the percentage-point scale is expected from the lower base rate alone; on the odds scale the association was OR 5.19 (95% CI 4.64-5.82) in participants who completed the module and OR 3.11 (95% CI 2.67-3.63) in those who did not, a narrower difference than the percentage-point contrast implies. In either case, the pooled estimate should be read as an average over two groups whose data differ in completeness.

Genotype did not predict goal-setting beyond body size. FTO risk was weakly associated with a weightloss goal when adjusted for age and sex (+1.2 percentage points per allele, 95% CI 0.3 to 2.0), but not after adjustment for BMI (+0.2, 95% CI -0.7 to 1.0), and the FTO+MC4R score was null after BMI adjustment (+0.0, 95% CI -0.6 to 0.7). Childhood obesity was associated with goal-setting at the same current BMI (+3.7 percentage points, 95% CI 1.0 to 6.4), but not once current self-perception was included (+0.2, 95% CI -2.4 to 2.7). Unadjusted percentages throughout.

3.7 Men recognised overweight less often and, having recognised it, chose weight loss less often

The two steps compound in men. Among the 11,797 participants who were overweight or obese by BMI with both a body type and a goal recorded (6,533 men, 5,264 women), fewer men than women described themselves as overweight or obese at all: 47.0% against 75.4%, so 53.0% of men did not recognise their weight against 24.6% of women. The broader under-classification measure runs in the same direction (73.5% of men vs 51.6% of women; Section 3.4). Among those who did describe themselves as overweight or obese, men then set a weight-loss goal less often than women (42.0% vs 50.2%; adjusted OR for women vs men 1.38, 95% CI 1.26-1.52, p = 1 × 10⁻¹¹), choosing to gain lean muscle more often instead (12.5% vs 4.9%).

The net effect of the two steps is large. In the same sample, 25.5% of men set a weight-loss goal against 45.4% of women -a gap of 19.9 percentage points. Assigning men the female recognition rate while holding their own goal rates constant raises the male figure to 34.5%, so approximately 45% of the gap follows from the first step and the remainder from the second. This decomposition is descriptive and depends on the order in which the two steps are substituted, but it shows that neither step accounts for the difference on its own.

4 Discussion

Most adults who were overweight or obese by BMI placed themselves in a lighter category than their BMI indicated, and those who did so were about half as likely to set a weight-loss goal as participants of the same BMI who did not. Under-perception was concentrated in men, younger adults and those at lower BMI within the overweight and obese range.

It was not explained by genetic risk of obesity: FTO rs9939609 raised BMI and the odds of childhood obesity, but at a given BMI neither it nor MC4R predicted how participants saw their weight. A recalled history of childhood obesity, by contrast, was associated with heavier self-perception at the same adult BMI. The rest of this section takes these strands in turn: who misperceived and why, what recognition predicts, what genotype and early history did and did not do, and what this means for digital weight-management services.

The extent and direction of misperception agree with previous reports that overweight and obese adults frequently perceive themselves as lighter than they are (Duncan et al., 2011; Gruszka et al., 2022; Shabu et al., 2024); under-classification outnumbered over-classification by more than twenty to one (44.8% vs 2.0% under the primary mapping). The 'lean/fit' response option describes fitness as well as size, so men may select it on the basis of muscularity. The sex difference was smaller when waist-to-height ratio rather than BMI was the reference, consistent with this explanation, although no direct measure of body composition was available and the excess was present on both measures. The cross-sectional design also cannot separate age from birth cohort. Younger cohorts, raised amid higher population adiposity, may have recalibrated their reference for normal weight upward (Robinson, 2017), which would produce the same gradient; we therefore describe it as an age-cohort gradient rather than as change within individuals.

Recognition tracked the weight-management goal participants set. At the same BMI, perceiving oneself as overweight or obese was associated with a 26.5-percentage-point higher probability of setting a weight-loss goal, and under-classification with approximately half the odds. Placed on a common scale, perception and BMI were of similar strength when entered separately (+15.8 and +12.7 percentage points per standard deviation), but when entered together perception retained +12.9 and BMI fell to +4.7: adding perception absorbs most of the association between measured size and the goal set, whereas adding measured size absorbs little of the association between perception and the goal. Error in self-reported BMI would push the estimates in the same direction, so the asymmetry may be overstated. Because perception and goal were reported in the same questionnaire, we do not claim that recognition causes intention; the two may be aspects of a single self-appraisal, and their association is partly definitional. Three observations nonetheless suggest that perception predicts which goal a person selects rather than simply restating it: goals were substituted rather than relabelled across perceived categories, most participants who recognised overweight chose a goal other than weight loss, and the association persisted when the outcome was broadened. The association was weaker among participants who did not complete the extended questionnaire module (+14.2 vs +34.7 percentage points), so the pooled estimate averages over two groups whose data differ in completeness.

In men, the two steps compound. Men were less likely to recognise overweight (among those overweight or obese by BMI, 47.0% of men described themselves as overweight or obese against 75.4% of women) and, having recognised it, less likely to set a weight-loss goal (42.0% vs 50.2%), choosing to gain lean muscle more often instead. The two steps together leave 25.5% of men who were overweight or obese by BMI setting a weight-loss goal, against 45.4% of women. The 'lean/fit' option and the smaller sex difference against waist-to-height ratio suggest that muscularity explains part of the first step, but not all of it, and it does not explain the second.

FTO risk was associated with heavier self-perception only because carriers are heavier. Adjustment for BMI abolished the association, and at a given BMI neither FTO, MC4R nor their combined score predicted misperception, with the FTO estimate bounded between OR 0.93 and 1.02. To our knowledge, this is the first explicit test of a perception-specific effect at the FTO locus. The result parallels FTO's associations with type 2 diabetes and related metabolic traits, which are explained by its effect on BMI (Frayling et al., 2007; Freathy et al., 2008), and contrasts with its association with depression, which appears to persist, or even reverse, after BMI adjustment (Samaan et al., 2013). Because BMI adjustment does not remove every FTO association, the null observed here is informative. FTO has been linked to eating behaviour and affect beyond BMI (Harbron et al., 2014; Koike et al., 2018; Brown et al., 2026), but our results provide no evidence that these links extend to how carriers perceive their body size.

Two analytical choices qualify this conclusion. Restricting the analysis to BMI ≥ 25 kg/m² and adjusting for BMI both condition on a variable that FTO influences. If unmeasured factors affect both BMI and selfperception, such conditioning can bias the estimate in either direction (VanderWeele, 2016). Bias of this kind would need to be large to produce an estimate so tightly centred on the null, but it cannot be excluded, and the BMI-adjusted estimate answers a conditional question rather than providing an unbiased causal contrast.

At a given BMI, self-perception was associated with developmental history rather than with genotype. A recalled history of childhood obesity was associated with heavier self-perception, and with better recognition of overweight, at the same current BMI. This is consistent with prospective evidence that childhood adiposity predicts later body dissatisfaction (Blundell et al., 2024) and with a lasting effect of early body size on adult body image. Childhood obesity was more common among FTO risk-allele carriers and marked a heavier adult trajectory, but the share of the genotype-perception association that passed through it operated mainly through current BMI. This finding is the most exposed to bias.

Childhood obesity was recalled retrospectively and reported in the same questionnaire as selfperception, recall of early-life body size is only moderately accurate (De Rubeis et al., 2019), and current self-image may shape recall. Its robustness to unmeasured confounding is accordingly modest: the Evalue for the BMI-adjusted association is 1.91 (lower confidence limit 1.72), against 3.34 before BMI adjustment (Supplementary Note S1). Longitudinal data are required to separate a developmental effect from correlated recall.

These results have two implications for digital health services. First, genotype at these loci identifies people at higher risk of weight gain but not those who under-perceive their weight; current BMI and early weight history carry that information. Genetic risk reports may therefore be more useful when paired with objective feedback on current weight status than when presented alone. Second, services that rely on self-referral or self-set goals will under-reach the groups least likely to recognise their weight (men, younger adults and those at lower BMI within the overweight and obese range), because at the same BMI participants who under-classified were approximately half as likely to set a weight-loss goal. Whether presenting objective weight status, or framing feedback around the discrepancy between perceived and measured size, changes goal-setting remains untested. We observed stated intention at a single time point and did not analyse engagement or weight outcomes, which are themselves associated with digital engagement in weight-management services, including pharmacotherapy-supported programmes (Johnson et al., 2025).

The study also illustrates what DTC genetics cohorts can contribute to the study of health behaviour.

They include people before, and independently of, any clinical encounter, and they link genotype to perception, developmental history and stated intention in the same individuals at scale. People who have not identified a weight concern and would not present for care are rarely represented in clinical datasets, yet they are the population in which the perception effects reported here matter most. Using such data to personalise body-image or health communication is a hypothesis to test prospectively, not an established application.

Several limitations apply. The design is cross-sectional: mediation is estimated statistically and does not establish causation, and temporal ordering is not observed. Height and weight were self-reported, and self-report typically underestimates weight (Connor Gorber et al., 2007); because the primary outcome is subjective perception, this matters less than it would for a pure misperception analysis, but residual bias remains. Recalled childhood obesity is subject to the recall limitations described above and was available for only about half the cohort, a subset defined by completion of an extended questionnaire module rather than at random (Supplementary Table S1); the mediation analysis therefore rests on a non-random subsample, and we treat it as exploratory. The concordance estimate depends on how the ambiguous 'average' label is mapped (53-68%). Genotypes departed mildly from Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, which may reflect population structure in a multi-ancestry cohort of mixed genotyping provenance or a genotyping artefact in the array subset and cannot be resolved with these data; ancestry was captured by self-reported ethnicity rather than genetic principal components, and ethnicity was missing for 44.1% of participants, so ancestry-stratified analysis is warranted.

The cohort is a self-selected sample of paying customers, more health-motivated and demographically skewed than the general population, and self-perceived body type was measured with a single non-validated item.

Primary estimates are adjusted only for age and sex, and confounders of the relation between childhood obesity and self-perception, such as socioeconomic position, were unmeasured. MC4R is a weak marker of obesity risk in this cohort: its own association with BMI did not reach significance (+0.14 kg/m² per allele, p = 0.07), so its null results are less informative than FTO's. Two variants explain only a small fraction of BMI variance, so these findings do not address polygenic obesity risk more broadly. Finally, perception and goal were reported in the same questionnaire, so their association is exposed to sharedmethod variance. These constraints limit causal inference but do not alter the central observation: adults' stated weight-management intention follows how they perceive their weight more closely than it follows their measured size or the FTO and MC4R variants examined here.

In conclusion, around two in five adults who were overweight or obese by BMI did not describe themselves as such, most often men, younger adults and those at lower BMI within the overweight and obese range, and at the same BMI those who placed themselves in a lighter category than their BMI were about half as likely to set a weight-loss goal. The obesity risk allele at FTO was associated with higher body size in childhood and adulthood but, at a given BMI, not with how adults saw their weight; a recalled history of childhood obesity was, a finding that requires longitudinal confirmation. Genetic risk therefore identifies who is likely to be heavier but not who will recognise it. Self-perception is a candidate target for intervention, and whether feedback on objective weight status can recalibrate it, and thereby change the goals people set, is a question for prospective studies.
