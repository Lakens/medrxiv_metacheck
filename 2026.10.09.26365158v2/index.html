To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 8 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Introduction

Incretin-based obesity medications (IOMs), particularly glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) and dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP)/GLP-1 RAs, have transformed obesity treatment by producing substantial weight loss and improvements in cardiometabolic health. However, achieving and sustaining clinically meaningful weight loss in routine clinical practice remains challenging, underscoring the need to identify approaches that enhance the effectiveness of IOM-based therapies. Real-world 12-month persistence and adherence to GLP-1-based therapies have historically been suboptimal, with earlier studies reporting persistence rates of 24.5%-32.3%, mean proportion of days covered (PDC) of 51.0%-54.3%, and only 27.2%-29.6% of patients achieving PDC ≥80% [1, 2]. However, more recent evidence from a retrospective claims-based cohort of commercially insured adults without diabetes who initiated weight loss-indicated GLP-1-based therapies in 2024 reported higher 12-month persistence rates of 58.6% for semaglutide and 64.8% for tirzepatide [3]. Nevertheless, medication switching, treatment interruptions, dose changes, and discontinuation are common and often unavoidable features of real-world obesity pharmacotherapy, underscoring the need for care models that can accommodate these dynamic treatment trajectories while providing continuity of support and individualized management [2, 4]. The ability to navigate these treatment challenges is particularly important given the substantial and relatively rapid weight regain that can occur following medication discontinuation, estimated at approximately 0.5 kg per month overall and up to 0.8 kg per month following discontinuation of newer incretin therapies [5, 6].

Digital health offers a scalable approach for integrating IOM pharmacotherapy with longitudinal clinical monitoring, medication management, behavioral support, and health coaching. Emerging evidence supports the integration of obesity pharmacotherapy with digitally delivered behavioral and lifestyle interventions, including appbased self-monitoring, remote clinical oversight, and individualized coaching [7]. Real-world studies of digitally delivered obesity care have demonstrated substantial weight loss among individuals receiving semaglutide or tirzepatide within these multimodal care models [7, 8, 9, 10]. These findings derive largely from small observational cohorts and service evaluations without comparison groups, making it difficult to separate the contribution of digital support from that of pharmacotherapy itself, and, more importantly, to isolate the contribution of any specific component of care, such as nutrition.

Nutrients 2025, 17, x FOR PEER REVIEW4 of 36 Beyond digital delivery, lifestyle modification remains an important component of comprehensive obesity management alongside IOM pharmacotherapy, with potential contributions extending beyond weight loss alone.

Nutritional intervention, physical activity, and behavioral support can address dietary quality, physical function, body composition, and behaviors relevant to long-term weight management. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis of 33 randomized trials involving 12,028 participants demonstrated that GLP-1 RAs delivered alongside lifestyle modification produced substantial improvements in body weight, waist circumference, glycemic measures, blood pressure, and lipid parameters [11]. Lifestyle intervention may also be particularly relevant to the quality of weight loss during IOM treatment, as substantial weight reduction can include loss of lean mass; a 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis found that incorporating resistance training and adequate protein intake may help preserve lean mass during weight-loss treatment [12]. Consistent with this evidence, contemporary clinical guidelines recommend that GLP-1-based pharmacotherapy be delivered alongside dietary, physical activity, and behavioral interventions [13, 14]. However, this evidence has important limitations. Trials and meta-analyses of GLP-1 RAs with lifestyle modification generally evaluate broad, multicomponent lifestyle support rather than a defined nutritional strategy, and they typically compare medication plus lifestyle with medication alone or placebo, not one nutritional approach against another or against usual medication care within the same program [11]. Consequently, it remains unknown whether a structured, individualized nutritional therapy such as carbohydrate reduction adds additional cardiometabolic benefits beyond IOM treatment delivered with standard coaching and clinical support, particularly in real-world settings where dose changes, switching, interruptions, and prior GLP-1 exposure are common.

Participants were classified as having prior GLP-1 exposure if they were receiving a GLP-1 receptor agonist at program enrollment and had a documented pre-GLP-1 weight in clinician notes. All other participants were classified as GLP-1-naïve, that is, participants not receiving a GLP-1 at enrollment

Medication data. Medication data were extracted from the structured medication records in the electronic health record. We identified records for semaglutide, tirzepatide, and liraglutide, and extracted the ingredient, dose start and end dates, route, and daily dose. For each participant, we derived the earliest and latest dose dates, the ingredients used, and the number of distinct ingredients. Dose escalation was assessed monthly: for each ingredient, the highest daily dose recorded in a calendar month was compared with that of the previous month with a recorded dose, and a month was classified as an escalation month if the dose was higher. We summarized the number and percentage of escalation months, the maximum dose reached, and the time from the first dose to the maximum dose. Medication coverage was assessed using the proportion of days covered (PDC), calculated as the number of unique days covered by a GLP-1 dose record divided by the number of days in the 12-month period after the start of the primary Rx pathway. Overlapping records and ingredient switches were counted once per day, and PDC≥80% was considered high coverage.

Side effects. Participants self-reported IOM-related side effects through the app as part of daily symptom and biomarker logging. Reported symptoms included fatigue, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, constipation, and abdominal pain. Each entry was date-stamped, and we extracted the daily symptom values and the number of entries per symptom. Side effects were summarized descriptively. Because logging was voluntary and participant-initiated, the data reflect reported symptoms rather than clinician-adjudicated adverse events.

Outcomes Primary outcomes

The primary outcome was 12-month weight change from program enrollment, evaluated using both adjusted body weight change derived from longitudinal mixed-effects models and observed percentage weight loss. Clinically meaningful weight-loss response was assessed as the proportion of participants achieving ≥5%, ≥10%, ≥15%, and ≥20% weight loss at 12 months. The primary analysis focused on GLP-1-naïve participants who received a single GLP-1-based active pharmaceutical ingredient (semaglutide or tirzepatide) throughout the 12-month intervention period to minimize the potential influence of prior medication exposure and ingredient switching on weight-loss outcomes. Ancillary analyses evaluated weight-loss outcomes in the overall prescription cohort and across additional subgroups, including the broader GLP-1-naïve population, participants with prior GLP-1 exposure, and those who switched medication ingredients during follow-up. Among participants with prior GLP-1 exposure, weight-loss outcomes were further examined separately for single active pharmaceutical ingredient semaglutide and tirzepatide users. Exploratory outcome

The distribution of starting GLP-1 ingredients was similar between pathways, with approximately half of participants receiving semaglutide and half tirzepatide at the beginning of the observation period (Supplementary Table S7).

Ingredient-specific analyses showed a higher PDC distribution with VINT + IOM among semaglutide users, whereas PDC distributions were similar between pathways among tirzepatide users. The proportion with PDC ≥80% did not differ significantly between pathways for either active ingredient (Supplementary Table S11). At 12 months, 80.0% of participants in VINT + IOM and 82.8% in IOM only pathway was documented as actively receiving GLP-1 therapy (P=0.02).

Because medication exposure and status were derived from clinical medication records rather than pharmacy dispensing data, these findings should be interpreted as measures of documented medication coverage and management rather than pharmacy-dispensing adherence or persistence.

Medication regimens changed frequently over time, reflecting individualized clinical management. Approximately 38%-39% of participants switched GLP-1 ingredients during follow-up, with no significant difference between pathways. Among participants with a documented GLP-1 active ingredient switch, transitions occurred almost exclusively between semaglutide and tirzepatide (Supplementary Table S7). In IOM only, 789 of 1,028 participants with an ingredient switch (76.8%) transitioned from tirzepatide to semaglutide, whereas 230 (22.4%) transitioned from semaglutide to tirzepatide. Similarly, in VINT + IOM, 494 of 623 participants with an active ingredient switch (79.3%) transitioned from tirzepatide to semaglutide, and 121 (19.4%) transitioned from semaglutide to tirzepatide.

Escalation-only trajectories were more common in VINT + IOM than in IOM only pathway (27.7% vs 20.9%), whereas ingredient switching occurred in 27.3% and 29.3% of participants, respectively (Supplementary Table S8).

After validation of medication records, approximately 13% of participants in each pathway discontinued and subsequently restarted therapy, whereas confirmed discontinuation without a documented restart was uncommon (1.7% in VINT + IOM and 1.6% in Lifestyle + IOM). Active ingredient switching followed by confirmed discontinuation was similarly rare (3.2% vs 3.0%).

Despite differences in starting doses and titration patterns, medication intensity generally converged over follow-up.

To further characterize dose exposure, starting and final doses were examined among GLP-1-naïve participants who received a single GLP-1 active ingredient during follow-up. Among semaglutide users, 66.1% of participants in VINT+IOM, and 55.8% in IOM only reached 2.4 mg as their final observed dose (P=0.003). Among tirzepatide users, 45.5% and 40.8% had 15 mg as their final observed dose (P=0.32) (Supplementary Table S10). Thus, a substantial proportion of participants in both pathways remained below the maximum maintenance doses commonly evaluated in obesity clinical trials, particularly for tirzepatide. Although VINT + IOM participants were somewhat more likely to reach and remain at the maximum semaglutide dose, differences in maximum-dose exposure were not consistently observed across medications.

Weight loss across medication adherence and ingredient switching

In exploratory subgroup analyses, participants in the VINT+IOM pathway generally achieved greater 12-month weight loss than those in the IOM-only pathway across GLP-1 active ingredient-switching directions and medication adherence (PDC) categories (Supplementary Table S12). Among participants switching from tirzepatide to semaglutide, VINT+IOM was associated with significantly greater absolute (30.8 vs.

22.3 lb) and percentage weight

When stratified by medication adherence, participants in the VINT+IOM pathway also achieved significantly greater absolute weight loss in both the PDC <50% (27.8 vs. 20.2 lb; P=0.035) and PDC 50% to <80% categories (28.5 vs.

22.0 lb; P<0.001). Percentage weight loss was also significantly greater in the PDC 50% to <80% category (11.9% vs. 9.7%; P=0.004). Overall, these exploratory findings suggest a consistent pattern of greater weight loss with VINT+IOM across medication-switching and lower-adherence subgroups, although not all comparisons were statistically significant.

Reported medication side effects

Reported medication side effects were similar between pathways (Supplementary Table S13). At least one side effect was documented for 64.6% of participants in VINT+IOM and 63.6% in IOM only (P=0.50). Among participants with any reported side effect, the median number of reporting days was 4 (IQR, 2-8), and the median number of distinct symptom types was 2 (IQR, 1-3). Approximately 74% of reported side effects occurred within the first 6 months after program enrollment. Nausea was the most frequently reported symptom in both pathways, followed by constipation, fatigue, diarrhea, and vomiting. With the exception of constipation, which was reported more frequently in VINT+IOM (37.2% vs. 32.8%; P=0.004), the frequencies of nausea, fatigue, diarrhea, vomiting, and other reported side effects were similar between pathways.

Exploratory Outcome: HbA1c

In the primary single-ingredient, GLP-1-naïve cohort, HbA1c was available for 915 participants at enrollment, 395 at 6 months, and 479 at 12 months. Baseline HbA1c was in the normal range (about 5.4-5.5%). In adjusted mixed models, HbA1c decreased significantly in every medication and pathway group (all P<0.001; Supplementary Table S14), by 0.27 to 0.47 percentage points at 12 months. Reductions were greater with tirzepatide than with semaglutide (time × medication, P<0.001). Adjusted 12-month HbA1c did not differ significantly between VINT+IOM and IOM only, although the reduction was numerically larger with VINT+IOM for both medications. This small difference is expected given that baseline values were already in the normal range. Because follow-up HbA1c was available for a subset of members, these findings are exploratory.

Discussion

In this real-world cohort of adults receiving incretin-based obesity medication within a continuous remote care program, adding individualized carbohydrate-reduced nutritional therapy to IOM (VINT+IOM) was consistently associated with greater adjusted 12-month weight loss than IOM alone. This held for GLP-1-naïve participants and those with prior GLP-1 exposure, and for both semaglutide and tirzepatide. Medication coverage and program retention were high in both pathways despite frequent dose changes and ingredient switching. These findings should be interpreted against established evidence that real-world obesity pharmacotherapy is considerably more dynamic than the standardized exposure achieved in randomized trials. Adherence, dose escalation, switching, and discontinuation vary substantially in practice, and this variability is frequently not captured in claims-based analyses.

Earlier real-world studies reported mean PDCs of approximately 51%-54%, with only 27%-30% of participants achieving PDC ≥80% [1, 2], and 12-month GLP-1 persistence estimates ranging from 24.5% in a large claims-based cohort to 58.6% for semaglutide and 64.8% for tirzepatide in more contemporary cohorts [1, 2]. In contrast, approximately 80% of participants in our cohort achieved PDC ≥80%, and 12-month program retention was 77% with VINT+IOM and 72% with IOM only. Program retention and medication persistence are distinct measures and cannot be directly compared, but these results appear favorable. They are also consistent with an earlier analysis by Marshall et al. of commercially insured members who may have also received Virta-supported obesity care through the KeepWell program offered by Prime Therapeutics, in which 90.1% remained on GLP-1 therapy at 6 months with a mean weight reduction of 22.2 lb [20].

The additional weight loss with VINT+IOM (5.1-11.5 lb across cohorts, approximately 19%-52% greater than IOM only) is consistent with emerging, but still limited, evidence that nutritional strategies, particularly carbohydrate reduction, may add value alongside incretin therapy. HbA1c also declined in all groups, more with tirzepatide than with semaglutide and numerically more with VINT+IOM than with IOM only. However, differences between pathways were small and not statistically significant, which is unsurprising given that baseline values were normal (about 5.4-5.5%), leaving very little room for further reduction. In addition, limited follow-up laboratory data reduced the power to detect between-group differences. In a randomized pilot trial, Anyiam et al. found that semaglutide combined with a very-low-calorie diet produced approximately 7 percentage points greater weight loss than semaglutide alone, with greater fat-mass loss and improvements in metabolic measures [21].

Parker et al. similarly reported substantial weight loss with a personalized ketogenic diet plus low-dose semaglutide, with 92% of weight Nutrients 2025, 17, x FOR PEER REVIEW19 of 36 loss attributable to estimated fat mass and skeletal muscle mass largely preserved [22].

With tirzepatide, Schiavo et al.

reported that a low-energy ketogenic intervention achieved similar overall weight loss but better preservation of fatfree mass, muscle strength, and resting metabolic rate than a conventional low-calorie diet [23], and a subsequent analysis also showed greater improvements in hepatic steatosis and stiffness [24]. A preliminary post hoc analysis of body composition data from a VINT clinical trial in individuals with T2D further suggests that a greater degree of carbohydrate reduction may support lean-mass preservation and physical function during weight loss [25]. Together, these studies raise the possibility that carbohydrate-reduced strategies may influence not only the magnitude of weight loss but also its composition, as well as other cardiometabolic outcomes, although most of the published studies on body composition were small or uncontrolled. Body composition was not assessed in our study, so we cannot determine whether the additional weight loss with VINT+IOM reflects fat or lean mass. Future studies that include body composition measures would help clarify whether carbohydrate-reduced nutritional therapy affects the quality, and not only the amount, of weight lost with incretin therapy.

Women made up three quarters of our cohort, so we examined whether sex modified these results. Randomized and real-world data generally show greater percentage weight loss with GLP-1 receptor agonists in women. A 2026 meta-analysis of sex-stratified trials found weight loss was greater among women (10.9%) than men (6.8%), and a real-world tirzepatide cohort reported greater weight loss in women than men at 15 months (15.1% vs 10.7%) [26, 27].

We did not see this pattern. Men lost more absolute weight, which largely reflects their higher baseline weight, while observed 12-month percentage weight loss and most responder rates were similar by sex, and the sex difference did not vary by medication. A larger male advantage appeared only within VINT+IOM and was not consistent across models. One possible explanation is that men respond more strongly to the structured nutrition-therapy component, since a review of sex differences in obesity treatment reports that lifestyle modification, on average, produced larger weight losses in men [28]. We did not test this, and the finding should be treated as hypothesis-generating. Overall, the benefit of VINT+IOM over IOM alone was not restricted to one sex.

The magnitude of weight loss observed with VINT+IOM should be considered in the context of medication dose and duration of exposure. In pivotal efficacy trials, semaglutide was titrated by protocol toward the 2.4-mg maintenance dose, whereas in the present study initiation and titration occurred under routine clinical conditions.

Among GLP-1-naïve participants who received semaglutide throughout follow-up, 66.1% had 2.4 mg as their final

Nutrients 2025, 17, x FOR PEER REVIEW20 of 36 observed dose, and approximately one-third remained at lower doses. Observed weight loss in this group (13.3%) was similar in magnitude to the 14.9% reported at 68 weeks in STEP 1 [29] and to values reported in real-world studies of persistent semaglutide users maintained at 2.4 mg, including 14.1% at 12 months in SHAPE [30], 14.5% at 52 weeks in SCOPE [ [31], and 17.6% at 12 months in WeGoTogether [32]. In addition, 12 months in VINT+IOM represents approximately 52 weeks of program participation rather than 52 weeks of semaglutide exposure, because participantsinitiated treatment after enrollment and likely had shorter cumulative exposure, particularly to their final or higher doses.

A similar pattern was seen with tirzepatide. Among GLP-1-naïve participants who received tirzepatide throughout follow-up in VINT+IOM, 45.5% had 15 mg as their final observed dose, and cumulative exposure was likely less than 52 weeks. Weight loss of 19.7% was similar to the 19.5% and 20.9% reductions reported after 72 weeks with tirzepatide 10 and 15 mg, respectively, in SURMOUNT-1 [33], and higher than the 16.5% reported among tirzepatide users in SHAPE [30]. These observations indicate that substantial weight loss occurred in routine care among participants who did not reach maximum medication doses. They do not establish that the therapeutic effect is independent of medication dose or duration of exposure, and we did not evaluate dose-response relationships. In this setting, medication intensity was adjusted over time according to individual response and tolerability, alongside ongoing carbohydrate-reduced nutritional support from VINT. Among GLP-1-naïve participants, weight loss with VINT+IOM continued between 6 and 12 months without a clear plateau, suggesting that 12-month outcomes may not represent the maximal treatment response, particularly because initiation and titration occurred during program follow-up. However, these comparisons with clinical trials and other real-world studies should be interpreted cautiously. They are descriptive and involve differences in study populations, medication doses, treatment duration, follow-up, and analytic approach.

Substantial additional weight loss was also observed among participants who entered VINT + IOM with prior GLP-1 exposure. Among prior users remaining on a single ingredient, mean additional weight loss during 12 months of VINT + IOM was 8.8% with semaglutide and 13.8% with tirzepatide, despite pharmacotherapy and associated weight loss having begun before program entry. From the pre-GLP-1 reference, cumulative mean weight loss reached 19.2% and 23.0%, respectively, numerically exceeding the corresponding weight-loss estimates reported in pivotal clinical trials [26, 30]. Notably, participants in the VINT+IOM pathway achieved approximately 12% weight loss at 12

Nutrients 2025, 17, x FOR PEER REVIEW21 of 36 months despite lower GLP-1 medication coverage (PDC <80%), with significantly greater absolute weight loss than IOM-only participants across both lower-PDC categories. This contrasts with real-world studies reporting attenuated weight loss with reduced GLP-1 exposure. A contemporary semaglutide study reported 6.4% weight loss among nonpersistent users and 7.2% among those following nonstandard titration patterns [34]. Similarly, another study reported 12-month weight loss of 4.7% with tirzepatide and 2.3% with semaglutide among early discontinuers, compared with 11.8% and 6.3%, respectively, among persistent users [35]. Although differences in populations, medication exposure, adherence definitions, and study designs preclude direct cross-study comparisons, the substantial weight loss observed with VINT+IOM despite prior GLP-1 exposure or inconsistent medication coverage suggests that carbohydrate-reduced nutritional therapy with longitudinal clinical support may remain clinically relevant across the variable medication trajectories encountered in routine obesity care. Given the observational design, however, the independent contribution of VINT cannot be established.

Taken together, these findings support conceptualizing IOM-based obesity treatment as a longitudinal and evolving care trajectory rather than a conventional prescription-centered approach characterized by intermittent clinical touchpoints and limited support between visits for navigating nutrition, adherence, persistence, tolerability, dose changes, switching, and treatment interruptions. In this study, both pathways included telemedicine infrastructure, clinician oversight, behavioral support, and access to IOM pharmacotherapy, while individualized carbohydrate-reduced nutrition therapy was the key additional component of VINT+IOM. The consistently greater weight loss observed with VINT+IOM, including among participants with prior GLP-1 exposure and across heterogeneous medication-use patterns, supports the potential incremental value of integrating individualized nutrition therapy within medication-supported obesity care. Importantly, 12-month program retention was high in both pathways (77% with VINT+IOM and 72% with IOM only), suggesting that structured carbohydrate-reduced nutritional therapy can be integrated with GLP-1-based pharmacotherapy while maintaining sustained participant engagement. These findings are consistent with previous real-world evidence demonstrating sustained engagement with VINT delivered independently of GLP-1-based pharmacotherapy (36).

These findings should be interpreted in the context of the observational study design. Treatment pathway selection was not randomized, and residual confounding, including differences in motivation, engagement, or other unmeasured characteristics, may have contributed to the observed between-group differences; therefore, the independent effect of VINT cannot be established. Although both pathways included health coach support, coaching Nutrients 2025, 17, x FOR PEER REVIEW22 of 36 frequency and intensity were not formally compared, and differences in coaching contact, particularly the dietary support provided in VINT+IOM, may have contributed to the observed differences. Medication selection, adherence, dose escalation, switching, and treatment duration were determined during routine clinical care rather than standardized by protocol, and analyses requiring continued pathway engagement and available weight measurements may have introduced selection bias toward more engaged participants. Because dose and duration of exposure were not randomized, the contribution of medication exposure cannot be separated from that of the nutrition pathway, and we did not evaluate dose-response relationships. Sex analyses were exploratory: they used absolute weight rather than percent change, included only 36-53 men per tirzepatide subgroup. We could not estimate effects for one participant with a third gender category, and we lacked data on menopausal or hormonal status, body composition, energy expenditure and dietary adherence, so we could not test the mechanisms that may underlie sex differences.

Comparison with randomized trials and external real-world cohorts should therefore be considered contextual rather than direct. Finally, 12 months may be insufficient to characterize maximal weight loss or longer-term trajectories, particularly among participants initiating and titrating IOMs after enrollment. Longer follow-up is needed to determine weight-loss plateau and durability, medication continuation or discontinuation, and whether the differences observed between nutritional treatment pathways persist over time.

Within this model, participants receiving IOMs may engage in either Virta Individualized Nutrition Therapy (VINT), which emphasizes personalized carbohydrate reduction with continuous monitoring, or IOM only intervention delivered within the same multidisciplinary care infrastructure.

Because the VINT+IOM and IOM-only pathways share digital delivery, clinical oversight, coaching, and medication access while differing primarily in the intensity and individualization of nutritional therapy, they provide an uncommon opportunity to examine the added value of nutritional therapy under routine clinical conditions, with the care infrastructure held largely constant. The primary objective was to compare 12-month weight loss between VINT+IOM and IOM-only among GLP-1-naïve participants receiving a single GLP-1-based active pharmaceutical ingredient (semaglutide or tirzepatide) during follow-up and remaining engaged in a single prescription pathway for at least 9 months. This analysis was intended to evaluate the additional weight loss associated with integrating carbohydrate-reduced nutritional therapy with GLP-1-based pharmacotherapy compared with pharmacotherapy alone. Ancillary analyses examined weight-loss outcomes in the overall prescription cohort and across additional medication exposure subgroups, including the broader GLP-1-naïve population, participants with prior GLP-1 exposure, and those who switched medication ingredients during follow-up. Among participants with prior GLP-1 exposure, cumulative weight loss from the pre-GLP-1 reference weight was also evaluated to contextualize additional reductions achieved during program participation. Longitudinal weight trajectories were examined to characterize the pattern of weight reduction over 12 months, including changes between 6 and 12 months. Secondary objectives included characterizing medication utilization patterns, dose distribution and escalation, medication switching, adherence, and reported side effects, as well as exploring associations between medication use patterns and weight-loss outcomes, and changes in HbA1c as an exploratory outcome. This study advances existing knowledge in three ways. First, it compares individualized carbohydrate-reduced nutritional therapy plus IOM with IOM alone within a single care infrastructure, isolating nutrition support as the main difference between pathways, although residual confounding cannot be excluded. Second, it characterizes real-world medication patterns, including ingredient switching and prior GLP-1 exposure, and relates them to weight outcomes. These factors are typically excluded or minimized in controlled trials and are rarely captured in claims-based realworld studies, yet they are essential for understanding obesity care in practice. Third, it describes real-world program retention, medication coverage, and treatment patterns (dosing, escalation, and switching), and shows how a continuous remote care model can accommodate these trajectories while supporting weight loss through 12 months.
