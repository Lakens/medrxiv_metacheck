Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Full text

Abstract

COVID-19 provides an unprecedented opportunity to evaluate the contribution of public health and social measures (PHSMs) to controlling transmission and inform responses to future emerging respiratory pathogens. We analysed SARS-CoV-2 transmission across 126 countries spanning multiple epidemic waves and diverse policy environments during periods when PHSMs were the primary interventions available to influence the epidemic. By explicitly representing key epidemiological drivers of transmission and measuring reductions in residual transmission scaling after incorporating OxCGRT policy time-series data, we isolated the effect of PHSMs on transmission. PHSMs frequently explained a substantial proportion of the observed variation in transmission intensity, with particularly strong effects seen in many high-income countries. In countries with strong evidence for policy effects, reductions in transmission reached 60-80% or above, although the individual policies responsible for these effects varied considerably. Harmonised analyses considering the effect of community mobility showed comparably strong effects in a similar subset of countries.

.

Introduction

Preparedness against emerging respiratory pathogens remains a critical global challenge, as increasing environmental disruption and global connectedness create opportunities for pathogen emergence and spread. 1, 2 The COVID-19 pandemic provides the most important recent test of public health responses to such threats and so presents an unprecedented opportunity to evaluate the interventions used to control transmission. 3 While the effect of medical countermeasures such as vaccination can be more easily quantified through direct clinical investigations, understanding the effect of public health and social measures (PHSMs) must be largely inferred from epidemiological analyses. [4] [5] [6] As such, the scale, duration and geographic breadth of the PHSMs implemented during the COVID-19 pandemic provide an unparalleled opportunity to evaluate their contribution to controlling transmission and inform future responses to emerging respiratory pathogens.

Previous studies have generally concluded that PHSMs contributed to reductions in SARS-CoV-2 transmission, but have differed regarding the estimated magnitude of these effects and the relative importance of specific interventions. 5, [7] [8] [9] [10] [11] [12] These studies have variously identified stay-at-home orders, gathering restrictions, school closures and business restrictions as among the most effective measures, despite the inconsistency in the associated effect estimates. These discrepancies may reflect differences in study settings, analysis periods, intervention packages and methodological approaches, including sensitivity to model specification. 13 Estimating policy effects from epidemic time series remains challenging because interventions are typically implemented in combination, vary across settings and become increasingly difficult to distinguish once medical countermeasures such as vaccination are introduced. 14, 15 Moreover, most previous analyses have been restricted to specific regions, subsets of countries or particular pandemic phases. As a result, analyses that systematically integrate data across diverse geographic settings and throughout the period during which PHSMs constituted the primary response to the pandemic should provide a stronger basis for understanding the contribution of policy measures to transmission dynamics.

To address this challenge, we analysed SARS-CoV-2 transmission across 126 countries, encompassing multiple epidemic waves and diverse policy environments. We developed a mechanistic framework to quantify the explanatory value of policy measures through reductions in residual transmission scaling when time series from the Oxford COVID-19 Government Response Tracker were incorporated into the analysis. 16 Applying this approach across all countries for which robust inference was possible, we present a globally comprehensive assessment of the extent to which PHSMs influenced transmission dynamics over the course of the pandemic.

Results

We calibrated a renewal model with and without accounting for time series of policy changes to the COVID-19 epidemics of 126 countries. By examining the extent to which including policy effects in the analysis reduced the need for residual nonmechanistic scaling of transmission, we quantified the evidence that policy changes influenced transmission in these settings. The model incorporated epidemiological features including susceptible depletion, 17 variant-specific transmissibility, 18 incomplete immunity to emerging variants 19 and partial case ascertainment, 20 and we calibrated it to COVID-19 epidemiological targets that included time series profiles for weekly new deaths, case notifications, up to one hierarchically selected hospitalisation indicator and the proportional prevalence of key variants of concern. For the baseline model, our methods matched those of our previous publication considering the effects of population mobility on transmission and focused on epidemiological contexts with low pre-existing natural immunity, including a later post-vaccination analysis window for Oceania and Singapore. 21 In the current analysis, we incorporated scaling for the effects of policy measures obtained from the Oxford COVID-19 Government Response Tracker (OxCGRT) database, which provides time series of severity for each of several policy indicators on ordinal scales. 16, 22 We achieved close matches to all calibration targets for each country under six approaches to scaling transmission: a baseline configuration with no scaling of transmission for policy effects, two alternative approaches to scaling for policy measures and three approaches to scaling for mobility inputs (Supplemental Files 2 and 3).

Effect of policies on transmission

Policy time series compared to baseline residual transmission scaling

To motivate more formal analyses, we considered whether the OxCGRT time series appeared to track with the residual non-mechanistic scaling of transmission (henceforward "residual scaling") under the baseline model configuration that did not incorporate policy effects. OxCGRT provides recommended sets of policies that can be combined to create overall indices of government policies, including the Containment and Stringency indices, and we constructed our own custom index for further analyses. Supplemental File 4 and Figure 2 show comparisons of each of these three indices against baseline residual scaling using unweighted averages across policy domains (i.e. 1 ∑ , , where , represents the value of policy at time divided by its maximum value). Although some countries showed little correspondence between variation in transmission and these composite policy time series (third column Figure 2), in many countries variation in transmission corresponded closely with the temporal trends of these indices (first two columns).

Comparison of weighted policy time series to residual transmission scaling

Our primary model configuration with policy effects included ("OxCGRT floored") incorporated weights for the contribution of each constituent policy to scaling, as well as a floor parameter for the maximum effect of policy changes and an exponential scaling parameter to map from the policy time series to the effect on transmission. Supplemental File 6 and Figure 3 show the comparison between the residual transmission scaling (the same output as shown in Figure 2) and the calibrated policy scaling function after fitting to data. This approach generated functions that closely matched residual transmission scaling in several settings (e.g. Belgium, Aruba), suggesting that much of the unexplained variation in transmission captured by the residual process could alternatively be reproduced using policy information alone.

Reduction in residual scaling with the inclusion of policy inputs

Supplemental File 6 and Figure 4 show the remaining residual scaling required after policy effects were included in the model. If policy measures explain transmission dynamics, inclusion of policy scaling should reduce the residual need to adjust transmission. Consistent with this expectation, the residual process became substantially flatter in most countries, with fewer abrupt fluctuations than under the baseline configuration.

Evidence of an association between policies and transmission

We estimated the effect of policy changes on transmission by quantifying the reduction in variation in the residual process under the model configuration with scaling for policy effects compared to the baseline configuration without policy scaling. To quantify this formally, we considered the dispersion parameter that governs the prior distributions for each update to the residual process compared to the preceding value. This parameter was regularised by assigning a prior distribution with mode at zero to encourage the model to accept the minimum extent of residual scaling achievable while still matching the calibration targets. Supplemental File 5 and the right-hand panels of Figure 5 show example posterior distributions for countries for which this dispersion parameter reduced substantially (e.g. Norway), showed intermediate reduction (e.g. Estonia) and for which there was little or no reduction (e.g. Angola). Because this parameter quantifies the magnitude of the residual adjustments required to fit observed epidemiological data, reductions in its posterior value indicate that policy data explain variation in transmission that would otherwise need to be captured by the residual process. We therefore interpreted the proportion of calibration iterations under which this quantity reduced as the strength of evidence for an association between policies and transmission.

The left-hand panel of Figure 5 presents the results for all countries, showing strong evidence for an effect of policy changes in many high-income countries of Europe and North America, intermediate evidence in several countries of Eastern Europe, South America and Southern Africa, and minimal or no evidence for an effect in many countries of the rest of Africa and Oceania. Among the 52 high-income countries analysed, 29 showed a marked reduction in residual scaling (dispersion ratio reduced in 75% of runs or more), which was not seen in any of the 15 lowincome countries. The strongest evidence was observed predominantly in European countries, including Poland, Greece, Slovenia, Luxembourg, Ireland, Czechia, Croatia, Romania, Austria, Sweden, Norway, Andorra, United Kingdom, Italy, Finland, France, Portugal, Switzerland, Netherlands, Denmark, Germany, Belgium, as well as Aruba, Israel, Cyprus, Japan, Canada, Bahamas, United States. However, substantial evidence was also identified in several geographically heterogeneous middle-income countries, (e.g. Eswatini, Belarus, Zimbabwe, Dominican Republic, Lesotho, Kosovo, South Africa, Iran, Nepal, Cuba, Moldova, Mexico, Tunisia, Serbia, Sri Lanka), possibly reflecting greater capacity to characterise transmission dynamics through routine surveillance in some of these countries. We found no evidence for an effect of policy changes in countries for which our analysis spanned the post-vaccination roll-out Omicron period (Australia, New Zealand and Singapore), rather than the earlier pre-vaccination period considered in most of our analyses.

In countries for which there was little evidence for an effect of policy-related scaling of transmission, our alternative policy configuration (OxCGRT independent) performed less well than OxCGRT floored, because its priors were less able to support the absence of an effect.

Inferred strength of the combined policy package on mitigating transmission

We next quantified the maximum transmission reduction implied by the calibrated policy scaling function, 1 . Although simultaneous implementation of all policies with the custom suite did not often occur during our analysis windows, we used this theoretical maximum scaling of transmission as our primary metric of the effectiveness of the overall policy package implemented in each country. Figure 6 shows this estimate for the strength of policy effects across all countries analysed. Because this metric is only meaningful when evidence for policy effects was detected, countries without such evidence are displayed in grey. The strongest policy package effects were typically seen in high-income countries of Western and Northern Europe and North America, Japan and some countries of Southern Africa. Intermediate effects were seen in some other African countries and several countries of South America and South Asia. For several of the remaining African countries, as well as Oceania (for which the analysis period and methods differed), no evidence of an effect of policies on transmission was found.

Comparison to mobility analyses

To compare the effect of policy scaling to the effect of scaling for population mobility time series available from Google and Facebook, we adapted our previous mobility analyses for consistency with the policy analyses which are the primary objective of this manuscript. We harmonised the scaling function for the mobility analyses with the function used for the policy analyses to include a "floor" parameter on the maximum reduction in the scaling process prior to the exponential transformation of the scaling function, whereas this floor was not incorporated in our previous mobilityfocused analyses. Figure 7 shows that results for the policy and mobility analyses were strongly correlated, with strong effects of policy changes found in a similar group of countries as for those in which strong effects of mobility were observed.

Figure 8 compares the floor values between the policy and mobility analyses for the countries in which we found evidence of an effect under both of these analysis configurations. For 39 of 50 countries under the Google comparison and for 36 of 52 under the Facebook comparison, the floor value for the mobility analysis was lower than for the policy analysis (by a median 8.5% and 7.2% respectively for the two analyses). These findings suggest that mobility measures often captured a greater proportion of transmission-related behavioural change than policy measures alone.

Specific policy indicators

Our main analysis (OxCGRT floored) approach assigned individual weights to each of the eight policies of our custom index, which were calibrated to create an overall weighted time series for the effect of policies on scaling transmission. In many countries, specific policies were markedly upweighted by the calibration algorithm, such that the individual policy was assigned greater influence on the overall scaling function. The posterior distributions of each policy weight for each country is shown in Supplemental File 10, with randomly selected country examples presented in Figure 9. The relative extent of up-or down-weighting is presented in Figure 10, with restriction to countries for which there was evidence for an effect of the overall policy package and for which at least one policy was significantly up-or down-weighted. No policy displayed consistently strong effects across all countries, suggesting substantial heterogeneity in policy effectiveness and implementation contexts. However, school closures often upweighted in countries of Europe and Asia, while movement restrictions were down-weighted in countries across several continents.

Discussion

Across 126 countries spanning diverse policy environments and epidemic trajectories, policy stringency metrics provided substantial information regarding temporal variation in SARS-CoV-2 transmission intensity during periods when PHSMs were the primary pandemic response. Incorporating policy data frequently reduced the model's need for residual transmission scaling, indicating that policy measures explained a substantial proportion of the variation in transmission beyond what was captured by the established epidemiological drivers we explicitly modelled. Evidence for policy effects was strongest in many high-income countries of Europe and North America, although the magnitude of these effects and the policies most clearly associated with transmission varied considerably between settings. Policy effects were easier to identify when interventions were analysed as an integrated package of measures rather than as independent effects, and the strength of their effects was often comparable to that observed for population mobility under equivalent analyses for the same countries.

Evidence for policy effects was strongly structured by economic setting and region. Excluding the distinct epidemiological context of Oceania, policy signals were clearly identifiable across many high-income European and North American settings, but were largely absent in several low-income countries, particularly in Africa. This inability to detect an effect mirrored our findings for mobility metrics, suggesting that failure to translate policy recommendations into population behavioural changes was unlikely to be the primary explanation. Because the surveillance data typically provided clear epidemic trajectories despite lower case ascertainment estimates, non-detection is more likely to reflect misalignment between formal policy databases and on-the-ground implementation, rather than limitations in estimating transmission intensity itself.

Where policy effects were identifiable, they were often large. In many high-income countries, particularly in Europe, the estimated maximum policy effect exceeded 0.6 and often reached 0.8 or above. Under our modelling framework, this implies that the full package of non-pharmaceutical interventions represented in the OxCGRT database could reduce transmission by 60-80% or more when implemented simultaneously at maximum intensity. These estimates reflect the maximum potential weighted effect of the intervention packages, rather than the independent effect of individual measures. While stronger policy effects were moderately associated with higher gross domestic product and some geographic clustering was observed, these relationships were not homogeneous. This suggests that local country-specific factors, such as differences in background contact structures, policy implementation, community uptake and capacity to adhere to recommendations, played an important role in determining policy effectiveness.

Countries with strong evidence for policy effects generally also showed strong mobility effects. This was particularly evident when mobility was measured using the Facebook metric describing the number of 600 m × 600 m tiles visited, although similar patterns were observed using Google's location-based mobility indicators. This concordance is intuitive, given that many of the non-pharmaceutical interventions included in our analysis are expected to influence transmission primarily through reductions in population movement and social contact patterns. 23 Despite this alignment, the estimated transmission floor parameter () was on average 12% lower under mobility models than policy models. This could plausibly reflect the theoretical bound of mobility data -where near-zero movement would conceptually imply a near absence of transmission -whereas policy models achieve equivalent observed transmission reductions by steepening the scaling response (via the exponent mapping parameter ) while maintaining a higher baseline floor of contact.

A further notable finding was that specific interventions were often identified as important contributors to transmission control within a given country, although the specific policies selected varied markedly between settings. This heterogeneity implies that the apparent effectiveness of individual interventions was strongly influenced by the local context and implementation approach, rather than supporting a single set of universally effective policies. Policy indicators were highly correlated on principal component analysis of the raw policy time series values, with the first principal component explaining a median of 58% of temporal variation across countries. This limited the extent to which independent policy effects could be disentangled and reflects the fact that interventions were typically implemented as part of broader policy responses rather than in isolation. We therefore placed greater emphasis on results from the policy-package formulation (OxCGRT floored), which is more consistent with these implementation patterns. Furthermore, the package formulation allowed the overall policy effect to approach zero, whereas the independent policy formulation effectively required all policy effects to approach zero simultaneously to infer no effect. Consequently, our findings provide stronger evidence that changes in the overall policy response influenced transmission than for a single set of intervention-specific effects that generalises across settings.

Early studies of the effects of PHSMs inferred variation in transmission from the first epidemic wave of SARS-CoV-2 and were influential in guiding the initial response to COVID-19. 5, [7] [8] [9] [10] Our analysis advances from these works by analysing how PHSMs dynamically influenced multiple waves of transmission that were driven by heterogeneous variants of SARS-CoV-2 and span repeated cycles of policy implementation and relaxation. A study of European countries spanning the first two waves of COVID-19 found effects of PHSMs often declined over these phases of the epidemic, supporting our finding that these effects were not constant over time and depended on the local epidemiological and policy context. 11 These previous studies frequently estimated substantial effects of PHSMs but arrived at differing conclusions regarding the effectiveness of individual measures. Our findings suggest that this variation may reflect both differences in the ability of available data to capture policyrelated effects on transmission across settings and that policy packages are more readily identifiable than the effects of individual measures. Consistent with several past studies we found that mobility and transmission often varied in concert, and further show that the countries with strongest effects of mobility and of policies often coincided. 21, 24, 25 Limitations of our study include that the OxCGRT input data pertain to policy recommendations rather than compliance, 26 which we mitigated by comparing to equivalent analyses of the effects of mobility. Our national-level framework did not capture subnational policy variation, which may be substantial in large or federated countries, 27, 28 though we sought to mitigate this by analysing periods of widespread community transmission rather than localised outbreak responses. Because many interventions were implemented concurrently, our primary analysis considered policies as packages, and we did not find consistent evidence for the independent effects of specific interventions across all countries. Further, the policies we considered were largely restricted to face-covering requirements and measures expected to influence attendance at workplaces, schools and other community venues. Other behaviours that may influence transmission, including voluntary behavioural responses to changing epidemic conditions, were not explicitly represented and so may contribute to residual unexplained variation. 29 Policy measures substantially influenced SARS-CoV-2 transmission across many countries during periods in which PHSMs formed the primary public health response. Using a framework based on the reduction in residual transmission scaling required to fit epidemiological data, we identified strong evidence that policy measures explained variation in transmission beyond that captured by established epidemiological drivers. The close correspondence between policy-and mobilitybased analyses suggests that much of this effect was mediated through changes in population behaviour, although behavioural influences on transmission appear to extend beyond those directly attributable to formal policy measures. While the magnitude of policy effects varied considerably between settings, our findings demonstrate that policy signals remained detectable across multiple epidemic waves, SARS-CoV-2 variants and epidemiological contexts.

Figure 1. Illustration of study methods. Panel A, country inclusion. Included countries shaded in blue, excluded countries shaded in grey. Panel B, schematic of approach to inference. Data/parameter inputs coloured in green. Candidate model configurations coloured in red (although two different approaches to the inclusion of OxCGRT data were used for the "policy-included model", along with three approaches to including mobility data). Primary comparison for inference coloured in yellow with dotted arrow indicating main target for comparison. *Only one hospitalisation indicator used for each country, through a hierarchical selection algorithm. **Seroprevalence targets used where available, with lower weight assigned to this indicator compared to the time series indicators listed in the preceding bullets.

Figure 2.

Figure 2. Comparison between the average OxCGRT policy inputs and residual transmission scaling under the baseline model configuration (without scaling for policy changes). OxCGRT series are plotted as an unweighted average applied to the normalised policy indicators. "Stringency" and "containment" policies are the subsets of the OxCGRT policies reported that are recommended by OxCGRT. "Custom" policies are our chosen subset of policies, used throughout our subsequent analyses. Median residual scaling (navy line) and 95% credible interval (navy shaded area) presented. Rows show representative countries from each continent except Oceania. Columns illustrate the range of agreement between the median residual scaling function and the custom-policy average time series. Column 1: lowest mean relative difference over time between residual scaling and the custom policy scaling curve. Columns 2 and 3: highest and lowest Spearman rank correlation between those two series. Y-axis labels shown in full figures in Supplemental File 4; all residual scaling curves start from a value of one.

Individual policy weights obtained through calibration to epidemic data. Median scaling value (line) and 95% credible interval (shaded area) presented for baseline (navy) and OxCGRT analysis (purple). Same countries selected as for Figure 2. Y-axis labels shown in full figures in Supplemental File 6; all residual scaling curves start from a value of one.

Figure 4.

Figure 4. Comparison of residual scaling functions with (purple) and without (black) OxCGRT policy scaling. Median scaling value (line) and 95% credible interval (shaded area) presented. Same countries selected as for Figure 2.

Mobility analyses not performed for Oceania.
