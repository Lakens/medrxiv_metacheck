explanation_of_foreknowledge_and_managing_unintended_influences What is known. The author designed and conducted the discovery study using SIA/SUS medication files from January 2018 to December 2025 (142,758 new users who started a cholinesterase inhibitor between October 2019 and December 2025). Its results are public in this project. They include the main finding to be replicated (lower 24-month risk of memantine add-on with rivastigmine than with donepezil; risk difference -4.1 percentage points), its variation by age and by rivastigmine formulation, the proportion of patients lost to follow-up by drug, the distribution of baseline covariates, and data quality problems: unavailable monthly files, a health card number shared by many patients, and the rarity of recorded deaths. The hypotheses of this plan were therefore derived from observed data. This is stated openly: the plan is a confirmatory replication of a known finding, not a test of a hypothesis formed before any data were seen.

What is not known. No SIA/SUS file with a competence month after December 2025 has been downloaded, opened or summarized by the author. No eligibility, exposure, outcome or covariate data for patients who start treatment from January 2026 have been observed.

Actions taken to reduce unintended influences.

1. Separate cohort. Validation patients start treatment after the end of the discovery cohort, and anyone with anti-dementia dispensing at any time since January 2018 is excluded. No patient contributes to both analyses.

2. Frozen analysis code. A difference of 1 percentage point would therefore be detected with about 90% power at a two-sided alpha of 0.05. Even with half of the expected sample, a difference of 1.5 points would be detected with similar power. The discovery estimate at 24 months was -4.1 points. These are projections and do not change the hypotheses, the decision rule or the analysis.

measured_variables All variables are derived from SIA/SUS medication dispensing records (APAC, file family AM), aggregated into person-months.

Exposure (predictor). Cholinesterase inhibitor dispensed in the index month, identified by SIGTAP procedure code: donepezil (reference), galantamine or rivastigmine. For hypothesis H3, rivastigmine is split into oral (capsules or oral solution) and transdermal patch.

Secondary outcome (sensitivity). Composite of memantine add-on or end of dispensing (loss to follow-up or recorded death), dated at the first month without dispensing.

Censoring variables.

1. Recorded death: exit reason 41 to 43 or death indicator.

2. Loss to follow-up: more than 3 observed months without any dispensing; months without an available file in the state of the last dispensing are not counted.

3. Administrative end: 12 months of follow-up or end of available data in that state.

1. Age, as a natural spline with 3 degrees of freedom, plus an indicator for missing age; age 100 or over is coded as 100.

2. Sex.

3. Race/colour, with “not informed” kept as a category.

4. ICD-10 code of the main diagnosis.

5. Body mass index category, calculated from recorded weight and height within plausible ranges (25 to 250 kg, 100 to 220 cm), with a missing category.

6. State of residence (states with fewer than 50 new users pooled with a neighbouring state).

7. Calendar month of initiation, as a natural spline with 4 degrees of freedom.

Variables used for eligibility and exclusion. ICD-10 code (G30.x or F00.x); dates of all anti-dementia dispensings since January 2018 (to define new users and exclude prior users); drugs dispensed in the index month (to exclude memantine and more than one cholinesterase inhibitor); number of records per identifier per month (to flag identifiers shared by many patients).

Variables used for subsetting.

1. Age group (under 75, 75 to 84, 85 or over) for H2.

2. Rivastigmine formulation for H3.

3. Year of initiation (2026) for the 24-month analysis.

4. Dose of the first dispensing: the lowest dose of each formulation, for the sensitivity analysis restricted to patients starting titration.
indices No composite scales or indices are created. The derived variables are:

1. Body mass index: weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in metres, computed only when weight is between 25 and 250 kg and height between 100 and 220 cm; otherwise missing. Categorized as in the discovery study, with a missing category.

2. Index month: the first competence month with any dispensing of donepezil, galantamine, rivastigmine or memantine for the pseudonymous patient.

3. New-user status: a patient is a new user if there is no anti-dementia dispensing in any month from January 2018 to the month before the index month.

4. Shared-identifier flag: an identifier is flagged when it has more than 20 records in a single person-month, or when it exceeds the placeholder threshold used in the discovery study (5%); records of flagged identifiers are excluded.

5. Follow-up time, in months: from the index month to the first of memantine add-on, recorded death, loss to follow-up, 12 months, or the end of available data in the state of the last dispensing. Loss to follow-up is dated at the last dispensing month when more than 3 consecutive observed months follow without any dispensing; months without an available file in that state are skipped.

6. Stabilized inverse probability of treatment weight: for each patient, the overall proportion of patients who started the same drug, divided by the patient’s predicted probability of starting that drug given the baseline covariates. The predicted probability comes from a multinomial logistic regression of drug on the baseline covariates. Weights are truncated at the 1st and 99th percentiles.

7. Inverse probability of censoring weight: for each patient and month, the running product over the months of follow-up of the probability of remaining uncensored given drug and time only, divided by the probability of remaining uncensored given drug, time and baseline covariates. Both probabilities come from pooled logistic regressions on person-months for censoring by loss to follow-up or recorded death; time since initiation enters as a natural spline with 4 degrees of freedom. The administrative end of data is treated as non-informative.

8. Weighted cumulative incidence for each drug: one minus the weighted Kaplan-Meier survival curve for memantine add-on, using the product of the treatment and censoring weights for each patient-month.

9. Effect measures at 12 months:

. risk difference: cumulative incidence with galantamine or rivastigmine minus cumulative incidence with donepezil;

. risk ratio: cumulative incidence with galantamine or rivastigmine divided by that with donepezil;

. for H2: risk ratio in patients aged 85 or over divided by risk ratio in patients under 75.

Confidence intervals are the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of 200 bootstrap replicates, with all models refitted in each replicate.
statistical_tests All analyses use the frozen code deposited in this project (pipeline_coorte_alz.qmd) and are run in R. The unit of analysis is the patient; time is measured in months.

1. Propensity score model (all hypotheses). Multinomial logistic regression (nnet::multinom) of the drug started (donepezil, galantamine, rivastigmine) on age (natural spline, 3 df) with a missing-age indicator, sex, race/colour, ICD-10 code, BMI category, state of residence and calendar month of initiation (natural spline, 4 df). No interaction terms. Stabilized weights are truncated at the 1st and 99th percentiles.

2. Censoring model. Pooled logistic regression on person-months for censoring by loss to follow-up or recorded death. The denominator model includes drug, time since initiation (natural spline, 4 df) and all baseline covariates; the numerator model includes drug and time only. Censoring weights are multiplied by the treatment weights.

3. Outcome model. Weighted cumulative incidence of memantine add-on in each arm, estimated with a weighted Kaplan-Meier estimator (one minus the weighted survival curve). This is a nonparametric estimator; no outcome regression model is fitted.

4. Hypothesis tests and contrasts.

. H1 (primary): risk difference at 12 months, rivastigmine versus donepezil. Supported if the upper limit of its 95% bootstrap confidence interval is below zero.

. Galantamine versus donepezil and rivastigmine versus galantamine: risk differences and risk ratios at 12 months, reported with confidence intervals and no hypothesis test.

. H2: the whole procedure (propensity model, censoring model and weighted cumulative incidence) is refitted separately in patients under 75 and in patients aged 85 or over. Supported if the 95% bootstrap confidence interval of the ratio of the two rivastigmine versus donepezil risk ratios lies entirely below 1. Patients aged 75 to 84 are analysed and reported but not part of the test.

. H3: the procedure is refitted in two subsets, each containing all donepezil and galantamine users plus either oral or transdermal rivastigmine users. The two rivastigmine versus donepezil risk differences at 12 months are reported with confidence intervals. No formal threshold.

. 24-month analysis: repeated for patients who started treatment in 2026.

5. Confidence intervals. 200 nonparametric bootstrap replicates resampling patients, with all models refitted in each replicate (seed 20261001 plus the replicate number). Percentile intervals (2.5th and 97.5th percentiles).

6. Sensitivity analyses. Composite outcome of memantine add-on or end of dispensing, weighted by treatment weights only. Untruncated weights. A 1% threshold for shared identifiers. Treatment weights only, without censoring weights. Analysis restricted to patients whose first dispensing is at the lowest dose of each formulation. E-values for the risk ratios of the primary contrast.

7. Checks and decision rules.

. Covariate balance: absolute standardized mean differences after weighting must be below 0.1 for every covariate in every pair of arms. If not, the propensity model is extended once by adding interactions of age with sex and with state; the model with better balance is used, and both are reported.

. Positivity: the distribution of weights and the share of each drug by state are reported. States in which any drug has fewer than 5% of new users are reported, and a sensitivity analysis excluding them is run.

. Convergence: if the multinomial model does not converge with the default settings, the maximum number of iterations is increased. Bootstrap replicates that fail to converge are discarded, and their number is reported.

. Minimum size for subgroup analyses (H2 and H3): at least 200 patients and 30 memantine add-on events in each arm of the subset, the same go/no-go thresholds used in the pilot study. Subsets below these thresholds are reported descriptively only, and the corresponding hypothesis is declared not testable.

. There are no positive controls, negative controls or manipulation checks. The discovery estimate serves as the reference for consistency: the 12-month risk difference of the validation cohort is reported next to the discovery estimate, with the difference between them and its confidence interval.
transformations No centering or scaling is applied. Categorical variables enter the models as indicator (dummy) variables with treatment coding (R default), with one reference level each:

1. drug: donepezil as reference;

2. sex;

3. race/colour, with “not informed” (code 99) kept as its own category;

4. ICD-10 code;

5. BMI category: under 18.5, 18.5 to 25, 25 to 30, 30 or more, and missing;

6. state of residence, with states with fewer than 50 new users pooled with a neighbouring state and Amapá pooled with Pará.

In any categorical covariate, levels with fewer than 30 patients are pooled into a single “other” category. Age (coded 100 when 100 years or more) enters as a natural cubic spline with 3 degrees of freedom; calendar month of initiation enters as a natural cubic spline with 4 degrees of freedom; time since initiation in the censoring model enters as a natural cubic spline with 4 degrees of freedom. Body mass index is computed from weight and height and then categorized; values outside plausible ranges are set to missing before categorization.
inference_criteria Inference is based on point estimates and 95% bootstrap percentile confidence intervals (200 replicates). No p-values are used.

1. H1 (primary): supported if the upper limit of the two-sided 95% confidence interval of the 12-month risk difference (rivastigmine minus donepezil) is below zero. This corresponds to a one-sided test at the 2.5% level in the predicted direction.

2. H2: supported if the 95% confidence interval of the ratio of the rivastigmine versus donepezil risk ratios (85 or over versus under 75) lies entirely below 1.

3. H3 and the galantamine contrasts: estimation only, interpreted with their confidence intervals, without a decision threshold.

Multiplicity: H1 is the single primary hypothesis, so no correction is applied to it. H2 is secondary and is interpreted as supportive evidence only, whatever the result of H1. Sensitivity and subgroup analyses are not corrected for multiplicity and are not used to support or reject H1; they are reported in full. Estimates are also reported on the risk ratio scale, with E-values for the primary contrast.

2. Age: missing values receive a missing-age indicator in the propensity model.

3. Race/colour: “not informed” is analysed as a separate category.

additional_comments Relation to the discovery study. How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Abstract

Background. Donepezil, galantamine and rivastigmine are dispensed free of charge to people with mild to moderate Alzheimer's disease in the Brazilian Unified Health System (SUS), but their comparative long-term effectiveness in routine care is unknown. Under the national clinical protocol, memantine is added to a cholinesterase inhibitor (ChEI) when the disease reaches the moderate stage, so memantine add-on can serve as a marker of clinical progression.

Nationwide new-user cohort study emulating a target trial in which adults with Alzheimer's disease eligible for a ChEI would be randomly assigned to start donepezil, galantamine or rivastigmine and followed for memantine add-on. We used outpatient medication dispensing authorizations (APAC records of the Outpatient Information System, SIA/SUS) from all 27 federative units, January 2018 to December 2025. We included patients who started a single ChEI between October 2019, after memantine was first recorded nationally, and December 2025, after at least 12 months without anti-dementia dispensing. The outcome was the first memantine dispensing after the index month. Follow-up ended at add-on, recorded death, loss to follow-up (more than 3 observed months without dispensing) or end of data. Deaths were rarely recorded and were treated as censoring. One pseudonymized health card number shared by many patients was excluded, and follow-up ended earlier in the two affected states. We estimated the risk of add-on at 12 to 48 months had all patients started each drug and remained under observation, using stabilized inverse probability of treatment and censoring weights, with 95% confidence intervals (CI) from 200 bootstrap replicates. The analysis assumed no unmeasured confounding or informative censoring given the measured covariates. 1.

1 Introduction Background

The number of people living with dementia worldwide was estimated at 57.4 million in 2019 and is projected to reach 152.8 million in 2050, with the largest relative increases in North Africa and the Middle East and in eastern sub-Saharan Africa [1]. In Brazil, the national dementia report estimated a prevalence of 8.5% among people aged 60 years or older in 2019 [2]. Alzheimer's disease is the most common cause.

Cholinesterase inhibitors (ChEIs) are the standard symptomatic treatment for mild to moderate Alzheimer's disease, and the 2024 Lancet Commission recommends that ChEIs and memantine be provided to people with the disease [3]. Placebo-controlled trials, most lasting about 6 months, show modest benefits on cognition, global function and activities of daily living for galantamine [4], donepezil [5] and rivastigmine, including the transdermal patch [6]. The same Cochrane review found no evidence of differences in efficacy between the three drugs [4]; the few head-to-head trials gave conflicting results, and adjusted indirect comparisons found no significant differences in cognition [7]. Evidence on long-term outcomes in routine care comes mainly from registries in high-income countries [8].

In the Brazilian Unified Health System (SUS), the three ChEIs are dispensed free of charge through the Specialized Component of Pharmaceutical Assistance, under a national clinical protocol that defines eligibility by cognitive and functional staging [9]; they have been provided since the first version of the protocol, in 2002 [10]. Memantine was incorporated in 2017 for moderate disease, in combination with a ChEI, and for severe disease as monotherapy [9, 11].

Each dispensing is authorized and recorded monthly in a national information system, which makes it possible to follow every treated patient over time. Under the protocol, the addition of memantine to a ChEI corresponds to clinical progression to the moderate stage, so time to memantine add-on is a pragmatic marker of progression that is recorded for all patients. An earlier Brazilian study described failures in monitoring compliance with the protocol at dispensing [12], but no study has compared the three ChEIs nationwide. A randomized trial comparing the three drugs over several years in routine care is unlikely to be conducted, because all three are approved, off patent and available in the SUS. Emulating such a trial with the national dispensing records makes explicit the comparison of interest and helps avoid common design errors, such as immortal time and the inclusion of prevalent users [13, 14]. The specification of the target trial (eligibility, treatment strategies and outcome) was informed by the placebo-controlled trials of the three ChEIs [4] [5] [6] and by the national clinical protocol [9].

Objectives

The primary objective was to estimate the effect of starting donepezil, galantamine or rivastigmine on the risk of memantine add-on up to 48 months, in patients with Alzheimer's disease treated in the SUS. The causal question was: among patients eligible for a ChEI under the national protocol, how would the cumulative risk of memantine add-on differ if all had started galantamine or rivastigmine instead of donepezil, regardless of later switching or discontinuation (intention-to-treat effect)? 2.11 Statistical methods

Estimands and identifying assumptions. For each of the three pairwise contrasts, the estimands were the differences and ratios of the cumulative risk of memantine add-on at 12, 24, 36 and 48 months had all eligible patients started one drug rather than the other, in the hypothetical absence of censoring (observational analogue of the intention-to-treat effect). Identification relied on four assumptions: conditional exchangeability, that is, no unmeasured confounding of the drug choice given the measured baseline covariates (age, sex, race/colour, ICD-10 code, BMI, state of residence and calendar month); positivity, that is, a non-zero probability of starting each drug in every covariate stratum, checked by the distribution of propensity scores and weights; consistency, that is, a well-defined intervention, supported by the restriction to patients starting a single ChEI in the index month; and no informative censoring given drug, time since initiation and the same baseline covariates. All covariates were measured in the index month from the APAC record, as described under Data sources and measurement. Correct specification of the treatment and censoring models was also assumed.

Confounding.

Treatment weights were estimated with a multinomial logistic model for the three drugs [23, 24], including all baseline covariates listed above. Stabilized weights were the marginal probability of the drug received divided by its predicted probability [25, 26]. Covariate balance was assessed with standardized mean differences for all pairwise comparisons, with 0.1 as the threshold for meaningful imbalance [27, 28].

Outcome analysis.

Censoring by loss to follow-up or recorded death was modelled with pooled logistic regression on person-months, including drug, time since initiation (natural spline, 4 degrees of freedom) and baseline covariates in the denominator and drug and time in the numerator [29]. Administrative end of data was treated as non-informative. The product of treatment and censoring weights was truncated at the 1st and 99th percentiles. Cumulative incidence of memantine add-on was estimated as one minus the weighted Kaplan-Meier estimator on person-month data [30]. We report cumulative incidence, risk differences and risk ratios at 12, 24, 36 and 48 months, rather than hazard ratios [31]. Percentile 95% CIs were obtained from 200 non-parametric bootstrap replicates that resampled patients and refitted all models [32].

Crude (unweighted) estimates were obtained with the Kaplan-Meier estimator, with Greenwood standard errors and delta-method CIs for differences and ratios.

Subgroups and interactions.

Analyses within age groups and by rivastigmine formulation were done as additional analyses (see below); no formal tests of interaction were performed.

Missing data. Age, sex, ICD-10 code and state of residence were complete. Race/colour was not informed for 22.9% and BMI was missing for 2.7% of patients; both were analysed with an explicit missing category. No imputation was done.

Loss to follow-up. Patients without dispensing for more than 3 observed months were censored at their last dispensing, and IPCW was used to account for the dependence of this censoring on drug, time and baseline covariates.

Sensitivity analyses.

We repeated the main analysis with untruncated weights, without censoring weights, without any weighting, and in the cohort defined with the stricter 1% placeholder threshold.

Additional analyses on disease severity and unrecorded deaths. Because severity and death are not recorded, three additional analyses examined alternative explanations for differences between drugs. First, to address differential mortality and dropout, we used a composite outcome of memantine add-on or end of dispensing (loss to follow-up or recorded death, dated at the first month without dispensing), whichever came first; unrecorded deaths and dropouts are events in this analysis, so they cannot favour one drug. It was weighted by stabilized IPTW only, because the only remaining censoring was the administrative end of data, with 95% CIs from 200 bootstrap replicates. Second, to address the selection of frailer patients for the transdermal patch, oral and transdermal rivastigmine users were analysed separately, each together with all donepezil and galantamine users. Third, because unrecorded deaths are much more frequent at older ages, the main analysis was repeated within age groups (<75, 75-84 and ≥85 years). In the second and third analyses the propensity and censoring models were refitted in each subset.

Finally, we calculated E-values, the minimum strength of association on the risk ratio scale that an unmeasured confounder, such as disease severity, would need with both the drug and the outcome to explain away an estimate [33].

Analyses were done in R 4.5.3 [34] with the packages data.table, survival [35] and nnet [36], in a single Quarto document that reproduces the whole pipeline.

4 Discussion

4.1 Key results

In this nationwide cohort of 142 758 patients starting a ChEI in the Brazilian public health system, starting rivastigmine was associated with a lower risk of memantine add-on over four years than starting donepezil or galantamine, with an absolute difference of about 4 per 100 patients at 2 years and 7 per 100 at 4 years relative to donepezil. The difference between galantamine and donepezil was small and depended on adjustment. The results were robust to weight truncation, to the censoring model, to the handling of a data quality problem affecting two large states, to counting deaths and dropouts as events, and across age groups and both formulations of rivastigmine. The dispensing data were suitable for following patients over time, but not for ascertaining deaths.

4.2 Limitations

The emulation departs from the target trial in several ways (Table 1). Assignment was not random, so the comparison depends on conditional exchangeability given a small set of recorded covariates; eligibility could not be checked against cognitive and functional staging; time zero and follow-up were measured in calendar months; the outcome was a dispensing event used as a proxy of clinical progression; and deaths, which would be observed in a trial, were largely missing. The plausibility of the identifying assumptions is discussed below.

The most important limitation is that deaths were recorded for less than 0.5% of treated patients per year, far below the mortality expected in an older population with dementia. Death could not be analysed as an outcome or as a competing event, and the analysis estimates the risk of add-on in a hypothetical scenario without censoring, including censoring by death.

Rivastigmine users were older, so unrecorded deaths are probably more frequent among them; if deaths occur preferentially in patients who would otherwise have progressed, and are not predicted by the recorded covariates, the censoring weights would not remove the bias, and the lower risk of add-on with rivastigmine would be overestimated. Two additional analyses argue against a large bias of this kind, although they cannot exclude it: the difference was unchanged when deaths and dropouts were counted as events, and it was present in patients younger than 75 years, in whom deaths are uncommon. The slightly larger difference at older ages is, however, compatible with some contribution of unrecorded deaths.

Disease severity, education, comorbidities and co-medications are not recorded, a limitation of DATASUS administrative records for observational research that we have described previously [38]. If clinicians prefer rivastigmine for patients at an earlier or more stable stage, residual confounding would also favour rivastigmine; if they prefer it for frailer patients who are less likely to be considered for combination therapy, the effect would be in the same direction. We could not measure these biases directly; the E-value of 2.84 indicates that an unmeasured factor would need to be strongly associated with both drug choice and memantine add-on to explain away the difference between rivastigmine and donepezil. Access to specialists who can prescribe and the availability of each drug in state pharmacies may also influence which ChEI is started; the analysis was adjusted for state, but local access and stock shortages were not measured.

Dispensing does not guarantee use. Diagnostic codes may not reflect the clinical subtype: 53.8% of patients were coded as early-onset disease (G30.0), although the mean age was 77 years. The outcome is a proxy of progression that depends on the organization of care and on reassessments that are not always performed [12]. Follow-up was relatively short (median 14 months), and many patients were lost to follow-up, although the censoring model and sensitivity analyses gave consistent results. Retention checks showed irregular dispensing and low-volume months in some states, and 29 monthly files were unavailable; we treated these months as unobserved but could not correct months with incomplete files. Switching between ChEIs was ignored, so estimates refer to the initial choice of drug.

4.3 Interpretation

Randomized trials have not shown differences in efficacy between the three ChEIs [4, 7], and the size of the rivastigmine difference is larger than would be expected from the trial evidence.

Several explanations other than slower disease progression are plausible, and the data do not allow us to separate them. Memantine add-on depends on a clinical reassessment and a new authorization, so the outcome reflects prescribing and access as well as progression; if patients started on rivastigmine are followed by different prescribers or services, or reassessed less often, add-on would be less frequent without any difference in disease course. Most rivastigmine users started with the transdermal patch, which may be preferred for older or frailer patients and for those with swallowing difficulties or poor oral adherence; such patients may be less likely to be considered for combination therapy. Unmeasured severity, discussed above, would act in the same direction. The difference was smaller with oral than with transdermal rivastigmine, so part of it may relate to the patch itself, for example through better persistence, or to the patients for whom it is chosen.

In the Swedish Dementia Registry, ChEI users had slightly slower cognitive decline and a 27% lower risk of death than matched non-users over an average of 5 years, and galantamine was the only ChEI associated with a lower risk of severe dementia [8]. We did not observe an advantage for galantamine, and the lower risk of add-on with rivastigmine has no counterpart in that study; the outcomes, however, differ (severe dementia defined by cognitive score versus a prescribing event). Registries of this kind record cognitive scores and deaths, which the Brazilian dispensing data lack. Our findings are therefore best seen as a signal that deserves confirmation with linked mortality data and clinical measures, rather than as evidence that one drug slows progression. The analysis also showed that the design problems of administrative data can be large: without the detection of the shared health card number, thousands of patients in two states would have appeared to be lost to follow-up in a single month.

4.4 Generalisability

The cohort includes all patients who started a ChEI through the public specialized pharmaceutical component, which is the main source of these drugs for most of the Brazilian population.

The results apply to patients who meet the protocol criteria and obtain their medicines from the SUS, and may not apply to patients treated privately or outside the protocol. Coverage treatment from January 2026 has been registered prospectively, with the analysis code fixed before any of its data were accessed [39].

5 Conclusions

In routine SUS care, patients who started rivastigmine had a lower risk of memantine add-on than those who started donepezil or galantamine, while galantamine and donepezil were similar.

Because the dispensing data do not record disease severity or death reliably, these differences cannot be attributed to slower progression without confirmation in linked data. Nationwide SUS dispensing records can support target trial emulation when their quality is checked and data problems are handled explicitly.
