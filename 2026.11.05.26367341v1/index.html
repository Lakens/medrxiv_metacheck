How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

The apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) remains the standard metric for assessing obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) severity, although it does not fully capture the physiological consequences of respiratory events. Hypoxic burden (HB), which integrates the frequency, depth, and duration of oxygen desaturations, has been proposed as a more comprehensive measure of OSA-related hypoxic stress. This study compared the prognostic value of HB and AHI for subjective and objective treatment outcomes in patients undergoing continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) therapy.

Data from 412 patients with OSA who underwent diagnostic and CPAP-treated polysomnography were analyzed, including 52 patients with available follow-up questionnaires. HB was calculated using a validated event-based algorithm. Multivariable regression models were used to predict patientreported outcomes and changes in sleep architecture, adjusted for age, sex and body mass index.

HB consistently demonstrated stronger associations with symptomatic outcomes than AHI. Models including HB showed improved model fit and explained variance. HB remained independently associated with lower follow-up fatigue and better sleep quality after adjustment for AHI. In contrast, AHI was the dominant predictor of objective sleep architecture improvements, including increases in N3 sleep, reductions in N1 sleep, and reductions in arousal index. HB provided additional independent information only for changes in arousal burden.

These findings suggest that HB and AHI capture complementary dimensions of OSA severity. While AHI primarily reflects sleep fragmentation and physiological response to CPAP, HB appears more closely related to subjective treatment benefit. Incorporating HB alongside AHI may improve clinical phenotyping and prediction of treatment response in OSA.

1 Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is one of the most common sleep-related breathing disorders and represents a major public health burden worldwide [1]. It is characterized by recurrent episodes of partial or complete upper airway obstruction during sleep, resulting in intermittent hypoxemia, sleep fragmentation and excessive daytime sleepiness. Current estimates suggest that nearly one billion individuals worldwide are affected by OSA, with prevalence increasing due to rising obesity rates and an aging population [2]. Established risk factors include older age, obesity, male sex, craniofacial anatomy, enlarged tonsils, genetic predisposition, and lifestyle-related factors such as alcohol use and smoking [3], [4].

OSA is associated with impaired quality of life, neurocognitive dysfunction, depressive symptoms, and increased cardiovascular and metabolic risk [5]. Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) therapy remains the first-line treatment for moderate to severe OSA and is effective in reducing respiratory events and improving symptoms [6]. However, treatment response varies substantially between patients, and conventional measures of disease severity do not fully explain this heterogeneity [7], [8].

Traditionally, OSA severity is classified using the apnea-hypopnea index (AHI), defined as the number of respiratory events per hour of sleep [9]. Although widely used in clinical practice and guideline recommendations, the AHI has important limitations [10]. It quantifies event frequency but does not account for event duration, depth of oxygen desaturation, cumulative hypoxic stress, or the physiological consequences of respiratory disturbances [10]. As a result, patients with similar AHI values may differ considerably in symptom burden, cardiovascular risk, and therapeutic benefit [11]. Increasing criticism has therefore emerged regarding the use of AHI as the sole marker of OSA severity [12].

Hypoxic burden (HB) has been proposed by Azarbarzin et al. [13] as a more physiologically meaningful metric that quantifies the cumulative area under oxygen desaturation curves associated with respiratory events [10]. By integrating frequency, depth, and duration of desaturations, HB may better reflect the biological burden of OSA than AHI alone. Large cohort studies have shown that HB predicts cardiovascular morbidity, mortality, and adverse outcomes more strongly than traditional metrics such as AHI [14], [15]. These findings have generated growing interest in HB as a tool for risk stratification and treatment monitoring [12].

Despite this promising evidence, current research on HB has focused predominantly on cardiovascular outcomes and survival analyses [15]. Whether HB also provides clinically meaningful information for treatment decision-making, symptom improvement, and physiological response to CPAP therapy remains insufficiently studied [12].

The present study aims to systematically compare AHI and HB as predictors of both subjective and objective treatment outcomes in patients with OSA undergoing CPAP therapy. Specifically, we examined whether baseline HB provides incremental prognostic value beyond AHI for (1) patientreported outcomes (PROs) and (2) sleep architecture. Therefore, this study seeks to clarify the potential clinical value of HB for individualized therapy decisions and treatment evaluation in OSA.

2 Methods

2.1 Dataset

This study is based on data from our local clinical sleep dataset. It was recorded in the sleep laboratory of the Department for Neurology at the University Hospital Carl Gustav Carus in Dresden. The dataset comprises routine clinical sleep studies from patients, who were referred for the evaluation of sleep disorders, including sleep-related breathing disorders, insomnia, hypersomnia, and movement disorders.

All recordings are standard in-laboratory overnight PSGs. Recordings were carried out using Alice 5 diagnostic sleep system (Philips Respironics) between 2007 and 2021 and Domino software (SOMNOmedics AG) between 2021 and 2025. The PSG data includes multimodal physiological signals, such as electroencephalography (EEG), electrooculography, electromyography, respiratory effort, airflow, oxygen saturation (SpO 2 ), and electrocardiography, enabling comprehensive assessment of sleep architecture and respiratory events. CPAP therapy recordings were typically conducted one night after the diagnosis night, using a CPAP therapy device.

Sleep stages and respiratory events were scored by trained and experienced PSG technologists and somnologists according to standard clinical criteria from the AASM [16]. Diagnoses of sleep disorders were based on the International Classification of Sleep Disorders (ICSD) [17]. PROs were assessed using validated questionnaires, including the Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS), Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS), Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), and Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9). Baseline assessments were obtained at the time of diagnostic PSG night, and follow-up assessments were collected approximately one year later. PSG recordings from different acquisition systems and file formats were imported and processed using the open-source Sleep Harmonizer framework, which is part of SleePyPhases [18]. The Sleep Harmonizer provides a unified interface for loading and harmonizing polysomnographic recordings and annotations across multiple vendor-specific formats, enabling standardized downstream analyses.

For the present analysis, we included all patients diagnosed with obstructive sleep apnea who underwent diagnostic night and CPAP therapy nights within three days of each other, both of which were monitored using PSG. Only cases with available covariate data were included, resulting in a total of 412 cases. For the analysis of symptomatic outcomes, we additionally only included patients with good CPAP adherence and with available follow-up questionnaire data. Good CPAP adherence was defined as an average daily use of the CPAP device of at least 4 hours and treatment effectiveness. This resulted in a total of 52 cases with PROs (see Figure 1). 2.2 Calculation of Sleep Apnea Severity Metrics

The hypoxic burden (HB), as defined by Azarbarzin et al. [13], describes the cumulative impact of oxygen desaturations associated with respiratory events. It is calculated as the sum of the areas between a pre-event baseline and the oxygen saturation curve, associated with each apnea and hypopnea event. The HB is designed to integrate both the depth and duration of each desaturation event, thereby providing a more comprehensive picture of hypoxic stress than simple event counts such as AHI.

The algorithm for HB calculation was implemented as follows. Individual SpO 2 segments associated with manually scored respiratory events are averaged and processed to calculate the search window. The boundaries of the search window are determined by the two peaks before and after the end of the event of the averaged SpO 2 curve. The pre-event baseline for each respiratory event is defined as the maximum SpO 2 value within 120 seconds prior to the end of the event. The area between the baseline and the SpO 2 curve is calculated over the subject-specific search window for each event. The HB is the cumulative sum of the individual areas, divided by the total sleep time.

We implemented the HB algorithm in Python, based on the MATLAB implementation by Philip de Chazal [19]. We adopted it for different sampling rates and optimized the filter function for the averaged SpO 2 signal by using a lowpass FIR filter with cutoff frequency of 0.1 Hz to smooth the signal. Our implementation can be found under the MIT License on GitLab, see https://gitlab.com/sleep-is-all-you-need/hypoxic-burden.

We evaluated and tested our HB implementation thoroughly on the Sleep Heart Health Study (SHHS) dataset [21], comprising nearly 5,800 polysomnographic recordings. The dataset was provided by the National Sleep Research Resource (NSRR) [22]. The implemented HB algorithm showed excellent agreement with the reference implementation in MATLAB by Philip de Chazal. This was demonstrated by near-perfect Pearson correlation (r > 0.999) and minimal systematic bias (bias = -1.15 %min/h).

However, the definition and selection of the respiratory events used as input for the algorithm have a substantial impact on the resulting HB values. We observed that the annotated apnea and hypopnea events in the SHHS polysomnography data do not directly correspond to the events used to calculate the specified AHI value in the SHHS dataset files. Although there are quite a few different AHI definitions, the number of annotated respiratory events exceeds the values included in all of these definitions. This is most likely because all scored apnea and hypopneas events are listed in the annotations, and even basic criteria for AHI calculation are applied afterwards. For instance, if an apnea was scored during a wake epoch, it would still be listed in the respiratory event list but not considered in any AHI calculation. While this is conclusive in itself, it creates the additional task of event selection for event-based algorithms.

Therefore, a harmonized event selection was required to ensure consistency between calculation of HB and AHI. We replicated the AHI according to the definition "ahi_a0h3" from the SHHS dataset. The AHI was defined as the total number of apneas and hypopneas divided by the total sleep time in hours. For apneas, the criteria were a ≥ 90 % drop in airflow for ≥ 10 seconds without oxygen desaturation threshold and with or without arousals. For hypopneas, the criteria were a ≥ 30 % drop in airflow for ≥ 10 seconds, as well as a ≥ 3 % oxygen desaturation from a pre-event baseline with or without arousals. Oxygen desaturation was computed relative to a pre-event baseline, defined as the maximum of a 120-second window preceding event onset. Additionally, the calculation only includes apneas and hypopneas that began during sleep.

After automatically applying these filtering criteria to the annotated respiratory events, the resulting AHI values showed strong agreement with the SHHS reference values. The comparison between the implemented AHI and the ahi_a0h3 reference demonstrated a very high correlation (Pearson r = 0.981, p < 0.001), with a small systematic bias of -0.50 events/h, indicating a slight underestimation relative to the reference method, most likely due to slight implementation differences of the event selection rules.

Using the same respiratory event definitions, HB and AHI were calculated for our local clinical dataset.

2.3 Statistical Analysis

Multivariable linear regression analyses were performed to evaluate the incremental prognostic value of HB beyond the AHI in predicting symptomatic and physiological responses to CPAP treatment in patients with OSA.

The two outcome domains, PROs and PSG-derived outcomes, were analyzed. PROs included daytime sleepiness (ESS), fatigue (FSS), sleep quality (PSQI), and depressive symptoms (PHQ-9). For these analyses, follow-up questionnaire scores obtained approximately one year after diagnostic night served as dependent variables while adjusting for baseline questionnaire scores. PSG-derived outcomes included N3 sleep (%), N1 sleep (%), REM latency, and arousal index. For PSG-derived outcomes, treatment effects were quantified as the difference between the CPAP therapy night and the diagnostic night: Model performance was evaluated using the coefficient of determination (R²), reflecting explained variance, and the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), which assesses model fit while penalizing model complexity. Comparisons across Models A, B, and AB were used to determine the relative predictive value of AHI and HB. The inclusion of both predictors in Model AB enabled evaluation of whether HB contributed unique explanatory information after accounting for AHI, while also allowing assessment of the independent associations of each OSA severity metric with treatment outcomes.

Model assumptions were assessed using residual diagnostics, including tests for normality (Omnibus and Jarque-Bera tests). Due to deviations from normality in several models, robust standard errors (HC1) were calculated as sensitivity analyses. Results were considered robust if statistical significance remained consistent. Statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using Python's statsmodels library. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, unless otherwise stated.

3 Results

In the following, the results of the HB and AHI during diagnostic and therapy night, as well as the multivariable linear regression models for PROs and PSG-derived outcomes are depicted.

3.1 Hypoxic Burden and Apnea Hypopnea Index

Descriptive statistics and correlations of the derived sleep apnea severity metrics for all OSA patients in our local clinical dataset are presented in Table 2. Both AHI and HB decreased markedly from the diagnostic night to the CPAP therapy night. AHI and HB were strongly correlated in both the diagnostic night (r = 0.80, p < 0.001) and the therapy night (r = 0.82, p < 0.001).

3.2 Patient-Reported Outcomes

A broadly consistent pattern emerged across all investigated PROs. Models including HB generally showed better model fit than models including AHI alone, as reflected by higher R² values and lower AIC values. Results of the multivariable linear regression models for PROs are summarized in Table 3. In the AHI-only models (Model A), higher AHI at diagnostic night was associated with lower follow-up ESS scores (β = -0.075, p = 0.003), whereas no significant associations were observed for fatigue (FSS), sleep quality (PSQI), or depressive symptoms (PHQ-9). In contrast, higher HB at diagnostic night was significantly associated with lower follow-up symptom burden across all investigated PROs in the HBonly models (Model B), including ESS (β = -0.017, p = 0.001), FSS (β = -0.026, p = 0.023), PSQI (β = -0.009, p = 0.023), and PHQ-9 (β = -0.007, p = 0.041).

When both metrics were entered simultaneously (Model AB), associations between AHI and followup symptom burden were no longer statistically significant. In contrast, HB remained independently associated with lower follow-up fatigue (β = -0.074, p = 0.015) and better sleep quality (β = -0.026, p = 0.018), whereas associations with daytime sleepiness and depressive symptoms were attenuated and no longer significant after adjustment for AHI.

Overall, models including HB demonstrated superior explanatory performance compared with models including AHI alone. The largest improvements in model fit were observed for fatigue and sleep quality, for which the combined models achieved the highest R² values and lowest AIC values for these outcomes (Table 3).

Figure 2 shows the adjusted regression coefficients and 95% confidence intervals from the fully adjusted models. HB demonstrated consistently negative effect estimates across all PROs, indicating a tendency toward lower residual symptom burden with increasing baseline HB. In contrast, AHI estimates were associated with wider confidence intervals and none remained statistically significant after adjustment for HB.

3.3 PSG-Derived Outcomes

AHI at diagnostic night was consistently associated with CPAP-related improvements in sleep architecture, higher AHI at diagnostic night was associated with stronger improvements in sleep architecture at therapy night. Although similar associations were observed for HB, models including AHI generally demonstrated better explanatory performance than the corresponding HB-only models.

Results of the multivariable linear regression models for PSG-derived outcomes are summarized in Table 4. In the AHI-only models (Model A), higher baseline AHI predicted larger increases in N3 sleep (β = 0.161, p < 0.001), larger reductions in N1 sleep (β = -0.310, p < 0.001), shorter REM latency (β = -0.933, p < 0.001), and greater reductions in arousal index (β = -0.400, p < 0.001). Similar associations were observed in the HB-only models (Model B), including N3 sleep (β = 0.038, p < 0.001), N1 sleep (β = -0.070, p < 0.001), REM latency (β = -0.242, p < 0.001), and arousal index (β = -0.099, p < 0.001), although model performance was consistently lower than in the corresponding AHI models.

When both metrics were entered simultaneously (Model AB), AHI remained independently associated with increases in N3 sleep (β = 0.133, p < 0.001), reductions in N1 sleep (β = -0.275, p < 0.001), and reductions in arousal index (β = -0.291, p < 0.001). In contrast, the corresponding HB effects were substantially attenuated and no longer statistically significant. For REM latency, neither AHI nor HB remained independently associated after mutual adjustment.

Overall, inclusion of HB provided little additional explanatory value beyond AHI for most PSG-derived outcomes. The notable exception was the arousal index, for which both AHI (β = -0.291, p < 0.001) and HB (β = -0.038, p = 0.019) retained independent associations in the combined model. Accordingly, the combined model achieved the highest explained variance (R² = 0.239) and the lowest AIC (3476) for this outcome.

Figure 3 displays the adjusted regression coefficients and 95% confidence intervals from the fully adjusted models. AHI showed consistent associations with CPAP-related changes in sleep architecture, particularly for N3 sleep, N1 sleep, and arousal index. In contrast, HB coefficients were generally small and centered near zero, with confidence intervals overlapping the null effect for most outcomes. The only independent HB association was observed for the arousal index.

4 Discussion

In this study, we assessed the incremental value of HB for OSA treatment. Therefore, we compared the prognostic value of HB with AHI for both PROs and PSG-derived parameters. As the main finding, HB consistently provided clinically relevant information beyond AHI for symptomatic outcomes, whereas AHI remained more strongly related to changes in sleep architecture as a response to CPAP therapy. These results suggest that AHI and HB reflect complementary pathophysiological dimensions of OSA.

4.1 Hypoxic Burden and Symptomatic Treatment Response

Across multiple PROs, including daytime sleepiness, fatigue, sleep quality, and depressive symptoms, HB emerged as the more consistent independent predictor of follow-up symptom burden than AHI. Models including HB generally demonstrated better fit than models including AHI alone, and associations between AHI and symptomatic outcomes disappeared after adjustment for HB. This pattern indicates that the apparent relationship between AHI and symptom improvement is largely driven by the hypoxic component of respiratory events rather than event frequency itself.

Baseline questionnaire scores were the strongest predictors of follow-up values, resulting in overall high explained variance (R² values 0.565-0.843). Adding HB to the models consistently improved model fit, as reflected by higher R² and lower AIC values compared with models including AHI alone.

From a clinical perspective, these findings support the concept that OSA severity and expected treatment benefits should not be assessed solely by the number of respiratory events. While AHI quantifies event frequency, it does not adequately capture the physiological burden imposed by repeated oxygen desaturations. HB may therefore represent the clinically more relevant dimension of subjective disease severity, particularly with regard to daytime functioning and treatment-related symptom improvement.

This was particularly evident for fatigue and sleep quality, where higher baseline HB was associated with lower residual symptom burden at follow-up. These findings suggest that patients with greater nocturnal hypoxic stress showed lower residual symptom burden following CPAP therapy after adjustment for baseline symptom severity. This suggests that HB may identify patients more likely to experience favorable symptomatic response to CPAP.

Our findings support the clinical relevance of HB as a marker of disease severity and treatment response, as HB is a stronger predictor of symptomatic outcomes, such as daytime sleepiness and fatigue. This aligns with prior evidence from pediatric populations, where HB was shown to correlate more closely with symptom burden than the AHI [23].

4.2 PSG-Derived Outcomes: Complementary Information from AHI

HB was associated with sleep architecture changes in single-predictor models. However, after adjustment for AHI, most associations disappeared, indicating that these effects were largely shared with respiratory event frequency. AHI remained the dominant predictor of sleep architecture response, particularly increases in N3 sleep, reductions in N1 sleep, shorter REM latency, and lower arousal burden. In contrast, HB provided additional information only for changes in arousal burden.

The consistent pattern of best R² for Model AB, but best AIC for Model A reflects, that the improvements in sleep architecture are best explained (R² ) by the combination of AHI and HB, but simplest explained (AIC) by just AHI.

These findings suggest that respiratory event frequency primarily reflects the mechanical burden of repetitive airway obstruction and associated sleep fragmentation. Patients with higher AHI may therefore show greater immediate normalization of sleep continuity once obstructive events are eliminated during CPAP. By comparison, HB is less relevant to predict these acute changes in sleep structure.

Additionally, regression models were carried out for sleep microstructure parameters such as the spindle index and delta power. However, no statistically significant results could be obtained for either AHI or HB, and the model performance was very poor for these outcomes. Therefore, they were not reported in detail in this article.

Taken together, our results indicate that AHI and HB should not be viewed as competing metrics, but rather as complementary measures. By that interpretation, AHI characterizes fragmentation-related sleep disruption, whereas HB represents the physiological consequences of intermittent hypoxemia and their relationship to subjective clinical symptoms.

4.3 Clinical Implications

Current clinical classification of OSA relies predominantly on AHI thresholds. However, patients with similar AHI values often differ substantially in symptom burden, cardiovascular risk, and treatment response [11], [13]. Our findings add to the growing evidence that metrics incorporating hypoxic stress may improve phenotyping and risk stratification.

HB showed independent incremental value in multivariable models and may help identify patients more likely to benefit symptomatically from CPAP therapy. This could be particularly relevant for individuals with inconsistent clinical presentations, such as a high symptom burden despite a moderate AHI, or limited symptoms despite a severe AHI.

Accordingly, future approaches to OSA assessment may benefit from integrating both event frequency and hypoxic burden rather than relying on AHI alone.

4.4 Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. Firstly, the number of available follow-up questionnaires was comparatively small, resulting in a small sample size for PRO analyses (n = 52), which limits statistical power and generalizability. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory and require replication in larger and more diverse cohorts. In contrast, analyses of PSG-derived outcomes were conducted in a substantially larger cohort (n = 412).

One limitation relates to the scoring of hypopneas. According to the AASM criteria, hypopneas may be defined either by a ≥ 3 % oxygen desaturation or by an associated arousal. In the present study, hypopneas were scored based on the oxygen desaturation criterion only, whereas arousal-related hypopneas without accompanying desaturation were not included. This may have led to an underestimation of respiratory events compared with full AASM scoring. However, identical apnea and hypopnea events were used for the calculation of both AHI and HB, ensuring internal methodological consistency across metrics.

Regarding the HB calculation, no universally accepted standard algorithm currently exists. Different computational approaches have been proposed, limiting comparability across studies and currently restricting routine clinical implementation [10], [12]. Furthermore, clinically meaningful threshold values for HB have not yet been clearly defined, which currently constrains direct translation into treatment algorithms or severity classification.

Existing HB algorithms may incompletely capture certain respiratory event characteristics, particularly prolonged events or complex desaturation patterns. Although the algorithm is designed to capture the depth and duration of desaturations, it calculates a patient-specific search window that averages the length of the desaturations and defines a uniform duration for each patient. Consequently, longer events are not fully captured.

In addition, HB depends on accurate scoring of respiratory events and therefore remains influenced by manual event annotation. In contrast, alternative approaches such as time under 90 % oxygen saturation (T90) or accumulated desaturation area metrics quantify nocturnal hypoxemia directly from the oximetry signal. An ideal metric would capture hypoxic stress independently of manually annotated respiratory events.

Finally, HB is highly correlated with AHI, which may complicate simultaneous modeling and limit interpretability in multivariable analyses due to collinearity.

5 Conclusion

HB improved prediction of several symptomatic outcomes as a response to CPAP treatment, particularly fatigue and sleep quality, whereas AHI remained the dominant predictor of short-term sleep architecture changes. These findings support the interpretation that respiratory event frequency and hypoxic burden capture distinct but complementary aspects of OSA severity. Incorporating HB alongside AHI may improve clinical phenotyping and better explain heterogeneity in treatment response.
