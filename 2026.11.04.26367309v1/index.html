How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Abstract

The development of biologically targeted therapeutics (BTTs) that slow Parkinson's disease progression has been hampered by a reliance on testing interventions in clinically defined populations with already advanced pathobiology. Publications on the biological definition and classification of neuronal -synuclein disease (NSD), and the development of the NSD integrated staging system (NSD-ISS), have generated a framework for identifying individuals with NSD prior to the emergence of a clinically defined syndrome. Path to Prevention (P2P) is a phase 2A, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled platform trial designed to assess the safety and efficacy of interventions in an early-stage NSD population. The trial is embedded within the observational Parkinson's Precision Medicine Initiative (PPMI), a structure that enables recruitment of participants from the PPMI cohort, leverages existing operational infrastructure, and utilizes data from non-P2P enrolled PPMI participants in the Bayesian statistical modeling. P2P will enroll participants at least 60 years of age with NSD Stage 2B (abnormal -synuclein, dopaminergic dysfunction, and prodromal signs or symptoms without functional impairment) without a NOTE: This preprint reports new research that has not been certified by peer review and should not be used to guide clinical practice. clinically defined diagnosis of PD or a related disorder. As a platform trial, P2P will enable the testing of multiple interventions under a Master Protocol at approximately 35 sites across North America and Europe. Interventions are selected from industry-submitted applications based on biological relevance of the targets, and on the adequacy of the safety data appropriate to the target population. Each treatment regimen will have 125 participants on active treatment and at least 125 shared placebo control participants. Participants will remain on treatment for a maximum of 144 weeks or until 96 weeks since the randomization of the last participant. P2P has two primary endpoints, 1) rate of change in mean putamen specific binding ratio from baseline through follow-up and, 2) rate of change in the MDS-UPDRS Part III motor examination from baseline through follow-up or initiation of dopaminergic treatment. A range of secondary and exploratory biomarker-focused endpoints will be assessed. The protocol was developed with input from multiple stakeholders, including people with early-stage NSD, biopharmaceutical industry leaders, and regulatory authorities.

P2P is the first trial to test biologically targeted assets in biologically defined prodromal participants utilizing an efficient platform design, which will provide the field with tools to expand therapeutic development, and enable intervention at earlier stages of neurodegeneration. P2P is scheduled to begin enrollment in December 2026 and is committed to data sharing and broad dissemination of learnings from the study.

Introduction [5999/6000]

The single largest unmet need in Parkinson's disease and related disorders is for biologically targeted therapeutics (BTTs) that slow and ultimately prevent disease progression. In the absence of a biological definition for Parkinson's disease, potential BTTs have historically been tested in clinically defined populations. However, by the time a clinical diagnosis of Parkinson's disease can be established, the underlying pathobiology is already fairly advanced, [1] [2] [3] and may be too advanced for successful disease modification. Intervening before the onset of a clinically defined syndrome may be a more successful strategy for slowing disease progression and preventing disability. While Parkinson's disease has characteristic prodromal features (including hyposmia, REM sleep behavior disorder, dysautonomia, and mood impairment) that enable the identification of individuals before a clinical diagnosis, the lack of understanding of the natural history of the earliest (prediagnosis) stages of disease and the absence of a clear regulatory-acceptable definition for prodromal Parkinson's disease has prohibited the conduct of clinical trials of BTTs in this population.

However, with recent advancements, we are now prepared to initiate such trials. The development of reliable biomarkers for α-synuclein pathology has led to the emergence of biological disease frameworks, including the neuronal α-synuclein disease integrated staging system (NSD-ISS) and SynNeurGe, that define disease based on the presence of α-synuclein pathology. 4, 5 These frameworks have enabled the identification of individuals much earlier in the disease course, prior to the emergence of the clinically defined syndromes of Parkinson's disease and dementia with Lewy bodies, and empowered therapeutic development. The Michael J. Fox Foundation for Parkinson's Research (MJFF)-sponsored Parkinson's Precision Medicine Initiative (PPMI), which has been investigating clinical, imaging, biological, and digital progression in Parkinson's disease since 2010, 6 has helped shape understanding of prodromal Parkinson's disease and pioneered scalable methods for identifying biologically at-risk individuals for research participation. 7 Lastly, the landscape of BTTs currently in development is rich and includes, among others, α-synuclein- 8, 9 , inflammation- 10, 11 , and gene-targeted approaches. [12] [13] [14] [15] These developments have directly enabled the Path to Prevention (P2P) trial, which will test the safety and efficacy of multiple BTTs for the treatment of biologically defined prodromal Parkinson's disease. P2P will be embedded within PPMI, thereby enabling the use of existing study infrastructure and recruitment of participants directly from PPMI. P2P will use an innovative platform design that will enable the testing of multiple BTTs under a master protocol with efficient use of shared placebo controls and unifying study infrastructure. As the first interventional clinical trial in the field to examine the potential for BTTs to slow progression in a biologically defined prodromal Parkinson's disease population, P2P is also a learning study intended to inform the broader field and advance the development of BTTs in this population. Key anticipated learnings relate to the paradigm for participant selection and recruitment, measurement of disease progression in the context of an early interventional study, endpoint performance in a population pre-clinical diagnosis, and advancement of regulatory science. Here, we describe the design of P2P and discuss key trial design considerations. The companion manuscript by Wendelberger et al describes the modeling that informed the design of the trial and statistical considerations.

Study Design

P2P is a multi-center, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled platform trial designed to evaluate the safety and efficacy of multiple investigational products for the treatment of early-stage NSD (Figure 1). While there are other platform trials in Parkinson's disease, [16] [17] [18] all are targeting clinically defined Parkinson's disease, setting P2P apart as the only platform trial focused on early intervention and secondary prevention of clinically defined disease in a biologically-defined prodromal population. Similarly, while there are other examples of ongoing or soon-to-be initiated prevention trials testing investigational drugs (Table 1), all are targeting populations with REM sleep behavior disorder, and this will be the first study to enroll based on NSD status. [19] [20] [21] [22] P2P will take advantage of the robust existing PPMI infrastructure to test multiple investigational products and targets of interest in participants with early-stage NSD at approximately 35 sites across North America and Europe. A Master Protocol provides the overall framework for the trial and enables the testing of multiple investigational products both simultaneously and sequentially. A regimen consists of an investigational product and matched placebo control and each regimen is described in a Regimen Specific Sub-Protocol (RSSP) that dictates procedures, assessments, and testing unique to that investigational product. In a two-step randomization process, participants are randomized first in an equal manner to one of K actively enrolling regimens and then in a K:1 manner to active treatment or placebo control within the regimen. The first step (regimen assignment) is unblinded and the second step (treatment assignment) is blinded. Each regimen will have approximately 125 participants on active treatment and approximately 125 shared placebo control participants; the number of shared placebo controls will depend on the number of prior and actively enrolling regimens. Participants will remain on treatment for a maximum of 144 weeks or until 96 weeks since the randomization of the last participant. The Master Protocol is provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Advantages of a Platform Trial

Platform trials like P2P have many operational efficiencies. With traditional clinical trial designs, the number of treatment arms and the trial duration are prespecified and usually limited. Moreover, trial infrastructure must be assembled prior to study initiation and then disassembled at the conclusion. In contrast, platform trials can test multiple investigational products over time (either simultaneously or sequentially) under the same master protocol using the same trial infrastructure and have no defined end date, enabling investigational products to be added as they become available. The use of the same sites, investigators, coordinators, and procedures provides both cost and time advantages that may support faster and more efficient decision-making regarding whether or not to move therapeutic research forward. 18 Platform trials also enable uniform clinical, biological, and neuroimaging data acquisition across the testing of multiple investigational products, which minimizes the need to train staff on new procedures each time a new regimen is added. Beyond operational efficiencies, platform trials have certain statistical efficiencies, as they enable sharing of placebo controls across different treatment arms and enable learnings about endpoint behavior to be incorporated into future iterations of the Master Protocol, which is important given the novel study population.

Placebo Considerations in a Platform Trial

Placebo effects are a well-recognized challenge in Parkinson's disease clinical trials 23 and require special consideration in platform trials, where multiple agents are tested against a shared placebo control group. P2P will consider testing agents with oral, intramuscular, and intravenous routes of administration, but not more invasive routes of administration (e.g., intrathecal, neurosurgical) given safety and feasibility considerations. In P2P, each regimen will include a placebo control that is matched to the investigational product associated with that RSSP based on formulation, route of administration, and dosing regimen.

P2P will have multiple types of controls. For analysis of a given treatment regimen, placebo data across regimens will be shared to produce a single placebo control dataset for analyses.

The sharing of placebo between regimens has clear statistical advantages and enables modeling of differential responses to placebo (e.g., intravenous versus oral). As a perpetual platform trial, there may be substantial time gaps between enrollment in the first arm and enrollment in the last arm. The use of Bayesian modeling with hierarchical borrowing allows for heterogeneity across individual baseline values and individual slopes, as well as heterogeneity across regimens due to minor differences in the inclusion/exclusion criteria or mode of administration and will partially mitigate the impact of temporal and placebo differences. See Wendelberger et al for more on the use of dynamic borrowing.

Leveraging PPMI Infrastructure for Operational Success

PPMI has a robust infrastructure, which is critical to the success of P2P. Importantly, PPMI will enable the enrollment of an engaged and biologically characterized population. 7 Additionally, P2P will use existing PPMI Cores, including the Data Management, Statistics, Site Management, Participant Engagement, Recruitment, Imaging, and Biorepository Cores, to recruit participants, support retention, and collect and analyze trial data. 7 Lastly, after participants complete their participation in P2P, they will be encouraged to continue followup within PPMI, providing valuable long-term follow-up data. Leveraging the PPMI infrastructure for P2P is an efficient use of resources, ensures the availability of essential expertise in the standardization of biomarker collection and processing, and supports harmonization with PPMI.

Study Governance

The MJFF-sponsored P2P trial is governed by a steering committee comprised of leading Parkinson's disease researchers with representation of individuals with lived experience. In addition to a lead Principal Investigator (PI; TS), each RSSP has a PI. The sponsor and its operational teams are responsible for trial conduct, obtaining regulatory approval, and contracting while the industry partner provides investigational product and matching placebo as well as investigational product-related documentation, and is closely involved in the development of the RSSP. Each RSSP has a working group that includes representatives from industry partners who work in close collaboration with the P2P team to ensure appropriate study design, provide operational input, and facilitate on-going exchange of information.

Study Population

Eligible participants are those who are enrolled in PPMI, meet criteria for NSD Stage 2B, are at least 60 years of age, do not have a clinical diagnosis of Parkinson's disease, dementia with Lewy bodies or another neurodegenerative disease, and are not on dopaminergic therapy or a cholinesterase inhibitor. NSD Stage 2B participants are identified based on the presence of 1) neuronal α-synuclein as determined in this study by a positive cerebrospinal fluid α-synuclein seed amplification assay (SAA) collected and analyzed under standardized conditions (Amprion, San Diego, CA, USA), 24 2) dopamine dysfunction as determined by dopamine transporter single-photon emission computed tomography (DAT SPECT) lowest age-and sex-adjusted putamen specific binding ratio (SBR) < 75%, 25 and 3) clinical symptoms or signs without functional impairment as determined by motor, cognitive, and other non-motor clinical anchors (Figure 2). 26

Rationale for NSD-based Definition of the Study Population

Enrollment based on biological disease definition and staging has many advantages in prodromal cohorts. One, it ensures a homogeneous study population, defined by the presence of neuronal α-synuclein and dopamine dysfunction. In comparison, clinically defined prodromal cohorts are quite heterogeneous. While α-synuclein SAA assays have a high rate of positivity in RBD, up to 15% of individuals have a negative assay, [27] [28] [29] [30] and the positivity rate in older adults with hyposmia is approximately 50%. 31 The necessity of biologically characterizing prodromal cohorts was further highlighted in a recent analysis of PPMI data, which demonstrated that 28% of RBD participants and 13% of hyposmic participants who were given diagnoses of clinically defined syndromes did not have the expected biological profile of synuclein pathology and dopamine dysfunction. 32 Two, including a requirement for dopamine dysfunction in addition to α-synuclein enriches for individuals more likely to develop clinically defined syndromes. In the Parkinson Associated Risk Syndrome (PARS) study, 48% of older adults with median age of 65 years had a positive α-synuclein SAA assay, yet the majority did not have DAT deficit at baseline and did not develop DAT deficit over a median of 4 years of follow-up. 31 However, the majority of participants with a positive α-synuclein SAA assay and DAT deficit at baseline developed parkinsonism during followup. Three, enrollment based on NSD Stage 2B status provides a clear and reproducible definition to replace the prior poorly defined term of prodromal disease. Lastly and essentially important, NSD Stage 2B demonstrates progression within a typical clinical trial timeframe; 21% of stage 2B PPMI participants without a clinical diagnosis progressed to functional impairment at year 1, 34% at year 2, and 43% at year 3. 33 While earlier stage NSD populations (Stages 1-2A, individuals with a positive α-synuclein biomarker but no evidence of dopaminergic deficit and/or nor clinical symptoms or signs) may be reasonable for inclusion in future trials, at the present time, sufficient longitudinal data does not exist to inform understanding of progression and enrichment in these earlier stage populations. 34 Lastly, later stages (Stages 3-6) are defined by the presence of functional impairment and the majority of participants in these stages are expected to have a clinically defined diagnosis. 4 However, even among NSD Stage 2B there is heterogeneity of the clinical phenotype (see below). These phenotypic subgroups may display differential rates of progression and develop different clinical syndromes (e.g., Parkinson's disease, dementia with Lewy bodies). 32 35 Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 36 and American Academy of Neurology 37 have all launched initiatives that promote brain health and the prevention of neurologic disease, and prevention-focused clinical models are emerging. 38 Coupled with the increasing availability and accessibility of biomarkers, we anticipate that new recruitment paradigms will develop. However, at the present time, recruitment of biologically characterized prodromal populations into an interventional clinical trial would be impossible in the absence of a feeder cohort. P2P participants will be recruited directly through PPMI, which has pioneered a staged recruitment paradigm for the identification of individuals with α-synuclein pathology. 39 This staged recruitment paradigm begins with broad outreach to the community soliciting interest to remotely undergo smell testing and is followed by in-clinic screening of identified hyposmic individuals. Data have confirmed the utility of this approach for enriching for the presence of α-synuclein pathology and dopamine dysfunction. 40 PPMI has remotely screened more than 133,000 individuals without PD, including over 25,000 with self-reported RBD and/or dream enactment behavior, since 2022. The clinical cohort includes more than 2800 classically defined prodromal participants with hyposmia, with RBD, and genetically at-risk for PD. As of July 2026, there are 2593 active prodromal participants in PPMI, of whom 1748 have NSD. Of these, 476 are NSD stage 2B; this includes participants with polysomnography-confirmed REM sleep behavior disorder (14%, n = 65), hyposmia (40%, n = 191), and hyposmia plus dream enactment behavior (43%, n = 207). Furthermore, 437 are at least 60 years of age, do not have a clinical diagnosis of Parkinson's disease, dementia with Lewy bodies, or another neurodegenerative disease, and are not taking a dopaminergic medication. Outreach and screening will continue to meet P2P enrollment needs.

While we cannot accurately predict the proportion of these potentially eligible participants who will enroll in P2P, PPMI has set the foundation providing encouraging indicators. PPMI has pioneered the Return of Research Information (RORI) initiative, which enables interested PPMI participants to access their individual research results, including α-synuclein SAA status, DAT SPECT results, Movement Disorder Society-Sponsored Revision of the Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS) Part III scores, and smell test results. 41 As of July 2026, 1983 PPMI prodromal participants have enrolled in RORI and 1771 have viewed their α-synuclein SAA results, indicating that interest in knowing one's biological status is high. Additionally, qualitative interviews were conducted with prodromal PPMI participants regarding interventional study participation and these demonstrated strong interest. 7 Complementing this work, a broad scale survey designed to assess perspectives on early intervention trials was administered to PPMI prodromal participants and will be published separately. P2P is thus well-positioned to successfully screen and enroll participants based on NSD status and to test biologically targeted interventions in this population.

Study Endpoints

P2P is the first interventional trial to enroll participants based on NSD status. As a result, no regulatory precedent exists to inform the selection of endpoints. Outcome measures developed to assess clinical benefit in Parkinson's disease may or may not be appropriate for use in an at-risk population. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) defines meaningful clinical benefit as the effect an intervention has on how someone feels, functions, or survives. 42 However, individuals with early-stage NSD may have minimal symptoms, and by definition, do not have functional impairment. This creates difficulties in the selection of an appropriate clinical endpoint. Thus, one of the goals of P2P is to generate the evidence needed to establish endpoints for this population.

Primary Endpoints

We have done extensive modeling based on PPMI longitudinal data and arrived at the concept of using multiple primary endpoints which means that if a regimen is positive on one, it is considered a positive result. P2P will test whether treatment slows the rate of progression in a biomarker endpoint, DAT SPECT mean putamen SBR from baseline through follow-up, and a clinical endpoint, MDS-UPDRS Part III 43 score from baseline through follow-up or initiation of dopaminergic treatment. The use of multiple primary endpoints allows for the simultaneous evaluation of biomarker and clinical progression, avoids the forced selection of a single primary endpoint when it is unknown which aspect of NSD will be more likely to respond to intervention, and provides more opportunities to demonstrate success, with a favorable outcome on either endpoint sufficient to establish efficacy. 44 See Wendelberger et al for detailed rationale for endpoint selection and the underlying statistical modeling. In the eventual analysis of the data, we might identify other endpoints that show more sensitivity to change, and this is one of the learning aspects of the study. Endpoints assessing cognitive and neuropsychiatric progression are of essential importance and are included as exploratory endpoints, however, more data are necessary to inform sensitivity to change. We anticipate that P2P will inform the design and selection of future endpoints, both within the platform and in future trials in this population.

Safety and Secondary Endpoints

Safety (as measured by treatment emergent adverse events (AEs) and serious adverse events (SAEs) for the active treatment versus placebo control within each RSSP), and secondary endpoints for feasibility and tolerability will also be assessed, as detailed in Table 2.

Key Exploratory Endpoints

A number of key exploratory endpoints will be assessed (Table 2). One key exploratory endpoint is the number who progress to NSD Stage 3 or higher. As noted above, approximately 20% of analyzed stage 2B PPMI participants progressed to functional impairment by year 1, 34 suggesting that this will be an informative endpoint. However, progression to Stage 3 (and subsequent stages) is based on meeting clinical anchors (see Supplementary Materials), and valid concerns have been raised about the use of clinical anchors that are sensitive to dopaminergic medication. 45 In a longitudinal analysis of PPMI data, 41% of participants who were Stage 4 at the last visit before starting dopaminergic medication reverted to an earlier stage at the first visit afterward. 34 Notably, the same effect was not seen in Stage 2B participants and the majority progressed in staging following dopaminergic medication initiation, suggesting that initiation of dopaminergic medication is not a significant confounder in early-stage NSD. Concerns have also been raised regarding the lack of adequate representation of non-motor symptoms amongst the anchors and the insensitivity of the cognitive clinical anchors. 46 Underscoring this, in a longitudinal analysis of PPMI data, 91% of those who progressed to Stage 3 did so only on motor anchors. 34 Additional work is underway exploring novel approaches for clinical staging, which could change the NSD staging paradigm in the future.

In P2P, other key exploratory endpoints will bolster assessment of cognitive and other nonmotor change. These endpoints include the rate of progression in MDS-UPDRS total score from baseline through follow-up or the initiation of dopaminergic treatment, rate of progression in MDS-UPDRS Part I and Part II subscores from baseline through follow-up, change in cognition as defined by the number of participants who develop new syndromes of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or dementia, change in functional status as measured by the change in the Penn Parkinson's Daily Activities Questionnaire-27 (PDAQ-27) from baseline through follow-up, and the time to progression milestones. 47

Exploratory Endpoints

A number of additional exploratory endpoints will also be assessed. As an example, time to phenoconversion to clinically manifest Parkinson's disease or dementia with Lewy bodies will be included as an exploratory endpoint. While this endpoint has face validity as a clinical endpoint, it is inherently subjective. Moreover, annual phenoconversion rates are low; in an analysis of PPMI participants with RBD who were NSD Stage 2B or higher, only 33% phenoconverted over four years of follow up. 30 Similarly, in an analysis of NSD Stage 2B PPMI prodromal participants, an estimated 4% phenoconverted at year one, 10% at year two, and 18% at year 3. 33 Use of time to phenoconversion as a primary endpoint would therefore require prolonged trial follow-up.

An essential part of P2P is extensive biomarker characterization of the participants. Whole blood, urine, CSF, and skin biopsies will be collected for biomarker analysis using PPMI protocols, which will bolster biological understanding of the study population. For example, integration of biomarkers of neurodegeneration (e.g., amyloid, tau) will inform understanding of the influence of multi-pathology on treatment response. Similarly, biomarkers of different mechanistic pathways (e.g., neuroinflammation) will inform understanding of the influence of different etiological mechanisms on treatment response. The complete list of endpoints, including other exploratory endpoints, are included in the Supplementary Materials.

Novel Endpoints for Biologically Targeted Therapeutics Trials in Early-

Stage NSD

In trials for the earliest stages of Alzheimer's disease, the FDA has demonstrated willingness, to consider changes on neuropsychological testing based on the size, breadth, and statistical robustness of the effect, as sufficient to establish meaningful clinical benefit when supported by biomarker evidence of effect. 48 The European Medicines Agency is developing new guidelines to inform the development of treatments for Alzheimer's disease that will consider appropriate endpoint selection in the early stages of Alzheimer's disease and trial design in biologically defined pre-clinical Alzheimer's disease. 43 However, it is unclear how best to translate this to early-stage NSD, where individuals may meet criteria for Stage 2B based on motor symptoms, cognitive symptoms, or other non-motor symptoms (e.g., RBD, hyposmia) and exhibit different trajectories to different clinically defined syndromes. The MDS-UPDRS Part III is the best measure of motor performance currently available and has clinical meaningfulness. 49, 50 However, it has a floor effect 51 and was not designed for use in a population without clinically defined Parkinson's disease, raising questions regarding whether change in MDS-UPDRS Part III is an optimal primary endpoint.

Notably, P2P will provide additional evidence that may support the use of digital measures as clinical trial endpoints specifically suitable for this early NSD population and inform the development of novel outcome measures. Digital measures derived from digital health technologies may provide regulatory-acceptable 48 sensitive measures of motor progression that can help to establish meaningful clinical benefit. P2P participants will wear a lumbar sensor (Axivity AX6) and a wrist sensor (Ametris ActiGraph LEAP) for 7 days every six months. This will enable passive, free-living data collection and capture of mobility and sleep measures using technically and analytically validated algorithms. Ongoing initiatives are collecting data to establish and validate digital measures for this context of use (disease progression monitoring) in early-stage NSD. Another opportunity lies in the development of novel fit-for-purpose patient-reported outcomes and clinician-reported outcomes, such as the Prodromal Synucleinopathy Rating Scale, 52 which was modeled after the Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) and has undergone preliminary cross-sectional validation in a large RBD cohort. 53

Regulatory Acceptance

There is no established regulatory pathway for studies testing therapeutics in prodromal synucleinopathies in part due to the lack of an operational definition for prodromal disease.

Biologically defining prodromal disease based on NSD status fills this critical gap. In early stages of preparation for the study launch, the team engaged with the US and European regulatory authorities to initiate the dialogue on the acceptance of the definition of the population, platform study design, selection of the endpoints and other aspects of the study design. Both regulatory authorities provided valuable input in the context of Type C meetings in US and Trial Innovation Consultation with EMA, followed by country-specific Scientific Advise in the Netherlands and Germany. We received constructive feedback that was incorporated into the protocol. The regulatory authorities specifically highlighted the importance of biologically defining and staging the target population using the NSD-ISS framework in these early-stage development studies. The FDA Investigational New Drug (IND) application was filed using the NSD definition of the population and was granted "you may proceed status." An EMA package will be submitted.

Statistical Considerations Primary Analyses

The Bayesian primary analysis models estimate a difference in the rate of change of each primary endpoint from baseline through last visit (or, for the MDS-UPDRS Part III, through initiation of dopaminergic treatment). Each primary endpoint will be tested with equal priority with respect to active treatment superiority versus shared placebo controls. For both primary endpoints, a Bayesian repeated measures model of outcome will be used to compare the slope of active treatment versus a shared group of placebo and non-randomized controls.

The model allows for heterogeneity across individual baseline values and individual slopes, as well as heterogeneity across regimens due to minor differences in the eligibility criteria or mode of administration. For each endpoint, if the Bayesian posterior probability of active treatment superiority (H a ) is greater than or equal to a prespecified threshold of 0.90, the null hypothesis (H 0 ) will be rejected, and the active treatment will be deemed superior to placebo control (i.e., less progression on active treatment) with respect to mean endpoint. This decision threshold of 0.90 has been calibrated via simulation to control the regimen-specific one-sided type I error at approximately 10% per endpoint per regimen (or 20% joint when considering both primary endpoints). It is hypothesized that an effective treatment will (1) decrease the progression rate and slow the decline in mean putamen SBR and/or, (2) decrease the progression rate and slow the increase in clinical symptoms as measured by the MDS-UPDRS Part III score.

The primary analyses for each analysis regimen will be based on the intent-to-treat (ITT) principle and include all participants randomized to the active treatment arm of the analysis regimen and all shared controls, regardless of the adherence to the assigned RSSP or duration of follow-up. Sensitivity analyses will be conducted using an ITT population that consists of participants randomized to the active treatment arm of the analysis regimen and (1) all shared P2P placebo controls (RSSP placebo, concurrent placebo, and non-concurrent placebo controls), (2) P2P concurrent controls (RSSP placebo, concurrent placebo), and (3) RSSP placebo controls. Lastly, we will also replicate the primary analyses using a per-protocol population. No interim efficacy or futility analyses are planned.

Safety Analyses

Analyses to examine safety of each active treatment versus RSSP placebo controls will involve comparisons of the percentage of participants within each RSSP reporting treatment emergent AEs, treatment emergent SAEs, and unanticipated SAEs within each MedDRA (Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities) system organ class and preferred terms.

Secondary Analyses

To examine tolerability, within each RSSP we will compare the percentage of participants on treatment vs regimen-specific placebo control group who complete the full RSSP on their initially assigned treatment regimen, regardless of reason for non-completion. Key feasibility metrics include enrollment timelines, retention, and visit compliance. Secondary analyses will include an ITT population consisting of all participants randomized to the active treatment arm or shared placebo control arm within an RSSP. Handling of key exploratory endpoints are as detailed in the Supplementary Material.

Missing Data and Sensitivity Analyses

To assess the potential dependence of the results of the primary analysis on missing values, we will conduct a series of sensitivity analyses including comparison of RSSP treatment group to randomized placebo controls (excluding concurrent non-randomized controls), supplemented intention to treat analysis using PPMI data, and various multiple imputation methods. For a regimen, "power" is defined as the probability of demonstrating success on at least one of the primary endpoints. Regimens that enter the platform trial later are expected to see increased power relative to the first regimen due to the accumulation of shared, randomized placebo controls. In addition, the number of non-randomized controls from PPMI that will be available for each regimen analysis are unknown, and a range from 0 to 100 non-randomized control participants were considered in clinical trial simulation. Simulation results estimate power for the first three regimens between 73%-84% to detect treatment effects when both endpoints achieve 40% slowing of progression. Similarly, simulation results demonstrate 67%-77% power to declare success if there is truly a 40% or greater reduction in slope/progression of MDS-UPDRS Part III if there is at least a simultaneous 30% or greater reduction in mean putamen SBR. Finally, we have around 66%-79% probability of declaring success if there is truly a 40% or greater reduction in slope/progression on the DAT SPECT imaging endpoint and at least a 30% reduction on slope/progression on the MDS-UPDRS Part III endpoint. The simulations also demonstrated that the overall type I error probability (probability of meeting the criteria for success when there is no true reduction on either endpoint) was well controlled at 18%. Further details of Bayesian modeling for this study can be found in the companion paper.

Sample Size Considerations

Selection of Investigational Products

Potential industry and academic partners are invited to submit letters of interest for testing an investigational product within P2P. As of July 2026, 28 letters of interest have been received.

A recent review of phase 1-3 Parkinson's disease drug trials registered on clinicaltrials.gov from 2015-2024, identified 165 trials of potential disease modifying agents 15, 54 ; the 28 agents submitted thus represents ~17% of all BTTs currently in development. Thus far, interested industry partners have predominantly been those interested in early-stage NSD as an opportunity for label expansion for assets moving forward in Parkinson's disease and those with assets with an initial indication for dementia with Lewy bodies or multiple system atrophy. A Therapeutic Evaluation Committee, comprised of international experts in pharmacology, biomarkers, and clinical trials design, is charged with the review and prioritization of potential investigational products for testing. The selection of interventions for P2P is based on biological relevance to an early-stage NSD population, safety data, appropriateness for testing in a platform trial infrastructure, and partnership readiness (e.g., availability of drug supply and willingness to comply with the P2P governance). 15 As earlystage NSD is defined by the presence of neuronal α-synuclein pathology, α-synuclein provides a compelling target; however, other mechanistic pathways (e.g., inflammation) are also relevant at this stage of disease. Testing BTTs in a population without functional impairment that may never develop clinically defined disease, requires careful consideration of safety. Accordingly, only investigational products that have been tested in humans and have an established, acceptable safety profile will move forward. The Therapeutics Evaluation Committee also takes operational aspects, such as frequency and route of administration, into consideration. The final decision regarding the selection of investigational products for testing is made by the P2P Steering Committee. The first phase of P2P aims to test up to three compounds, the first of which is the oral, brain-penetrant c-Abl inhibitor risvodetinib. 55 Other partnerships will be announced once the selection and contractual processes are completed.
