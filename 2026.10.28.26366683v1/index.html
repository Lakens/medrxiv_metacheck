Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Electronic health record (EHR)-based risk prediction offers opportunities for early intervention in mental health, but models may remain in use as patient populations, care practices, and documentation change. Such temporal shifts can reduce predictive performance, while updating models from recent data alone may be unstable when adverse outcomes are uncommon. We developed Adversarial Drift-Aware Predictive Transfer (ADAPT), a distributionally robust approach to improve the temporal durability of mental health risk prediction. ADAPT uses variation in models estimated from previous calendar periods to characterize plausible changes in predictor-outcome relationships. Data from the current period constrain which historical patterns remain relevant, allowing the method to seek robustness against subsequent shifts while preserving contemporary discrimination. We retrospectively evaluated ADAPT using EHRs from 889,445 patients with mental health diagnoses at Mass General Brigham and Duke University Health System, covering one-year risk prediction for 28 outcomes. In the worst 20% of year-pair comparisons, ADAPT improved AUC over all comparators by 0.016-0.036 at Mass General Brigham, 0.011-0.018 at Duke, and 0.008-0.037 after transport. For suicide-attempt prediction, contemporary AUC at Mass General Brigham was 0.82 compared with 0.80 for the best comparator. Eight-year relative AUC degradation was 7.7%, compared with 12.1% for pooled-data training. These results suggest that information from prior temporal shifts can improve robustness to future changes without sacrificing predictive performance for current patients.

1 Introduction

Clinical AI is increasingly moving from research towards clinical implementation. This transition is reflected in the increasing number of machine-learning (ML) and AI-enabled clinical technolo-2 gies authorized by the US Food and Drug Administration and in the adoption of predictive AI within health systems (Shick et al., 2024). In a 2024 national survey of US hospitals, more than half reported using predictive AI integrated with their electronic health record (EHR) systems (Everson et al., 2025). As AI models become embedded within EHR systems and integrated into clinical workflows to support clinical decision making, attention must extend beyond their performance at initial development and validation to their reliability after deployment. Models may remain in use for years while the patients, care practices, technologies, and health-care environments around them continue to change. Maintaining predictive performance over time is therefore becoming an increasingly important component of safe and effective clinical AI.

Mental health provides a particularly important clinical context to study this problem. Mental disorders impose a substantial and growing burden on patients, families, health systems, and society and contribute extensively to global disability (GBD 2023 Mental Disorder Collaborators, 2026). Because treatment alone has not adequately reduced this burden, prevention and early intervention have become central priorities, including strategies that identify individuals at elevated risk before serious adverse outcomes occur (Arango et al., 2018). Risk prediction models using longitudinal EHR data offer an opportunity to support these goals by identifying patients who may benefit from earlier intervention. EHR-based prediction models have been developed for antidepressant treatment response, psychiatric readmission, mental health crises, self-harm, and suicide attempts (Sheu et al., 2023a; Ren et al., 2025; Garriga et al., 2022; Barak-Corren et al., 2017; Simon et al., 2018; Sheu et al., 2023b). Importantly, some of these models have progressed beyond retrospective development to prospective or trial-based evaluation within clinical workflows, highlighting their potential use as clinical decision-support tools (Garriga et al., 2022; Walsh et al., 2025). As such models move closer to routine clinical use, ensuring that their performance remains reliable after deployment becomes increasingly important.

Mental health may also be particularly susceptible to temporal shift because both the underlying risk and its representation in EHR data can be influenced by social, environmental, and health-system conditions (Choudhary et al., 2025). Changes in insurance coverage, access to care, treatment availability, screening practice, documentation, substance use, and patterns of healthcare utilization can alter both who is observed within a health system, how risk factors and outcomes are captured, and the distribution of the predictors themselves. Major external disruptions such as COVID-19 can affect several of these mechanisms simultaneously. During the COVID-19 pandemic, for example, primary-care-recorded self-harm changed abruptly and differently across patient subgroups, with observed reductions potentially reflecting changes in help-seeking as well as changes in underlying incidence (Steeg et al., 2021; Reger et al., 2020).

Changes in policy and EHR systems can have similar disruptions. Implementation of universal suicide screening in emergency departments nearly doubled documented detection of suicide risk (Boudreaux et al., 2016), while transitions between coding systems, including the change from ICD-9 to ICD-10, can alter the granularity and comparability of recorded diagnoses, injuries, and mental-health-related outcomes (Mainor et al., 2019; Cartwright, 2013). Mental health therefore provides a clinically consequential setting in which changes in the external environment, clinical practice, and EHR data generation can occur together.

For a deployed AI model, these changes can produce data shift, a mismatch between the data on which a model was developed and the post-deployment data, which is an important cause of the failure to perform reliably (Finlayson et al., 2021). As patient populations, treatments, screening practices, coding, documentation, access to care, and clinical workflows evolve, models trained using older data may become progressively less reliable. Such degradation can directly undermine clinical decision support: false-negative predictions may create missed opportunities for intervention, whereas false-positive alerts can increase screening and follow-up burden and divert limited clinical resources. Temporal degradation has been observed across clinical AI applications and can emerge within only a few years of model development (Vela et al., 2022; Fernández-Narro et al., 2025).

As such, models intended for longitudinal clinical use must not only demonstrate strong per-formance at initial development and validation, but also maintain their accuracy across changing clinical environments and over time. This creates an additional challenge beyond initial model development and validation: how to maintain predictive performance once a model is deployed (Feng et al., 2022). In practice, clinical prediction models have often been treated as static tools, with updating performed periodically, ad hoc, or after evidence of performance deterioration has accumulated (Jenkins et al., 2021). Maintaining performance through repeated updating can itself be difficult: reliable outcome labels may not become available promptly, and model changes require continued performance assessment, validation, and governance (Davis et al., 2024; Subasri et al., 2025).

Common model-updating strategies also involve important trade-offs. Pooling all historical observations can improve precision but may dilute recent changes, whereas fitting a model using only the most recent period can be unstable especially when the contemporary sample contains few adverse outcomes. Transfer-learning and domain-adaptation methods can borrow information from historical data, but they generally focus on performance in a known current target population rather than durability under subsequent shifts not yet observed (Gretton et al., 2009; Reddi et al., 2015; Liu et al., 2023; Li et al., 2022; Tian and Feng, 2023). Therefore, a complementary need is methods that can use variations across prior data sources to improve robustness to potential future shifts while preserving strong performance in the observed training target data.

To address this need, we developed Adversarial Drift-Aware Predictive Transfer (ADAPT), an approach designed to improve the temporal durability of EHR-based prediction models while preserving performance for contemporary patients. ADAPT uses variations across model estimates from prior calendar periods, together with limited data from the most recent patient population, within a distributionally robust optimization (DRO) framework to guide model development toward robustness against potential future shifts. ADAPT leverages how the predictoroutcome relationships have varied across earlier temporal periods while using contemporary data 5 to constrain the influence of historical patterns that may no longer be relevant. As such, ADAPT balances strong performance for patients seen today with reduced vulnerability to changes that may occur after deployment.

ADAPT was also designed to facilitate implementation across health systems. Historical calendar periods contribute through summary-level fitted-model estimates without requiring historical patient-level data to be repeatedly pooled or transferred, and only limited contemporary data are needed to anchor the model to the current population. The resulting model retains the same predictor structure as earlier models and can thus be applied to new patients using the same architecture. This reduces the data-sharing and computational requirements associated with model maintenance.

We examined whether ADAPT could support more durable mental health risk assessment.

Using longitudinal EHR data from Mass General Brigham and Duke University Health System, we retrospectively evaluated one-year risk across 28 mental health outcomes, both within and across health systems. Our evaluation spanned both the transition from ICD-9 to ICD-10 coding and the COVID-19 pandemic, testing whether models developed in an earlier year remained reliable for future patients through major changes in clinical documentation and care delivery.

ADAPT improved discrimination across a range of conditions, including psychotic disorders, bipolar disorder, and suicidal ideation, with gains extending to the periods in which predictive performance was poorest. In a focused suicide-attempt case study, ADAPT combined strong current-year discrimination with reduced deterioration when the same fitted model was applied to patients seen years later, and this advantage was retained after transfer to another health system. Together, these findings support the potential for greater model longevity, i.e., preserving predictive performance beyond the year of development while maintaining accuracy for contemporary patients, and for mental health decision-support tools that remain informative as patient populations and care practices evolve.

2 Methods

2.1 Study design and data sources

We conducted a retrospective, multi-health-system study to evaluate the temporal durability and cross-institutional transportability of EHR-based risk prediction models for a range of mental health outcomes. We evaluated ADAPT independently at both Mass General Brigham (MGB) and Duke University Health System (Duke), developing models sequentially across calendar years and assessing contemporaneous performance and temporal degradation within each health system. We additionally applied models developed at MGB directly to Duke data across calendar years to assess cross-institution transportability. We conducted two complementary analyses across both institutions: 1) a multi-outcome analysis evaluated ADAPT across 28 mental-health outcomes; and 2) a suicide-attempt case study focused on incident suicide attempts. Unless otherwise specified, both analyses used the same predictor construction, model-development, and validation procedures.

The MGB EHR cohort was obtained from its Research Patient Data Registry, a centralised EHR repository containing records for more than 7 million patients across multiple affiliated hospitals, including Massachusetts General Hospital and Brigham and Women's Hospital (Sheu et al., 2023a). The MGB cohort included records from Jan 1, 2005, to Dec 31, 2022. The Duke database contains longitudinal EHR records for more than 6 million patients with records spanning from Jan 1, 2014 to Dec 31, 2022. The same general cohort-construction, outcomedefinition, feature-generation, and model-evaluation procedures were applied at both institutions.

2.2 Study population, outcome, and predictors

Multi-outcome analysis. Patients were eligible if they had at least one recorded diagnosis of mental health-related condition, defined using PheCodes 295-318, and at least six months of follow-up. PheCodes group related ICD codes into categories to represent clinically meaningful broader clinical phenotypes such as PheCode 296.22 for major depressive disorder and 297.1 for suicide ideation (Denny et al., 2013). The landmark visit was defined as the recorded visit closest to one year after the first mental health diagnosis, and patients with less than one month of follow-up after the landmark visit were excluded. We evaluated ADAPT across 28 mental-health outcomes, selected as the subset of PheCodes 295-318 with at least 15 cases within one year of the landmark visit at both MGB and Duke. Each outcome was treated as a separate prediction task, with the objective of predicting its occurrence within one year after the landmark visit.

Outcome definitions were harmonised across MGB and Duke using corresponding PheCodes. For each outcome, we focused on patients without a prior diagnosis of that outcome. Predictors were measured during the three years preceding the landmark visit.

Suicide-attempt case study. The suicide-attempt case study used a separate landmark definition. Following Sheu et al. (2023b), the landmark date was randomly selected from the patient's recorded visits between the first mental health diagnosis and the 75th percentile of the visit dates after that diagnosis. We defined an incident suicide attempt as an event occurring within one year after the landmark visit and identified attempts using the ICD-9-CM and ICD-10-CM code lists from two previous EHR-based suicide-prediction studies (Barak-Corren et al., 2020 , 2017).

Predictors were measured from one year before the landmark visit.

2.3 ADAPT framework

2.4 Validation Studies

2.4.1 Benchmark Methods

We compared ADAPT with four benchmark methods representing common strategies for multisource model development. The current-period-only model used only the most recent data and was fitted as an ℓ 1 -regularized logistic regression only to data from year t. The cumulative pooled-data model combined all patient-level data available up to and including year t and fitted the same ℓ 1 -regularized logistic regression to the pooled data. TransGLM borrowed information from historical periods while adapting the model to the current target population by shrinking the target estimate toward similar source models (Tian and Feng, 2023). As a robustness benchmark, the conventional maximin estimator considered all possible mixtures of the observed source and current target populations and optimized against the worst-performing mixture, without restricting the mixture to remain close to the current target population (Meinshausen and Bühlmann, 2015). Because the original maximin formulation was developed for squared-error regression, it was adapted to logistic regression using a second-order local approximation to the negative log-likelihood around the current target; the resulting quadratic objective used a simplex constraint and a zero-vector initial model. Each comparator was applied separately to each mental-health outcome and within each health-system analysis.

Sensitivity analysis with non-linear feature representations As a sensitivity analysis,

we assessed whether incorporating non-linear feature representations changed the relative performance of ADAPT and the benchmark methods. We trained source-specific XGBoost models 13 and appended their prediction scores to the original EHR features. For each calendar year, data were split into a held-out evaluation set (one half), an XGBoost training set (one quarter), and a regression set (one quarter) used to fit all regression-based models. Each year-specific XGBoost model was then applied to the regression and evaluation sets, other source years, and the full target-year data to generate prediction scores. Thus, the augmented features used to fit and evaluate the regression-based models were not derived from in-sample data, thereby avoiding overfitting. We compared ADAPT and the benchmark methods using both the original and augmented feature sets. Details are provided in Supplementary Methods, Section A.3.

Temporal and cross-health-system validation. The target year t was separately set as 2010, . . . , 2022 in MGB and t P t2015, . . . , 2022u in Duke, with all years before t used as historical sources. Three sets of validation analyses were conducted. First, contemporaneous internal validation was performed separately at MGB and Duke, with models trained using part of the observed target data and validated on the remainder target data within the same calendar year.

Second, temporal validation was performed separately at both institutions by applying models developed for year t to data from subsequent calendar years within the same health system.

Third, to assess cross-institution transportability, models developed at MGB for year t were applied directly to Duke data from the calendar year closest to t and all the subsequent years.

No data from calendar years after the model-development year were used to fit the historical or current-period models. Each target-year dataset was randomly split 10 times, and performance was averaged across repetitions.

Synthetic data validation.

We additionally conducted a synthetic-data validation to evaluate ADAPT under controlled temporal-shifts. We generated 15 sequential periods in which predictor-outcome relationships evolved gradually over time, with an additional abrupt perturbation introduced at period 8. We varied the amount of current-period data relative to historical data and the magnitude of the abrupt shift, allowing us to assess ADAPT under both limited contemporary data and different degrees of temporal change. Full data-generating details are provided in Supplementary Methods, Section A.4.

2.5 Evaluation Metrics

Performance metrics for mental-health outcomes. For each mental-health outcome, prediction method, target year t, validation year v ě t, we evaluated area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), sensitivity, and lift. Lift was defined as precision divided by outcome prevalence, representing enrichment of positive cases relative to random selection. For sensitivity and lift, a method-and target-year-specific threshold was selected using the held-out target-year validation data to achieve 0.95 specificity, or the closest attainable value. The threshold was held fixed when the model developed for year t was evaluated in later calendar years v ą t. For cross-health-system validation, the threshold selected at MGB was transported with the fitted model and applied unchanged at Duke. For all temporal and cross-health-system validation, model coefficients were likewise held fixed and applied without refitting to each validation cohort.

3 Results

3.1 Study population and temporal patterns

The study included 889,445 patients with mental health diagnoses: 412,943 from MGB and 476,502 from Duke (Table 1). Females comprised 64.5% of the MGB cohort and 56.6% of the Duke cohort. The cohorts differed in racial composition: 6.0% of MGB patients and 27.3% of Duke patients were Black, whereas 79.6% and 64.0%, respectively, were White. Patients aged 26-55 years represented the largest age group at both sites. The full distribution of landmark years is provided in Supplementary Table S1. This pattern was consistent when different fractions of the lower performance distribution were considered (Figure 2, right). At α " 0.20, ADAPT had higher AUC-CVaR α than all four comparator methods. The improvement ranged from 0.016 to 0.036 within MGB and from 0.011 to 0.018 within Duke (all p ă 0.001). Within both MGB and Duke, ADAPT also had higher AUC-CVaR than all four comparators at α " 0.40, 0.60, and 0.80 (Supplementary Figures S3-S4). After MGB-to-Duke transport, ADAPT likewise exceeded each transported comparator, with gains ranging from 0.0079 to 0.037, at α " 0.2 (all p ă 0.001). As a contextual benchmark, transported ADAPT also slightly exceeded a current-period-only model trained locally at Duke by 0.0069 (p ă 0.001).

3.2 Performance across mental-health outcomes

Threshold-based results similarly suggested that ADAPT improved case detection without sacrificing enrichment among patients identified as high risk. At α " 0.20, sensitivity-CVaR for ADAPT exceeded that of the current-period-only model by 4.3, 1.6, and 2.7 percentage points in the within-MGB, within-Duke, and MGB-to-Duke analyses, respectively (all p ă 0.001). Within MGB, ADAPT also had substantially higher lift-CVaR than the current-period-only, pooled, and maximin models, by 0.23, 0.11, and 0.41, respectively (all p ă 0.001), while remaining similar to TransGLM. Within Duke, ADAPT improved sensitivity-CVaR over the four comparators by approximately 1.6, 0.9, 0.6, and 0.9 percentage points, respectively (p ă .001), while also achieving comparable or higher lift-CVaR, with gains of approximately 0.11, 0.065, 0.07, and 0.12.

After MGB-to-Duke transport, ADAPT improved sensitivity-CVaR over the transported currentperiod-only, TransGLM, pooled, and maximin models by approximately 2.7, 2.0, 1.0, and 0.2 percentage points, respectively, and improved lift-CVaR by approximately 0.24, 0.08, 0.28, and 0.19. These patterns were broadly similar at α " 0.40, 0.60, and 0.80; the main exceptions were that lift was often comparable between ADAPT and TransGLM, and sensitivity became similar between ADAPT and transported maximin at some larger α values (Supplementary Figures S3-S5). Compared with the current-period-only model locally trained at Duke, transported ADAPT had higher lift-CVaR by approximately 0.15 but lower sensitivity-CVaR by 1.47 percentage points at α " 0.20.

In the synthetic-data study, ADAPT performed well across current-period, future-generalization, and abrupt-shift settings. When models were evaluated in the same period used as the training target, ADAPT matched TransGLM with very limited current-period data (ρ " 0.1) and outperformed the other comparators; as ρ increased, its performance approached that of the target-only model (Figure S1, left). Under the abrupt perturbation introduced at period 8 to represent a regime change, performance was generally higher when model development and validation occurred within the same regime (pre-pre or post-post) than when they spanned the shift (Supplementary Figure S2). 4 Discussion

In this retrospective multi-health-system study, ADAPT was developed to address a practical problem in EHR-based mental health risk prediction: a model that performs well for patients seen at development can deteriorate as clinical populations and data-generating processes change.

ADAPT combined historical period-specific models with limited current-period data and explicitly balanced current accuracy against adverse performance under plausible future relationships.

Across 28 mental-health outcomes, ADAPT generally achieved stronger performance in the more challenging temporal settings within both health systems and after cross-health-system transport. In the focused suicide-attempt case study, ADAPT maintained strong contemporaneous discrimination while showing less performance degradation as the interval between model development and evaluation increased. Conventional maximin-based DRO learning was sometimes more stable over long intervals, but its lower baseline AUC showed that stability alone does not 24 ensure clinically useful prediction.

The findings highlight a distinction between adapting to the present and preparing for the near future. A current-period-only model directly represents recent patients but can be imprecise when the contemporary sample contains few outcomes. Cumulative pooling increases sample size but treats older and recent data as though they were exchangeable. TransGLM borrows from historical periods to improve a specified current target, but it does not explicitly optimize durability after that target period. ADAPT uses held-out current-period data to exclude historical mixtures that no longer fit the present and then protects against the most adverse model within the remaining range. The multi-outcome analyses showed that this strategy improved performance across a broad range of mental-health prediction tasks. The suicide-attempt case study further showed that the same approach could preserve contemporaneous discrimination while reducing deterioration as a model aged. Its advantage was also observed when models developed at MGB were evaluated at Duke, despite differences in patient composition and documentation environments.

Temporal robustness is especially relevant for suicide-attempt prediction. Recorded diagnoses, medication use, health-care encounters, and suicide-related codes reflect both underlying patient risk and the clinical processes through which that risk is observed. Changes in screening, coding, treatment, access to care, and help-seeking can therefore affect both predictor distributions and their associations with documented outcomes. ADAPT does not identify the cause of an observed shift, nor does it remove the need for model monitoring. Instead, it uses heterogeneity already observed across calendar periods to reduce dependence on a single development distribution. The summary-level implementation also means that historical periods or institutions need not contribute raw patient-level data after their models have been estimated, although labeled current-period data are still required.

The sensitivity analysis using XGBoost-derived nonlinear features produced similar relative performance patterns across methods, suggesting that ADAPT's advantage was not specific to the original feature representation. More generally, because ADAPT operates on a flexible feature map, the framework can be applied to representations derived from more complex prediction models while retaining the same strategy for learning from temporal heterogeneity. The synthetic-data experiments provided complementary evidence under controlled shifts:

ADAPT maintained current-period performance when contemporary data were limited, achieved the highest worst future-period AUC across varying amounts of current data, and deteriorated more slowly when models developed before an abrupt regime change were evaluated afterward.

These results help separate the intended robustness of the method from characteristics specific to the observed EHR cohorts.

The study has several strengths. It evaluated model durability over many calendar years rather than reporting only a random train-test split. It assessed performance across 28 mentalhealth outcomes and then examined temporal degradation in greater depth in a suicide-attempt case study. The study period spanned major changes in coding and care delivery, and an independent Duke cohort provided external validation. Comparisons included recent-period training, cumulative pooling, transfer learning, and conventional worst-case learning, allowing current accuracy and temporal stability to be considered jointly. Controlled simulations complemented the EHR analyses by separating gradual coefficient evolution from an abrupt perturbation. Several limitations should be considered. First, this was a retrospective analysis of EHRdocumented outcomes. Events occurring outside MGB or Duke might not have been captured, and temporal changes in documentation, screening, or care-seeking could appear as changes in outcome risk. Second, although the multi-outcome analysis included discrimination and threshold-based measures, these retrospective metrics do not establish clinical utility, calibration at specific deployment thresholds, or net benefit in practice. Third, external validation involved one additional health system; performance in other regions, care settings, and EHR environments remains unknown. Fourth, ADAPT defines plausible future conditional models through mixtures of historical and current models and therefore can protect only against changes represented by, or sufficiently related to, the historical variation observed during development.

Unprecedented shifts outside this range can still lead to performance loss. Finally, this study did not assess prospective workflow integration, clinician response, patient acceptability, fairness across demographic groups, or effects on patient outcomes.

ADAPT therefore should be viewed as a component of model stewardship rather than a substitute for surveillance or clinical judgement. Before deployment, a health system would still need to select clinically meaningful thresholds, evaluate calibration in the intended population, monitor performance and data quality, and define conditions for recalibration or retraining.

Prospective evaluation is needed to determine whether improved temporal durability translates into more reliable clinical assessment and better patient outcomes. Within these limits, our findings show that temporal heterogeneity already present in historical EHR can be used to improve the durability of mental-health risk prediction while preserving performance for contemporary patients.

ADAPT has three steps: estimation of historical and current-period models; construction of an uncertainty set for plausible future models; and adversarial optimization using a second-order approximation. To assess robustness under temporal shift, we applied models trained at period t to data from subsequent periods t 1 ą t and summarized performance by the worst AUC achieved across future periods (Figure S1, right). This captures how well models generalize once deployed on unseen data subject to distributional changes. ADAPT consistently attains the highest worst-case AUC on future data sets for all ρ, indicating greater stability under temporal shift. Maximin only surpasses target-only when ρ " 0.1 but otherwise yields the lowest generalizability, while transGLM ranks second for ρ ď 0.3 before falling below target-only. Pooling remains relatively stable but consistently below ADAPT and transGLM.

Generalization under perturbed design. We further assessed robustness by partitioning training and validation into pre-shift (t ď 7) and post-shift (t ě 8) periods (Figure S2). Evaluations within the same period type (pre-pre or post-post) yield higher AUC than those spanning the shift. Training on pre-shift data and validating on post-shift data shows the greatest degradation, which worsens with increasing p perturb , reflecting the difficulty of anticipating future changes.

ADAPT deteriorates more slowly in this setting and maintains the highest medians, indicating robustness to post-shift distributions. Within-regime evaluations are less sensitive to p perturb , though pooling and maximin continue to decline even when trained and validated post-shift, suggesting limited forward generalizability. Pooling and transGLM perform competitively when both training and validation are pre-shift, but drop sharply when applied across pre-to post-shift periods. The target-only model shows consistently poor generalizability to post-shift data, while maximin remains uniformly low across all settings, reflecting over-conservativeness.
