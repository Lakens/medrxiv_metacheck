Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists slow kidney disease progression in type 2 diabetes, and in the human kidney the receptor is localized mainly to arteriolar smooth muscle. Whether these cells and their receptor are preserved in diabetic kidney disease (DKD) is unknown. We combined single-cell-level spatial transcriptomics of 5,101 genes with registered periodic acid-Schiff histology in kidney biopsies from 28 people with DKD, 102 with other glomerular diseases and 24 living donors, and measured 2,531 arterioles and small arteries. Arteriolar walls in DKD contained about 20% fewer smooth muscle cells than those in other glomerular diseases, and the loss fell on cells without matrix gene expression. Transcripts of GLP1R, which encodes the receptor, were detected in 0.48% of the remaining smooth muscle cells in DKD, compared with 1.21% in other glomerular diseases and 1.75% in donors. The glutamate receptor gene GRIA2 and the smooth muscle regulator PRDM6 were also lower, whereas contractile genes were unchanged, a pattern not seen in the other diseases. In an independent single-nucleus atlas, smooth muscle GLP1R was lower in DKD than in reference kidneys, and smooth muscle content fell with arteriolar hyalinosis grade. In human DKD, arterioles lose contractile smooth muscle cells, and fewer of the remaining cells carry transcripts for the receptor targeted by GLP-1 receptor agonists at the disease stage in which these drugs are used.

1 Introduction

Diabetic kidney disease (DKD) is a leading cause of kidney failure, with injury to preglomerular vessels a defining feature. Arteriolar hyalinosis is graded in the pathological classification of diabetic nephropathy [1], is associated with cardiovascular events [2] and increases with age across kidney diseases [3]. Smooth muscle is thought to be replaced by hyaline material [1], but morphology alone cannot distinguish fewer cells from more matrix between preserved cells. Molecular correlates of the lesion are only beginning to be defined [4].

The same vessels have become relevant to treatment. In the FLOW trial, the glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist semaglutide slowed kidney disease progression in people with type 2 diabetes and chronic kidney disease [5], extending earlier kidney signals seen with liraglutide [6] and dulaglutide [7]. In the human kidney, an extensively validated monoclonal antibody localized the GLP-1 receptor to the smooth muscle of preglomerular arteries and arterioles rather than to tubular epithelium [8], and this localization has remained the reference since earlier antibodies proved unreliable [9]. In experimental models, receptor activation on vascular smooth muscle reduces afferent arteriolar autoregulation and increases kidney blood flow [10], induces renin along the vascular tree [11] and lowers blood pressure through the smooth muscle receptor [12]. In the REMODEL trial, semaglutide reduced the intimal area of the most diseased arterioles after one year [13].

Arteriolar smooth muscle is therefore both a principal site of injury in DKD and the main site of the GLP-1 receptor in the kidney, yet whether these cells and their receptor are preserved in human DKD is unknown.

In this study, we combined single-cell-level spatial transcriptomics with registered periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) histology in kidney biopsies from people with DKD, people with other glomerular diseases and living donors. We counted smooth muscle cells in the walls of arterioles and small arteries, defined which smooth muscle cells were lost, measured GLP1R and other mural genes in the cells that remained, and compared DKD with other glomerular diseases to separate changes associated with diabetes from those shared with glomerular injury.

2 Results

2.1 Cohort and arterioles

The spatial cohort contained 5,195,294 cells from 154 participants: 28 with DKD, 102 with other glomerular diseases (60 with IgA nephropathy [IgAN], 25 with minimal change disease [MCD], 13 with membranous nephropathy [MN] and four with focal segmental glomerulosclerosis [FSGS]) and 24 living kidney donors (Table 1; Fig. 1a). All living donors had an eGFR above 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 and a urine protein-to-creatinine ratio below 0.15 g/g, none had diabetes, and 3 of 24 had hypertension (Table 1). Participants with DKD had lower estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) than those with other glomerular diseases (median 54.1 versus 93.2 to 103.5 mL/min/1.73 m 2 in IgAN, MCD and MN) and more often had hypertension (18 of 24 versus 27 of 82); 19 of the 28 had an eGFR between 25 and 75 mL/min/1.73 m 2 . The morpho-transcriptomic definition identified 2,531 arterioles and small arteries in 144 participants, including 417 from all 28 participants with DKD (fig. S1). Registered PAS walls coincided with ACTA2 -expressing mural cells and an endothelial lining in the spatial data (Fig. 1b).

2.2 Fewer contractile smooth muscle cells in diabetic arteriolar walls

Smooth muscle density was lower in DKD than in other glomerular diseases (-1,941 cells/mm 2 of wall; 95% confidence interval [CI], -2,544 to -1,338), about 20% of the comparator mean (Holm-adjusted P < 0.001; Fig. 2, a to c; Table 2). The difference was -1,413 cells/mm 2 (-2,022 to -804) after age, sex and batch adjustment, and the deficit was also seen per 100 µm of outer perimeter (Table 2). The deficit was present against donors and against other diagnoses on the same slides (table S1).

The lower density reflected fewer cells rather than a change in cell identity. All nucleated wall cells were fewer by 2,679 cells/mm 2 (-3,357 to -2,001) and cells identified by ACTA2 transcripts alone by 2,058 (-3,017 to -1,100), whereas non-smooth-muscle cells were not more numerous (-479; -1,042 to 83; Fig. 2d).

Matrix gene expression separated the cells that were lost from those that remained. Among cells with at least one nuclear transcript, 27.3% of DKD arteriolar smooth muscle cells carried at least one COL1A1, COL1A2, FN1 or TNC transcript, compared with 20.2% in other glomerular diseases (ratio, 1.53; 1.26 to 1.85), whereas the proportion carrying contractile transcripts did not differ (0.93; 0.83 to 1.04). Counted per wall area, the deficit fell on cells with contractile transcripts only (-1,476 cells/mm 2 ; -2,047 to -904) and on cells with neither (-773; -1,288 to -258), whereas cells carrying matrix transcripts were not fewer (Fig. 2e). Accordingly, the remaining smooth muscle cells had a higher matrix score (0.61; 0.32 to 0.89) with an unchanged contractile score (-0.04; -0.28 to 0.21). Matrix scores did not differ in fibroblasts (0.17; -0.45 to 0.79) or endothelium (-0.06; -0.27 to 0.16) of the same walls (table S2).

The loss tracked the wall lesion. Within arterioles, each 10 percentage points of PAS-dense acellular wall was associated with 793 fewer smooth muscle cells/mm 2 (-934 to -653), including within DKD (-579; -860 to -297; table S1). The DKD deficit persisted after adjustment for eGFR and proteinuria or for hypertension (table S1). Adjustment for recorded arteriolar hyalinosis attenuated the deficit (-1,416; -2,531 to -302), and its interval included zero once age, sex and batch were added (-855; -1,974 to 265). Thick-walled profiles with fewer than three smooth muscle cells, which lay outside the primary set, were more frequent in DKD, and including them enlarged the deficit (-2,440 cells/mm 2 ; table S1).

2.3 Fewer GLP1R-positive smooth muscle cells in diabetic arteriolar walls

GLP1R detection was higher in arteriolar smooth muscle than in mural cells outside the outlined arterioles (356 of 29,229 versus 1,010 of 225,415 cells), and GLP1R transcripts were rarely detected in renin-associated cells (7 of 7,122; Fig. 3b). In DKD, 0.48% of arteriolar smooth muscle cells (20 of 4,145) were GLP1R-positive, compared with 1.21% (247 of 20,371) in other glomerular diseases and 1.75% (89 of 5,078) in donors (Fig. 3, a and c; table S3). The ratio of GLP1R detection rates relative to other glomerular diseases was 0.33 (95% CI, 0.17 to 0.66; Holm-adjusted P = 0.002) and 0.36 (0.15 to 0.85) after adjustment for age, sex, batch, eGFR and hypertension (Table 2 and table S4). The reduction was consistent across participant-level and background-aware models and within batch and caliber subsets (table S4).

Wall positivity exceeded the false-positive rate expected from intergenic probes 17.0-fold in donors, 11.2-fold in other glomerular diseases and 4.5-fold in DKD (95% CI, 1.5 to 8.7; fig. S2), whereas proximal-tubule, endothelial and intergenic-probe positivity did not differ between DKD and other glomerular diseases. GLP1R-positive cells per arteriole were 66% fewer in DKD (ratio, 0.34; 0.14 to 0.81); lower positivity per cell accounted for 86% of this difference and fewer cells per arteriole for 14% (Fig. 3d). Without the PAS outlines, mural-cell GLP1R expression was also lower in DKD (table S4). Relative to the 23 participants with hypertension but without diabetes, the adjusted ratio was 0.34 (0.13 to 0.91).

2.4 Selective changes in the remaining smooth muscle cells

Of the 118 mural genes, five differed between DKD and other glomerular diseases (Fig. 4a and Table 3). GLP1R (log 2 fold change, -0.90; 95% CI, -1.36 to -0.44), GRIA2 (-1.12; -1.54 to -0.69), PRDM6 (-0.79; -1.22 to -0.36) and REN (-1.23; -1.98 to -0.48) were lower, whereas P2RX1 remained at donor levels in DKD and was lower in the other glomerular diseases. The reductions in GLP1R and GRIA2 persisted after adjustment for transcripts per cell and after excluding the two participants with other glomerular diseases who had diabetes (table S5). For GLP1R, GRIA2 and PRDM6, IgAN, MCD, MN and FSGS showed no significant reduction relative to donors (Fig. 4b). Across the 48 panel vasoregulatory genes, including AGTR1, EDNRA and CACNA1C, only GLP1R, GRIA2, PRDM6 and REN were lower in DKD than in other glomerular diseases, and ACTA2 did not differ (fig. S3). Lower REN was not explained by renin-angiotensin system (RAS) inhibitor use before biopsy (-1.24; -1.94 to -0.54; table S5). After adjustment for transcript yield, extracellular-matrix structural constituents and transforming growth factor-β response genes were enriched in DKD smooth muscle, in line with its higher matrix score (Fig. 4c and table S2).

2.5 Independent cohort

In the Kidney Precision Medicine Project (KPMP) single-nucleus atlas, smooth muscle GLP1R was lower in DKD than in reference kidneys (log 2 fold change, -1.18; 95% CI, -2.10 to -0.25; Fig. 5a), and GLP1R-positive smooth muscle nuclei numbered 5 of 622 versus 42 of 892 (Fig. 5b). GRIA2 was also lower (-0.79; -1.54 to -0.04). PRDM6, by contrast, was higher in KPMP DKD than in reference kidneys (1.61; 1.00 to 2.21), and REN and P2RX1 did not differ (Fig. 5a). Mural-gene effects against reference kidneys correlated between this study and KPMP (ρ = 0.65; fig. S4). The smooth muscle fraction fell with each grade of arteriolar hyalinosis (-0.47 log 2 units per grade; -0.83 to -0.10; Fig. 5c).

3 Discussion

In kidney biopsies from people with DKD, arterioles and small arteries contained fewer smooth muscle cells, and the cells that remained less often carried GLP1R transcripts. The cells that were lost were mainly contractile cells without matrix gene expression, so the remaining wall was weighted toward cells that express matrix genes. In the remaining cells, GLP1R, GRIA2 and PRDM6 were lower while contractile genes were preserved, a pattern that distinguished DKD from four other glomerular diseases sampled by the same biopsy route. Lower smooth muscle GLP1R in DKD was reproduced in an independent single-nucleus atlas.

These observations are relevant to how GLP-1 receptor agonists act in the diabetic kidney. Most DKD participants had kidney function within the range enrolled in FLOW, in which semaglutide slowed kidney disease progression [5], so the biopsies represent the stage at which treatment is usually started. At this stage, both smooth muscle cellularity and the fraction of cells carrying receptor transcripts were already reduced. Most of the deficit in receptor-carrying cells per arteriole, however, reflected lower receptor detection in the cells that remained rather than loss of the cells themselves, so the arteriolar target of these drugs was still present. A reduction in receptor expression within surviving cells may be more amenable to treatment than loss of the cells, which supports treatment at this stage. In mice, semaglutide lowers blood pressure through the smooth muscle receptor [12], and in REMODEL the intimal area of the most diseased arterioles was smaller after a year of treatment [13]; whether arteriolar responses depend on the receptor remaining at baseline can be tested in paired biopsies from treatment trials. Whole-kidney Glp1r downregulation has been reported in diabetic mice [14], and our measurements place that loss within the vascular wall of the human kidney. GLP-1 receptor activation also induces renin expression along the arteriolar tree in mice [11]. In our sections, renin-associated cells rarely carried GLP1R transcripts, and detection was concentrated in arteriolar smooth muscle, consistent with antibody localization [8].

The loss of smooth muscle cells connects the molecular data to the classical lesion. Arteriolar hyalinosis has long been described as the replacement of smooth muscle by hyaline material [1].

In keeping with this, walls with more PAS-dense acellular matrix contained fewer smooth muscle cells in every diagnosis, adjustment for recorded hyalinosis attenuated the diabetic deficit, and in an independent cohort smooth muscle content fell with each hyalinosis grade. The lost cells were mainly contractile cells without matrix gene expression, and higher matrix scores were confined to smooth muscle rather than wall fibroblasts or endothelium. Vessels with fewer than three smooth muscle cells were more frequent in DKD and lay outside the primary set, so the measured deficit excludes the most depleted vessels. Pericytes and perivascular fibroblasts [15] and Gli1-positive progenitors [16] contribute to matrix in experimental fibrosis, and their share cannot be separated in these sections.

The other selective changes point in different directions. GRIA2 is expressed by human arterial smooth muscle and decreases after arterial injury in rats [17], and in cultured human smooth muscle cells it promotes proliferation and migration [18]. Lower GRIA2 alongside fewer smooth muscle cells is consistent with a reduced capacity to maintain or replace the arteriolar smooth muscle layer, and GRIA2 was also lower in KPMP. PRDM6, which promotes the proliferative gene program in smooth muscle cells [19], was lower than in other glomerular diseases in our cohort but higher in DKD in KPMP, so this change was not consistent between cohorts. By contrast, P2RX1, which mediates pressure-dependent afferent arteriolar constriction [20], was preserved in the remaining diabetic cells. Lower wall REN was not explained by RAS inhibitors and is in line with the hyporeninemic state of DKD [21].

The comparison with other glomerular diseases places these changes within diabetes rather than within kidney injury in general. IgAN, MCD and MN cover a wide range of proteinuria and kidney function, yet none showed significant reductions in arteriolar GLP1R, GRIA2 or PRDM6 relative to donors. The diabetic difference also survived adjustment for eGFR, proteinuria and hypertension, and participants with hypertension but without diabetes retained GLP1R detection in arteriolar smooth muscle. Given that hyperglycemia lowers receptor expression in beta cells [22] and that receptor loss accompanies diabetic injury in glomerular endothelium [23], these findings suggest that the diabetic milieu itself shapes the population of smooth muscle cells carrying receptor transcripts.

This study has several limitations. Receptor protein was not measured, and detection in only 1% to 2% of donor wall cells makes the transcript estimates sparse. The cross-sectional design cannot order cell loss and transcriptional change. Arteriolar walls were outlined by automated segmentation without pathologist review, and arterioles were not distinguished from small arteries by morphology. Batches, eGFR and hypertension were imbalanced, and donor biopsies carried procurement signatures. Biopsies from people with diabetes but without kidney disease were not available. The panel lacked several smooth muscle genes, including MYH11, CNN1, DES and

TAGLN.

In human DKD, the arteriolar wall loses contractile smooth muscle cells and the cells that remain carry less GLP1R, a pattern not shared by other glomerular diseases. These changes link the classical arteriolar lesion of diabetic nephropathy to the cellular target of GLP-1 receptor agonists at the disease stage in which these drugs are used.

4 Materials and Methods

4.1 Study design

This cross-sectional study asked whether arteriolar smooth muscle cells and their GLP1R expression are preserved in human DKD. All KORNERSTONE participants whose kidney biopsy sections had undergone single-cell-level spatial transcriptomics were included: participants with DKD, participants with IgAN, MCD, MN or FSGS as disease comparators, and living kidney donors. No sample size calculation was performed. One primary section per participant was analyzed, and the participant was the unit of analysis. Arterioles were identified by the same morphological and transcriptomic criteria in all sections, and these criteria did not use diagnosis. The principal outcomes were smooth muscle density and GLP1R positivity in arteriolar walls in DKD compared with other glomerular diseases; mural-gene expression was a secondary outcome, and the KPMP single-nucleus atlas served as an external cohort. Participants were grouped by clinical and pathological diagnosis, and randomization and blinding were not applicable.

4.2 Participants

Kidney biopsies were obtained from 154 participants in KORNERSTONE, a prospective Korean kidney biopsy cohort and biobank [24]: 28 with DKD, 60 with IgA nephropathy (IgAN), 25 with minimal change disease (MCD), 13 with membranous nephropathy (MN) and four with focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS), together with 24 living kidney donors who underwent zerotime biopsy at transplantation. One primary section per participant was analyzed. Among the 104 disease participants with linked pre-biopsy medication records, including 23 of 28 with DKD, none was receiving a GLP-1 receptor agonist. Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated with the 2021 CKD-EPI creatinine equation [25], and hypertension and diabetes were recorded at biopsy; for living donors, creatinine, proteinuria, hypertension and diabetes were taken from the pre-donation evaluation. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Hospital (approval No. 2504-168-1637), and all participants gave written informed consent after the nature and possible consequences of the study had been explained.

4.3 Spatial transcriptomics and morpho-transcriptomic arteriole definition

Sections underwent single-cell-level spatial transcriptomics with the Xenium Prime 5K panel and a 100-gene add-on [26], and registered PAS histology was available for 146 participants.

Arterioles were identified by a morpho-transcriptomic definition that combined the morphology of each structure on PAS with the transcriptomic identity of the cells it contained (Fig. 1a; fig. S1).

A deep-learning segmentation model outlined tissue structures on the PAS section (Supplementary Materials and Methods). The morphological criteria required an enclosed central lumen of low optical density, a lumen-to-outer diameter ratio of at least 0.2 and no overlap with a glomerulus; the wall was the area between the outer contour and the lumen. The transcriptomic criteria were applied to the registered cells: structures in which glomerular or tubular cells predominated, or with tubular cells in the lumen or more than two in the wall, were excluded, and at least three arteriolar smooth muscle cells had to lie within the wall. Structures meeting both sets of criteria were analyzed as arterioles and small arteries, and outer-diameter tertiles defined three calibers. The outline provides the wall area and places each cell inside or outside the wall, and the cells within the wall provide the counts and expression measured in that arteriole.

4.4 Wall measurements

Smooth muscle cells were counted per wall area and per 100 µm of outer perimeter, and all wall nuclei and ACTA2 -positive cells were also counted irrespective of annotation. Each smooth muscle cell with at least one detected nuclear transcript was classed by whether it carried at least one nuclear transcript of the contractile-score genes and of the matrix genes COL1A1, COL1A2, FN1 and TNC. The PAS-dense acellular fraction of each wall was measured by color deconvolution. Gene scores summarized contractile, mural-identity, matrix and proliferation expression.

4.5 GLP1R detection

Detection and pseudobulk expression analyses used mural-lineage smooth muscle cells within the arteriolar wall. Positivity required at least one nuclear GLP1R transcript. Negative-control probes, codewords and intergenic probes were processed identically and converted to false-positive rates expected for GLP1R. A cell mixed model estimated ratios of detection rates adjusted for nuclear transcripts per cell (Supplementary Materials and Methods). Proximal-tubule, endothelial and intergenic-probe positivity served as negative-control outcomes.

4.6 Gene expression

Counts from arteriolar smooth muscle cells were summed by participant, retaining the participant as the biological replicate [27]. Pseudobulk counts underwent trimmed mean of M-values normalization [28], mean-variance precision weighting [29] and empirical-Bayes moderation [30]. Mural genes were the 118 panel genes with higher mean expression in mural cells than in any other lineage, selected without diagnosis labels, and each non-diabetic disease was also compared with donors.

The same participant pseudobulk was formed for fibroblasts and endothelial cells in the wall. Gene Ontology [31] gene-set enrichment [32] used rankings adjusted for transcript yield. Immediate-early and glucocorticoid-response scores quantified procurement effects in donor biopsies.

4.7 External cohort

Vascular smooth muscle in the Kidney Precision Medicine Project (KPMP) single-nucleus atlas [33] was analyzed by participant-level pseudobulk, comparing DKD with reference kidneys (table S6).

Arteriolar hyalinosis grades were taken from the atlas pathology annotations.

4.8 Statistical analysis

Smooth muscle density and wall GLP1R positivity in DKD versus other glomerular diseases were the principal outcomes, with Holm adjustment [34]; mural-gene tests used Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate control within the 118 genes [35]. Wall models included diagnosis, caliber and a participant random intercept. Other glomerular diseases were weighted by participant frequency, and each diagnosis was also reported separately. Adjusted models added age, sex and batch, and further models added eGFR, proteinuria, hypertension or recorded hyalinosis. Comparisons with donors are reported descriptively. Tests were two-sided, and estimates are given with 95% CIs. The Supplementary Materials and Methods provide model equations and resampling definitions.

Materials and Methods

Participants and clinical data

Clinical variables were taken from the deidentified clinical database of the cohort and from the KORNER-STONE case report form. Records were linked to Xenium sections by pathology number in an access-controlled environment, and identifiers were not retained in analysis files.

Section-to-participant linkage was confirmed against restricted sample metadata for all 60 participants with IgAN. Matching by baseline laboratory eGFR, integer age and sex uniquely linked all 130 disease participants.

Donor sex was taken from donor records, and donor age was calculated in completed years from the birth date to the date of donation. Donor creatinine, urine protein-to-creatinine ratio (UPCR), urine dipstick albumin, hypertension, diabetes and glycated hemoglobin were taken from the pre-donation evaluation in the electronic medical record, and donor eGFR was calculated from that creatinine with the 2021 CKD-EPI equation [S1] using donor age and sex. Creatinine was measured a median of 1 day before donation (range, 1 to 4 days) and UPCR a median of 98 days before donation (range, 22 to 462 days); urine dipstick albumin, measured 1 to 22 days before donation, was negative in 22 donors and trace in 2. No donor had diabetes (glycated hemoglobin, 4.7% to 5.8%).

In disease participants, hypertension and diabetes were recorded at biopsy. Pre-biopsy medication use was classified from case-report-form drug names in the 104 disease participants with available medication records.

Cell annotation

Cells were annotated following the cell-type hierarchy of the KPMP kidney atlas [S2]. Spatial transcriptomic data were integrated with KORNERSTONE single-nucleus RNA-sequencing data by scVI

Xenium processing and counts

Each section's cell-feature matrix was matched to the analysis cell table by centroid. Nuclear counts comprised transcripts that were assigned to a cell, had a quality value of at least 20 and overlapped the segmented nucleus; all-assigned counts used the same rule without the nuclear criterion.

Technical control features were counted per cell with identical filters: 40 negative-control probes, negativecontrol codewords, unassigned codewords and intergenic genomic-control probes. Deprecated codewords were not used as background. Counts in each control category were converted to a GLP1R-equivalent rate, λ = 3 × count / (codewords × cells), because GLP1R is encoded by three codewords, and the expected number of false-positive cells among n cells was n(1 -exp(-λ)).

Automated segmentation of kidney structures

Kidney structures were outlined by an instance segmentation model built on Mask R-CNN [S4] with a

ResNet-50 feature pyramid network backbone [S5, S6], initialized with weights trained on the COCO dataset [S7], to which convolutional block attention modules (CBAM) [S8] were added at each feature-pyramid level and before the box and mask heads (fig. S5a). The model took 512 × 512-pixel PAS tiles at 10× magnification (1.05 µm per pixel) and returned a mask for each structure; glomeruli, tubules and vessels were segmented as a single class, lumens were not delineated, and vessels were identified among the segmented structures after registration. For training, a trained annotator outlined glomeruli, tubules and vessels by hand in 94 patches of 512 × 512 pixels sampled at random from two PAS-stained whole-slide images of kidney biopsies outside the 31.

study cohort (3,268 structures), and the patches were split at random into training (60), validation (15) and test (19) sets. Both slides were obtained at Seoul National University Hospital, and their use was approved by its Institutional Review Board (approval No. H-2103-091-1205), which waived the requirement for informed consent. Training used flips, 90° rotations and color jitter, the Mask R-CNN losses with the box and mask terms weighted 3, the AdamW optimizer [S9] and cosine annealing with warm restarts [S10], and the weights with the highest validation mean average precision were kept. On the test patches, mask average precision at an intersection over union of 0.5 was 0.839, compared with 0.652 for Mask R-CNN without CBAM (fig. S5b).

The model was applied to tiles of the whole PAS slides carrying the 146 sections with registered histology, with adjacent tiles overlapping by half and tiles with more than 20% tissue retained, and detections with a confidence score above 0.3 were pooled across the tiles of each slide. Duplicate detections from overlapping tiles were removed or merged according to their overlap relative to the area of the smaller detection, which approaches 1 when a partial detection lies within a complete one. For morphological cluster labels, each instance was taken as a 224 × 224-pixel image at 10× magnification, color-normalized by the Vahadane method [S11], masked to its contour and embedded with the UNI pathology foundation model [S12]; the embeddings were reduced to 32 dimensions with UMAP [S13] and grouped by k-means, both fitted on a random 10% of the instances from the 24 slides of the first batch. Sixteen clusters, chosen among 16, 32, 64 and 128 by the best agreement with the dominant cell type of each instance in the registered spatial data (adjusted mutual information), labeled every instance, and the glomerular cluster was the one whose instances contained, on average, at least 30% podocytes, mesangial cells and parietal epithelial cells.

Periodic acid-Schiff images were registered to cell coordinates by landmark alignment with tile-wise local correction, and contours were mapped to cell coordinates with the same registration. Within each contour, the lumen was the connected low-optical-density region containing the contour center, defined by an Otsu threshold on mean optical density within the contour at 1.05 µm per pixel. Contours were set aside if this lumen touched the outer contour along more than 5% of its boundary, if the lumen was smaller than 4 µm 2 or if the wall had fewer than three pixels.

Of 250,248 segmented contours intersecting the 146 sections, those centered outside the section, those in the glomerular cluster, those in which glomerular cells made up at least a quarter or tubular cells at least half of the enclosed cells (minimum three cells), those without smooth muscle or endothelial cells within 5 µm and those without an enclosed central lumen were set aside, leaving 9,722 vascular profiles.

Morpho-transcriptomic arteriole definition and wall measurements

The definition combined morphological criteria on the PAS contour (an enclosed central lumen, a lumento-outer equivalent-diameter ratio of at least 0.2, no glomerular overlap and exclusion of the glomerular morphological cluster) with transcriptomic criteria on the registered cells (no glomerular or tubular predominance among enclosed cells, smooth muscle or endothelial cells within 5 µm, no tubular cells in the lumen, no more than two tubular cells inside the outer contour and at least three smooth muscle cells in the wall).

Profiles with at least three smooth muscle (mural-lineage) cells whose centroids lay in the wall were analyzed as arterioles and small arteries, and outer-diameter thresholds of 23.42 and 35.74 µm, the tertiles of the candidate profiles, defined three calibers.

Wall cellularity was expressed per wall area and per 100 µm of outer perimeter and compared with linear mixed models that had diagnosis and caliber as fixed effects and a participant random intercept. The The PAS-dense acellular wall fraction was derived by optical-density color deconvolution with hematoxylin (0.644, 0.717, 0.267) and PAS (0.175, 0.972, 0.155) vectors and batch-specific Otsu thresholds. Thick-walled candidates with zero to two smooth muscle cells, which passed the same anatomical exclusions, had endothelial support and had a wall at least as thick as the donor median for the same caliber, were added only in sensitivity analyses.

Smooth muscle co-expression and wall matrix

Among smooth muscle cells with at least one nuclear transcript, contractile positivity required at least one transcript of ACTA2, ACTG2, SMTN, MYLK, KCNMB1, SYNPO2 or CSRP1, and matrix positivity at least one transcript of COL1A1, COL1A2, FN1 or TNC. Densities of the four combinations were counted per arteriole wall area and compared by linear mixed models with diagnosis and caliber as fixed effects and a participant random intercept. Matrix and contractile positivity were analyzed by cell-level complementary log-log mixed models with a log nuclear transcript offset and participant random intercept, with age, sex and batch added in adjusted models. Matrix scores were calculated from lineage-specific participant pseudobulk with at least ten cells of the lineage, using the same normalization and gene-standardization rule as the smooth muscle scores, and compared by participant regression with HC3 standard errors. Associations of smooth muscle density with PAS-dense acellular wall fraction, per ten percentage points, used linear mixed models with caliber and a participant random intercept, with diagnosis added in the pooled diagnosis-adjusted model and separate fits within diagnosis groups.

GLP1R models

Detection and pseudobulk expression analyses used mural-lineage smooth muscle cells assigned to the vascular wall. A wall smooth muscle cell was GLP1R-positive if it contained at least one nuclear GLP1R transcript.

The primary model was a cell-level binomial generalized linear mixed model with a complementary log-log link, a participant random intercept and an offset for log nuclear transcripts per cell, fitted by maximum likelihood with adaptive Gauss-Hermite quadrature (15 nodes). Profile-likelihood intervals and a parametric bootstrap likelihood-ratio test (400 replicates) supplemented the Wald intervals, with lme4 providing reference likelihood fits.

Sensitivity analyses used participant beta-binomial models; a background-aware model in which each participant's intergenic-probe GLP1R-equivalent rate entered as a fixed hazard; further adjustment for eGFR and hypertension; restriction to Batch 1 or to the largest caliber; comparison with participants with hypertension but without diabetes; and downsampling of comparator walls to the distribution of wall-cell numbers in DKD (2,000 replicates). GLP1R-positive cells per arteriole were decomposed into wall cells per arteriole and positive fraction on the log scale, with 5,000 participant bootstrap resamples. The same models were applied to GLP1R in proximal tubule, endothelium and all cells and to wall intergenic-probe positivity as negative-control outcomes.

For the disjoint localization display, renin-associated mural cells were assigned to a separate group before division of the remaining mural cells by their position inside or outside the PAS-defined arteriolar wall. This display contains 29,229 arteriolar, 225,415 other mural and 7,122 renin-associated cells. Primary detection and expression inference retains all 29,594 arteriolar smooth muscle cells, including 365 renin-associated wall cells, none positive for nuclear GLP1R. Analyses without PAS used cell-defined vessel profiles or all mural cells and participant pseudobulk expression, with 151 and 154 eligible participants, respectively.

Expression, receptors and gene sets

Participant pseudobulk counts were normalized by trimmed mean of M-values, weighted by the mean-variance trend and moderated by empirical Bayes [S14-S16]. Basic models contained diagnosis, and adjusted models added age, sex and batch. False discovery rates were controlled within the 118 panel genes with maximal mean expression in mural rather than other cell lineages, a filter computed from all cells without diagnosis labels. The vasoregulatory receptor set comprised 48 panel genes and was tested within its own family.

Procurement scores were means of standardized immediate-early (FOS, EGR2, EGR3, ATF3, NR4A1 ) and glucocorticoid-response (PER1, ZBTB16 ) genes in wall cells, all cells and proximal tubule.

Gene-set enrichment used moderated statistics from models that included transcript yield, with GO biologicalprocess and molecular-function sets restricted to the panel [S17, S18]; the two reported sets concern extracellular matrix structural constituents and the response to transforming growth factor beta.

External cohort

Vascular smooth muscle (subclass VSMC) in the KPMP single-nucleus atlas [S2] was obtained from the KPMP data portal and analyzed by participant-level pseudobulk, comparing DKD with reference kidneys.

Participants needed at least ten smooth muscle nuclei to enter pseudobulk analyses, which left 15 participants with DKD and 22 reference participants. Pseudobulk counts were processed as in the discovery cohort.

Positivity was compared by participant permutation (20,000) and cluster bootstrap (5,000), with exact intervals for pooled rate ratios. Mural-gene effects were compared with those of this study, DKD versus donors, for the 115 mural genes estimable in both datasets by Spearman correlation, as a descriptive measure without a P value.

For arteriolar hyalinosis, the smooth muscle fraction of each sample was log 2 {(M + 0.5)/N }, where M is the number of smooth muscle nuclei and N the number of all nuclei; the 0.5 correction retained samples without smooth muscle nuclei. A linear model regressed this fraction on the atlas arteriolar hyalinosis grade (0 to 3), with age, sex and DKD as covariates, in the 62 participants with a hyalinosis grade and known sex.

Statistical reporting

The participant was the unit of inference throughout.

Two principal outcomes, smooth muscle density and wall GLP1R positivity in DKD versus other glomerular diseases, were Holm-adjusted; all other analyses were secondary or exploratory, with false discovery rates controlled within the families stated in the tables and legends [S19]. Wall models were also compared within slides that carried a DKD section. Confidence intervals are 95%, and tests are two-sided. Analyses used Python 3.12 (pandas, statsmodels, scipy, shapely, pyarrow) and R 4.5 (lme4, ggplot2, patchwork).

Solid outlines mark the outer contour and dashed outlines the lumen; cell colors identify smooth muscle, endothelial, fibroblast, immune, tubular epithelial and other cells; transcript colors distinguish ACTA2, MYLK, PECAM1 and GLP1R. Scale bars are shown within each field.

Fig S5.

Fig.S5. Kidney structure segmentation model. (a) Architecture. A ResNet-50 feature pyramid network computes feature maps P2-P6 from each 512 × 512-pixel PAS tile, and a convolutional block attention module (CBAM) refines each map. The refined maps P′2-P′6 enter the region proposal network, and region-of-interest alignment pools features for each proposed region from P′2-P′5, at 7 × 7 for the box branch and 14 × 14 for the mask branch. A further CBAM in each branch refines the region features before the box head, which returns the class score and box of each structure, and the mask head, which returns its mask. At inference, the mask branch uses the detected boxes (dashed arrow). (b) Manual annotation (top row) and predictions of Mask R-CNN (middle row) and of Mask R-CNN with CBAM (bottom row) on five test patches of 512 × 512 pixels at 10× magnification, one patch per column; colors distinguish individual structures.
