Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

INTRODUCTION: Web-based registries support remote cognitive assessment for Alzheimer's disease research, but participant-driven reassessment raises questions about long-term data yield and interpretation of repeated scores. We evaluated data contribution, selective observation, and retest-dependent estimates of cognitive change in the Japanese Trial-Ready Cohort (J-TRC) Webstudy. METHODS: We analyzed over six years of J-TRC Webstudy data (launched October 2019; registrations through March 31, 2025; assessments through May 31, 2026). We examined data contribution, inactivity, reactivation, score-associated observation, first-exposure contrasts, and sensitivity to retest models and estimators. RESULTS: Among 14,912 registered accounts, 10,863 participants were eligible; 10,645 (98.0%) completed the Cognitive Function Instrument (CFI), 6,419 (59.1%) completed Cogstate, and 5,792 (53.3%) contributed assessments on multiple days. Among those with sufficient opportunity, 19.4% and 14.1% contributed data at or beyond three and five years. After 365 days without assessment, the estimated probability of resuming within two years was 7.2%. Worse prior cognitive scores were associated with lower odds of subsequent assessment (adjusted odds ratios per standard deviation: CFI, 0.952; Cogstate, 0.845). By assessment 12, returnee changes were +0.43 and +1.14 favorable baseline standard deviations (CFI, Cogstate), versus -0.06 and +0.08 in first-exposure contrasts. Marginal CFI time estimates differed under categorical versus flexible retest adjustment (+0.080 versus -0.227 standard deviations/year). DISCUSSION: High initial completion was accompanied by limited long-term data contribution. Subsequent assessment was associated with prior scores, and estimated time trends varied by retest model. Reporting assessment histories and testing model sensitivity are important for interpreting repeated online cognitive scores.

Methods

Study design and participants

We conducted a longitudinal observational study of the ongoing Japanese Trial-Ready Cohort (J-TRC) Webstudy, launched in October 2019 for AD prevention research [Sato2021(PMID: 34292159); Sato2021(PMID: 33778148)]. The Webstudy's public landing page is shown in Figure 1A. Participants register and consent online; the Cognitive Function Instrument (CFI) is integrated into the assessment process, whereas Cogstate testing is optional. The same instrument becomes available for voluntary reassessment 90 days after completion, without scheduled study visits (Figure 1B).

For Cogstate, participants received an email notification approximately three months after completing an assessment, when retesting became available. This notification was sent once per completed assessment and was not repeated if the participant did not retest. We distinguished registered accounts, accounts with explicit affirmative consent, participants with ascertainable eligibility information, and eligible consented participants. The primary registry-level analytical population comprised individuals who had provided affirmative . consent, had ascertainable age and self-reported AD status, were aged 50-85 years at registration, and did not report a previous AD diagnosis. Completion of all registration workflow stages or a valid cognitive assessment was not required for inclusion in this population. Assessment-specific populations were subsequently defined according to the availability of valid CFI or Cogstate records, and regression analyses were restricted to participants with the variables required by each model.

Accordingly, the denominators for registered accounts, eligible participants, participants completing valid assessments, and longitudinal modeling populations were distinguished throughout the analyses (Figure 2B; Table 1; Supplementary Table S1).

Cognitive assessments and scoring

The CFI is a 15-item self-reported measure of cognitive and functional difficulties, with higher scores indicating greater reported difficulties [Amariglio2015(PMID: 25706191); Walter2020(PMID: 32920625)]. Item responses were coded as 0 for "No," 0.5 for "Maybe," and 1 for "Yes" [Amariglio2015(PMID: 25706191)]. A "Does not apply" response counted toward questionnaire completion but did not contribute a numerical item score. A valid CFI record required completion-valid responses to all 15 items, an assessment date, and an available recorded total. The primary CFI score was reconstructed as the sum of scorable item responses without prorating. Two separate sensitivity analyses were conducted: one restricted to assessments with all 15 items numerically scorable, and another using total scores prorated to 15 items according to the number of scorable responses. Exact duplicate CFI records were removed, and when multiple valid assessments occurred on the same calendar day, the latest record was retained.

Cogstate comprised Detection (DET; psychomotor speed), Identification (IDN; visual attention), One Card Learning (OCL; visual recognition learning), and One Back (ONB; working memory) [Maruff2013(PMID: 25566378); Sato2021(PMID: 33778148)]. Standard primary measures are reaction time for DET and IDN and accuracy for OCL and ONB. Four task scores were exported, treated as higher-is-better without further sign reversal, and re-standardized using the task-specific means and standard deviations at participants' first observed Cogstate assessments. Their average defined the study-specific composite when at least two task scores were available. Duplicate sessions were removed. For longitudinal analyses, the Cogstate composite and CFI were scaled by their respective first-assessment standard deviations, with CFI changes sign-reversed so that positive values indicated improvement.

Participation and longitudinal data contribution

We quantified progression from registration to consent, eligibility, initial assessment, repeated assessment, and long-term data contribution. Assessment activity was defined using calendar dates; CFI and Cogstate assessments occurring on the same day contributed one day to the combined activity count. Repeated participation was defined as activity on at least two distinct calendar days.

Longitudinal data contribution was assessed by determining whether participants provided at least one assessment at or beyond 12, 36, and 60 months after registration. For each threshold, the denominator was restricted to eligible participants whose registration dates allowed sufficient observation opportunity before May 31, 2026. We also calculated the proportion with assessment activity during the final 365 days of observation. These measures describe observed data contribution rather than adherence to scheduled visits, which did not exist in the Webstudy. Assessment occasion was each participant's cumulative number of valid assessments for an instrument, not a scheduled visit. We summarized occasion-specific participant counts and score medians and interquartile ranges (Figure 2C-E) and examined their relationship with elapsed time (Supplementary Figure S1). Participation was compared between 2019-2021 and 2022-2023 registration cohorts within a common 24-month window. Characteristics across participation stages were described using standardized mean differences (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2), without seeking independent determinants of engagement.

Dynamic participation and reactivation

To characterize changing participation states, we divided each participant's follow-up period into nonoverlapping 90-day intervals starting from their registration date. At the end of each complete interval, eligible participants were classified into three mutually exclusive states: no previous valid assessment, recently active (an assessment less than 365 days earlier), or inactive (previous assessment activity followed by at least 365 consecutive days without assessment). State proportions were calculated among participants with sufficient observation opportunity at each interval endpoint.

Incomplete terminal intervals were excluded. Kaplan-Meier analyses estimated time from the first assessment to the first 365-day gap without an assessment, with administrative censoring. Reactivation was defined as the next assessment after such a gap; time zero was when the first qualifying gap reached 365 days.

Cumulative reactivation probabilities used Kaplan-Meier estimation with log-log confidence intervals (Figure 3A,B). For reactivation episodes with at least 365 days of subsequent observation opportunity, we assessed whether assessments occurred on at least two additional days within the following year. Sensitivity analyses varied the inactivity threshold (180 or 730 days) and registration-anchored panel width (60 or 120 days; Supplementary Figure S2).

Associations with subsequent assessment completion

We investigated whether previously observed scores and assessment histories were associated with the probability of subsequent assessment completion. For each instrument, outcome-specific rolling 90-day risk intervals were constructed beginning when reassessment became available, 90 days after the preceding assessment. The initial assessment was conditioned on, and subsequent intervals were classified according to whether an analyzable assessment was observed. We fitted logistic regression models for interval-specific observation, including elapsed time and its squared term, age, sex, educational attainment, registration year, the last observed score, prior assessment count, previous observation history, and days since the most recent Webstudy activity. Missing prior-score history was represented using a median-imputed value and a missingness indicator. Confidence intervals for the association between prior score and subsequent observation were obtained using participant-clustered robust sandwich estimators and expressed as odds ratios per 1-standard-deviation worse prior score. Model-standardized probabilities were obtained by averaging predictions over the empirical covariate distribution while varying the prior score or time since the most recent activity. Recency categories were used to summarize these standardized probabilities for presentation (Figure 3C,D).

Analysis of longitudinal cognitive score patterns

The score analyses addressed three related but distinct quantities: within-person score change among observed returnees, contrasts between matched first-exposure assessments from different participants, and model-specific elapsed-time associations conditional on the specified retest terms. We compared retest-model specifications (Supplementary Figure S3; Supplementary Table S3), constructed contemporaneous first-exposure references, and examined sensitivity to selective observation. We examined these quantities separately because they capture different aspects of the observed score data. To contextualize score changes among repeatedly assessed participants, we constructed an internal reference using contemporaneous first-observed assessments from other participants (Figure 4A). This comparison is conceptually related to replacement-participant approaches [Elman2018(PMID: 30003138)], and provided an empirical benchmark for score changes observed among returnees. For returnee assessment occasions 2-12 occurring within 36 months of the participant's first assessment, we calculated within-person score changes. Two distinct participants completing their first observed J-TRC assessment were separately matched to each returnee's initial and subsequent assessment dates. Matching used greedy nearest-neighbor matching without replacement within each matching analysis, requiring the same sex, calendar dates within ±180 days, attained ages within ±2 years, and educational attainment within ±2 years. The reference contrast was calculated as the difference between the two independently matched first-exposure scores, whereas the returnee change was calculated within the same participant.

. We summarized occasion-specific mean changes, reference contrasts, and their differences, with 95% confidence intervals (Supplementary Table S4A). An overall reference slope was estimated using participant-equal-weighted regression through the origin. Confidence intervals for this slope were obtained from 500 participant-level bootstrap replicates with repeated matching.

Sensitivity analyses restricted first-exposure assessments to within 90 days of registration or narrowed the calendar matching window to ±90 days (Supplementary Figure S4; Supplementary Table S4B). The comparator pool could include participants registering after the primary March 2025 cutoff. The reference contrasts compared different participants at their first recorded J-TRC assessments, and previous cognitive testing outside J-TRC could not be assessed.

We examined the dependence of elapsed-time estimates on retest specification using assessments collected within 36 months of each participant's first valid assessment of the respective instrument (Figure 4B). For each instrument, the latest analyzable assessment within each nonoverlapping 90-day interval anchored to registration was retained. Five representations of cumulative assessment exposure were evaluated, including categorical assessment numbers and linear or spline functions of log assessment number. Each specification included the interval since the preceding assessment and either linear elapsed time or a three-degree-of-freedom natural spline of elapsed time. Models adjusted for baseline age, sex, educational attainment, and registration year.

Elapsed time was measured in years since the first valid assessment, whereas baseline age was defined at registration.

For each specification, we compared working-independence marginal Gaussian regression with participant-clustered robust inference, maximum-likelihood linear mixed-effects models, and participant fixed-effects models. Mixed-effects models included participant-specific random intercepts and time slopes where supported by the data, whereas fixed-effects models required at least two observations per participant. Linear-time coefficients were reported per year, and spline-time estimates were summarized using the model-implied 0-36-month contrast divided by three years. All coefficients were expressed in favorable baseline-standard-deviation units. Model fit was compared using AIC within comparable marginal and mixed-effects models, and predictive performance was evaluated using five repetitions of participant-clustered 10-fold cross-validation for marginal models. The full specification grid is provided in Supplementary Table S3. These analyses quantified the sensitivity of elapsed-time estimates to retest specifications and statistical estimators. Sensitivity to selective observation was examined using inverse-probability-of-observation weighting (IPOW) across instrument-specific rolling risk intervals [Seaman2013(PMID: 21220355); Cole2008(PMID: 18682488)] (Supplementary Figure S5; Supplementary Table S4C).

Interval-specific stabilized weights came from logistic observation models: numerator models included elapsed time and baseline covariates; denominator models additionally included previous scores, cumulative assessment history, recent activity, and lagged observation. The first assessment received weight one, and interval weights were not accumulated across previous intervals. We assessed 1st-99th percentile and alternative truncation, observation probabilities, weight distributions, and effective sample sizes. Complete-case outcome models did not impute unobserved scores. We also evaluated alternative time origins using the same assessment records (Supplementary Table S5).

Analyses were performed in R using data.table, survival, lme4, and related packages. Two-sided 95% confidence intervals were calculated using participant-clustered inference or bootstrap resampling as specified for each analysis.

Results

Registry growth and longitudinal data yield

Between October 2019 and the registration cutoff of March 31, 2025, the J-TRC Webstudy accumulated 14,912 registered accounts, of which 12,068 (80.9%) had provided explicit affirmative consent and 10,863 met the eligibility criteria (Figure 2A,B; Table 1). Among eligible participants, the mean age was 62.5 years (SD 8.4), 54.6% of those with recorded sex were women, and the mean educational attainment was 15.2 years (SD 2.0). Registration increased substantially during 2022, which accounted for 4,326 (39.8%) of eligible participants. A total of 10,645 eligible participants (98.0%) completed at least one valid assessment, all of whom had CFI data; 6,419 (59.1%) completed at least one valid Cogstate assessment. Overall, 5,792 participants (53.3%) contributed assessments on at least two distinct days.

Longitudinal data contribution decreased substantially with increasing time since registration (Table 1; Supplementary Table S2). At least one assessment at or beyond 12 months was available from 3,611 of 10,863 eligible participants (33.2%). Among participants with sufficient observation opportunity, 1,936 of 9,977 (19.4%) contributed data at or beyond 36 months and 651 of 4,602 (14.1%) at or beyond 60 months. The corresponding proportions for CFI were 33.1%, 19.3%, and 14.0%, compared with 26.8%, 16.9%, and 12.9% for Cogstate. Only 1,825 eligible participants (16.8%) had completed a valid assessment within the 365 days preceding the administrative observation cutoff of May 31, 2026. Thus, high initial assessment completion was accompanied by substantially lower rates of long-term data contribution.

Participation patterns were similar across 2019-2021 (n=5,133) and 2022-2023 (n=5,212) registration cohorts during a common 24-month window (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2). Initial CFI completion was 98.1% versus 97.9%, repeated CFI completion 51.8% versus 51.9%, initial Cogstate completion 60.4% versus 57.8%, and data contribution beyond 12 months 34.9% versus 32.3%.

Repeated assessments and observed score distributions

Contributing participant numbers decreased across assessment occasions (Figure 2C): for CFI, 10,645 completed occasion 1, 5,610 occasion 2, 2,072 occasion 9, and 435 occasion 21; for Cogstate, 6,419, 3,994, and 384 completed occasions 1, 2, and 21. Occasions were not scheduled visits; corresponding elapsed times varied across participants (Supplementary Figure S1).

Observed score distributions also shifted across assessment occasions (Figure 2D,E). The median CFI total score, for which higher values indicate greater self-reported cognitive-functional difficulties, was 3.0 (interquartile range [IQR], 1.5-5.0) at the first assessment, 2.5 (IQR, 1.5-4.5) at the second, and 1.5 (IQR, 0.5-3.5) at the 21st. The median Cogstate composite score increased from 0.04 (IQR, -0.34 to 0.39) at the first assessment to 0.21 (IQR, -0.15 to 0.54) at the second and 0.83 (IQR, 0.52-1.20) at the 21st. These distributions summarize the participants observed at each assessment occasion, whose composition changed over time.

Dynamic participation, inactivity, and selective observation

Participation status changed markedly with time since registration (Figure 3A; Supplementary Table S2). At approximately 1.23 years after registration, 5,375 of 10,833 participants with sufficient observation opportunity (49.6%) had completed an assessment within the preceding 365 days, whereas 5,208 (48.1%) had previously been assessed but were inactive for at least 365 days. At approximately 3.20 years, the proportion classified as inactive had increased to 75.7% (7,279/9,617), . and by 6.16 years it reached 83.5% (2,348/2,813). The Kaplan-Meier median time to the first assessment gap of at least 365 days was 1.00 year for CFI and 1.37 years for Cogstate; the probabilities of remaining without such a gap at three years were 20.3% and 29.6%, respectively (Supplementary Table S2).

Prolonged inactivity was not invariably followed by permanent cessation of assessment activity (Figure 3B). Following the first period of at least 365 consecutive days without a valid assessment, the Kaplan-Meier cumulative probabilities of reactivation were 3.6% (95% CI, 3.3-4.0%) at approximately six months, 5.2% (95% CI, 4.8-5.7%) at 12 months, and 7.2% (95% CI, 6.7-7.8%) at 24 months. The corresponding numbers at risk were 8,456, 7,983, and 6,896. Estimates varied with the operational definition of prolonged inactivity: when inactivity was defined using a 180-day threshold, cumulative reactivation at 24 months was 13.1%, compared with 3.5% using a 730-day threshold (Supplementary Figure S2; Supplementary Table S2). Among 666 reactivation events with at least 365 days of subsequent observation opportunity, 203 (30.5%) were followed by assessment activity on at least two additional days within the following year.

Subsequent observation was associated with both prior assessment scores and recent participation history (Figure 3C,D; Supplementary Table S2). In adjusted models of 168,263 CFI risk intervals from 9,611 participants, each 1-SD worse prior CFI score was associated with lower odds of a subsequent observed assessment (odds ratio [OR], 0.952; 95% CI, 0.929-0.976; cluster-robust P < 0.001). The corresponding association for Cogstate was stronger (OR, 0.845; 95% CI, 0.819-0.872; P < 0.001), based on 102,479 intervals from 5,894 participants. Model-standardized probabilities of subsequent observation also differed according to time since the most recent Webstudy assessment activity, whether CFI or Cogstate. For CFI, the predicted probability decreased from 22.8% for a recency category of less than 90 days to 13.1% for 730 days or longer; the corresponding values for Cogstate were 34.3% and 2.6%. These findings indicate that subsequent assessment completion was associated with previously observed scores and assessment histories.

Interpretation of longitudinal score changes

Among participants who returned for repeated assessments, observed score changes were consistently more favorable than the corresponding contrasts derived from contemporaneous first-observed J-TRC assessments (Figure 4A; Supplementary Table S4A). By the 12th assessment occasion, the estimated change from the first assessment among matched returnees was +0.433 favorable baseline SD (95% CI, 0.381-0.484) for CFI and +1.140 SD (95% CI, 1.063-1.217) for Cogstate. The corresponding first-exposure reference contrasts were -0.058 SD (95% CI, -0.149 to 0.033) and +0.080 SD (95% CI, -0.029 to 0.189), yielding differences of +0.491 SD (95% CI, 0.385-0.596) and +1.060 SD (95% CI, 0.925-1.196), respectively. At this occasion, 931 CFI returnees (83.7% of eligible returnees) and 628 Cogstate returnees (67.8%) were successfully matched. Across the matched observations, the participant-equal first-exposure reference slopes were -0.033 favorable SD/year for CFI (95% rematching-bootstrap CI, -0.057 to -0.007) and +0.025 SD/year for Cogstate (95% CI, -0.005 to 0.054). Restricting the reference to participants whose first J-TRC assessment occurred within 90 days of registration yielded similar estimates of -0.038 SD/year (95% CI, -0.058 to -0.009) and +0.021 SD/year (95% CI, -0.004 to 0.058), respectively (Supplementary Figure S4; Supplementary Table S4B). These comparisons quantify differences between within-person returnee changes and matched first-exposure contrasts from different participants.

The estimated elapsed-time associations varied substantially depending on how repeated-assessment exposure was represented (Figure 4B; Supplementary Figure S3; Supplementary Table S3). In working-independence marginal models over the first 36 months after the first valid assessment, categorical adjustment for assessment number (1/2/3/4+) yielded annual coefficients of +0.080 favorable baseline SD/year for CFI (95% CI, 0.061-0.099) and +0.265 SD/year for Cogstate (95% CI, 0.245-0.285). With a post-first-assessment indicator and a spline of log assessment number, the corresponding coefficients were -0.227 SD/year (95% CI, -0.327 to -0.127) and -0.049 SD/year (95% CI, -0.153 to 0.055). Similar dependence on retest specification was observed with mixed-effects and participant fixed-effects models. For CFI, the categorical and spline-log specifications yielded coefficients of +0.078 versus -0.115 SD/year in mixed-effects models and +0.081 versus -0.094 SD/year in fixed-effects models. The corresponding Cogstate estimates were +0.248 versus -0.060 SD/year and +0.252 versus -0.071 SD/year, respectively. Among linear-time models, AIC favored more flexible retest representations. Across marginal models with linear or spline elapsed-time functions, cross-validated prediction errors differed little; the maximum excess root mean squared error was 0.125% for CFI and 0.377% for Cogstate. Thus, substantial variation in elapsed-time estimates was accompanied by only small differences in cross-validated prediction error among marginal models. Sensitivity analyses using time since registration or additionally adjusting for the delay from registration to the first valid assessment are reported in Supplementary Table S5.

Inverse-probability-of-observation weighting sensitivity analyses remained dependent on retest specification and weight truncation (Supplementary Figure S5; Supplementary Table S4C).

Estimated observation probabilities were below 0.01 for 49.9% of CFI intervals and 43.4% of Cogstate intervals, while effective sample sizes under the primary weighting scheme were only 13.3% and 26.8% of observed weighted assessments, respectively. Longitudinal data contribution was concentrated early: almost all eligible participants completed CFI initially, approximately half contributed on multiple days, and far fewer contributed after three or five years. These proportions are not conventional fixed-visit retention rates because assessments were voluntary and observation opportunity differed. Inactivity did not necessarily mean withdrawal: reactivation within two years after a 365-day gap occurred with 7.2% cumulative probability, sometimes followed by further assessments. Similar 24-month patterns across registration cohorts suggest that reduced longer-term contribution was not confined to early recruitment. Registries should distinguish activity, inactivity, and return, and report recent activity separately from longer-term contribution. Neither directly establishes readiness for an onsite study or trial.

The association between prior cognitive scores and subsequent observation has additional implications for the interpretation of longitudinal data. Participants with less favorable prior scores were less likely to complete subsequent assessments, particularly for Cogstate, while recent assessment activity was strongly associated with further observation. Consequently, participants contributing data at later assessment occasions represented a progressively selected subset of the original eligible cohort. The occasion-specific score distributions observed in this study therefore cannot be interpreted as longitudinal trajectories of the full registry population. Similar concerns have been raised in statistical research on informative observation processes, in which the probability of obtaining an outcome measurement is related to observed or unobserved participant characteristics Elapsed-time coefficients varied materially across retest specifications. This dependence persisted when CFI analyses were restricted to fully scorable responses or used prorated totals (Supplementary Table S1). With linear time, AIC favored more flexible retest models in marginal and mixed-effects analyses (mixed-effects AIC differences: 76.1 for CFI; 99.0 for Cogstate), but participant-clustered cross-validated RMSE was nearly unchanged. Categorical occasion adjustment gave favorable time coefficients, whereas flexible specifications yielded less favorable or negative estimates across marginal, mixed-effects, and fixed-effects models. Estimators also used different sources of variation and, for fixed effects, a restricted population (Supplementary Table S3). Time and cumulative assessment count were strongly correlated, limiting their empirical separation [Ferrer2004(PMID: 15222818); Hoffman2011(PMID: 21639642); Vivot2016(PMID: 26825924)].

The marked variation in elapsed-time estimates despite similar prediction errors highlights the importance of retest-model specification for interpreting longitudinal cognitive change.

The contemporaneous first-exposure comparisons provided an empirical reference for observed returnee changes. Constraining elapsed-time coefficients to the reference slopes had little effect on cross-validated prediction error (Supplementary Table S3). IPOW and alternative time-origin analyses further showed that elapsed-time estimates remained sensitive to retest specification, while low observation probabilities and reduced effective sample sizes limited weighted inference (Supplementary Figure S5; Supplementary Tables S4C and S5). Together, these findings identified retest-model specification as an important source of variation in estimated longitudinal trends.

These results have practical implications for trial-ready registries. Reports should separate enrollment, eligibility, recent activity, and multiyear data contribution using opportunity-adjusted denominators. Assessment dates, cumulative exposure, intervals, inactivity, and reactivation should be preserved for analysis. Future studies should test whether reminder timing and frequency improve long-term assessment participation. Longitudinal modeling should report sensitivity to plausible retest specifications, time origins, and estimators, rather than a single adjusted trajectory. Repeated online scores also require validation against independently measured cognitive or biomarker outcomes before use in natural-history estimation or risk enrichment.

In J-TRC, A4-derived models incorporating CFI and Cogstate scores have been used to This study has several strengths, including its large nationwide registry population, more than six years of observation, explicit accounting of registration and eligibility denominators, and repeated assessment data from both CFI and Cogstate. The availability of assessment dates and histories allowed us to evaluate long-term data contribution, inactivity, reactivation, score-associated subsequent observation, and sensitivity to retest-model specification within the same cohort. Several limitations should be considered. Participants were self-selected internet users with relatively high educational attainment, and eligibility was partly self-reported, limiting generalizability [Hall2015(PMID: 25156311); Ashford2020(PMID: 32478165)]. Cogstate assessment was optional, and experiences with unsupervised cognitive testing may differ across participants [Ashford2023(PMID: 37357303)]. Assessment timing was not randomized, the reasons for stopping or resuming assessments were unknown, and cognitive scores during unobserved periods were unavailable. Thus, we could not determine the direction or magnitude of bias related to selective observation. First-exposure reference contrasts compared different participants matched on a limited set of measured characteristics, leaving possible differences in unmeasured factors, calendar time, and the composition of new registrants. These contrasts do not isolate causal effects of repeated testing or individual cognitive aging trajectories. Fewer participants contributed data at later assessment occasions. Some models required complete baseline covariates or repeated observations, resulting in analytic samples that differed from the eligible cohort. Those excluded because of incomplete covariate information were more often women (Supplementary Table S1). IPOW analyses were additionally limited by very low estimated observation probabilities and small effective sample sizes. Finally, we did not directly validate longitudinal scores against independently adjudicated clinical progression or AD biomarkers. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the observed cognitive score patterns.

Conclusions

The J-TRC Webstudy achieved high initial assessment completion in a large nationwide cohort, while sustained data contribution became concentrated among a smaller group. Subsequent assessment was associated with prior scores and participation history, and returnee changes were substantially more favorable than contemporaneous first-exposure contrasts. Estimated elapsed-time coefficients differed markedly across retest specifications. These findings demonstrate the complementary importance of opportunity-adjusted data yield, assessment history, and retest-model sensitivity for evaluating web-based registries and interpreting longitudinal cognitive scores in AD research.

Introduction

Identifying individuals at risk of Alzheimer's disease (AD) and maintaining their participation over extended periods are important challenges for observational research and prevention trials [Grill2010(PMID: 21172069); Aisen2020(PMID: 32920621)]. Web-based registries offer opportunities to recruit large numbers of participants, collect cognitive and functional assessments remotely, and facilitate referral to in-person studies, potentially reducing geographical and logistical barriers [Weiner2018(PMID: 29754989); Walter2020(PMID: 32920623)]. Several web-based research initiatives, including the Brain Health Registry (BHR) [Weiner2018(PMID: 29754989)], Alzheimer Prevention Trials (APT) Webstudy [Walter2020(PMID: 32920623)], Healthy Brain Project (HBP) [Lim2019(PMID: 30909237)], and PROTECT study [Wesnes2017(PMID: 28128869)], have demonstrated the feasibility of large-scale recruitment and remote cognitive assessment. The Japanese Trial-Ready Cohort (J-TRC) Webstudy, launched in October 2019 as the web-based component of the J-TRC program and adapted from the APT Webstudy, was established to identify and longitudinally monitor potential participants for AD prevention trials in Japan [Iwatsubo2024(PMID: 38842037); Sato2021(PMID: 34292159)]. The J-TRC Webstudy combines online registration with repeated self-reported cognitive-functional assessments using the Cognitive Function Instrument (CFI) and optional computerized testing using the Cogstate Brief Battery [Sato2021(PMID: 33778148)]. The J-TRC program has used amyloid-risk estimates derived from models trained on A4 screening data and applied to Webstudy participants' demographic characteristics and available CFI and Cogstate scores to prioritize invitations to the onsite study [Sato2021(PMID: 33778148); Nakashima2026(PMID: 41536849)]. Thus, the interpretation of online cognitive assessments is relevant to both longitudinal monitoring and risk stratification within a trial-ready cohort. Beyond initial recruitment, the longitudinal value of trial-ready registries depends on sustained data contribution and the interpretation of repeated assessments. Sustaining participation over time remains challenging: the Brain Health Registry reported follow-up return rates of 41% at six months and 25% at 24 months [Weiner2018(PMID: 29754989)]. Subsequent research has examined participant characteristics associated with repeated assessment completion, including sociodemographic differences in online registry participation [Ashford2020(PMID: 32478165); Ashford2024(PMID: 38188606)]. However, return rates alone do not characterize irregular assessment intervals, prolonged inactivity followed by reactivation, or how these assessment histories relate to the scores that are observed. Evaluating participation and score interpretation together is therefore important when reassessment is participant-driven. Repeated assessments pose an additional challenge for interpreting cognitive and cognitive-functional scores. Repeated exposure can improve cognitive test performance [Stricker2020(PMID: 32010922); Murchland2026(PMID: 42224199)], and estimates of longitudinal cognitive change can vary depending on how retest exposure is modeled [Hoffman2011(PMID: 21639642); Vivot2016(PMID: 26825924); Chen2023(PMID: 37199336)]. At the same time, reduced practice effects have been investigated as potential markers of cognitive impairment and future decline [Jutten2020(PMID: 32671181)]. Changes in self-reported scores may also reflect changes in reporting behavior [Shrout2018(PMID: 29255039)]. In addition, participants with poorer cognitive performance may be less likely to complete subsequent assessments [Hernandez2024(PMID: 38453191)], potentially altering the composition of the observed sample. Together, retest effects and selective follow-up complicate the interpretation of observed score trajectories [Vivot2016(PMID: 26825924); Gasparini2020(PMID: 31894164); Weuve2015(PMID: 26397878)].

We included registrations through March 31, 2025, allowing at least 12 months of potential follow-up, and assessments through May 31, 2026. The University of Tokyo Hospital Institutional Review Board approved the Webstudy (2019132NI). All participants included in the eligible analytical cohort provided electronic informed consent. Initial recruitment activities included a press release at the Webstudy launch in October 2019, followed by newspaper and online media coverage. Additional recruitment efforts included search-engine listing and banner advertisements, paid newspaper advertisements, and email invitations to participants in an existing cognitive registry. The timing and short-term recruitment effects of these activities have been reported previously [Sato2021(PMID: 34292159)]. A further public announcement highlighting progress in the J-TRC program and encouraging participation in the Webstudy was issued in February 2022 [UTokyoAMED2022].

DiscussionUsing over six years of J-TRC Webstudy data, we found high initial assessment completion but limited multiyear contribution; frequent inactivity, occasional reactivation, and score-associated subsequent observation; and more favorable returnee changes than first-exposure contrasts. In marginal models, estimated elapsed-time coefficients varied markedly across retest specifications despite similar cross-validated prediction errors. Together, these findings show the importance of sustained participation, selective observation, and retest-model specification when interpreting longitudinal cognitive scores from web-based registries. The Brain Health Registry and Alzheimer Prevention Trials Webstudy have demonstrated large-scale remote recruitment [Weiner2018(PMID: 29754989); Walter2020(PMID: 32920623)], while other studies have examined unsupervised cognitive assessment [Banh2022(PMID: 35542999)]. Early J-TRC recruitment strategies and their short-term effects have been reported previously [Sato2021(PMID: 34292159)], as have Webstudy usability and questionnaire participation [Sato2024(PMID: 38706282); Nakashima2025(PMID: 40115874)]. Because assessment schedules and follow-up definitions differ, retention rates cannot be directly compared across registries. Our study extends this literature by characterizing opportunity-adjusted multiyear data contribution, inactivity and reactivation, score-associated subsequent observation, and the model dependence of repeated cognitive score interpretation.

Practice-related gains have been documented in longitudinal cognitive research [Salthouse2010(PMID: 20804244); Hoffman2011(PMID: 21639642); Chen2023(PMID: 37199336)]. Recent randomized evidence also showed that the number and frequency of previous Cogstate assessments influence subsequent performance [Murchland2026(PMID: 42224199)]. For the self-reported CFI, changes in symptom reporting [Shrout2018(PMID: 29255039)] and the handling of non-scorable items may also contribute. . Together, these findings highlight the importance of assessment exposure and changing participant composition when interpreting longitudinal online cognitive scores.
