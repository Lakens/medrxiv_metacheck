Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. Abstract

Background: Multi-site research networks have become central infrastructure for NIH-supported health research, yet network-level coordination has focused on harmonization, common data models and FAIR principles, while rarely characterizing the heterogeneous data landscapes within a network first. The Helping to End Addiction Long-term ® Initiative (HEAL Initiative) Data2Action (HD2A) program comprises ten autonomously designed innovation and acceleration projects in opioid use disorder treatment and pain management, with no pre-existing networklevel view of what data each project used or how it was governed.

Objectives: To make the HD2A data-infrastructure landscape visible by systematically characterizing the data environments of all ten projects and identifying cross-project themes, coordination opportunities, and lessons for multi-site networks.

Methods: Data Infrastructure Support Center staff extracted each project's data-environment characteristics from project documentation into a structured 15-element assessment, which project investigators reviewed and corrected. Two analysts independently coded the ten validated assessments (two-pass inductive content analysis; 19-code codebook), developed six crossproject themes from the coded passages by consensus, and judged each project against them.

An investigator reflection survey, summarized descriptively, contextualized the document-based findings.

[div-01]

Results: Six themes characterized the landscape: all projects integrate multi-source, cross-sector data; dashboards are the common data-to-action venue; adoption of standards-based interoperability is uneven; governance complexity increases nonlinearly with institutional partners; implementation and improvement science frameworks serve as data-quality scaffolding; and sustainability plans depend on institutional transfer. Five synergy clusters were identified.

Investigators consistently described data access and governance, not analytic capability, as the binding constraint, and distinguished sustaining technical products from sustaining their use.

Making a network's data infrastructure visible is not a substitute for harmonization but a prerequisite for deciding how and what to harmonize. HD2A's autonomously designed projects share a common structure of constraints, creating opportunities for coordination without uniformity. The structured elicitation approach is replicable in other innovation networks.

INTRODUCTION

Over the past two decades, multi-site research networks have become a central infrastructure for NIH-supported health research, including programs such as PCORnet, the CTSA consortium, the NIH Helping to End Addiction Long-term® Initiative (HEAL Initiative), and disease-specific clinical trial networks. [1] [2] [3] These programs bring together investigator teams spanning institutional, geographic, and disciplinary boundaries, each working with data environments shaped by local infrastructure, institutional partnerships, and regulatory contexts. 4, 5 The practical promise of these networks, cross-site learning, shared resources, and coordinated evidence generation, depends on their ability to make sense of the diverse data environments they encompass. [6] [7] [8] However, the dominant model for multi-site data coordination has focused primarily on harmonization: adopting common data models, building centralized platforms, and applying FAIR principles to make data findable, accessible, interoperable, and reusable. 9, 10 Harmonization approaches have generated substantial value, but they presuppose a step that is rarely undertaken: systematically characterizing the heterogeneous data landscapes within a network before determining how and what to coordinate. 11, 12 When a network includes projects drawing on electronic health records, prescription drug monitoring programs, criminal justice databases, community surveillance systems, patient-reported outcomes, and qualitative stakeholder data, each source operates under distinct governance regimes, refresh cycles, and quality standards. In this context, the question of what data exist across the network, how they are governed, and where projects converge or diverge in their data infrastructure is not answered by a common data model alone. 13, 14 Instead, it requires a different kind of work: understanding and making the network's data infrastructure visible to the network itself.

The concept of "invisible infrastructure" has a well-established lineage in science and technology studies. Bowker and Star introduced "infrastructural inversion" as a method for foregrounding infrastructure that, by design and habit, fades into the background when it is functioning well. 15, 16 In research networks, data environments are invisible in precisely this sense: each project's data infrastructure works well enough for that project's purposes, but the network lacks a mechanism for viewing the collective landscape, including where projects face similar challenges, where they have made divergent choices, and where shared investment could reduce duplicated effort. 17, 18 This invisibility is not a failure but a structural feature of networks designed to support locally appropriate data innovation. However, it limits what the network can learn about and from itself.

The NIH HEAL Initiative's Data2Action (HD2A) program illustrates this challenge. Funded by the National Institute on Drug Abuse beginning in 2022, HD2A comprises ten innovation and acceleration projects using real-world data to improve opioid use disorder treatment and pain management across clinical, public health, and community settings, supported by three resource centers focused on implementation science, data infrastructure, and health economics. The programmatic structure and support center architecture are described elsewhere, and a qualitative formative evaluation has been published. 19 HD2A projects were designed with considerable autonomy in selecting their data sources, analytic approaches, and implementation frameworks, a design reflecting the program's emphasis on locally relevant, stakeholder-driven data innovation. This autonomy meant that no mechanism existed at the network level to characterize what data each project was working with, how those data were governed, or what patterns of convergence or challenge were operating across the portfolio.

This paper reports what became visible when the Data Infrastructure Support Center (DISC), one of three HD2A resource centers, led the network in systematically examining its own data landscape. Using a three-phase qualitative study, structured data extraction from project documentation, two-pass qualitative content analysis producing a formal codebook, and an investigator reflection survey capturing experiential knowledge, we characterize the data infrastructure landscape of HD2A and identify cross-project themes, coordination opportunities, and lessons for multi-site networks. The contribution is not a new harmonization framework or common data model; instead, it provides empirical evidence about what a national health innovation network looks like when its data infrastructure is made legible, and a replicable approach for other networks seeking to move from unexamined heterogeneity to actionable intelligence about their own data environments.

METHODS

Setting and Study Design

We conducted a three-phase, multi-method qualitative descriptive study to characterize the datainfrastructure landscape of the HD2A network. Phase 1 was a structured extraction of dataenvironment characteristics from each project's documentation using a structured assessment instrument, validated by each project's investigators. Phase 2 was a two-pass qualitative content analysis of the ten validated assessments, yielding a 19-code codebook, six cross-project themes developed from the coded passages, and a theme-by-project mapping. Phase 3 was an investigator reflection survey, fielded after extraction was complete, that captured experiential knowledge the documentation did not surface and was used to contextualize, not to generate or revise, the themes. The study is reported per the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR). 20 We took a pragmatic stance and used a qualitative descriptive design because the study's purpose was to summarize each project's data environment in terms close to the source documentation and in a form the network could act on, not to develop theory or interpret lived experience. 21, 22 We chose conventional (inductive) content analysis for the same reason: prior work offers little guidance on characterizing data infrastructure across a multi-project research network, so codes were developed through close reading of the project assessments and consensus discussion between analysts, not imposed in advance. 23

Ethics Statement

The Wake Forest University Health Sciences Institutional Review Board reviewed this study and determined it to be exempt (Protocol #IRB00146435). No patients took part, and no patient-level data were collected or analyzed. Phases 1 and 2 used project documentation and the structured assessments derived from it. For Phase 3, investigators and staff of the ten HD2A projects received an invitation that described the survey and explained that participation was voluntary; completing the REDCap survey was taken as consent to participate. Because the study was determined to be exempt, signed consent forms were not required and were not collected. The survey asked about project-level data infrastructure and did not request personal or sensitive information. Responses were stored in REDCap on secure institutional servers accessible only to the study team and are reported in aggregate, without attribution to individual respondents.

Research Team and Reflexivity

The study was designed and carried out by the DISC, whose funded role is to support the data infrastructure of the ten projects examined here. Both analysts (MCBA, [PI, pain/addiction informatics]; RWH, [Co-I, pain/addiction informatics]) are DISC members with established working relationships with the project teams; they were embedded in the network, not independent of it. This position gave the analysts access to project documentation and a contextual understanding of each project's data environment. It also carried two risks: that the analysts' support role and familiarity with the projects would shape what was extracted and how it was interpreted, and that teams might describe their data environments favorably to a center that supports them. We addressed these risks in several ways. Each project's principal investigator and data team reviewed and corrected their own assessment before analysis, so the analyzed assessments reflect project-verified content and not DISC's reading alone. Both analysts coded all ten assessments independently before comparing results. Every code and theme judgment was anchored in assessment text, discrepancies were resolved by consensus with a third DISC team member (CC) available to adjudicate, and decisions were logged in an audit trail. Findings are framed as coordination opportunities, not as evaluations of individual projects. Two authors (RAT, JAH) are investigators on projects included in the analysis. They did not extract data, code, or derive themes; like all project investigators, they reviewed only their own project's assessment for accuracy, and they contributed to interpretation of the findings and revision of the manuscript. The data for Phase 2 comprised the ten PI-validated structured project assessments, one per project. The grant applications, data-management plans, and related materials from which the assessments were extracted were not themselves coded; project documentation also supplied descriptive context for the Results (for example, the size of a health system or the agencies with which a project coordinates), but this material was not coded. The unit of coding was the assessment entry: each phrase or sentence the extraction team recorded in response to an instrument item. Entries were double coded when they met the inclusion criteria for more than one code, with all code assignments agreed by consensus.

Qualitative Content Analysis

The assessments were analyzed using a two-pass, inductive (conventional) content analysis. 23 The two DISC analysts independently coded all ten assessments. In the first pass, preliminary codes were derived inductively from the entries, each assigned a brief name, a definition grounded in the assessment text, and one to three exemplar entries quoted verbatim from the assessments; no codes were imposed a priori. In the second pass, the preliminary codes were refined into a formal codebook with explicit definitions, evaluating each code for redundancy, breadth, and operational clarity. Overlapping codes were merged (e.g., NLP and interoperability standards into a single technical data-processing code), overly broad codes were split (e.g., quality measurement into external standards compliance and internal performance benchmarking), and vague definitions were operationalized with inclusion and exclusion criteria.

The final codebook comprises 19 codes organized in four clusters: Infrastructure and Technical Capacity, Governance and Partnership, Measurement and Quality, and Stakeholder Engagement and Capacity, and is provided in S1 Appendix.

The two analysts then independently applied the final codebook to every entry in all ten assessments and resolved all discrepancies by discussion to consensus, with a third DISC team member (CC) adjudicating unresolved cases. Rigor was supported by independent dual coding, anchoring of every code in verbatim text, explicit inclusion/exclusion criteria, and a decision audit trail. Consensus code assignments were tabulated in a code-by-project matrix recording which codes were applied to each project and the entries that supported them.

Derivation of cross-project themes

After coding was complete, the two analysts read the coded passages across all ten projects together and, by consensus, developed six themes, each an interpretive statement about a pattern shared across projects. A theme could draw on passages from more than one code, and codes whose passages did not converge into a cross-project pattern relevant to the study objective remained in the codebook but were not reported as themes. For each theme the analysts wrote an operational definition stating what a project's assessment had to contain for the theme to be considered present. Table 1 gives each theme's definition and contributing codes.

The two analysts then jointly judged each project as present or absent on each theme by reading that project's entries coded to the contributing codes against the theme definition. A theme was marked present when at least one coded entry met the definition, an analyst judgment anchored in coded text rather than an automatic roll-up of code occurrence. For example, Code 5 covers both natural language processing and interoperability standards, but Theme 3 was marked present only for projects whose entries named a national standard or a standards-based architecture, so a project whose only Code 5 entries concerned natural language processing was marked absent. In the resulting theme-by-project mapping (Table 2), a blank cell indicates that the validated assessment contained no entry meeting the definition; it does not establish that the project lacks the corresponding feature.

Phase 3: Investigator Reflection Survey

After extraction was complete, we surveyed all project teams to capture tacit knowledge about data work that project documentation does not record; all ten projects participated, each submitting one response. The survey was fielded in April 2026 via REDCap and comprised 26 primarily open-ended items spanning the project's most significant data challenge; governance, sharing, and access constraints; data-quality validation; sustainability (distinguishing technical sustainment from continued use); and advice for incoming projects. The summarized results and the survey items were then discussed with project investigators at an in-person network meeting in April 2026, which served as member checking of the summary. The full instrument is provided in S3 Appendix.

Survey responses were analyzed separately from the project assessments. They were not coded with the 19-code codebook and were not used to develop or revise the codebook, themes, or theme-by-project mapping. Instead, one DISC team member (CC) summarized the responses item by item, grouping similar responses and counting the number of projects that raised each issue, with the project as the unit of analysis (n = 10). The survey served to contextualize and triangulate the document-based findings: to examine whether patterns identified in the assessments were consistent with investigators' experience, and to surface experiential knowledge that project documentation did not record.

RESULTS

Analysis of the ten project assessments identified six themes describing how projects acquire, integrate, govern, and sustain their data environments (Table 1). Themes were not equally present across projects (Table 2). The following sections synthesize each theme, using exemplar projects to illustrate convergence and variation within the portfolio.

Theme 1: All Ten Projects Integrate Multi-Source, Cross-Sector Data

All ten projects (identified hereafter by principal investigator surnames) integrate data from multiple, fundamentally distinct data sources, making cross linkage the defining feature of the HD2A data landscape (Codebook Codes 1, 17). This is more than simple data aggregation; projects perform complex record linkage across systems with different governance regimes, refresh cycles, and quality standards. Aalsma/Ray/Reda link criminal legal records, emergency department (ED) data, emergency medical services (EMS) interactions, and prescription dispensation across Indiana's population, refreshing weekly and monthly. Hoppe integrates prescription drug monitoring program (PDMP), electronic health record (EHR), and mortality data across a 12-hospital system with over 3,000 providers. Sun links a 50-institution surgical registry with EHR and Medicare claims. At the community level, Banta-Green and Hood integrate EMS medical incident reports with a county-level data hub spanning jail, substance use treatment, housing, and Medicaid utilization.

Projects diverge in how they manage this integration. Several adopt standards-based identifiers and architectures (Hoppe, Karnik), while others rely on project-specific matching algorithms or manual validation. This variation likely reflects differences in institutional data maturity and the nature of data sources; clinical systems are more likely to support interoperability standards than community surveillance or criminal justice systems.

Theme 2: Dashboards Are the Common Data-to-Action Venue for Translating Data to

Diverse Audiences

Eight of ten projects identified dashboards as a primary mechanism for translating data into actionable outputs (Codebook Code 2). Aalsma/Ray/Reda's Overdose Touchpoints Dashboard provides real-time population health data to fatality review teams. Banta-Green and Hood use EMS and mortality dashboards with heat maps for stakeholders. Venkatesh is developing an ED opioid use disorder quality dashboard within the American College of Emergency Physicians (ACEP) registry. Neighbors provides performance feedback dashboards stratified by race, ethnicity, and gender. Marshall/Wright's CADENCE project centers on an interactive maternalinfant outcomes dashboard. 26 Cruden was an informative exception: because existing dashboards in its setting made new dashboards problematic, the project is developing data products other than dashboards.

Dashboard audiences varied: dashboards serve county-level overdose fatality review (OFR) teams of local public health, legal, and treatment providers and people with lived experience (Aalsma/Ray/Reda), clinical quality improvement teams (Venkatesh), organizational leadership (Neighbors), and community stakeholders (Heckmann). This diversity of audiences, combined with the consistency of the dashboard form, points to dashboards as a dominant translational technology in applied health data innovation.

Theme 3: Adoption of Standards-Based Interoperability Is Uneven

Four projects reported explicit adoption of national data standards (Codebook Codes 5, 6), though the degree of standards-based infrastructure varied substantially. Hoppe implements SMART on FHIR (Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources) and clinical decision support (CDS) Hooks. 27 Karnik uses Health Level 28 (HL7) and FHIR for unifying screening data. Venkatesh utilizes ACEP data system ontologies (RxNorm, LOINC, SNOMED) and application programming interfaces. Sun develops computable phenotypes using standard ontologies. The remaining projects describe data integration approaches relying on project-specific linkage methods, manual validation, or institution-specific dictionaries without reference to national standards. This divergence emerged most clearly through cross-project comparison and likely reflects the projects' starting positions relative to existing infrastructure rather than differences in analytic sophistication. Projects embedded in health systems inherit standards-based architectures; those working within EHR environments had readier access to FHIR-compatible systems, whereas community-based or multi-agency projects working with administrative data from criminal justice or public health surveillance systems must construct interoperability from heterogeneous sources. interactive process mapping. 28 The remaining two drew on improvement science (Banta-Green and Hood, continuous process improvement) or systems science (Heckmann, system dynamics modeling). Quality

Investigator Reflection Survey

All ten projects responded to the reflection survey, with one response per project. Findings are reported in aggregate with the project as the unit (n = 10) and without attribution to individual projects.

Data challenges and governance. 26 of ten projects named cross-system integration as their most significant data challenge, and five named governance, sharing agreements, or access permissions explicitly; the two were typically described together rather than in isolation.

Investigators described several downstream consequences of constrained access: narrowed analytic scope when key linkages proved infeasible, dashboards displayed without key demographic breakdowns, county-level suppression that obscured local signal, and lengthened iteration cycles.

Data-quality validation.

Projects with raw-data access described concrete validation procedures, including exploratory data analysis with outlier detection, inter-annotator agreement for annotated data, and Sankey diagrams of cross-system linkage. Projects without raw-data access stated that their validation capacity was bounded by what governance permits.

Sustainability.

Several projects reported high confidence that their dashboards, pipelines, and data processes could survive the end of the award provided institutional or governmental partners absorb them, and described sustaining mechanisms already in place, including state government backing, multi-million-dollar institutional resourcing, and alignment with state-level opioid response governance. One project drew the distinction between sustaining technical products and sustaining their use explicitly: it had no concern about sustaining the technical artifacts themselves, only about whether community stakeholders would continue to use them. Projects named user training, dashboard benchmarking, and continued engagement of community partners as the activities most at risk of erosion after the grant period, and at least two reported being expected to sustain their products without the resources to do so.

Advice for incoming projects.

When asked what advice they would offer an incoming project, five of ten projects independently named some version of planning for governance friction before planning for analysis.

DISCUSSION

What the HD2A Data Landscape Reveals

By systematically characterizing the data environments of all ten HD2A innovation and acceleration projects and surfacing the cross-project themes that organize them (Table 1), we produced a portrait of a network whose projects share deep structural similarities in their data challenges despite having been designed with deliberate autonomy. Five synergy clusters showed where coordination could create value across projects without requiring uniformity. A complementary investigator reflection survey, conducted with HD2A teams after the structured assessment was complete, sharpened the interpretation of several of these themes and added findings that the assessment instrument alone did not surface. Considered together, the document analysis and the reflection survey reveal not only what a national health innovation network looks like when its data infrastructure is made legible, but where the network's most consequential constraints actually lie. Consistent with accounts of infrastructure as becoming visible chiefly at points of breakdown, 15, 16 these constraints surfaced where project data work met governance and access friction.

The Binding Constraint Is Governance, Not Analytics

The most consistent message from investigators, expressed across multiple reflection-survey items, is that data access and governance, not analytic capability, is the network's binding constraint. Integration and governance challenges were typically described together, validation capacity was bounded by what governance permits for projects without raw-data access, and five of ten projects advised incoming teams to plan for governance friction before analysis. This pattern mirrors reports from cross-sectoral linkage efforts in which legal, governance, and access barriers, rather than technical capacity, limit routine data linkage, 13, 14 and it exposes a gap in data-quality frameworks that presume analysts can inspect the underlying records. 11, 12 This finding reframes how the network's heterogeneity should be understood. The variation in interoperability adoption and in governance complexity is not principally a difference in technical sophistication; it reflects the context of the data systems from which projects begin. Health-system projects working within EHR environments inherit FHIR-compatible infrastructure and withininstitution governance pathways; community-based and multi-agency projects working with administrative data from criminal justice, public health surveillance, EMS, or Medicaid systems must construct linkage architectures while simultaneously negotiating sharing agreements across organizations with conflicting legal frameworks. The downstream consequences investigators described are governance artifacts more than analytic ones. A key finding was that no amount of additional analytic capacity solves a governance bottleneck, and network-level investment in linkage methods alone will under-deliver if it is not paired with investment in the legal, regulatory, and relational scaffolding that determines what can be linked at all.

Sustainability Is Not One Problem but Two

A second finding that emerged with greater clarity in the reflection survey than in the document review is that sustainability in HD2A is split between technical sustainment and use sustainment, and the human side is the weaker of the two. Several sites reported high confidence that their dashboards, pipelines, and data processes can survive the end of the award provided institutional or governmental partners absorb them, yet the concern investigators raised was whether community stakeholders would continue to use them. This distinction is consequential. The network-wide convergence on "institutional transfer" sustainability strategies presumes that the receiving institution has the technical capacity, staffing, and organizational incentive to maintain the product after handoff. The reflection survey suggests that an additional, underexamined condition must also hold, that the intended users continue to find the product worth using as practice and policy evolve.

Framed this way, sustainability planning requires two distinct strategies. Technical sustainment depends on integration with institutional or governmental data infrastructure, leadership-level resource allocation, and operational alignment with a clinical or public health workflow, mechanisms that several sites described as concretely in place, including state government backing, multi-million-dollar institutional resourcing, and alignment with state-level opioid response governance. Use sustainment depends on whether the audiences for whom the data product was built (clinicians, public health officials, community stakeholders, EMS responders) remain embedded in feedback loops that keep the product useful over time. Sites named user training, dashboard benchmarking, and continued engagement of community partners as the activities most at risk of erosion after the grant period. The implication for network design is that sustainability support should be conceptualized along both axes: technical maintenance templates and institutional partnership agreements address the first, but the second requires durable investment in stakeholder engagement, training continuity, and the measurement of actual use.

Implications for Innovation Networks

Several implications for network design emerge from these findings. First, network-level data characterization should be initiated at the outset of a network's operations, not retrospectively.

Early characterization allows the network to identify coordination opportunities while projects are still developing their infrastructure, rather than after approaches have solidified.

Second, the convergence on dashboards across HD2A suggests that network data infrastructure should be conceptualized as an interpretation system as well as a pipeline, with investment in how data reaches the people who act on it. Dashboard design, user testing, and audience segmentation may be as important to network data infrastructure as data standards or governance. The convergence may also reflect its timing: many projects were proposed when COVID-19 dashboards were ubiquitous, and funder and reviewer expectations may have favored the form, much as current expectations favor integrating artificial intelligence. Networks should consider how such top-down expectations shape the infrastructure projects adopt.

Third, the governance complexity theme suggests that networks should differentiate their governance support based on the number and type of institutional partners a project engages, rather than providing uniform guidance. A project linking data across four state agencies faces qualitatively different governance challenges than one working within a single health system, and network support should reflect that difference.

Fourth, sustainability support should address both technical and use sustainment as distinct problems; institutional transfer agreements solve the first but not the second.

Fifth, funder mechanisms should accommodate the adaptive character of two-stage transitions such as NIH R61 to R33 grants, allowing scope and approach to evolve in response to implementation learning through instruments such as protocol amendments, administrative supplements, exploratory pre-implementation aims, and implementation-science-focused opportunities. The rapid evolution of artificial intelligence since funding began has the potential to advance projects' analytic and technical capabilities, even as the underlying governance constraints remain unchanged.

LIMITATIONS

Nature of the data. The analysis did not include independent observation, primary qualitative data collection (e.g., interviews), or audit of data systems. Because Phase 2 coded the assessments and not the underlying source documents, the coded text reflects the extractors' condensation of those documents, and the exemplar quotations in S1 Appendix are assessment entries, not investigators' own words. This limits the depth of evidence for some themes and means that the characterization reflects how teams describe their data environments, which may differ from operational reality. Because all ten projects were included, the sample was a census of the network rather than one sampled to thematic saturation.

Analyst position.

The analysis was conducted by a resource center embedded in the network it studied. Investigator validation of each assessment, independent dual coding, and consensus procedures reduce, but do not eliminate, the possibility that the analysts' support role shaped extraction and interpretation.

Network-specific context. This study was conducted within a single network focused on opioid use disorder and pain management. Applicability to other networks, disease areas, or data contexts requires further evaluation.

Method as infrastructure.

The structured characterization approach used in this study is itself a form of data infrastructure, with its own design choices and limitations. The assessment's section structure inevitably shaped what became visible; themes that fall outside those sections may have been underrepresented. In particular, the assessment evolved to incorporate IS constructs during the data collection period, and elements added later (such as the CFIR-aligned inner/outer setting distinction) may not have been captured consistently across all projects.

CONCLUSIONS

Making a network's data infrastructure visible is not a substitute for harmonization, but a prerequisite for deciding what to harmonize. Although HD2A's data landscape was intentionally designed to be heterogeneous, its projects nonetheless share a common structure of constraints, governance as the binding limitation, dashboards as the primary translational interface, and institutional transfer as the dominant sustainability pathway. This shared structure creates concrete opportunities for coordination without requiring uniformity. The methods used here are replicable in other innovation networks. The findings suggest that what such networks most need from their support infrastructure is not better tools alone, but better visibility into the often-invisible conditions under which their tools must work.
