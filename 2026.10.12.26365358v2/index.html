To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 16 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Effect Sizes in t-tests and F-tests works

The Effect Size module checks if effect sizes are correctly reported in t-tests and F-tests.

The Effect Size check searches for regular expressions that match typical ways in which effect sizes are reported. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

The hearts of patients with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) are exposed to chronic wall stress and subclinical myocardial injury, yet conventional biomarkers such as cardiac troponin lack specificity for cardiomyocyte death. Here we evaluate whether cardiomyocyte-derived cellfree DNA (cfDNA-CM), quantified by whole-genome bisulfite sequencing and methylationbased cell-type deconvolution, can be measured in clinically stable HCM. We analyze 57 plasma samples from patients with HCM and 10 from patients with acute myocardial infarction as positive controls. Control samples show higher total cfDNA yield and cfDNA-CM fractions than HCM samples, supporting assay validity. In HCM, cfDNA-CM ranges from 0% to 6.4% of total cfDNA; higher cfDNA-CM is associated with higher body mass index, obesity and obstructive sleep apnea, but not with NT-proBNP or high-sensitivity troponin I. Quantifying cfDNA-CM in stable HCM is feasible, and its association with cardiometabolic burden independent of traditional biomarkers suggests that cfDNA-CM reflects a distinct domain of myocardial injury and remodeling.

Introduction

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) affects approximately 1 in 200 individuals, making it the most common inherited cardiomyopathy 1 . This autosomal dominant disease, frequently caused by mutations of sarcomeric genes, most commonly myosin heavy chain and myosin binding protein C3, carries a substantial lifelong disease burden. Up to 62% of individuals diagnosed before age 40 develop atrial fibrillation, and nearly half experience symptomatic heart failure by age 60 2, 3.

Although HCM exhibits a heterogeneous clinical phenotype, its underlying pathophysiology is driven largely by pathological hypercontractility and impaired myocardial relaxation, leading to hypertrophy and adverse ventricular remodeling. These chronic changes lead to progressive wall stress and myocyte injury, which may be reflected by elevated levels of N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) and cardiac troponin (cTn) 2 . cTn is considered the gold standard biomarker of myocyte injury and death, and represents an important diagnostic and prognostic tool 4 . However, it is chronically elevated in more than 50% of patients with HCM 5.

The underlying pathophysiology that results in this persistent cTn elevation remains unclear, but may result from pressure overload, hypercontractility, or myocyte stretch rather than overt myocardial necrosis. Additionally, the complex biology of cTn is best illustrated by its increase in healthy individuals following intense physical activity, such as marathon running or prolonged cycling [6] [7] [8].

Emerging data suggest that cTn release may occur through mechanisms beyond cardiomyocyte necrosis or apoptosis, including normal cell turnover, release of proteolytically cleaved troponin fragments, increased membrane permeability, and secretion via extracellular vesicles [9] [10] [11] . These complexities highlight the need for more specific biomarkers capable of distinguishing true cardiomyocyte injury from adaptive or non-cytotoxic processes in HCM.

Cell-free DNA (cfDNA), comprising extracellular DNA fragments circulating in bodily fluids such as plasma, urine, and cerebrospinal fluid, has emerged as a highly sensitive biomarker of cellular injury and death. Indeed, liquid biopsies using cfDNA have already established robust clinical utility in oncology detection, non-invasive prenatal testing, and solid-organ transplant rejection surveillance [12] [13] [14] . While total cfDNA levels fluctuate in cardiovascular conditions like hypertension and acute myocardial infarction (AMI), these conventional assays lack tissue specificity 15, 16 . Because they measure total cfDNA, prior cardiovascular studies have been unable to differentiate the exact tissue of origin. Overcoming this limitation requires leveraging tissue-specific epigenetic marks, such as DNA methylation (the addition of a methyl group to cytosine residues, predominantly in CpG dinucleotides), to deconvolve and quantify the cardiomyocyte-specific cfDNA fraction.

Recent advances in next-generation sequencing have enabled genome-wide, high-resolution detection of DNA methylation across diverse sample types. The most widely adopted approach is whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS), in which unmethylated cytosines are converted to uracil (read as thymine) by bisulfite treatment, while methylated cytosines remain protected.

Because DNA methylation patterns are tissue-specific 17 , they can serve as molecular fingerprints to determine tissue of origin through comparison with established reference atlases 18 . Applied to cfDNA, WGBS therefore enables the assignment of circulating DNA fragments to their tissue of origin. This approach has been applied in diseases such as pulmonary hypertension and AMI 19, 20 , but to the best of our knowledge, such an approach has not been employed to quantify cardiomyocyte-specific cfDNA in cardiomyopathies such as HCM.

In this study, we evaluated the feasibility of quantifying cardiomyocyte-specific cfDNA (cfDNA-CM) in patients with HCM and examined its association with traditional markers of disease severity. We hypothesized that circulating cfDNA-CM patterns would be distinct from those of conventional cardiac biomarkers (NT-proBNP and cTn) that are likely more reflective of wall stress and hypercontractility.

Results

Baseline characteristics of HCM and AMI patients

A total of 72 patients were enrolled in this study, including 62 patients with HCM and 10 with AMI (5 NSTEMI, 5 STEMI); 54 patients with HCM and all 10 patients with AMI were included in the final analysis. Three of the HCM patients contributed 2 samples each (57 HCM samples in total), separated in time by at least 1 year, and met stable enrollment criteria at both points of sampling. For the HCM cohort the mean age at time of sampling was 46.4 ± 16.6 years and 37 (64.9%) of the samples were from male patients. The median duration since HCM diagnosis was 5 years (IQR 2-14). A pathogenic variant was identified in 24 (42.1%) samples. A total of 42 (73.7%) samples were from patients who reported symptoms at the point of sampling, and 26 (45.6%) were associated with left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction. Late gadolinium enhancement was present in 35 (61.4%) samples. The cohort had typical comorbidities seen in patients with HCM (Table 1). Median NT-proBNP was 396 pg/ml (IQR 169-825), with 35 (61.4%) having ≥300 pg/ml when sampled. Median troponin was 20 ng/L (IQR 7-118), and 32 (56.1%) had elevated values >10 ng/L when sampled. Baseline clinical, imaging, and biomarker characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

In comparison, the AMI cohort was older (70.8 ± 6.8 years), predominantly male (80.0%), and had a high prevalence of diabetes mellitus (70.0%), hypertension (70.0%), and obesity (70.0%).

Mean left ventricular ejection fraction was 47.0 ± 12.5% in patients with NSTEMI and 41.0 ± 9.6% in patients with STEMI. Median troponin at collection was 5,926 ng/L (IQR 5,388-10,722) in NSTEMI and 49,200 ng/L (IQR 31,144-71,993) in STEMI. The AMI cohort served as a positive-control validation of the assay rather than a between-disease comparison. Baseline clinical and biomarker characteristics of both cohorts are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

cfDNA isolation and tissue deconvolution cfDNA was isolated from all 67 plasma samples included in the final analysis and subsequently underwent WGBS and methylation-based cell-type deconvolution using a curated subset of 14 cell types from the 39-cell methylation atlas. Median total cfDNA yield was 12.22 ng (IQR 7.77-18.94) in patients with HCM and 37.29 ng (IQR 22.96-64.80) in patients with AMI.

Across the study population, circulating cfDNA was predominantly derived from hematopoietic cells, with granulocytes and monocytes/macrophages representing the largest contributors.

Among samples from HCM patients, the predominant contributors were granulocytes (median 46.38%, IQR 38.85-55.47%), monocytes/macrophages (23.43%, IQR 17.09-30.05%), and endothelial cells (8.57%, IQR 4.59-12.71%). Median cardiomyocyte-derived and cardiac fibroblast-derived cfDNA fractions were 0.50% (IQR 0.00-1.52%) and 0.35% (IQR 0.00-2.94%), respectively. Complete tissue deconvolution results are shown in Figs. 1 and 2.

Biological and analytical validation of the methylation atlas

To assess whether the deconvolution approach detected the expected increase in cardiac-derived cfDNA during acute myocardial injury, cfDNA measures were compared across patients with HCM, NSTEMI, and STEMI (Table 2). Total cfDNA yield differed significantly across groups (P = 0.003), with median yields of 12.22 ng (IQR 7.77-18.94), 25.50 ng (IQR 22.96-64.80), and 39.48 ng (IQR 35.10-48.00) in HCM, NSTEMI, and STEMI, respectively. Cardiomyocytederived cfDNA fraction also differed significantly across groups (P = 0.002), with the highest fractions observed in patients with STEMI. Total heart-derived cfDNA fraction similarly differed across groups (P = 0.013), whereas cardiac fibroblast-derived cfDNA fraction did not (P = 0.775), underscoring that the group differences in total heart-derived cfDNA fraction were driven entirely by differing cardiomyocyte content. These findings supported the ability of the methylation-based deconvolution approach to detect cardiac-derived cfDNA in the setting of acute myocardial injury.

In independent purified-tissue datasets, the methylation atlas correctly identified blood, heart, and liver as the predominant tissue sources in their respective reference samples, with mean recovered fractions of 94.3%, 68.2%, and 65.5%, respectively. In the in-silico mixture experiments, deconvolution consistently identified blood as the predominant tissue source.

Estimated heart and liver fractions showed variable agreement with the input proportions, and a portion of each mixture was assigned to the "other" category. Low-level cardiomyocyte signal was observed in nominally pure blood and liver samples, consistent with background classification noise and shared methylation features across tissues. These findings highlighted uncertainty in low-abundance cardiac cfDNA estimates and informed their interpretation in the clinical cohorts (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2).

Cross-method agreement

To determine whether cell-type proportion estimates were method-dependent, we compared UXM's estimated cell type proportions with those from CelFiE-ISH across 50 HCM samples analyzed in a single batch with complete deconvolution results (Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4). Agreement was highest for hepatocytes (CCC 0.87, Pearson's r 0.88), with minimal bias (mean difference 0.009). Among the abundant blood cell types, agreement was highest for granulocytes (CCC 0.76, Pearson's r 0.82) and T cells (CCC 0.71, Pearson's r 0.79), followed by monocytes/macrophages (CCC 0.53, Pearson's r 0.64). Mean differences for these three cell types were minimal (-0.015 to 0.009). Cardiomyocytes showed moderate concordance (CCC 0.50, Pearson's r 0.50) with minimal bias (mean difference 0.002); however, limits of agreement (-3.4% to 3.8%) were wide relative to the mean estimated cardiomyocyte fractions (1.3-1.4%). Endothelial fraction estimates were consistently higher with CelFiE-ISH (mean difference 0.046; CCC 0.36, Pearson's r 0.53), reflecting a systematic offset for this cell type with limits of agreement ranging from -7.7% to 17%. Agreement was poor for low abundance cell types (B cells, NK cells, alveolar and bronchial lung, cardiac fibroblasts, skeletal muscle, and smooth muscle) where all had CCC less than 0.15 and estimated fractions were close to zero in most samples. For skeletal muscle, smooth muscle, and cardiac fibroblasts, UXM generally estimated higher fractions than CelFiE-ISH. These findings indicate that while both methods produce broadly concordant estimates for abundant cell types, estimates for lowerabundance populations are method-dependent. UXM was used for the primary analyses because its performance had been independently evaluated using pure-tissue reference samples and simulated tissue mixtures.

Total cfDNA yield and clinical associations

Patient samples were stratified into tertiles of total cfDNA yield (n = 19 per group). Despite substantial differences in median total cfDNA yield (6.59 ng vs 12.22 ng vs 24.15 ng; P < 0.001), cardiac-specific cfDNA fractions remained uniformly low and did not differ significantly across tertiles for any of the cardiomyocyte-derived, cardiac fibroblast-derived, or total heartderived cfDNA fractions (all P > 0.1; Table 3, top block).

Overall, clinical characteristics were largely similar across tertiles (Table 1). The total cfDNA yield was not associated with established markers of disease severity or myocardial injury. Specifically, NT-proBNP levels did not differ significantly across the lowest, middle, and highest cfDNA tertiles, with median concentrations of 304 pg/mL (IQR 58-871), 380 pg/mL (IQR 213-668), and 692 pg/mL (IQR 260-1769), respectively (P = 0.145). The proportion of patients with elevated NT-proBNP (>300 pg/mL) was likewise comparable across tertiles, representing 52.6% of the first tertile, 63.2% of the second tertile, and 68.4% of the third tertile (P = 0.702). Similarly, median troponin concentrations at collection were similar across the three tertiles at 20 ng/L (IQR 7-92), 22 ng/L (IQR 8-676), and 20 ng/L (IQR 7-139) (P = 0.475), with no significant difference in the prevalence of elevated troponin (>10 ng/L) between the groups (52.6% vs 57.9% vs 57.9%, respectively; P > 0.999).

Among baseline characteristics, BMI differed significantly across tertiles (32.3 ± 5.4 kg/m 2 vs 33.3 ± 5.6 kg/m 2 vs 29.1 ± 4.7 kg/m 2 ; P = 0.043), with corresponding differences in obesity prevalence (57.9% vs 84.2% vs 31.6%; P = 0.006). Furthermore, myocardial fibrosis severity on cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) differed significantly across tertiles (P = 0.020). Moderate fibrosis was most prevalent in the middle tertile (33.3%), whereas severe fibrosis was seen only in the highest tertile (13.3%).

Myocardial-derived cfDNA fraction and clinical associations

Patients contributing samples in the highest total myocardial cfDNA tertile (n = 19) were younger (mean age 38.3 ± 14.4 years) at the point of sampling compared with those in the lowest tertile (50.5 ± 19.5 years; P = 0.042). BMI increased across tertiles (28.4 ± 4.6 vs 32.3 ± 4.9 vs 34.0 ± 5.6 kg/m²; P = 0.004), with a significantly higher prevalence of obesity in the upper tertiles (26.3% vs 73.7% vs 73.7%; P = 0.004) (Supplementary Table 4).

Structural markers of disease also differed across tertiles. Late gadolinium enhancement differed across tertiles (P = 0.039), and the prevalence of LVOT obstruction was significantly higher in the middle and highest tertiles (P = 0.031). Fibrosis severity did not differ significantly across groups (P = 0.270). There were no significant differences in other characteristics, including NT-proBNP (P = 0.126) and troponin (P = 0.485) (Supplementary Table 4).

Similar findings were seen when cardiomyocyte cfDNA fraction was investigated instead of total myocardial cfDNA. Higher cardiomyocyte cfDNA fraction was associated with higher BMI (28.2 ± 5.0 vs 32.2 ± 4.5 vs 34.2 ± 5.4 kg/m²; P = 0.001) and with obesity prevalence (21.1% vs 73.7% vs 78.9%; P < 0.001). Obstructive sleep apnea was also more common in the highest tertile (31.6%) compared with the lower tertiles (5.3% each; P = 0.038). These associations suggest a possible relationship between cardiometabolic burden and cardiomyocyte-derived cfDNA release. There were non-significant trends towards more symptoms, LVOT obstruction, atrial fibrillation and late gadolinium enhancement in samples with higher cardiomyocyte cfDNA (Supplementary Table 5).

Cardiomyocyte-derived cfDNA fraction was not associated with NT-proBNP (P = 0.827), median troponin concentration (P = 0.293), or elevated troponin status (P = 0.127).

Per-sample relationships are shown in Fig. 3. Cardiomyocyte-derived cfDNA fraction was higher in STEMI than in HCM or NSTEMI samples (Kruskal-Wallis P = 0.002; Fig. 3a). Within HCM (n = 57), cardiomyocyte-derived cfDNA fraction correlated positively with BMI (Spearman ρ = 0.42, P = 0.001; Fig. 3b) and was higher in samples from patients with obesity than in those without (median 1.02% vs 0.00%; P < 0.001; Fig. 3c), whereas cardiac fibroblast-derived cfDNA fraction did not correlate with BMI (ρ = 0.01, P = 0.93). By contrast, cardiomyocyte-derived cfDNA fraction showed no correlation with hs-cTnI (ρ = -0.13, P = 0.34; Fig. 3d) or NT-proBNP (ρ = -0.08, P = 0.55; Fig. 3e).

Finally, cardiac fibroblast-derived cfDNA fraction was not significantly associated with BMI, obesity, or most cardiometabolic comorbidities (all P > 0.05; Supplementary Table 6).

Discussion

In this study, we systematically characterized circulating cfDNA in a well-phenotyped cohort of patients with HCM, demonstrating the feasibility of quantifying different sources of cfDNA using methylation-based deconvolution. Three principal findings emerged. First, quantification of cfDNA-CM in patients with HCM who are not suffering from an acute illness is feasible.

Second, higher cfDNA-CM levels were associated with increased BMI and obesity, potentially linking cardiometabolic burden to circulating markers of myocardial cell turnover. Third, cfDNA-CM fractions were not associated with established biomarkers of myocardial wall stress and cellular injury, including NT-proBNP and hs-cTnI. These findings suggest that cfDNA-CM may represent a distinct biological domain not captured by traditional circulating biomarkers.

Cell-free DNA has emerged as a powerful biomarker of tissue injury and cellular turnover.

Released into blood circulation following cell death, cfDNA provides a dynamic snapshot of ongoing biological processes occurring throughout the body. Unlike conventional protein biomarkers, which might be released through multiple pathways, cfDNA directly originates from cells undergoing injury or turnover [29] [30] [31] . Additionally, a unique characteristic of cfDNA is that it retains tissue-specific methylation patterns that permit identification of its cellular origin 32 . Using the tissue-specific characteristics of cfDNA to assess disease state has already demonstrated clinical utility in fields such as oncology, prenatal diagnostics, and transplant medicine, and has shown promise in acute cardiovascular conditions including myocardial infarction [12] [13] [14] 19 . By enabling direct quantification of cardiomyocyte-derived DNA fragments in the circulation, methylation-based cfDNA analysis offers a unique opportunity to assess myocardial injury and remodeling through a biologically distinct lens that may complement traditional biomarkers.

The association between cfDNA-CM and BMI/obesity is notable because, to our knowledge, it has not previously been described and, importantly, was not observed with traditional biomarkers. While this novel observation requires confirmation in a larger independent cohort, it can be leveraged to guide future studies to determine the possible mechanism behind the association between cfDNA-CM and BMI/obesity. Obesity is a well-established driver of systemic inflammation and increased hemodynamic load, both of which may exacerbate the underlying pathophysiology of HCM. As described by Spudich, HCM mutations that drive the hypercontractility in HCM act through altered ATPase kinetics and the recruitment of excess myosin heads into an active state, creating a baseline of high energetic demand 33 . Prior work by Fumagalli et al. has demonstrated that obesity in HCM is independently associated with increased left ventricular wall stress and adverse structural remodeling, which we believe superimposes an additional hemodynamic load onto an already inefficient system 34 . We speculate that the observed increase in cfDNA-CM may represent the molecular 'breaking point' of a heart pushed to its metabolic capacity. This 'multi-hit' stressor likely amplifies metabolic demand, promoting oxidative stress and the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS). Recent evidence from Zhuo et al. further links these oxidative stress-related transcriptional programs in HCM to apoptotic signaling pathways 35 . We therefore suggest that chronic metabolic and hemodynamic stress promotes a low-grade, ROS-mediated cardiomyocyte injury, resulting in an accelerated but 'silent' cellular turnover. In this context, cfDNA-CM emerges as a sensitive, realtime genomic indicator of metabolic exhaustion and subclinical cellular attrition, a signal that traditional protein biomarkers of overt necrosis may overlook. Consistent with this cardiometabolic phenotype, cfDNA-CM was also associated with obstructive sleep apnea. These findings raise the intriguing possibility that cardiometabolic burden may contribute to subclinical cardiomyocyte turnover or DNA release, independent of overt myocardial injury detectable by conventional biomarkers. By contrast, cardiac fibroblast-derived cfDNA (f-cfDNA) did not show similar associations, suggesting that fibroblast DNA release may be more tightly linked to structural remodeling or extracellular matrix turnover than metabolic stress per se.

An equally intriguing observation is the lack of correlation between cfDNA-CM and established markers of injury like hs-cTnI and NT-proBNP. While NT-proBNP is a sensitive indicator of ventricular stretch and remodeling in HCM, it does not necessarily reflect the rate of myocardial cell death. In our cohort, the lack of correlation between NT-proBNP and cfDNA-CM suggests that a patient can have significant hemodynamic stress (high stretch) without necessarily crossing the threshold into the accelerated, ROS-mediated cellular turnover (high cfDNA-CM) that we observed in the setting of obesity. The lack of correlation between cfDNA-CM and troponin, even in a cohort specifically enriched for chronically elevated troponin, highlights a potentially important 'biomarker uncoupling' in HCM. While these markers typically increase in tandem during acute myocardial injury 19 , our data suggest that in HCM, they reflect distinct cellular release pathways.

One possibility for this discrepancy is the differential partitioning of genomic and protein cargo into extracellular vesicles (EVs). While the presence of circulating cfDNA-CM typically implies lack of cellular integrity or true cell death, the associated troponin may be sequestered rather than free-floating. Recent evidence indicates that apoptosis triggers a substantial release of large EVs (LEVs), which carry a distinct proteomic and genomic signature 36 . This is supported by the work of Spanos et al., which demonstrated that in HCM, a substantial fraction of circulating troponin is sequestered within EVs, whereas troponin in acute syndromes remains primarily freefloating 37 . Because conventional high-sensitivity assays primarily detect non-vesicle-associated protein, the EV-bound troponin remains effectively 'masked' from standard detection. Furthermore, while Spanos et al. suggest that EV-associated troponin may represent an active secretory response to myocardial stress, the surge in cfDNA-CM we observed likely reflects a high-volume wave of cellular attrition where genomic material is released alongside these apoptotic vesicles. Consequently, cfDNA-CM may serve as a more direct indicator of actual cardiomyocyte death, capturing a 'silent' turnover that is biologically and analytically distinct from the active secretory pathways that drive chronic troponin elevation in HCM.

The lack of correlation between cfDNA-CM and hs-cTnI in our HCM cohort may further be influenced by the presence of autoantibodies to cardiac troponin I. Anti-cardiac troponin antibodies have been found in up to 20% of healthy individuals and up to 27.6% in those with cardiomyopathies 38, 39 . As demonstrated by Eriksson et al., autoantibodies can effectively mask the release of troponin from injured tissue, particularly in assays that target the mid-fragment of the protein. At high titers, these antibodies can block the signal of small amounts of circulating troponin, leading to a significant delay or total failure in detection 40 . Conversely, anti-cardiac troponin autoantibodies may also form circulating macrotroponin immune complexes that increase molecular size and prolong plasma half-life, potentially leading to persistently elevated hs-cTnI levels despite relatively low ongoing cardiomyocyte injury or turnover 41 . Although antitroponin antibodies were not measured in our cohort and their contribution cannot be determined, the hs-cTnI concentrations analyzed in this study represent the clinically measured values used in patient assessment, risk stratification, and increasingly as biomarkers of treatment response in HCM clinical trials. Thus, regardless of the mechanisms influencing circulating troponin concentrations, the observed dissociation between clinically measured hs-cTnI and cfDNA-CM supports the possibility that cfDNA-CM provides complementary biological information not captured by conventional troponin measurement. Whereas hs-cTnI is classically interpreted as a marker of acute or chronic cardiomyocyte injury, cfDNA-CM may instead capture ongoing cellular turnover or remodeling. Together, these findings challenge the assumption that traditional cardiac biomarkers and cfDNA reflect the same underlying pathophysiology and support a complementary role for cfDNA as a marker of myocardial stress.

The lack of association between cardiac fibroblast-derived cfDNA and LGE or fibrosis severity raises the possibility that circulating fibroblast-derived DNA reflects active fibroblast turnover or activation rather than the cumulative burden of replacement fibrosis captured by LGE. Future longitudinal studies integrating f-cfDNA with markers of fibroblast activation may help determine whether it provides a dynamic measure of active myocardial remodeling.

From a translational perspective, our findings have several implications. First, cfDNA profiling could provide a noninvasive window into myocardial biology, offering opportunities for early detection of cellular stress before the development of irreversible hypertrophy or fibrosis.

Second, the disconnect between cfDNA and traditional biomarkers suggests that current clinical metrics may underestimate subclinical myocardial injury or remodeling in certain patient subsets, particularly those with metabolic comorbidities. Third, these results lay the groundwork for integrating cfDNA analyses with genetic and imaging studies to better stratify risk and monitor disease progression in genotype-positive individuals. Additionally, the emergence of novel disease-modifying therapies highlights the clinical utility of differentiating physiologic biomarker shifts from true cell death. For example, the cardiac myosin activator omecamtiv mecarbil demonstrated favorable outcomes in the GALACTIC-HF trial but faced regulatory hurdles due to concerns regarding drug-related elevations in troponin 42 . Although this troponin rise originally raised alarms for cardiotoxicity, post-hoc analyses suggested it did not reflect myocardial necrosis 43 . In such scenarios, cfDNA could serve as a useful adjunctive biomarker, offering a more specific readout of actual cell death to clarify ambiguous troponin elevations and de-risk promising therapeutics. A similar challenge has emerged with gene therapy in cardiomyopathies and neuromuscular disorders characterized by chronically elevated troponin.

In the EMBARK trial of delandistrogene moxeparvovec for Duchenne muscular dystrophy, myocarditis occurred as a treatment-related serious adverse event following gene therapy 44 . In this setting, where baseline troponin may already be elevated and skeletal muscle involvement may further complicate interpretation of circulating biomarkers, identifying treatment-emergent myocardial injury can be particularly challenging. Cardiomyocyte-derived cfDNA could provide an opportunity to distinguish changes in baseline biomarker profiles from true cardiomyocyte injury, potentially facilitating the evaluation and monitoring of myocarditis or other cardiac toxicities during gene therapy. More broadly, this approach may advance our understanding of myocardial turnover in vivo and establish cfDNA as a clinically useful biomarker across a range of chronic cardiomyopathies, extending beyond HCM to other conditions characterized by progressive remodeling and heart failure.

This study has limitations. It was a single-center pilot study designed to assess the feasibility of quantifying cfDNA with its cellular origin in clinically stable outpatients with HCM. The sampling design was not fully independent, with a few examples of multiple samples contributed by the same patients. This was done to increase the sample size overall in view of our sampling time constraints for this pilot study. There were only a small number of repeated samples (3 of 54 patients contributed two samples each; the remaining 51 contributed one), and we took great care to make sure the repeated samples were separated by at least 1 year (which in our HCM cohort often translated to at least some changes in clinical presentation), but this poses a risk of mildly inflated associations owing to double-counting. Because of the limitations imposed by our small sample size, larger and better-powered studies are needed to confirm our findings and establish more robust correlations with underlying disease pathology. We used disease controls rather than healthy volunteers because little myocardial cfDNA is expected in healthy individuals. Additionally, the cross-sectional design precludes inference regarding temporal dynamics of cfDNA release relative to disease progression. Anti-cardiac troponin antibodies and extracellular vesicle-associated troponin were not measured, limiting our ability to determine whether these factors contributed to the observed dissociation between hs-cTnI and cfDNA-CM.

The association between cfDNA-CM and BMI was sensitive to the deconvolution algorithm and marker depth, and therefore requires confirmation in an independent cohort. Future studies incorporating longitudinal sampling, broader genotypic diversity, and mechanistic interrogation of cfDNA sources are needed to clarify the biological and clinical relevance of our observations. In summary, estimation of cardiac-specific cfDNA in HCM is feasible and may represent a distinct biomarker of myocardial cellular turnover, largely independent of conventional biomarkers of injury or functional impairment. This highlights cfDNA as a promising noninvasive tool for early detection and monitoring of subclinical disease processes in HCM. By capturing aspects of myocardial biology that escape standard imaging and laboratory assessment, cfDNA profiling has the potential to refine risk stratification, guide personalized management, and inform mechanistic studies aimed at understanding the cellular drivers of hypertrophy and fibrosis. Future longitudinal and multi-omic studies will be critical to validate these findings and translate cfDNA into clinically actionable insights.

Methods

Study design and participants

This was a prospective, single-center study conducted at Oregon Health & Science University (OHSU). All HCM patients were clinically stable and presented for routine follow-up; none had acute illness at the time of enrollment. The few repeated samples from the same HCM patients (3 of the enrolled 54 contributing 2 samples each) were taken at least 1 year apart. Patients with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) were enrolled within 48 hours of percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). Peripheral venous blood samples were collected in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) tubes and plasma was isolated by centrifugation at 1,500 rpm for 15 minutes at 4 °C and stored at -80 °C.

Ethics statement

The study was approved by the OHSU Institutional Review Board. All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment. Participant sex was recorded from the medical record; because of the sample size, analyses were not disaggregated by sex or gender, and sex is reported as a descriptive characteristic (Table 1). Participants did not receive compensation.

Clinical biomarker analysis

High-sensitivity cardiac troponin I (Beckman cellular assay) and NT-proBNP (Siemens Healthcare) were measured during the same index sample collection.

Total cfDNA isolation and purification

cfDNA was isolated using a cfDNA purification kit (Active Motif, 25504). Briefly, samples were thawed on ice, and 1.0 mL of plasma was mixed with 1.25 mL of lysis buffer and 25 µL of magnetic beads to lyse residual cellular components and selectively bind cfDNA, respectively.

Bead-bound samples then underwent two rounds of washing using ethanol wash buffer to remove contaminants. Finally, the cfDNA was released from the magnetic beads and eluted in molecular biology-grade water. Yield was assessed using the Qubit 1x dsDNA high sensitivity assay (Fisher Scientific). Extracted and purified cfDNA samples were then stored at -20 °C until library preparation.

Whole-genome bisulfite sequencing library preparation and sequencing

Whole genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) libraries were prepared using the Zymo-Seq Trio WGBS Library Kit (Zymo Research, D5462). For each sample, 5-10 ng of cfDNA was used as input into the library reaction. Bisulfite conversion was carried out in a thermal cycler with lightning conversion reagent (Zymo Research), followed by column-based clean-up and desulfonation. Purified, bisulfite converted DNA then underwent adapter ligation and subsequent purification using Zymo's Select-a-Size MagBeads. Finally, libraries were polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-amplified for 10 cycles with Zymo's unique dual index (UDI) primers, and PCR artifacts were removed using the MagBeads (Zymo Research). Final libraries were eluted in DNA elution buffer, quantified, and sequenced 150 bp paired-end on an Illumina NovaSeq platform to generate an average of 274 million raw reads per library.

Cell-free DNA methylation analysis and deconvolution

Sequencing quality was assessed with FastQC v0.11.9. Raw reads were trimmed with TrimGalore v0.6.7, and trimmed reads were aligned to the human reference genome hg38 with Bismark v0.24.1 using default settings. Duplicates were removed with the deduplicate_bismark script [21] [22] [23].

Cell-type deconvolution of the cfDNA methylome was performed using the UXM fragmentlevel deconvolution algorithm, which applies non-negative least squares (NNLS) modeling to estimate the relative contributions of 39 primary human cell types based on a reference methylation atlas created by Loyfer et al. This method reconstructs cfDNA methylation patterns as a linear combination of cell-type-specific methylation signatures and has been shown to sensitively identify the tissue of origin using the top 25 unmethylated regions per cell type (a total of 1,246 markers), each comprising multiple CpG sites and selected for their specificity across tissues 18 . For this study, we performed deconvolution using 14 cell types: granulocytes, B cells, monocytes/macrophages, natural killer cells, T cells, endothelial cells, cardiomyocytes, cardiac fibroblasts, kidney epithelium, hepatocytes, alveolar epithelium, bronchial epithelium, skeletal muscle, and smooth muscle.

For the methylation atlas and UXM deconvolution algorithm, the deduplicated BAM files were converted to PAT files using the wgbstools bam2pat function, followed by running UXM deconvolution on the PAT files.

Validation using independent tissue datasets WGBS datasets from purified human tissues were obtained from previously published studies, including cardiomyocyte nuclei isolated from left ventricular biopsies 24 , whole blood samples from participants in the London Life Sciences Prospective Population Study (LOLIPOP) 25 , and healthy control liver biopsies obtained during bariatric surgery 26 . Raw sequencing reads were processed using the same pipeline described above. Liver reference sample L1 was excluded from validation because it contained fewer than 20 million fragments. To normalize sequencing depth, each retained sample was subsampled to 20 million fragments. In silico mixtures were generated by randomly sampling fragments from each tissue dataset in defined proportions (heart:liver:blood ratios of 10:10:80, 5:10:85, 5:5:90) while maintaining equal total depth. Deconvolution was performed in accordance with the previously described approach and predicted tissue fractions were compared with the known mixture proportions.

Cross-method comparison

To assess the method dependence of the cell type estimates, we compared UXM's cell-type deconvolution results against an additional reference-based methylation-haplotype-aware algorithm, CelFiE-ISH 27 . The CelFiE-ISH reference atlas was constructed using the same 14 cell types and top 25 atlas markers used for UXM (Supplementary Table 3). The atlas construction workflow was adopted from Unterman et al. 27, 28 . Deconvolution was run with the CelFiE-ISH model at default settings. Ground-truth fractions were unavailable for our 50 clinical HCM samples; however, cross-method agreement on the cohort was assessed using Lin's concordance correlation coefficient (CCC), Pearson r, and Bland-Altman analysis.

Statistics and reproducibility

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, as appropriate, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Comparisons between groups were performed using Student's t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for continuous variables and χ² or Fisher's exact tests for categorical variables. All tests were two-sided.

Because cfDNA tissue fractions represent compositional data (i.e., proportions constrained to sum to one), fraction values were transformed using a center-log-ratio (CLR) transformation prior to statistical analysis to mitigate compositional bias. Correlations between cfDNA measurements and clinical variables were evaluated using Spearman correlation coefficients.
