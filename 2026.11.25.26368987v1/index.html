What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

How StatCheck works

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

The Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) lacks population-based estimates of the incidence of typhoidal Salmonella infection, despite clinical and mortality evidence of a high burden, and has yet to introduce typhoid conjugate vaccine (TCV). We estimated seroincidence in conflict-affected Angumu Health Zone, Ituri Province.

We conducted a cross-sectional serosurvey across 14 health areas within an operational malaria intervention survey in April-May 2024. Seroincidence was estimated from IgA responses to haemolysin E (HlyE) in dried blood spots (DBS) with models of postinfection antibody decay. Seropositivity was the proportion of participants with HlyE IgA at or above a cut-off indicating recent infection. Estimates were weighted by interventionzone population.

Findings We included 2586 participants from 582 households. Overall IgA seroincidence was 25 infections per 1000 person-years (95% CI 22-29), peaking in children younger than 2 years (91, 95% CI 46-180) and exceeding 20 per 1000 person-years in every older age group. The differences in age-standardised seroincidence by sex (28 in male and 22 in female participants, p=0.051) and by self-reported typhoid history among participants aged 5 years or older (p>0.99) were not statistically significant. HlyE IgA seropositivity was 3.1% (95% CI 2.4-4.0), with smoothed seropositivity ranging from 2.4% to 7.2% across the study area.

These findings indicate substantial typhoidal Salmonella transmission in Angumu that clinical surveillance alone could not detect. Among children, seroincidence was higher than in serosurveys in Nepal but lower than in Bangladesh and Pakistan, and among adults it was lower than in all three. Because seroincidence includes mild and asymptomatic infections, it cannot be mapped directly onto WHO clinical-incidence thresholds for TCV introduction. Integrating DBS serosurveillance into an existing household survey was operationally feasible. These findings support consideration of targeted TCV introduction in similarly under-surveilled, high-transmission settings in eastern DRC.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed and Embase from database inception to Nov 25, 2026, without language restriction, for population-based estimates of typhoidal Salmonella infection in central Africa. We combined terms for typhoid and enteric fever with terms for seroincidence, serosurvey, and haemolysin E, and with terms for the Democratic Republic of the Congo and for Africa. Incidence estimates for the DRC come from facility-based surveillance, which counts the patients who reach a hospital with a functioning bloodculture laboratory. Seroincidence estimated from antibody responses to haemolysin E measures infection rather than clinical disease, and has been reported from Juba in South Sudan, from coastal and western Kenya, and from cohorts in south Asia. The searches returned no seroincidence estimate for the DRC, and none from a conflict-affected population in central Africa.

Added value of this study

This is the first population-based estimate of typhoidal Salmonella seroincidence in the DRC. Infection was frequent in every age group, and highest in children younger than 2 years, in a health zone where no typhoid case has been confirmed by culture. Collecting dried blood spots within an existing operational household survey, and analysing them with published antibody-decay models, produced these estimates without dedicated surveillance infrastructure.

Implications of all the available evidence

Typhoidal Salmonella transmission can be intense where no diagnostic capacity exists to record it, so the absence of confirmed cases is not evidence of a low burden. Serosurveillance embedded in household surveys can supply the local burden data that countries need in order to decide where to introduce typhoid conjugate vaccine. National immunisation programmes and their partners could use it for that purpose in eastern DRC.

Introduction

Enteric fever, a systemic infection caused by Salmonella enterica serovars Typhi and Paratyphi, causes an estimated 9.3 million infections and over 100,000 deaths annually, 1 with the highest burden borne by populations in low-and middle-income countries with inadequate water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) infrastructure. 2 Children are disproportionately affected, and the burden among those younger than 5 years may be higher than previously recognised. 1, 3, 4 Antimicrobial resistance in S. Typhi increasingly limits treatment options, which strengthens the case for prevention through vaccination. 5, 6 Licensed vaccines target S. Typhi specifically; no vaccine is currently available for S. Paratyphi. Typhoid conjugate vaccine (TCV), which can be given from 6 months of age, showed 78-85% efficacy against blood culture-confirmed typhoid in phase 3 trials among children in Nepal, Bangladesh, and Malawi. [7] [8] [9] [10] The 2026 World Health Organization (WHO) position paper recommends TCV where typhoid incidence is high or very high, the case fatality ratio is high, or antimicrobial resistance is common. 11 Gavi has supported TCV introduction in several countries. [12] [13] [14] Nevertheless, uptake has been uneven, and large parts of sub-Saharan Africa are without access to TCV. 14 The Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), a Gavi-eligible country, has not introduced TCV into its national immunisation programme. 15 In the Severe Typhoid in Africa (SETA) programme, facility-based clinical surveillance at two health centres in Kisantu yielded an adjusted incidence of 3.2 symptomatic cases per 1000 person-years, the highest of the six countries. 6 SETA investigators also documented antimicrobial resistance and intestinal perforation among hospitalised patients in the DRC. 6 No community-based estimate of the incidence of typhoidal Salmonella infection is available for the DRC. A lack of population-based burden data has been a barrier to TCV introduction decisions. 12 Blood culture, the gold standard for typhoid diagnosis, is rarely available in resourceconstrained settings and detects approximately 60% of true cases. 16 Serological approaches offer an alternative. Seroincidence, the rate of new S. Typhi and S. Paratyphi A infections per person-time, can be estimated from cross-sectional, population-based samples. 4 The estimate is inferred from the distribution of antibody levels in the sample together with models of the rise and decay of haemolysin E (HlyE) antibodies after infection. Seropositivity is the proportion of participants whose HlyE IgA level is at or above a cut-off indicating recent infection. Because HlyE is expressed by both S. Typhi and S. Paratyphi A, these estimates cannot distinguish infections caused by the two pathogens.

Angumu Health Zone, in Ituri Province in north-eastern DRC, has experienced recurrent conflict and displacement since 2017. 17 Clinicians at the hospital serving the health zone, where surgical capacity is limited, have observed many patients with intestinal perforation consistent with severe enteric fever. At that hospital in 2023, peritonitis accounted for 18% of the approximately 45 operations each month, and 6 of the 8 monthly cases were in children (MSF, unpublished data). Typhoid and paratyphoid fever accounted for 4.2% (95% CI 3.1-6.2) of deaths among people aged 12 years or older in a 2022 verbal autopsy study in the same population. 18 Enteric fever was among the ten leading causes of adult death assigned by clinician review. The DRC meets WHO criteria for priority introduction of TCV. 15 In Angumu Health Zone, between April and May 2024, we estimated the seroincidence of typhoidal Salmonella infection as a baseline before any vaccination campaign. The primary outcome was HlyE IgA measured in dried blood spots. As secondary analyses, we estimated seroincidence from combined HlyE IgA and IgG and described spatial variation in HlyE IgA seropositivity.

Methods

Study design and setting

From April to May 2024, we conducted a cross-sectional population-based serosurvey in Angumu Health Zone, Ituri Province, DRC. This serosurvey was embedded in a household survey evaluating the effect of malaria prevention interventions on mortality and malaria prevalence. 19 On Lake Albert's western shore, Angumu Health Zone comprises 22 health areas (Figure 1). Insecurity restricted sampling to 14 health areas east of the Kakoyi River (estimated population approximately 172,000). 17, 20 In 2022, these 14 areas were allocated to three malaria intervention zones serving as sampling strata. These zones were routine care (Routine; 5 health areas), indoor residual spraying (IRS; 4), and mass drug administration combined with IRS (MDA+IRS; 5). The study population comprised residents of selected households, including internally displaced persons.

Participants and sampling

Within each zone, roofs were drawn at random from a satellite-image frame; each drawn roof and its four nearest neighbours formed a cluster of five households for the malaria survey, and one household per cluster joined the typhoid component (Supplementary Methods SM2). All household members who had lived in the household during the preceding 2 weeks were eligible, with no minimum age. Dried blood spot (DBS) samples were collected from consenting participants, or from children whose parent or legal guardian provided consent. Households with no member available after two visits, or in which participation was refused, were not replaced.

For the typhoid component, 3,062 individuals were required, assuming 3% recent-infection prevalence and ±20% relative precision for the overall estimate. The sample size was not calculated for age-specific estimates. To meet the malaria and serological requirements, 630 households (210 per zone) were selected.

Data collection and laboratory procedures

Data were collected using standardised electronic questionnaires on tablets. Trained survey enumerators administered a rapid diagnostic test (RDT) for Plasmodium falciparum histidine-rich protein 2 (SD Bioline Ag P.f. ; Standard Diagnostics, Republic of Korea). Participants with a positive RDT were treated following national protocols. Consenting participants then provided a capillary blood sample from the same finger prick. Approximately 60 µL of blood was collected on TropBio™ filter paper, air-dried for at least 30 minutes, and stored at -20 °C. Samples were shipped at room temperature to Massachusetts General Hospital, Boston, USA, and returned to -20 °C upon receipt until testing.

Between 7 May and 7 August 2025, DBS samples were eluted and tested for immunoglobulin A (IgA) and immunoglobulin G (IgG) responses to HlyE. We used kinetic enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) following published protocols. 4, 21 Elution, detection, and normalisation to ELISA units (EU) are described in Supplementary Methods SM1.

Outcomes, variables, and definitions

The primary outcome was typhoidal Salmonella seroincidence estimated from HlyE IgA, based on expert consensus that HlyE IgA kinetics are more stable across settings with different forces of infection. 22 Combined HlyE IgA and IgG estimates were secondary, and HlyE IgG-only estimates were exploratory. HlyE IgA seropositivity was defined as ≥9.23 normalised EU, a published diagnostic cut-off indicating recent infection. 23 Age groups (<2, 2-4, 5-15, and ≥16 years; Supplementary Methods SM7) followed the clinical enteric fever surveillance bands of the published kinetics. 4, 23 The <2 and 2-4 year groups shared the <5 kinetics. History of typhoid fever was self-reported (question wording in Table 1), although self-reported history may not reflect laboratory-confirmed disease because diagnostic capacity is limited.

Statistical analysis

We estimated seroincidence by maximum likelihood, comparing observed quantitative HlyE IgA and IgG distributions with those expected under different infection rates. 4, 24 Agespecific antibody kinetics were pooled from longitudinal blood-culture-confirmed enteric fever cohorts in Bangladesh, Nepal, Pakistan, and Ghana. We used the serocalculator package (version 1.4.1) in R (version 4.5.2). 25 We report seroincidence as infections per 1,000 person-years with 95% confidence intervals. Measurement-noise parameters were those estimated for the testing laboratory. Kinetic and noise parameters are given in Supplementary Table S1, and assumed antibody curves with Angumu titres in Supplementary Figure S1. Those kinetics came mostly from dried blood spots collected and dried as here, with plasma at enrolment. 4 To assess sensitivity to these inputs, we refitted the unweighted IgA analysis with alternative kinetic and measurement-noise parameters.

We used the serocalculator cluster-robust variance estimator for within-household clustering. To assess household clustering, we repeated the unweighted IgA analysis with one randomly selected participant per household and compared the result with the unweighted estimate from all participants.

To account for equal sample sizes across zones of unequal population, we fitted each zone separately and combined estimates using population weights, treating zones as independent. Zone populations were summed from gridded population estimates across each zone's health areas. 20 For the overall rate, we combined zone-by-age-stratum estimates and accounted for within-household correlation between strata. Seropositivity was estimated with the same weights, with zones as strata and households as primary sampling units. Age-group weighting, heterogeneity testing, and sensitivity analyses using unpublished MSF programme populations or age-pooled kinetics are described in Supplementary Methods SM3.

We estimated exploratory HlyE IgA seroincidence by sex and self-reported typhoid history (restricted to ages ≥5 years), directly age-standardised because age distributions differed between subgroups. Subgroup estimates were compared using Wald tests on the log rate ratio with joint cluster-robust covariance (Supplementary Methods SM4). Zone and healtharea rates, which the survey was not designed to compare, are reported in Supplementary Tables S2 and S3. We also modelled HlyE titres and IgA seropositivity as smooth functions of age using generalised additive models.

We estimated household-level HlyE IgA seropositivity using a random-intercept model with empirical-Bayes shrinkage and interpolated estimates by sample-size-weighted kernel smoothing with cross-validated bandwidth.

Post hoc, we estimated age-adjusted prevalence ratios for HlyE IgA seropositivity across recent illness groups (including malaria and/or anaemia) compared with no illness using the same models (Supplementary Methods SM5).

Missing data

No values were imputed, and we retained the first record for 11 duplicate HlyE specimen identifiers. Subgroup analyses excluded 34 participants with missing sex and 3 participants aged ≥5 years with missing typhoid history (inclusion criteria in Supplementary Methods SM6).

Results

Participant enrolment and characteristics

Of 630 households targeted, 597 (94.8%) were surveyed: 22 were not reached and 11 declined; neither group was replaced (Figure 2). Of 2,814 participants with a recorded DBSconsent response, 2,768 (98.4%) consented. DBS was collected for 2,744 participants; 24 did not provide a DBS. Of the 2,744 DBS, 147 were not available for testing (134 not shipped, 13 not received by the laboratory), leaving 2,597 assay records. After removal of duplicate records, the seroincidence analysis included 2,586 participants in 582 households, of whom 2,504 (96.8%) were linked to a household location and included in the spatial analysis.

Of 2,586 participants, 1,106 (43%) were aged 16 years or older and 979 (38%) were aged 5-15 years. Of the remainder, 371 (14%) were aged 2-4 years and 130 (5.0%) were younger than 2 years (Table 1; Supplementary Figure S2). Of participants with recorded sex, 1,393 (55%) were female. Prior typhoid fever was reported by 488 (19%) participants. Female participants were older than male participants (median age 15 vs 11 years). Participants reporting prior typhoid fever were older than those not reporting it (median age 22.5 vs 11 years; Supplementary Table S4).

Primary HlyE IgA seroincidence

Overall HlyE IgA seroincidence, weighted by zone population, was 25 infections per 1,000 person-years (95% CI 22-29; Table 2). Seroincidence was highest among children younger than 2 years, at 91 infections per 1,000 person-years (95% CI 46-180). It was 22 (13-36) among children aged 2-4 years, 21 (17-26) at 5-15 years, and 28 (25-32) at 16 years or older. In an exploratory analysis by health area, seroincidence ranged from 20 to 46 infections per 1,000 person-years (Supplementary Table S3).

In exploratory subgroup analyses, age-standardised HlyE IgA seroincidence was 22 infections per 1,000 person-years among female participants (95% CI 18-27) and 28 among male participants (24-33). The rate ratio was 0.80 (95% CI 0.64-1.00; p=0.051). Among participants aged 5 years or older, seroincidence was 24 per 1,000 person-years among those reporting a previous typhoid fever diagnosis (19-31). It was the same among those without such a history (21-28; p>0.99; Table 2).

Spatial variation in HlyE IgA seropositivity

Weighted HlyE IgA seropositivity was 3.1% (95% CI 2.4-4.0), with 76 of 2,586 participants seropositive, all of whom were included in the spatial analysis. Smoothed seropositivity ranged from 2.4% to 7.2% across the study area (Figure 1B).

Secondary and exploratory analyses

HlyE IgA seropositivity was 5.5% (25/454) among participants who reported an illness attributed to malaria and/or anaemia in the previous 2 weeks and had a negative malaria RDT at interview. It was 2.5% (37/1,454) among participants with no recent illness (ageadjusted prevalence ratio 1.98, 95% CI 1.21-3.25; p=0.006). The 25 seropositive participants in this group came from 24 different households. Seropositivity was 1.9% (6/320) among participants with the same illness and a positive RDT and 3.0% (8/266) among those with other illnesses (Supplementary Table S5).

The combined HlyE IgA and IgG model yielded an overall seroincidence estimate of 152 infections per 1,000 person-years (95% CI 146-159). By age group, the estimate was highest among children younger than 2 years (652, 95% CI 566-750) and lowest among participants aged 16 years or older (108, 95% CI 101-115; Supplementary Table S6).

HlyE IgG-only seroincidence was 399 infections per 1,000 person-years overall (95% CI 385-414) and exceeded both the IgA-only and combined-model estimates across all age groups (Supplementary Figure S3). HlyE IgA titres increased across the age range, whereas HlyE IgG titres increased until approximately 20 years of age and then remained stable. Most IgA values were below the seropositivity cut-off, including in the oldest age group (Figure 3).

In the one-participant-per-household sensitivity analysis, 582 participants were retained and the overall HlyE IgA seroincidence estimate was 26 infections per 1,000 person-years (95% CI 21-33; Supplementary Table S7), similar to the estimate of 28 (95% CI 25-31) from the unweighted analysis of all participants. With the MSF programme population figures in place of the WorldPop estimates, the weighted overall rate was 26 (95% CI 23-30). With each zone's rate estimated using kinetics pooled across ages, the weighted overall rate was 28 (95% CI 25-32).

Alternative antibody-kinetics parameters changed the overall rate by factors of 0.92 to 1.83 relative to the reference, the largest change coming from kinetics derived from the Ghana cohort alone. Alternative measurement-noise assumptions changed it by less than 10% (Supplementary Table S8).

Discussion

We report the first population-based seroincidence estimates of typhoidal Salmonella infection in the DRC. Among 2,586 participants, overall HlyE IgA seroincidence, weighted by zone population, was 25 per 1,000 person-years (95% CI 22-29). Seroincidence peaked among children younger than 2 years and remained above 20 per 1,000 person-years in every older age group. This pattern contrasts with SETA clinical surveillance in the DRC, where symptomatic typhoid peaked among school-aged children. 6 This difference may reflect what each method measures: serology detects infection regardless of symptoms or care-seeking, whereas blood-culture surveillance detects only confirmed disease among patients investigated at health facilities. 26 In the youngest children, infection may more often be subclinical or present as undifferentiated fever treated presumptively as malaria in this holoendemic setting. Consistent with this, HlyE IgA seropositivity was twice as high among participants with a recent illness attributed to malaria but a negative RDT as among those with no recent illness. The under-2 rate should also be interpreted with caution because few longitudinal antibody data exist for this age group. The kinetics applied came from 383 cases younger than 5 years, not resolved below 2 years. 4 Among older children and adults, these rates are consistent with the 2022 Angumu verbal autopsy study. In that study, typhoid and paratyphoid accounted for 4.2% of deaths at ages 12 years and older. 18 Comparisons with other published serosurveys require caution: populations, sampling designs, assay implementations, and model inputs differ sufficiently that direct ranking is of limited value. However, in a serosurvey in coastal and western Kenya, 27 IgG responses to the same HlyE antigen were measured in the same laboratory and analysed with the same IgG-only model. Seroincidence in Angumu (399 per 1,000 person-years) was similar to that in coastal Kenya (370) and far higher than in western Kenya (36), where TCV has since been introduced. 27 We re-estimated HlyE IgA seroincidence in Bangladesh, Nepal, and Pakistan from published data with the same model and age groups. 4 At ages 5-15 years, Angumu seroincidence was higher than in Nepal (12 per 1,000 person-years) but lower than in Pakistan (85) and Bangladesh (260). The same order held in children younger than 5 years, whereas at ages 16 years or older, Angumu seroincidence was lower than in all three settings.

Estimates varied more than 14-fold by antibody choice. With kinetics pooled across ages throughout, so that the three are comparable, IgA alone yielded 28 per 1,000 person-years (95% CI 25-32), combined IgA/IgG 152 (146-159), and IgG alone 399 (385-414). HlyE IgA declines rapidly after infection and is more specific for recent infection. 4 HlyE IgG persists longer and may accumulate with repeated infection, and its decay varies with local force of infection. IgG-based estimates are more sensitive to whether kinetics were derived from a population with comparable transmission intensity. 4 We therefore consider IgA-based estimates the more conservative primary measure for Angumu. Raw IgG levels increased with age into early adulthood, as expected for an antibody that persists, but age-stratified models estimate recent-infection rates. Consequently, adding IgG raised estimates in every age group, and seroincidence remained highest among children younger than 2 years under each antibody choice.

Incorporating DBS-based typhoid serosurveillance into existing household surveys is operationally feasible 24 and uses DBS collection, which is already routine in programmes such as HIV. 28 However, HlyE serology and seroincidence models still require specialised laboratory and statistical capacity that currently limits routine use.

Seroincidence could be biased in either direction because no DRC antibody-kinetics data exist. IgA decay appears more comparable across settings, 22 and alternative kinetic or noise inputs changed overall IgA seroincidence 0.92-to 1.83-fold (highest with Ghana kinetics). HlyE cannot distinguish S. Typhi from S. Paratyphi A (rarely isolated in sub-Saharan African enteric fever surveillance 4, 6 ), and Typhi-specific, TCV-preventable burden cannot be estimated. Seroincidence measures infection rather than clinical disease and cannot be mapped directly onto WHO clinical-incidence classifications. 11 Because insecurity restricted sampling to 14 of 22 health areas, zone-wide seroincidence could be underestimated or overestimated if excluded areas had higher or lower transmission, respectively. CIs omit gridded-population uncertainty, although MSF programme weights yielded a similar rate (26 per 1,000 person-years, 23-30). Because sample size was calculated for the overall estimate only, age-specific precision was lower, lowest in the under-2 stratum (±74%). Underrepresentation of young adult men (Supplementary Figure S2) may underestimate seroincidence because eating outside the home increases risk. 29 Broad age-band standardisation may leave residual confounding in sex-and typhoidhistory estimates, with prior typhoid unconfirmed. With a median of four participants per household and no clustering test, the smoothed surface cannot confirm localised foci. However, a one-participant-per-household analysis yielded a similar unweighted rate (26 vs 28 per 1,000 person-years).

Protracted displacement, disrupted water and sanitation infrastructure, and the absence of bacteriological diagnostic capacity plausibly drive transmission in this setting. These conditions are widespread across Ituri and neighbouring provinces in eastern DRC. The main finding, that substantial typhoidal transmission can proceed largely undetected, is therefore likely to be generalisable across the region. Integrating DBS-based typhoid serosurveillance within existing operational household surveys, as done here, is operationally feasible and efficient. This approach generates evidence where dedicated surveillance infrastructure does not exist. The DRC has hosted a TCV mass vaccination campaign in Kisantu 30 but has not introduced TCV into its national immunisation programme. 15 Where direct incidence data are lacking, WHO's 2026 position paper allows countries to infer probable typhoid incidence and severity from alternative data. 11 Seroincidence estimates such as these could contribute to those data. These findings support broader, geographically representative, enteric fever surveillance across eastern DRC, linked where possible to culture or molecular confirmation, to shape the public health response. These seroincidence estimates show frequent infection in every age group. They also provide a baseline for evaluating any future TCV campaign in the health zone. Where surgical capacity is limited, preventing typhoid fever is likely to avert deaths from intestinal perforation. Clinicians at the hospital serving the health zone have observed this complication in large numbers.
