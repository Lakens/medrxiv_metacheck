Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Full text

Abstract

Estimating dengue force of infection (FOI) is essential for understanding transmission dynamics and targeting intervention programmes, yet the surveillance data in endemic settings required for estimation are often incomplete, with varying formats. We developed a Bayesian hierarchical catalytic model that jointly fits age-stratified case data, aggregate case data, and seroprevalence surveys within a single framework, incorporating external covariates to improve parameter identifiability. Synthetic validation showed that FOI and seroprevalence were recovered with little bias even when most districts contributed only aggregate case data, whereas reporting rates were reliably recovered only where agestratified case data were available. Applied to 128 districts across Java and Bali, Indonesia (2016-2024), the model revealed substantial spatial heterogeneity in FOI and reporting rates. Many districts in Java exceeded the WHO-suggested seroprevalence threshold for vaccine introduction, yet were classified as low-priority when using reported incidence as prioritisation criterion, particularly in areas with weak surveillance. Model-based seroprevalence estimation, integrating multiple data sources, offers a more consistent basis for identifying high-priority districts for vaccine introduction, and is less susceptible to surveillance bias than reported incidence alone.

INTRODUCTION

Dengue is among the most rapidly expanding mosquito-borne viral diseases, with cases reported globally to the World Health Organization (WHO) increasing from ~500 thousand in 2000 to around ~14.6 million in 2024 across over 100 countries (1). However, reported case counts substantially underestimate the true burden of dengue (2, 3), as the majority of infections go undetected by routine surveillance systems. Interpretation is complicated by reporting rates that vary widely across settings (3, 4). Estimating the force of infection (FOI), the rate at which susceptible individuals become infected, is critical to understanding transmission dynamics and guiding intervention strategies. FOI estimates underpin key policy decisions, including the targeting of dengue vaccination programmes of recently licensed dengue vaccines, which the WHO recommends for use in high transmission intensity settings (5). Seroprevalence at age 9 has been suggested as one indicator of transmission intensity to guide these decisions, with a threshold of 60% proposed to identify high-transmission areas (5).

In practice, FOI estimation is constrained by the quality and completeness of available surveillance data. Many endemic countries, particularly in Southeast Asia, rely on routine surveillance systems that capture only a fraction of infections, with reporting rates that vary substantially across locations (3) and contain several sources of bias. In Indonesia, the world's fourth most populous country, age-stratified case data, which provide the richest information for FOI estimation, are available in only a subset of districts or provinces, while others report only aggregate annual totals. Seroprevalence surveys offer complementary information on cumulative exposure but are costly and resource-intensive (6), limiting their spatial and temporal coverage. This heterogeneity in data availability presents a fundamental challenge: how to estimate FOI consistently across locations when the underlying data differ in format, completeness, and quality.

Catalytic compartment models provide an established framework for estimating FOI from age-structured epidemiological data (7) (8) (9) (10) (11) (12) and seroprevalence survey data (12) (13) (14).

However, existing approaches are typically constructed around the assumption of uniform age-stratified data across all locations and estimate FOI for individual locations independently, limiting their applicability in settings with mixed data types. Recent work has extended catalytic models to accommodate multiple locations within hierarchical structures (10), but the joint integration of heterogeneously structured data-case data that are age-stratified in some locations, but aggregate in others, seroprevalence estimates with different age categorisations across studies, and external covariates with different spatial resolutions-within a single inference framework that appropriately pools information across all locations has not been attempted. Such models are important for rigorous data synthesis and addressing policy-relevant questions.

Here, we present a Bayesian hierarchical catalytic model that addresses these limitations.

The model estimates time-varying FOI across 128 districts in seven provinces of Java and Bali, Indonesia, across 2016-2024, jointly fitting age-stratified and aggregate case data alongside seroprevalence surveys within a two-level hierarchical structure. External covariates are incorporated to inform heterogeneity in both FOI and reporting rates, providing additional structure to aid parameter identifiability. We validated the framework using synthetic data under four scenarios of decreasing data completeness, with covariates explaining only a small share (10%) of between-district variation in FOI, as estimated for the real data, and assessed sensitivity for higher shares. To evaluate the contribution of each data source, we fit four model variants-a full model with covariates and seroprevalence, and three reduced models omitting covariates, seroprevalence, or both-to both synthetic and real data. We characterised spatial and temporal patterns of dengue transmission in Java and Bali, and used the resulting seroprevalence estimates to assess district-level vaccine eligibility under WHO-suggested criteria.

RESULTS

Synthetic data validation

The model recovered true parameter values well across all four data completeness scenarios in a setting where covariates explained only a small share (10%) of betweendistrict variation in FOI, comparable to that estimated for Java and Bali data (Fig. 1; see

Materials and Methods).

Each scenario was fitted with and without covariates, where covariates with known 'true' effects on FOI and reporting rates were specified in the datagenerating processes. The scenarios span progressively coarser age stratification: all five simulated provinces reporting nine age groups (S1); all reporting four age groups (S2); two provinces reporting nine age groups and three reporting four age groups (S3); and a mixed setting in which three of five provinces report aggregate annual totals only (S4), which most closely mirrors the Indonesian data (Table 1). FOI estimates closely tracked true values across provinces and years, with similar accuracy in models with and without covariates (Fig. 1A). Reporting rate estimates were well recovered in S1-S3 but were systematically underestimated in S4, where most districts report only aggregate case data, with estimates plateauing at around 18% for districts whose true reporting rates exceeded 20% (Fig. 1B).

Across scenarios, the completeness of age-stratified data mattered most for separating reporting rates from FOI (Fig. 1C). The error in reporting rate estimates was similar in S1-S3 (relative root mean square error, RMSE, 0.17-0.18), indicating that coarser age grouping cost little, but increased sharply in S4 (0.26). In contrast, FOI and seroprevalence at age 9 remained reasonably well estimated in S4, with RMSE only moderately higher than in S1-S3 (FOI: 0.018 versus 0.016-0.017; seroprevalence: 0.080 versus 0.063-0.065).

Covariates had little effect on the accuracy of FOI point estimates, but their inclusion made the credible intervals more reliable (coverage of 90% CrI 84-88% with covariates, compared with 79-82% without, Fig. 1D). A similar gain was seen for reporting rates, and, in most scenarios, seroprevalence. Reporting rate coverage in S4 remained well below other scenarios with covariates (68%, and 56% without), consistent with the shrinkage of high reporting rates described above.

Accuracy of seroprevalence estimates at age 9 depended on how much a cohort's exposure fell within the surveillance period (Fig. 2A). In S1-S3, RMSE was highest in 2016, when eight of the nine years of exposure precede the case data availability and rely on reconstructed historical FOI. Then it fell to its lowest around 2019 (S1: 0.065 to 0.034) and rose again towards 2024 (0.063), as FOI in the final years is informed only by cases in those years, without the additional constraint that later case data provide for earlier years.

In S4, RMSE remained higher and nearly constant throughout (0.07-0.08). Model without covariates had slightly higher error in all scenarios, particularly in later years. Predicted seroprevalence at ages 5, 9 and 15 in 2024 closely matched true values in model with covariates, with little systematic bias and credible intervals that captured most true values (Fig. 2B). Without covariates, estimates tended to be biased upward, most notably in S4.

Covariate coefficients were generally well recovered, although some FOI coefficients were biased in a consistent direction across all scenarios (Fig. 2C). These biases largely disappeared when covariates explained a larger share of between-district variation (Fig. S1), indicating that individual coefficients are weakly identified when covariate-driven heterogeneity is low, even though FOI itself is well estimated.

We conducted several sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of these results. As covariates explained a larger share of between-district variation in FOI, accuracy and coverage improved substantially, while reporting rates in S4 remained the least accurate at every level (Fig. S1). Estimate bias was consistently the highest in S4, where shrinkage in reporting rates towards the shared mean led to their underestimation and, on average, led to overestimation of FOI and seroprevalence (Fig. S2). Adding a single seroprevalence survey, at the end of either 2014 or 2019, mainly improved seroprevalence estimates for years whose exposure depended on historical FOI, with little effect on estimates for 2024 (Fig. S3-S7). The model was also robust to covariate misspecification, as an uninformative covariate was correctly estimated as having no effect (Fig. S8) and including only a subset of the true covariates yielded performance between that of the fulland no-covariate models (Fig. S9-S11).

Overall, the synthetic validation demonstrates that, even when covariates explain only a small share of between-district variation, the hierarchical catalytic model recovers FOI and seroprevalence with little bias and high CrI coverage across data completeness scenario, while reporting rates are reliably recovered only where age-stratified case data are available.

Application to Java and Bali data, 2016-2024

The study area comprised 128 districts across seven provinces in Java-the world's most populous island-and Bali (Fig. 3A). West Java reported the highest absolute case counts, while Bali had the highest reported incidence rate per 100,000 population (Fig. 3B-C).

Despite differences in magnitude, all provinces showed broadly synchronous outbreak timing, with peaks in 2016, 2019, 2022, and 2024. Seroprevalence data were available from surveys conducted in 2014, 2016, 2019, 2020, and 2024 within the study area (Fig. 3D). Only the 2014 nationwide urban serosurvey, covering 22 sites in Java and Bali, was included for model fitting, where it informs historical FOI through the reconstruction of historical immunity. The remaining surveys, from Kota Yogyakarta (2016, 2019, and 2024) and Kota Denpasar (2020), were held out for out-of-sample validation. Full model specification, prior choices, and covariate selection rationale are detailed in Materials and Methods. Unless otherwise stated, results are from the full model (covariates and seroprevalence included). Model comparison across all four variants is presented at the end of this section.

Baseline FOI and reporting rate across model scenarios

The estimated national baseline FOI trajectory (2016-2024) captured the major interannual peaks across all four model scenarios (Fig. 4A). Models with covariates produced a notably higher baseline FOI (the national temporal component before covariate adjustment) in 2022 compared to models without covariates. In the covariate model, the Oceanic Niño index (ONI) term accounts for El-Niño-driven transmission in 2016 and 2024 (July to June mean ONI were 1.80 and 1.35, respectively, indicating moderate-tostrong El-Niño period), so the baseline need not rise as high in those years. In 2022, a non-El-Niño year, the baseline must absorb the full outbreak signal from case data, suggesting additional drivers of transmission beyond El Niño-Southern Oscillation variability. The estimated baseline reporting rate was the lowest in the full model (2.6%, 90% CrI: 2.1-3.1%) and the highest in case-data-only model (3.4%, 90% CrI: 2.8-4.1%) (Fig. 4B).

Covariate effects on FOI were consistent between the full model and the no-seroprevalence (or covariates-only) model (Fig. 4C). Higher-than-average rainfall was associated with lower FOI, while ONI had the largest positive effect, though with the widest credible intervals. Urbanisation and elevation are both positively associated with FOI. The directions of the rainfall and elevation effects contrast with conventional expectations in dengue epidemiology and may reflect the specific ecological context of Java (see Discussion). District-level surveillance capacity was positively associated with reporting rate, while the province-level effect was not clearly distinguishable from zero (Fig. 4D).

Spatial and temporal FOI patterns

District-level FOI trajectories showed substantial heterogeneity both within and between provinces (Fig. 5A). Province-level median trajectories were broadly similar in magnitude and followed the national baseline pattern, with most provinces recording their highest estimated FOI during the study period in 2024 (though this may partly reflect improving surveillance absorbed into FOI estimates; see Discussion). However, individual districts showed considerable variation in both the timing and magnitude of transmission peaks, reflecting heterogeneous local transmission dynamics. The spatial distribution of mean FOI over the study period showed geographic clustering, with the highest FOI districts concentrated in the central part of West Java and in the southern and eastern parts of East Java (Fig. 5B). Lower FOI was estimated across most of Central Java and Yogyakarta.

Reporting rate and surveillance heterogeneity

Estimated district-level reporting rates were spatially heterogeneous, with pockets of districts with higher reporting (Fig. 6A). At the province level, Jakarta and Bali had the highest median reporting rate (~6.6% and ~5.6%, respectively), followed by Yogyakarta (~4.3%), while Banten, West Java, Central Java, and East Java had the lowest (~2%) (Fig. 6B). At the district level, estimates ranged from 0.35% (90% CrI: 0.21-0.63%) in Wonosobo, Central Java, to 9.4% (90% CrI: 7.0-12.9%) in Kota Cirebon, West Java. The relative reporting ratio for primary versus secondary infection (𝛾) was estimated at approximately 0.2 across most provinces, consistent with prior expectation, although district-level estimates in Central Java varied more widely. Bali was a striking exception, with 𝛾 estimated at approximately 0.65, suggesting that primary infections contribute substantially to reported cases in that province (Fig. 6C). However, Bali contributes only aggregated case data, meaning that 𝛾 estimates in Bali are primarily informed by the hierarchical structure and seroprevalence data rather than age-specific case patterns, and should therefore be interpreted with caution. Surveillance capacity was positively associated with reporting rate at the district level in several provinces, though this relationship was weak, absent, or negative in Banten, Central Java, and East Java, provinces with persistently low reporting overall.

Seroprevalence and vaccine eligibility

Predicted seroprevalence showed reasonable agreement with observed values across survey sites (R² =0.61, RMSE=0.20), with greater discrepancies among younger age groups for whom sample sizes were generally smaller (Fig. 7A; age-seroprevalence curves for each site in Fig. S12). Against held-out surveys, the model underpredicted the 2016 and 2019 surveys while closely matching the 2024 survey in Kota Yogyakarta, and slightly overpredicted seroprevalence among adults in Kota Denpasar (Fig. 7B).

Estimated median seroprevalence at age 9 exceeded 60% in 79 of 128 districts by the end of 2024, with the highest values in Jakarta, West Java and East Java, while districts in Central Java, Yogyakarta and Bali were spread around the threshold (Fig. 7C). Accounting for posterior uncertainty, 49 districts had at least a 75% posterior probability of exceeding 60%, concentrated in West Java and East Java (Fig. 7D). The policy prioritisation quadrant (Fig. 7E) identified these 49 districts as high-priority candidates for vaccine introduction, while a further 30 districts had median seroprevalence above 60% but with 'insufficient' certainty. In Bali, only Kota Denpasar was classified as high priority, despite the province having the highest reported incidence among study provinces.

Misalignment between model-based and incidence-based vaccine prioritisation

We compared the model-based vaccine eligibility classification (requiring both a posterior median seroprevalence at age 9 above 60% and a posterior probability of exceeding 60% greater than 75%) against an incidence-based criterion that selected the same number of districts with the highest mean reported incidence rate over 2016-2024 (a simple approach that might be used in the absence of seroprevalence data or modelled estimates). Of the 49 districts identified as high priority by each approach, only 23 were concordant, close to the 19 expected if districts had been selected at random (49 districts with each 49/128 chance of matching model-identified districts) (Fig. 8A).

Geographic agreement between the two approaches is shown in Fig. 8B-C. Districts classified as high-priority by modelled seroprevalence but missed by the incidence-based approach were concentrated in West Java, Central Java and East Java, while districts flagged by incidence but not by the model were concentrated in Bali, Yogyakarta and also East Java. Grouping the reporting rates of each district by agreement between modelbased and incidence-based approaches (Fig. 8D), districts identified as high-priority by the model missed by the incidence-based approach had lower estimated reporting rates than concordant high-priority districts, similar to districts classified as low-priority by both, while false positive districts had the highest reporting rates. A sensitivity analysis using more lenient thresholds (median seroprevalence at age 9 > 60% versus the same number of top-incidence rate districts) showed a similar pattern, with 58 of 79 highpriority districts concordant between the two approaches, compared with 49 expected by chance (Fig. S13).

DISCUSSION

We present a Bayesian hierarchical catalytic model that jointly estimates dengue FOI from age-stratified case data, aggregate case data, and seroprevalence surveys within a single inference framework. This extends previous catalytic modelling approaches (7) (8) (9) (10) (11) (12), which typically require age-stratified data from all locations, by allowing districts with different levels of data completeness (and different age grouping format) to contribute simultaneously through a hierarchical structure and shared covariate effects. Synthetic validation demonstrated robust parameter recovery across four data completeness scenarios, even when covariates explained only small share of betweendistrict variation in FOI. Age-stratified case data are a primary source of information for distinguishing FOI and reporting rates, since the age pattern of cases reflects cumulative exposure history and relative contribution of primary and secondary infections. Consistent with this, reporting rates were well recovered whenever age-stratified data were available, with little loss when only four age groups were reported, but were underestimated in the scenario where most districts reported aggregate data only (S4). In that setting, district reporting rates were shrunk towards the shared mean. FOI and seroprevalence, in contrast, remained reasonably well estimated in S4, with only moderately higher error than in scenarios with full age stratification. Covariates contributed little to the accuracy of point estimates but improved the calibration of credible intervals. These findings have practical implications for dengue surveillance in low-and middle-income countries that do not routinely report age-stratified case data. Age-stratified reporting from a subset of locations, even with coarse age groups, can support FOI and seroprevalence estimation across a wider network of districts reporting only aggregate totals, whereas reporting rate estimates for aggregate-only districts are better interpreted in relative terms, as an indication of which districts report more or less completely, than as absolute terms.

Adding seroprevalence data to the synthetic data fits mainly improved seroprevalence estimates for cohorts whose exposure depended on historical FOI, with little effect on estimates at the end of the surveillance period (Figs. S3-S7). In the real data, however, the serosurvey also improved predictions of held-out surveys conducted after the surveillance period began, most visibly in Kota Yogyakarta in 2024 (Fig. S14). This contrast likely reflects that the synthetic data were generated with a constant reporting rate, so case data alone could constrain recent transmission, whereas in reality, reporting may change over time with improvements or disruptions in surveillance. In the real data, fitting the 2014 serosurvey also slightly worsened the fit to case data, suggesting tension between the immunity implied by the survey and case patterns that a single reporting rate per district cannot fully accommodate. Because serosurveys measure population immunity directly, independent of reporting, periodic serosurveys could help anchor estimates of population immunity, particularly in settings where surveillance performance changes over time. We further note that our synthetic data were generated under the same model structure that was then fitted, so these experiments test identifiability under a correctly specified model, and the performance reported here should be regarded as an upper bound. Our misspecification checks provide some reassurance, as an uninformative covariate was correctly estimated to have no effect, and including only a subset of the true covariates gave performance between that of the full-and no-covariate models (Figs. S8-S11).

A well-known challenge in catalytic models is the identifiability of FOI and reporting rate from case data alone, as many combinations of these parameters can produce similar expected case counts. In our framework, this trade-off was resolved mainly by the age pattern of reported cases, which reflects cumulative exposure, and, for transmission history, by seroprevalence data. The contribution of covariates depended on how much of the between-district variation in FOI they explained. In the setting most comparable to Java and Bali, where covariates explained around 10% of this variation, they mainly improved the calibration of uncertainty rather than the accuracy of point estimates. Some of the individual coefficients were weakly identified, with some biased in a consistent direction across scenarios. As covariates explained a larger share of variation, FOI accuracy improved substantially and coefficient bias largely disappeared (Fig. S1), indicating that covariates become increasingly valuable where transmission is more strongly shaped by measurable drivers. In the real data, covariates explained a small share of variation in FOI, so the estimated covariate effects (Fig. 4C) are best interpreted as associations that help the model to estimate FOI across districts, rather than as precise measures of the effect of each driver.

Applied to nine years of surveillance data across Java and Bali, the model revealed a substantial spatial heterogeneity in both FOI and reporting rates at the district level. This within-province variation underscores the importance of subnational, district-level modelling for dengue policy decisions.

The national baseline reporting rate (2.6-3.4% across model scenarios) was somewhat lower than expert panel estimates of around 5% (15), although absolute reporting rates depend on model assumptions and are better interpreted in relative terms across districts.

Bali stood out as one of the provinces with the highest reporting rates and a strikingly high relative primary reporting ratio (𝛾=0.65, compared with ~0.2 elsewhere). Bali's healthcare infrastructure, oriented towards a large international and local tourist population, may facilitate detection of milder dengue presentations. Travelers who seek care locally but are absent from the population denominator would inflate both estimated reporting rate and relative reporting ratio, as the model needs to accommodate excess cases not predicted by the resident population structure. Alternatively, if FOI in Bali is indeed lower than elsewhere, primary infections would occur at older ages, when infections are more likely to be symptomatic, prompting healthcare-seeking (16). The model also slightly overestimated seroprevalence in Bali relative to survey data, including among adults in the held-out Kota Denpasar survey (Fig. 7B), which could reflect genuinely lower historical FOI than estimated, or the absence of waning immunity in the model. While the explanations above are plausible, we note that Bali's 𝛾 estimates are less well constrained than in provinces with age-stratified data, and the high value could partly reflect identifiability limitations.

When appropriate covariates are included, the model can partially disentangle climatedriven and non-climate-driven components of inter-annual variation. The covariate model attributed the 2016 and 2024 outbreaks partly to El Niño conditions (12-month mean ONI of 1.80 and 1.35, respectively), while producing a notably higher baseline FOI in 2022, a non-El Niño year (17). This residual signal may reflect unmeasured drivers such as serotype replacement, though direct evidence is limited. A shift toward DENV-3 was documented in Bali during this period (18), which has been associated with outbreak dynamics elsewhere in Southeast Asia (19). However, whether similar shifts occurred across Java remains unknown due to limited serotype surveillance. Additionally, our model assumes equal FOI across serotypes and cannot directly capture serotype-specific dynamics. The gradual return of population mobility following relaxation of COVID-19 restrictions may also have some influence during this period (20, 21).

The negative association between above-average rainfall and FOI, and the positive association between elevation and FOI, both contrast with conventional expectations in dengue epidemiology, where rainfall is generally assumed to promote Aedes breeding and higher elevation to limit transmission through lower temperatures (22). These associations may reflect the specific context of our study region. Above-average rainfall in an already wet environment may flush larval breeding sites (23, 24). Similarly, the positive elevation effect may reflect the fact that many of Java's densely populated urban centres are located at higher elevations, and that the temperature range across Java's populated areas, even at those with higher elevations, remains within the suitable range for Aedes survival and dengue transmission (25). These findings highlight that covariate effects estimated in one ecological setting should not be directly extrapolated to other contexts without consideration of local geography.

The year 2024 recorded the highest estimated FOI across most provinces during the study period. While this may reflect a genuine increase in transmission, the assumption of constant reporting rate over time means that temporal improvements in surveillance capacity will be absorbed into FOI estimates, potentially inflating recent values. Our synthetic validation also showed that seroprevalence estimates become less accurate towards the end of surveillance period, as FOI in the final years is less constrained (Fig. 2A). Allowing time-varying reporting in future model extensions could help distinguish genuine transmission increases from improved case detection or changes in surveillance criteria, though this would introduce additional identifiability challenges that would likely require informative covariates or external data, such as periodic serosurveys, to resolve.

A key application of this framework is informing subnational vaccine targeting. The WHO 2024 position paper recommends the use of TAK-003 (Qdenga) in settings with high dengue transmission intensity, for which seroprevalence at age 9 exceeding 60% has been suggested as an indicator (5). Our estimates indicate that more than half of districts in Java and Bali (79 of 128) exceed this threshold, but applying a more conservative criterion requiring posterior probability greater than 75% reduces this number to 49. This highlights the importance of propagating uncertainty into policy decisions rather than relying on point estimates alone. Although estimates towards the end of the surveillance period are less accurate, seroprevalence at age 9 in 2024 remained well calibrated in synthetic validation and the model closely matched the held-out 2024 serosurvey in Kota Yogyakarta (Figs. 2B, 7B). In practice, vaccination decisions may be made at province or national level, and district-level estimates could be aggregated using population-weighted averages to inform decisions at broader spatial scales.

The comparison between model-based seroprevalence classification and incidence-based prioritisation revealed substantial misalignment. When uncertainty in seroprevalence estimates was accounted for, an incidence-based selection of the same size performed just slightly better than a random selection in identifying high-priority districts. Districts identified as high-priority by seroprevalence but missed by incidence were concentrated in West Java, Central Java and East Java, provinces where most districts had low estimated reporting rates. This suggests that passive surveillance underestimates transmission intensity where case detection is less complete. Conversely, districts flagged by incidence but not by the model included nearly all districts in Bali and Yogyakarta, where higher estimated reporting rates inflate reported case counts relative to the underlying seroprevalence. This pattern suggests that vaccine allocation based solely on reported incidence would systematically under-serve areas with the greatest surveillance gaps while over-prioritising areas with better case detection. Model-based seroprevalence estimation offers a more consistent basis for identifying hightransmission districts, as it integrates multiple data sources and is less susceptible to surveillance bias than incidence-based approaches. However, this approach requires investment in both data infrastructure, particularly seroprevalence surveys, covariate data and age-stratified surveillance data, as well as analytical capacity and computing infrastructure. Seroprevalence surveys, even at limited spatial coverage, are particularly valuable as they constrain population immunity independently of reporting. In this study, we fitted only the 2014 serosurvey and retained others for validation, but in practice, all available serosurveys should be included to strengthen estimation. Several limitations should be noted. First, the model assumes a constant reporting rate over the study period. As discussed above, temporal changes in surveillance capacity or case definition would be absorbed into FOI estimates, potentially biasing recent estimates upwards. Second, the model assumes equal FOI across the four dengue serotypes and does not capture serotype-specific immunity or replacement dynamics. In hyperendemic settings where serotype composition fluctuates, this simplification may obscure important drivers of inter-annual variation. Third, the model does not incorporate waning of dengue antibodies, which may lead to overestimation of seroprevalence, particularly among adults.

Fourth, while the covariate framework improves parameter identifiability, substantial uncertainty in district-level estimates remains, particularly for districts with aggregateonly case data, where reporting rate tend to be shrunk towards the shared mean.

Expanding routine age-stratified case reporting to these districts would provide the information needed to separate transmission intensity from reporting and would improve the precision of future estimates. Fifth, the temporal structure is simplified, as historical FOI uses a constant level for the distant past and district deviations from the national baseline are fixed near zero in the first year of data, so that between-district differences in that year are attributed mostly to covariates, which may bias their coefficients. Two structural choices also remain untested. Provinces serve as the intermediate grouping unit in the hierarchy, but alternative groupings, such as ecological zones or urban-rural clusters, could yield different pooling behaviour, and we did not assess sensitivity to this choice. Similarly, the AR(2) specification for district-level temporal residuals was selected a priori, and more constrained or more flexible temporal structures may be preferable in settings with longer or shorter surveillance series.

Several directions for future work emerge from this study. Allowing time-varying reporting rates, informed by external data on surveillance system changes, would improve interpretation of temporal FOI trends. Incorporating serotype-specific dynamics, potentially informed by serotype surveillance data, would enable the model to capture serotype replacement as a driver of outbreak variation. Expanding the geographic scope beyond Java and Bali to other Indonesian islands, and to other dengue-endemic countries with heterogeneous surveillance systems, would further test the generalisability of the framework.

In conclusion, our framework enables district-level estimation of dengue transmission and population immunity across a heterogeneous surveillance landscape, in which districts contribute different combinations of age-stratified and aggregate case data, with varying age groupings and formats that may change over time, supplemented by serosurveys where available. Applied to Java and Bali, these estimates showed that reported incidence is a poor guide to where population immunity is high, because differences in case detection capacity between districts bias incidence-based rankings. Such modelling complements rather than replaces improvements in surveillance. Expanding the collection of agestratified case data remains crucial, and together with periodic serosurveys, they would provide a stronger basis for monitoring transmission and for targeting vaccination where it is most needed.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

We developed a Bayesian hierarchical catalytic model to estimate time-varying dengue force-of-infection (FOI) across multiple districts and provinces simultaneously, integrating routine surveillance data and seroprevalence surveys within the parameter inference framework. The model extends previous catalytic modelling approaches (10) by accommodating multiple provinces and districts within a hierarchical structure, incorporating external covariates to inform FOI dynamics and reporting rate, allowing districts to contribute data in different formats (age-stratified or yearly aggregate only), and jointly fitting to both incidence and seroprevalence data, leveraging complementary information regarding transmission history. The youngest age included in both seroprevalence and case data was 1 year (i.e., children who have completed their first year of life), by which time maternally transferred antibodies are expected to have waned.

Study areas and data sources

Study settings

We analysed dengue transmission dynamics across seven provinces in Java and Bali, Indonesia, comprising 128 districts over the period of 2016-2024. Java and Bali together account for approximately 60% of Indonesia's population and consistently report the highest dengue burden nationally. The study area spans a wide range of urbanisation levels, population densities, and ecological conditions, providing a suitable setting for examining spatial heterogeneity in dengue transmission and reporting.

Dengue surveillance data

District-level dengue case data were obtained from the routine surveillance system of the Arbovirus Control Programme of the Indonesia Ministry of Health. Indonesia's national surveillance system reports clinically diagnosed dengue haemorrhagic fever (DHF) cases.

All seven provinces contributed to nine years of surveillance data (2016-2024).

The availability and format of case data varied across provinces and over time (Table 1).

Some provinces provided age-stratified case counts for most years, with Jakarta reporting nine age groups (1-4, 5-9, 10-14, 15-19, 20-44, 45-54, 55-64, 65-74, >74), and other provinces reporting only four age groups (1-4, 5-14, 15-44, >44). Two provinces (East Java and Bali) contributed only aggregate annual case totals for the entire study period, while several provinces had a mix of years with and without age stratification.

Population data

District-level population data were obtained from linear interpolations and extrapolations of the 2010 and 2020 census data (26, 27). Interpolations were done by sex and age group for each district.

Seroprevalence surveys

We incorporated data from five seroprevalence surveys of dengue in Indonesia. The nationwide urban serosurvey was used for model fitting, and the remaining were held out for out-of-sample validation: 1.

Nationwide urban serosurvey (28). This cross-sectional survey was conducted in 2014 across 30 urban sites in Indonesia, including sites within six provinces (all except Yogyakarta) in the study area. Participants aged 1-18 years were tested for anti-dengue IgG antibodies. A total of 2,342 individuals from 22 sites within our study area were included.

2. Pre-wolbachia trial serosurvey in Yogyakarta (29). Baseline seroprevalence data were collected in 2016 prior to the Wolbachia trial in Yogyakarta. Participants aged 1-10 years from Yogyakarta City were tested for IgG antibodies. 314 individuals were included.

Covariates were selected based on established associations with dengue transmission ecology and data availability in the study region. Data generation

To validate the model's ability to recover known parameter values under varying data completeness, we conducted a simulation study. We generated synthetic data for 50 districts (5 provinces x 10 districts per province) over 9 years, mirroring the structure of the real data. The data generation procedure was as follows: 1.

6. Population immunity and expected cases by single year of age were computed using the same annual update equations, historical FOI structure and population weighting as the model.

To assess robustness to misspecified covariates, each scenario was refitted with an additional district-level covariate drawn as an independent noise, unrelated to FOI, and with a partial covariate set in which on province-level and one district-level covariate used to generate the data were omitted.

Validation metrics

We compared posterior medians and 90% credible intervals of FOI (𝜆 𝑑,𝑡 ), reporting rates (𝜌 𝑑 ), and covariate coefficients (𝛼 𝑘 ) to the true parameter values used in simulation, together with seroprevalence at ages 5, 9 and 15 at the end of 2024. We computed root mean squared error (RMSE) and 90% credible interval coverage for each parameter class.

We also assessed how accuracy varies over the surveillance period by computing RMSE of seroprevalence at age 9 at the end of each year from 2016 to 2024.

Computational implementation

The model is implemented in the Stan probabilistic programming language and compiled via the cmdstanr package in R (37, 38). Posterior inference is performed using Hamiltonian Monte Carlo with the No-U-Turn Sampler. We ran four chains, each with 2,000 warmup and 2,000 sampling iterations, using an adaptation target acceptance rate (𝑎𝑑𝑎𝑝𝑡_𝑑𝑒𝑙𝑡𝑎) of 0.95 and maximum tree depth of 12, with a fixed random seed. In the real-data fits, R-hat values were at or below 1.01, bulk and tail effective sample sizes exceeded 400 for all quantities, and each model had at most three divergent transitions out of 8,000 draws. The energy Bayesian fraction of missing information (E-BFMI) was below the conventional threshold of 0.3 in all chains (0.23-0.30), associated with the overdispersion parameter for aggregate case data, but chains agreed closely (Table S2).

To improve sampling efficiency for hierarchical parameters, we employ non-centred parameterisations throughout. Large intermediate matrices (𝑆(𝑎, 𝑑, 𝑡), 𝑀(𝑎, 𝑑, 𝑡), 𝐼 1 (𝑎, 𝑑, 𝑡), and 𝐼 2 (𝑎, 𝑑, 𝑡)) are declared as local variables within the model block and not stored in the output, reducing memory usage and output file size. These quantities can be reconstructed from the saved parameter posterior samples.

Post-hoc analyses

Model comparison

We compared the four model scenarios using approximate leave-one-out-cross-validation (LOO-CV) via Pareto-smoothed importance sampling (PSIS-LOO (39)). Expected log pointwise predictive density (ELPD) differences between models were computed from generated quantities in Stan. We assessed the reliability of LOO estimates using Pareto-k diagnostics, with k > 0.7 indicating potentially unreliable estimates for individual observations. LOO-CV was performed separately for case-only and joint caseseroprevalence likelihoods to evaluate model fit under both data configurations.

Seroprevalence prediction and vaccine eligibility

Using posterior FOI samples from the full model, we predicted age-specific seroprevalence for each district at the end of 2024 by computing cumulative exposure from birth year through 2024. For children aged 9 years, this exposure (2016-2024) lies entirely within the surveillance period.

The WHO 2024 position paper on dengue vaccines recommends the use of TAK-003 (Qdenga) in children aged 6-16 years in settings with high dengue transmission intensity (5). To determine transmission intensity, the WHO suggests that a dengue seroprevalence exceeding 60% by age 9 could be considered an indicator of high transmission, while noting that threshold cut-offs should be decided by individual countries. We adopted this criterion to assess vaccine eligibility across districts. For each district, we estimated the median posterior seroprevalence at age 9 and computed the posterior probability that this seroprevalence exceeds 60%, 𝑃(𝑠𝑒𝑟𝑜𝑝𝑟𝑒𝑣𝑎𝑙𝑒𝑛𝑐𝑒 𝑑,𝐴=9 > 0.60). Districts were classified as high-priority candidates for vaccine introduction when this posterior probability exceeded 75%, providing a more conservative criterion that accounts for estimation uncertainty.

Comparison with incidence-based prioritisation

To assess whether routine surveillance data alone could identify the same high-priority districts, we compared the model-based seroprevalence classification against a simpler incidence-based criterion, which selected the 𝐾 districts with the highest mean reported incidence rate over 2016-2024, where 𝐾 is the number of districts classified as high priority by the model. We evaluated agreement using the strict criterion (median seroprevalence at age 9 > 60% and posterior probability > 75%) and the lenient criterion (median > 60%), and compared the number of districts selected by both approaches with that expected if 𝐾 districts were selected at random.
