Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. Abstract

Wildfire smoke has become an increasing public health concern in the contiguous United States (CONUS). Previous studies have provided high-resolution estimates of either total PM 2.5 or wildfire-related PM 2.5 during the past two decades, but few have focused on the most recent wildfire-intensive years, and jointly estimated both to quantify wildfire contributions to total PM 2.5 exposure. This study developed a high-resolution machine learning framework to estimate daily 1-km concentrations of total and wildfire-related PM 2.5 across the CONUS from 2018 to 2024. This framework incorporates chemical precursors and wildfire smoke density as predictors and relies on publicly accessible data sources enabling future updates. The model achieved high performance with cross-validated R² of 0.72 and 0.89 for daily and annual estimates, respectively. Elevated total PM 2.5 concentrations were observed along the west coast and in several metropolitan areas across the CONUS. Wildfire smoke contributed substantially to total PM 2.5 concentrations during major wildfire years, accounting for approximately 35% of PM 2.5 along the west coast in 2020, and over 25% in the eastern U.S. in 2023. Wildfire-related PM 2.5 mortality accounted for 20% of PM 2.5 -attributable deaths along the west coast in 2020 (30 times that in 2019), and 24% in the northeastern U.S. in 2023 (14 times that in 2019). These findings highlight the growing role of wildfire smoke as a major driver of total PM 2.5 exposure and associated health risks in both western and eastern U.S., underscoring the need for sustained nationwide public health interventions to reduce wildfire smoke exposure.

1 Introduction

Fine particulate matter (PM 2.5 ) is associated with many adverse health outcomes, such as lung cancer, diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases. [1] [2] [3] [4] The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) ranked PM 2.5 as the third leading risk factors globally in 2023. 5 Therefore, accurately quantifying population exposure to PM 2.5 at high spatial and temporal resolution is essential for assessing exposure disparities and associated health burdens. Higher spatial resolution can substantially reduce exposure misclassification in health risk assessment and environmental epidemiological studies.

Higher temporal resolution can also help identify short-term peak exposures that would be obscured in annual averages. Due to stringent regulation of anthropogenic emissions in the United States (U.S.), ambient PM 2.5 concentrations have shown an overall decreasing trend over the past two decades. 6 However, increasingly severe wildfire events in recent years have dramatically elevated short-term PM 2.5 exposure at the daily scale. 7 Such episodic pollution events cannot be adequately captured by annual average estimates.

Previous studies have applied machine learning (ML) algorithms to estimate high-resolution ambient ground-level PM 2.5 concentrations in the U.S. As one of the pioneering and most widely cited studies, Di et al. developed an ensemble model combining random forest, gradient boosting, and neural networks to estimate daily average PM 2.5 concentrations at 1-km spatial resolution across the contiguous United States (CONUS) from 2000 to 2015. 8 Their model incorporated satellite-derived aerosol optical depth (AOD), chemical transport model (CTM) simulations, meteorological variables, and land-use characteristics as predictors. 8 Subsequent studies have adopted similar frameworks to estimate 1-km daily PM 2.5 concentrations across the U.S. for different periods, using slightly different predictor sets and ML algorithms, such as deep forest and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost). [9] [10] [11] [12] [13] [14] [15] [16] However, most existing studies either focused on periods before 2020 or trained models over long historical timeframes starting in the early 2000s, when ambient PM 2.5 concentrations were primarily driven by anthropogenic emissions. [8] [9] [10] [12] [13] [14] [15] [16] [17] [18] [19] In contrast, few studies have specifically focused on the most recent decade with substantial wildfire smoke impacts in the U.S. 11, 20 Among the studies covering these wildfire-influenced years, Wei et al. developed a global model, which may not fully capture U.S.-specific emission patterns, regulatory environments, and wildfire characteristics. 20 Moreover, few studies explicitly incorporated wildfire-related predictors to characterize the complex relationships between wildfire activity and PM 2.5 concentrations. 14, 16, 21 As wildfire smoke has become the major driver of ambient PM 2.5 in U.S. recently, many studies have attempted to apportion total PM 2.5 concentrations to wildfire and non-wildfire sources. The first type of studies has relied on fire contributions simulated by CTMs. Some of these studies directly derived wildfire-attributable PM 2.5 using CTMs with fire emission inventories, 22, 23 while others first obtained the proportion of CTM-based wildfire PM 2.5 in CTM-based total PM 2.5 and then applied the proportion to total PM 2.5 concentrations predicted from ML models. 24, 25 Although these CTM-based approaches offer strong physical interpretability, they may introduce substantial uncertainty in wildfire attribution due to uncertainties in fire emission inventories and CTM simulations, as the attribution is not directly constrained by ground-level observations. As a complement to CTM-based approaches, the second type of studies has leveraged statistical approaches to partition wildfire and non-wildfire air pollutants based on ground-level and satellite-based data sources. 7, 16, [26] [27] [28] [29] One widely used statistical approach, developed by Burke et al., integrated ground-level PM 2.5 measurements with smoke plume data to estimate the difference between observed total PM 2.5 and counterfactual non-wildfire PM 2.5 concentrations. 7 This framework was further extended by Childs et al., who incorporated these wildfire PM 2.5 estimates into a ML model to produce 10-km daily estimates of wildfire PM 2.5 concentrations across the CONUS. 28 Although the smoke plume boundaries drawn by National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) analysts used in this approach may lead to bias in wildfire attribution, this approach has been well validated against ground-level observations and can be extended to large spatial scales due to relatively low computation costs. 28 Nevertheless, a gap remains in the proportion of wildfire PM 2.5 in total PM 2.5 across the U.S., 16 particularly during recent wildfire-dominated years.

To address these critical gaps in recent wildfire-era exposure characterization, this study focuses on the wildfire-influenced period after 2018 and incorporates wildfire-related predictors into a machine learning framework. Specifically, the objectives of this study are to (1) provide accurate 1-km daily estimates of ambient PM 2.5 concentrations across the CONUS from 2018 to 2024; (2) estimate 1-km daily wildfire-related PM 2.5 concentrations and their annual contribution to total PM 2.5 ; and (3) quantify county-level long-term mortality burdens attributable to both total and wildfire PM 2.5.

2 Methods

2.1 Data collection

Data were collected across the CONUS between 2018 and 2024 from multiple sources.

Hourly ground-level PM 2.5 mass concentrations were obtained from the Air Quality System (AQS) operated by United States Environmental Protection Agency (U.S. EPA), with a total of 1013 regulatory monitoring stations across the CONUS. 30 To improve our ML model performance, a series of relevant and available features were selected, including aerosol optical depth (AOD), atmospheric precursor concentrations, meteorological variables, population, economy level, land-use variables, and wildfire indicator. Details can be found in the Supporting Information (SI) Table S1.

AOD has been successfully used to predict ambient PM 2.5 , and is commonly used in ML-based PM 2.5 studies. Satellite AOD at 550 nm was retrieved from the Multi-angle Implementation of Atmospheric Correction (MAIAC) AOD product (MCD19A2), which combined Terra and Aqua Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) products and had a 1-km resolution. 31 To address missing values of the satellite AOD product (e.g., cloud coverage), additional gap-free modeled hourly AOD product was used from Goddard Earth Observing System composition forecast (GEOS-CF) in 0.25° 0.25° resolution (method for imputation of missing 1-km satellite AOD described in Section 2.2). 32 In addition, simulated ground-level PM 2.5 concentrations were extracted from the GEOS-CF model (0.25° 0.25°, hourly). 32 The GEOS-CF product also provided the simulated concentrations of multiple other atmospheric PM 2.5 precursors, including ammonia (NH 3 ), nitrogen oxide (NO), nitrogen dioxide (NO 2 ), sulfur dioxide (SO 2 ), formaldehyde, benzene, toluene, xylenes, and isoprene. 32 We also included anthropogenic emission of monoterpenes and non-methane volatile organic compounds (VOCs) from the Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service (CAMS) global dataset (0.1° 0.1°, monthly) to capture the possible impact of other precursors. 33 Meteorological conditions and their vertical profiles are also vital in predicting ambient PM 2.5 concentrations with high spatiotemporal resolution. Therefore, we included temperature, dewpoint temperature, precipitation, solar radiation, northward and eastward components of wind speed, evaporation, surface pressure, and boundary layer height (BLH) from ERA5-Land (0.1° 0.1°, hourly) and ERA5 (0.25° 0.25°, hourly) reanalysis datasets. 34, 35 Since the CONUS experienced severe wildfire events during the study period (2018-2024), we obtained wildfire smoke density (polygon, daily) from NOAA's Hazard Mapping System (HMS) smoke product. 36 Four levels of smoke density are reported in the HMS dataset, including none, light, medium, and heavy smoke.

As indicators of anthropometric activity, emissions, and terrain effects, we included additional datasets. We collected WorldPop population (1 km, annual) and Visible Infrared Imaging Radiometer Suite (VIIRS) nighttime lights (500m, monthly). 37, 38 We also considered several land-use variables as proxies of local emissions, including normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) from MOD13A3 (1 km, monthly), 39 percentage of 16 land cover types from National Land Cover Database (NLCD, 30 m, annual), 40 road density using all roads from Topologically Integrated Geographic Encoding and Referencing (TIGER) database (annual). 41 In addition, elevation data were obtained from Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM, 90 m). 42 All hourly data were aggregated to daily average using UTC time zone for consistency across the CONUS. And all raster data were projected onto a predefined 1-km resolution grid covering the CONUS using the NAD83 / CONUS Albers Equal Area Conic projection coordinated system (EPSG:5070).

2.2 Model development of total PM 2.5

As other studies have previously described, total PM 2.5 prediction was developed using a four-dimensional space-time extremely randomized trees (4D-STET) model. 20, 43 Extremely randomized trees (extra-trees) is a tree-based ensemble learning algorithm, which introduces additional randomness in split selection, thereby improving robustness to noisy data and highly correlated predictors compared with the random forest model. The "four-dimensional space-time" framework incorporates the Euclidean spherical spatial coordinates and triangular spiral temporal representations to account for spatiotemporal heterogeneity. To address the missingness of satellite AOD data, we adopted a two-stage modeling strategy. 20 The first stage fit the 4D-STET model to fill the AOD gaps using simulated AOD from GEOS-CF, selected meteorological data, NDVI, elevation, and spatiotemporal terms.

Then the second stage applied the 4D-STET model with the gap-filled AOD, all meteorological data, population, nighttime lights, all land-use variables, and wildfire smoke density to predict ground-level daily average total PM 2.5 concentrations. The hyperparameters in the two 4D-STET models were carefully tuned based on minimizing the out-of-bag (OOB) errors. Details about model structures and hyperparameter tuning are available in SI Section S1. With the optimal hyperparameters, the final 4D-STET model was used to predict the daily average total PM 2.5 concentrations at each grid across the CONUS and in each day between 2018 and 2024.

The final 4D-STET model of ground-level total PM 2.5 was evaluated by 10-fold cross-validation (CV) approaches. Three CV strategies were applied: Sample-based CV, station-based CV, and day-based CV. 20 Specifically, all data samples, regulatory monitoring stations, or days were randomly divided into ten folds separately, with nine folds used for training and the remaining one for validation. This procedure was repeated ten times so that each fold served as the validation dataset once. Although sample-based CV was most commonly used in previous ML-based studies, the assumption of independent samples in sample-based CV was likely violated for spatiotemporally correlated PM 2.5 data in this study, which potentially resulted in data leakage and overly optimistic estimates of model performance. In contrast, station-based and day-based CV can account for spatial and temporal autocorrelation within the dataset and evaluate model generalizability from both spatial and temporal perspectives. Spatial generalization was particularly important because the regulatory monitoring stations were not evenly distributed across CONUS. We pooled the observations and predictions from validation datasets in ten runs, and then calculated a series of out-of-sample evaluation metrics, including the coefficient of determination (R² ), root mean squared error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), mean bias error (MBE), and normalized RMSE (NRMSE, defined as RMSE divided by the mean of all observations). To evaluate monthly and annual averages of daily total PM 2.5 prediction, we also estimated the out-of-sample performance metrics of monthly and annual average predictions based on the above pooled daily CV results, using grid-months and grid-years with at least 80% of observations, respectively.

In addition to evaluating the model predictive performance, we examined model interpretability using the mean absolute SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values to quantify the relative contributions of individual features to PM 2.5 predictions. 44, 45 Partial dependence plots (PDPs) were further generated for selected features ranking among the highest according to their mean absolute SHAP values, in order to characterize the marginal relationships between total PM 2.5 levels and individual features. 46

3.4 PM 2.5 -related mortality burden

Between 2018 and 2024, the number of deaths attributable to total PM 2.5 across the CONUS ranged from 110,000 (95% uncertainty interval (UI): 79,500 -138,000) in 2019 to 152,000 (110,000 -191,000) in 2021 (Table S7). Unlike the decline between 2000 and 2020, the PM 2.5 -attributable mortality did not exhibit a monotonic trend between 2018 and 2024. Figure 5a further shows the county-level mortality rate attributable to total PM 2.5 across the CONUS (details in Table S8). The southeastern U.S. consistently experienced the highest mortality burden from ambient PM 2.5 exposure during 2018-2024, whereas the U.S. west coast showed relatively high mortality burden only in 2020 and 2021, which were severely wildfire-influenced years. Taking 2021 as an example, the East South Central, West South Central, and Pacific Divisions had attributable mortality rates of 82.3 (59.7 -103.7), 72.8 (52.9 -91.6), and 63.4 (46.2 -79.7) per 100,000, respectively. In contrast, the lowest attributable mortality rate was observed in the New England Division in northeastern U.S., which reached only 35.2 (25.5 -44.5) per 100,000 (Table S7).

For wildfire-related PM 2.5 , the attributable number of deaths across the CONUS ranged from 2,870 (2,080 -3,610) in 2019 to 25,100 (18,200 -31,600) in 2023 (Table S7). Figure 5b provides the county-level mortality rate due to wildfire PM 2.5 exposure (details in Table S8). It can be observed that the wildfire-related mortality burden was higher along the U.S. west coast in 2018, 2020, and 2021, whereas higher burdens were found in the eastern U.S. in 2022 and 2023. Compared with 2019 which experienced the lowest wildfire impact, the wildfire-related mortality burden increased nearly 30-fold in the Pacific Division in 2020 (0.48 to 13.83 per 100,000, accounting for 1.1% to 20.4%) and about 6-fold in the East South Central Division in 2023 (2.59 to 15.48 per 100,000, accounting for 4.1% to 20.6%). In contrast, the non-wildfire PM 2.5 corresponded to relatively stable attributable deaths across the study period (Table S7), except slightly increase in 2020 and 2021 due to the COVID-19 pandemic. Therefore, the variations in attributable deaths of total PM 2.5 during the study period were dominated by wildfire smoke events.

4 Discussion

This study provides high-resolution estimates of total and wildfire-related PM 2.5 concentrations across the CONUS during recent wildfire-influenced years and highlights the growing role of wildfire smoke in shaping PM 2.5 exposure patterns.

Our results suggest that while the U.S. west coast experienced the most severe episodic pollution events, elevated total PM 2.5 concentrations were also observed in several metropolitan areas across both western and eastern regions. The contribution of wildfire smoke to total PM 2.5 was over 30% during major wildfire years in the west coast and over 20% during long-range smoke transport events in the east coast, highlighting the increasing influence of climate-driven wildfire activity on air quality.

Consistent with these exposure patterns, the mortality burden attributable to wildfire-related PM 2.5 also showed considerable interannual variability, underscoring the growing public health importance of wildfire smoke in the U.S.

4.1 Comparison of ML performance with previous studies

The CV performance of the ML model in this study was broadly consistent with, and in some cases slightly better than, those reported in previous studies focusing on the prediction of daily average total PM 2.5 (Table S3).

Since exposure and health risk assessments primarily depend on the accuracy of absolute exposure estimates, RMSE was considered as the primary CV metric. However, as different studies covered different time periods with quite different total PM 2.5 concentrations, NRMSE provides a more appropriate basis for comparison. The sample-based CV NRMSE at the daily scale in this study was 0.38 across the CONUS, which was similar to values reported by Wei et al. (0.40; CONUS, 2000-2020) 15 and Kayastha et al. (0.37; CONUS, 2018-2022). 11 The sample-based CV NRMSEs in the Mountain and Pacific Divisions reached 0.45 and 0.47, respectively, which were lower than that reported by Reid et al. (0.67; western U.S., 2008-2018). 12 Similar patterns were observed for station-based CV. Notably, the comparable or improved performance in this study was achieved under more challenging modeling conditions. For example, Wei et al. and Reid et al. covered the early 2000s with higher total PM 2.5 concentrations which may be easier to predict due to stronger signal-to-noise ratios; 12, 15 and Kayastha et al. developed predictions at a coarser 4-km spatial resolution instead of 1-km resolution. 11

Many previous studies did not report NRMSE values, but instead reported CV R² , which we considered as our secondary evaluation metric. The sample-based CV R² in this study (0.72) falls within the range reported in previous studies in U.S. and North America (0.62-0.83). 9, 11, 12, [14] [15] [16] 18 Similarly, the station-based CV R² in this study (0.63) is comparable to previously reported values (0.59-0.86). 8, 9, 11-13, 15, 16 The highest station-based CV R² (0.86) was reported in Di et al. 8 However, many of these studies were conducted under substantially different pollution regimes. Di et al.

developed daily PM 2.5 estimates from 2000 to 2015, when anthropogenic emissions dominated the ambient PM 2.5 levels across the CONUS. 8 In contrast, the 2018-2024 period in this study was characterized by generally low background PM 2.5 concentrations on non-wildfire days but episodic extreme concentrations on wildfire days. Because R² depends on the total variance of the observations, it can be lower in settings with limited background variability but occasional extreme events, even when absolute prediction errors remain small. Therefore, NRMSE may provide more informative measures of model performance for exposure assessment in recent wildfire-influenced periods.

In addition, a limited number of previous studies reported CV performance on aggregated monthly and annual average concentrations. The station-based CV NRMSE of annual averages was 0.19 in this study, lower than 0.26 in Wei et al. 15 and 0.25 in Shen et al. 17

4.2 Interpretation of important features in the ML model

Section 3.1 identified the most important features in the 4D-STET model and further examined their trends with ground-level total PM 2.5 predictions using PDPs (Figure S3), including GEOS-CF simulated PM 2.5 , formaldehyde, ammonia, as well as AOD, temperature, dewpoint temperature, wildfire smoke density, BLH, and northward wind speed. Some trends are easily understandable, such as the positive associations with GEOS-CF simulated PM 2.5 , AOD, and wildfire smoke density, whereas other trends require more explanations.

GEOS-CF simulated PM 2.5 was the most influential feature based on its mean absolute SHAP value and exhibited a positive marginal association with predicted ground-level total PM 2.5 (Figure S3 (a)). As a global chemical transport modeling system, GEOS-CF provides spatially and temporally complete, process-based estimates that integrate information on emissions, atmospheric chemistry, transport, deposition, and meteorological conditions. It therefore served as a baseline estimate of regional and temporal PM 2.5 variation for the ML model. A conceptually similar modeling strategy has been used in previous studies, in which spatially interpolated regulatory monitoring observations provided an observation-based baseline estimate for subsequent ML prediction. 52 Compared with interpolation based primarily on the geographic configuration and AQS monitoring data, the GEOS-CF field can represent pollution transport and chemical processes across areas with sparse monitoring coverage.

Positive marginal associations were observed between total PM 2.5 predictions and GEOS-CF simulated ammonia and formaldehyde from PDPs in Figure S3 (i) and (c), respectively. This can be interpreted by secondary aerosol formation mechanisms.

Ammonia is one of the most important precursors to PM 2.5 and plays a vital role in the formation of secondary inorganic aerosols (SIAs), such as ammonium nitrate (NH 4 NO 3 ) and ammonium sulfate ((NH 4 ) 2 SO 4 ). [53] [54] [55] Several studies also suggest that reducing ammonia emissions can efficiently reduce ambient PM 2.5 pollution. 56, 57 One source apportionment study across the CONUS shows that secondary nitrate and secondary sulfate contributed 30.8%-74.6% of total PM 2.5 mass across different regions in the CONUS, which are the dominant components of PM 2.5. 58 This can possibly explain why ammonia was identified as an important feature in our model. In contrast, although formaldehyde is not a direct precursor of PM 2.5 , it is typically used as a proxy of volatile organic compound (VOC) reactivity as formaldehyde is the oxidation product of many anthropogenic and biogenic VOCs. [59] [60] [61] [62] As VOCs play critical roles in the formation of secondary organic aerosols (SOAs), formaldehyde may function as an indicator of SOAs. [59] [60] [61] [62] The source apportionment results also show that SOAs accounted for 1.2%-18.2% across the CONUS. 58 That is possibly why formaldehyde was selected as one of the most important features in this study.

Besides the above precursors or proxy compounds, some essential meteorological features were also identified. Figure S3 (e) shows a decreasing trend between BLH and total PM 2.5 predictions. The BLH can depress the diffusion of ground-level PM 2.5 to the upper atmosphere, so PM 2.5 usually accumulates under the BLH and a lower BLH will then exacerbate the ground-level PM 2.5 pollution. 63, 64 Many field papers have observed their negative associations. [63] [64] [65] The associations with temperature and dewpoint temperature are more complicated.

Figure S3 (b) exhibits a U-shaped trend between total PM 2.5 predictions and temperature. Higher temperatures over 20 are associated with higher PM 2.5 concentrations, probably because higher temperatures can promote atmospheric photochemical reactions to form SOAs 54, 66, 67 and higher temperatures are often correlated with more wildfire events and more biogenic VOC emissions. 68 By contrast, lower temperatures below 5 are also associated with higher PM 2.5 concentrations. This is because wintertime lower temperatures are typically accompanied by a shallow boundary layer and frequent near-surface temperature inversions, which reduce atmospheric mixing and trap PM 2.5 near the ground surface. 65, [69] [70] [71] Relatively low temperatures may be also correlated with more residential heating emissions, such as oil and wood combustion. Besides, low temperature favors ammonium nitrate formation, which is a major component of PM 2.5. 72, 73 The above complicated underlying mechanisms shape the nonlinear patterns of the trend with temperature. A similar U-shaped pattern was found for dewpoint temperature in Figure S3 (f). Lower dewpoint temperatures, i.e., low humidity, are associated with higher total PM 2.5 predictions, probably because low humidity corresponds to weaker wet removal of PM 2.5 , 74, 75 and is also typically related to wintertime with shallow BLH and stagnant air. As the dewpoint temperature increases over 10, the PM 2.5 predictions also slightly increase. One of the possible underlying mechanisms is the hygroscopic growth of particles, whereby high relative humidity promotes water uptake by aerosols, increasing particle size and mass concentrations. 64, 76, 77 In addition, higher humidity may promote aqueous-phase secondary aerosol formation as well as the gas-to-particle partitioning, especially ammonium nitrate. 64, 78, 79

4.3 Interpretation of spatiotemporal variations in PM 2.5

The spatiotemporal variations in total PM 2.5 can be influenced by multiple factors, including primary emission sources, secondary aerosol formation, and topography effects. The U.S. west coast shows a relatively higher annual average total PM 2.5 concentrations than other CONUS regions, especially in north California, central valley of California, and Los Angeles Basin. Specifically, north California, as well as south Oregon and east Washington, show substantially higher total PM 2.5 concentrations in severe wildfire years, such as 2018, 2020, and 2021. Additionally, in these areas, Figure S6 supports that wildfire-related PM 2.5 was higher than other regions, and Figure S7 demonstrates that average total PM 2.5 was higher in summer and fall than the other two cold seasons. Since these three areas do not have very populated metropolitan areas, anthropogenic emissions cannot be the major driver of the elevated PM 2.5 level. All the above evidence validates that the high total PM 2.5 concentrations in these areas are dominated by severe wildfire smoke. In contrast, as the second largest metropolitan area in the U.S., the poor air quality in the Los Angeles Basin is dominantly impacted by anthropogenic emission sources and topography effects. Previous source apportionment analysis reveals that fresh traffic emissions, residual oil combustion from ships and oil refineries, and biomass burning possibly due to residential heating accounted for 13.4%, 13.5%, and 4.1% of total PM 2.5 mass, respectively. 80 The strong solar radiation and warm temperature in the Los Angeles Basin can also promote photochemical secondary aerosol formation.

Besides, due to proximity to the Pacific Ocean, sea salt contributed 20.6% of total PM 2.5 , which is a unique but very important source and may act as a critical role for secondary aerosols near the sea-land interface. 80, 81 Last but not least, the Los Angeles Basin is surrounded by several mountains and often suffers from marine inversions, so the air pollutants are typically trapped in the cooler air near the ground surface. 80 All the abovementioned factors exacerbate the PM 2.5 pollution in the Los Angeles Basin. The central valley of California combines the adverse characteristics of both north California and Los Angeles Basin, which makes it one of the worst PM 2.5 polluted region across the CONUS. The central valley is impacted by both severe wildfire smoke in summertime and anthropogenic emissions from traffic and agricultural burning sources. 82 Additionally, the topographical features of this valley as well as the strong temperature inversion and shallow BLH in wintertime further aggravate the PM 2.5 pollution in this area (Figure S7 (d)). 82 In addition to the U.S. west coast, vast areas in eastern U.S. also show moderately elevated total PM 2.5 levels, where some hotspots can be observed around those metropolitan areas. Overall, SIA formation, including secondary sulfate and nitrate, had a higher contribution to PM 2.5 in eastern U.S. (48.6%-74.6%) than western U.S.

(30.8%-44.2%) and came from slightly different sources. The Ohio River Valley showed relatively high total PM 2.5 concentrations, especially in summer and winter (Figure S7). In summer, secondary sulfate dominated the PM 2.5 mass concentrations, which was likely contributed from industrial emissions, such as vehicle manufacturing, steel production, petroleum refining, and coal power plants. 58, 83 More sunlight, higher temperature, and higher humidity in summer can promote photochemical reactions for secondary sulfate formation. By contrast, in wintertime, secondary nitrate became the major contributor of PM 2.5 concentrations, which possibly originated from vehicle exhaust, agricultural emissions, and power plants, 58, 83 because lower temperature favors ammonium nitrate formation and lower BLH and more stagnant air accumulates these particles near the ground surface. 72, 73 Compared with the Ohio River Valley, the South Division, especially Texas, demonstrated a quite different seasonal pattern, in which total PM 2.5 concentrations were higher in spring and summer. One previous study near U.S.-Mexico border in Texas reveals that biomass burning and crustal dust accounted for 12.8% and 12.7% of PM 2.5 mass, respectively. 84 The agricultural burning and wildfire smoke, as well as dust were transported from Mexico and southwest U.S. to Texas during spring and summer.

Besides, SOA concentrations were much higher in South Division than other areas in eastern U.S., because there were higher VOC emissions in these areas from industry, transportation, and biogenic sources. 58, 85 The photochemical reactions with these VOCs are the major pathways to form SOAs in summer. Different from Ohio River Valley and south U.S., the total PM 2.5 hotspot between Washington D.C. and New York City was mostly contributed by urban anthropogenic emissions, including road traffic, biomass burning (e.g. wood combustion for heating), and residual oil (e.g. ships and industries). 86, 87

4.4 Implications for population exposure

The high-resolution mapping of PM 2.5 concentrations is critical for reducing possible exposure misclassification. Many EPA regulatory monitoring stations are located far away from the local sources, so using the nearest EPA regulatory monitoring data or their spatial interpolations, such as inverse distance weighting and kriging, may not adequately capture the impact of emission sources on PM 2.5 concentrations in those areas without regulatory monitoring. Machine learning models could address this limitation by incorporating many source-related predictors, such as road density and emission inventories. Some widely used PM 2.5 products are available only at relatively coarse spatial resolutions (e.g., 10 km and 12 km). 88, 89 To evaluate the impact of spatial resolution, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by aggregating our 1-km estimates into a 10-km resolution. In addition to high spatial resolution, this study also predicted PM 2.5 at a high temporal resolution, i.e., daily averages, which can further reduce exposure misclassification associated with temporal averaging. Although annual average estimates are very useful for long-term health risk assessment, annual averages may obscure substantial day-to-day variations in PM 2.5 concentrations, particularly in those regions influenced by mask short-term episodic pollution such as wildfire smoke. In contrast, daily exposure estimates can better characterize exposure dynamics, capture peak exposure metrics in addition to long-term cumulative exposure, and facilitate future epidemiological studies about short-term health effects.

Furthermore, the daily estimates can enable the separation of wildfire and non-wildfire PM 2.5 contributions. Although many PM 2.5 models have been developed in previous studies, relatively few distinguish between wildfire and non-wildfire contributions.

This separation is essential because the two sources typically exhibit distinct temporal patterns and potentially different exposure-response relationships, thus possibly contributing disproportionately to short-term health risks. Previous studies have suggested that short-term exposure to wildfire-related PM 2.5 may have much stronger respiratory health effects than non-wildfire PM 2.5 exposure. 90, 91 Future epidemiological studies can also rely on this separation to examine the associations between wildfire PM 2.5 and more health outcomes. Therefore, the finer temporal-resolution estimates can further improve the precision in exposure and health risk assessment.

Understanding population exposure to PM 2.5 differs from simply mapping PM 2.5 concentrations, since highly polluted areas may have relatively low population densities. Table S6 show the population-weighted annual average total PM 2.5 exposure in nine divisions, which can be compared with unweighted annual average total concentrations in Table S5. Taking Pacific Division as an example, the population-weighted average exposure of total PM 2.5 (9.46 μg/m 3 ) was higher than the average concentration (6.82 μg/m 3 ), whereas the population-weighted average exposure of wildfire-related PM 2.5 (0.75 μg/m 3 ) was lower than the average concentration (1.31 μg/m 3 ). The higher value of total PM 2.5 exposure was possibly because a large fraction of population resides in the metropolitan areas, such as Los Angeles and the Bay Area, where total PM 2.5 mass is dominated by urban anthropogenic emissions. The opposite finding for wildfire-related PM 2.5 is also plausible, since wildfires often occur in mountainous and forested regions, where smoke concentration can be relatively high, but population density is low. Taking Middle Atlantic Division as another example, the finding about total PM 2.5 was similar to that observed in the Pacific Division. The population-weighted average exposure of total PM 2.5 (7.52 μg/m 3 ) was higher than the average concentration (6.22 μg/m 3 ). Nevertheless, the population-weighted average exposure of wildfire-related PM 2.5 (0.71 μg/m 3 ) was slightly higher than the average concentration (0.64 μg/m 3 ).

This pattern is likely attributable to the long-range transport of wildfire smoke from Canada, which often affects densely populated urban corridors in this region. Hence, the above findings highlight the importance of considering population distribution when interpreting PM 2.5 concentration patterns in exposure assessment.

4.5 Implications for public health

Unlike the declining temporal trend observed in the first two decades of the 21st

Century across the CONUS, 15 the mortality burden attributable to total PM 2.5 did not show a clear decline between 2018 and 2024. One important reason is that recent increases in wildfire smoke offset the improvements in anthropogenic PM 2.5 reduction.

As shown in Figure S9, non-wildfire PM 2.5 exposure driven by anthropogenic emissions continued to show a gradual decline in most divisions from 2018 to 2024.

However, wildfire-related PM 2.5 exposure was the primary driver of the year-to-year variation in total PM 2.5 exposure. Besides, population aging and increase in baseline mortality due to COVID-19 pandemic may also have contributed to the stagnation in attributable mortality burden.

Since wildfire was the major driver of the variations in total PM 2.5 exposure and associated mortality risks, we compared our wildfire PM 2.5 related mortality burden with those reported in previous studies. Ma et al. estimated around 11,400 deaths per year attributable to wildfire PM 2.5 during 2007-2020, 92 while Zhang et al. reported approximately 24,100 deaths per year during 2006-2020 and Qiu et al. reported about 24,200 deaths per year during 2011-2020.93, 94 Our estimate of approximately 12,300 deaths per year during 2018-2024 was therefore comparable to that of Ma et al. but approximately half the estimates reported by Zhang et al. and Qiu et al. [92] [93] [94] These differences may largely result from different exposure-response functions. The three previous studies applied their own developed wildfire-specific exposure-response functions, which generally indicated substantially higher health effects than those functions for all-source PM 2.5 . In a sensitivity analysis, Qiu et al. estimated roughly 6,000-9,000 deaths per year using several all-source PM 2.5 exposure-response functions, substantially lower than 24,200 deaths estimated using wildfire-specific functions. 93 In the present study, applying the same all-source PM 2.5 exposure-response function to wildfire and non-wildfire PM 2.5 enabled a consistent comparison of the mortality burdens attributable to the two components. Nevertheless, this approach assumes equivalent toxicity and may yield conservative estimates of wildfire-related mortality if wildfire PM 2.5 is more harmful than average total PM 2.5. 95 Differences in study periods, wildfire PM 2.5 exposure estimates, population, and baseline mortality data may also contribute to variation among the reported mortality burdens.

We then examined the spatial variability of wildfire-related PM 2.5 -related mortality.

The county-level results in Figure 5b reveal that western U.S. counties suffered from a dramatically higher mortality burden due to wildfire PM 2.5 than eastern U.S.

counties in 2018, 2020, and 2021, whereas eastern U.S. counties experienced moderately higher mortality burdens attributable to wildfire PM 2.5 in 2022 and 2023.

While county-level patterns generally follow fire geography, spatially aggregated division-level estimates exhibited different patterns after considering population weighting.

Since severe wildfire smoke hit both western and eastern U.S. in 2021, we use this year as an illustrative example.

The average concentration of wildfire PM 2.5 in Pacific Division (2.31 μg/m 3 ) was higher than that in Middle Atlantic (0.77 μg/m 3 ) and West North Central (1.55 μg/m 3 ) Divisions. However, the population-weighted average exposure of wildfire PM 2.5 in Pacific Division (0.82 μg/m 3 ) was comparable to that in Middle Atlantic Division (0.84 μg/m 3 ) and substantially lower than that in West North Central Division (1.43 μg/m 3 ). Consistent with these exposure patterns, the mortality rate due to wildfire PM 2.5 in Pacific Division in 2021 (5.98 deaths per 100,000) was similar to that in Middle Atlantic Division (5.85) and much lower than West North Central Division (10.77). When averaged over 2018-2024, the highest wildfire-attributable mortality rates were observed in West North Central (6.80 deaths per 100,000) and East South Central (6.69) Divisions, rather than in Pacific Division (4.68 ).

These findings highlight that the health impacts of wildfire smoke in recent years were comparable between eastern and western U.S. regions when evaluated from a population exposure perspective. This suggests that wildfire smoke should be considered a nationwide public health concern rather than solely a western U.S.

environmental issue, and that public awareness and preparedness for wildfire smoke events should be strengthened across the entire country.

The separation of wildfire and non-wildfire related mortality burden (Tables S7 and S8) is critical for public health policies, because different sources require fundamentally different regulatory and public health responses. Emission control strategies are highly effective for reducing anthropogenic PM 2.5 , while preventive measures to mitigate exposure should be emphasized for wildfire PM 2.5 . During severe wildfire smoke events, individuals are advised to stay indoors and limit outdoor activities. Keeping windows closed and using portable air cleaners with HEPA filters can further reduce indoor exposure. 96 When outdoor activities are unavoidable, wearing properly fitted N95 or equivalent respirators is strongly recommended. In addition, smoke forecasting and early warning systems should be strengthened by local governments to improve risk communication and enhance public health preparedness.

4.6 Strengths and limitations of this study

This study has several strengths. First, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to predict 1-km daily average concentrations of both total and wildfire-related PM 2.5 across the CONUS with a focus on recent wildfire-influenced years. Many previous studies have covered long timeframes beginning in the early 2000s, during which anthropogenic emissions, rather than wildfire events, were the primary drivers of total PM 2.5 variability. By contrast, our study focused on the 2018-2024 period, when wildfire activity increasingly dominated interannual variability in total PM 2.5 concentrations.

Second, our machine learning framework incorporated predictors specifically relevant to wildfire and atmospheric chemistry. We included wildfire smoke, which many previous studies have not done. This focus allows for a clearer characterization of the growing influence of wildfire smoke on air quality under substantially reduced anthropogenic emissions. We also incorporated multiple chemically relevant precursor species from the GEOS-CF model, including key gaseous pollutants associated with secondary PM 2.5 formation, which helped improve model performance and better capture the chemical processes influencing PM 2.5 variability.

Third, few studies have simultaneously estimated the total and wildfire-related PM 2.5 concentrations during recent wildfire-influenced years, which is essential for quantifying the contribution of wildfire smoke to total PM 2.5 exposure. Future epidemiological studies can use this distinction between wildfire and non-wildfire PM 2.5 to examine source-specific health effects.

Fourth, this study provided a comprehensive evaluation of model performance, including sample-, station-, and day-based CV metrics (i.e., R² , RMSE, MAE, MBE, and NRMSE) for daily, monthly, and annual average estimates, whereas previous studies only reported a more limited set of these metrics, making direct inter-study comparison challenging. Finally, our framework relied entirely on publicly accessible data sources, facilitating future updates and extensions, and we released the resulting prediction datasets to support further research on total and wildfire PM 2.5 exposure and its health impacts.

However, several limitations should also be noted. First, the cross-validation results suggest that our model slightly underestimated extremely high total PM 2.5 concentrations during wildfire episodes, a limitation also reported in previous ML-based PM 2.5 prediction studies. Possible reasons include the relatively low frequency of episodic wildfire events, the use of mean squared error as the loss function, and the leaf-averaging effect in tree-based models. In addition, uncertainties in the HMS smoke product may affect the model's ability to characterize wildfire-related PM 2.5 . Because HMS smoke polygons rely largely on daytime visible satellite imagery, they may fail to detect smaller or lower-intensity smoke plumes, including those from some prescribed fires. Furthermore, the two-dimensional smoke polygons provide limited information on plume altitude and therefore cannot reliably distinguish elevated smoke plumes from smoke that reaches the surface and contributes to ground-level PM 2.5 concentrations.

Second, the proportional PAF approach was used in this study to separate the health impacts from wildfire-related and non-wildfire PM 2.5 . Although it ensures that the sum of source-specific health burdens equals the total attributable burden, it assumes that wildfire-related and non-wildfire PM 2.5 share the same exposure-response relationships with all-cause mortality. While some studies have suggested potentially differentiated health effects of wildfire-related PM 2.5 , 95 more epidemiological evidence is required to better characterize source-specific health risks.

Third, this study only focused on the long-term health impacts of total and wildfire-related PM 2.5 . Future studies could further investigate short-term health impacts, especially those associated with episodic wildfire-related PM 2.5 peak exposures.

5 Conclusion

This study developed a high-resolution machine learning framework to estimate daily 1-km concentrations of total and wildfire-related PM 2.5 across the CONUS from 2018 to 2024. The model had a good predictive performance and revealed substantial spatiotemporal variability in total PM 2.5 pollution during recent wildfire-influenced years. The U.S. west coast experienced the most severe episodic pollution events, while many metropolitan areas across both western and eastern regions had considerable numbers of highly polluted days. Wildfire smoke contributed substantially to total PM 2.5 concentrations during major wildfire years, accounting for approximately 30-35% of total PM 2.5 along the west coast during 2020-2021, and over 25% in the eastern U.S. in 2023. In severe wildfire years, wildfire smoke also contributed over 20% of population exposure in both western and eastern regions, highlighting its growing influence on air quality nationwide.

For long-term health impacts, the mortality burden attributable to total PM 2.5 peaked in 2021 (62.7 per 100,000), while wildfire-related PM 2.5 mortality was highest in 2023 (10.23 per 100,000), accounting for 18.4% of total PM 2.5 -related deaths.
