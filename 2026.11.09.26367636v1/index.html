What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Kidney disease is a rapidly growing global health concern, tightly linked to cardiovascular and metabolic disease within the recently defined cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome. Routine clinical assessment of kidney function primarily relies on creatinine-based estimated glomerular filtration rates (eGFRcr) which can be affected by non-renal factors and declines only after substantial kidney damage.

Using data from the MetaCardis study (N=1,723), we derived metabolomic kidney function gaps (mKFGs)-the difference between metabolome-predicted eGFRcr and measured eGFRcr. A physiologyguided feature-selection strategy -anchored on three cardiometabolic markers (haemoglobin, albumin and the Neutrophil-to-Lymphocyte Ratio) that associate with mKFGs but not with eGFRcr -yielded compact sex-stratified metabolite panels (40-98 out of 1,514 metabolites) that tracked cardiovascularkidney-metabolic risk. Machine-Learning models revealed distinct metabolic signatures, including biomarkers of meat consumption and androgens, highlighting potential sex-specific pathways linked to CKM health. External validation in the general-population Canadian Longitudinal Study on Aging (N=9,958) and German Chronic Kidney Disease Study (N=5,113) cohorts revealed that mKFGs added incremental predictive value for future CKM events and all-cause mortality beyond eGFRcr and established risk equations. Our findings open new avenues for personalised risk stratification for CKM disease progression and targeted interventions based on metabolically defined kidney health.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a growing health burden 1 , largely driven by rising rates of type-2 diabetes (T2D) and hypertension 1, 2 . Even mild declines in kidney function significantly increase the risk of Cardiovascular Disease (CVD) and all-cause as well as cardiovascular mortality 3 . This is greatly amplified in persons with T2D, where CKD can lead to a tenfold rise in cardiovascular death 4 . Renal, cardiac and vascular pathology can each bidirectionally affect the others within a broader cardiovascular-kidneymetabolic (CKM) syndrome framework that also encompasses metabolic disorders 5 . Integrated readouts of CKM health, rather than any single-organ measure, may therefore enable timely and effective risk stratification and intervention.

Kidney function is commonly assessed as the estimated Glomerular Filtration Rate, derived from serum creatinine, age and sex using the CKD-EPI formula (eGFRcr, thereafter) 6 . While eGFRcr is inexpensive, routinely measured in clinical practice, sensitive to cardiometabolic risk factors such as insulin resistance 7 , and can reflect the risk of future CVD and CKD 8 , it has limitations: serum creatinine levels are influenced by muscle mass and diet 9 , eGFRcr underestimates kidney function in women 10 and it is a lagging indicator of renal decline, becoming abnormal only after considerable kidney damage has occurred 9 . These limitations underscore the need for earlier, more sensitive markers that capture CKM risk beyond eGFRcr alone.

Recent studies coupling Machine Learning (ML) with multi-omics data (proteomics, metabolomics, metagenomics, organ imaging) have predicted biological organismal or organ-specific age [11] [12] [13] [14] , cardiorespiratory fitness 15 , disease trajectories 16, 17 or BMI 18, 19 . By integrating multiple signals related to health status not captured by their conventional equivalents, these omics-derived scores can complement existing biomarkers and provide deeper insights into metabolic health and disease trajectories, including response to intervention 18, 20.

The circulating metabolome can capture multi-organ human pathophysiology, with two-thirds of significant metabolite-disease associations spanning two or more noncommunicable diseases 21.

Here, we hypothesise that a metabolome-derived score anchored on eGFRcr, by integrating multi-organ cues across cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic axes, could complement eGFRcr and established clinical risk scores [22] [23] [24] in predicting future cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic events in both general and CKD populations and identify candidate metabolic pathways for follow-up mechanistic and clinical studies.

Using ML and data from 16,794 participants from three independent studies, i.e., MetaCardis 25- 28 , Canadian Longitudinal Study on Aging 29, 30 (CLSA), German Chronic Kidney Disease study 31 (GCKD), we demonstrate that circulating metabolites track personalised risk of kidney function decline or CVD events beyond eGFRcr and established clinical risk scores (Fig. 1A). We also uncover sex-specific metabolome signatures predictive of kidney function, offering insights into purported mechanistic differences in CKM physiology between sexes. To expedite translation, we use a physiology-guided feature selection strategy to devise generalisable and sex-specific metabolomic panels (40-98 serum metabolites) that outperform models using all available metabolites. Our work suggests application of ML algorithms and serum metabolites for monitoring risk of future cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic events and informs mechanistic and clinical studies to uncover the role of metabolic perturbations in CKM health.

Results

In the cross-sectional MetaCardis [25] [26] [27] [28] cohort (N=1,723; Suppl.Table .1), we used Lasso regression to predict eGFRcr from circulating metabolites and nine other data modalities (Fig. 2A). We reasoned that models trained across the cardiometabolic disease spectrum would better capture cardiovascularkidney-metabolic health in the presence of co-morbidities and generalise across the range of cardiometabolic and kidney disease burden.

Serum metabolites outperform other variable groups in predicting eGFRcr in the European MetaCardis study We took an agnostic approach to determine which of the ten data modalities (including clinical, lifestyle and dietary information, biochemical variables, serum and urine metabolomes and gut metagenomes) collected in MetaCardis participants (Suppl.Data.1) better predicts eGFRcr (Fig. 2A).

The serum metabolome (1,514 metabolites, excluding creatinine) predicted eGFRcr similarly to the full model with averaged explained variance of 0.61 (Spearman ρ=0.78) versus 0.66 for the full model (Spearman ρ=0.80) and 0.33 for traditional clinical risk factors (smoking, age, sex, systolic blood pressure, glycated haemoglobin and serum lipoprotein levels) 27, 28 (Fig. 2A&B). To confirm the appropriateness of Lasso to predict eGFRcr in our population we demonstrated significant linear associations between input metabolites and eGFR (Suppl.Fig. 2A) consistent with Lasso assumptions 32 . Moreover, an alternative treebased ML algorithm (boosted-trees 28, 33 "Methods") achieved modest improvements in eGFR explained variance with high overlap (12/20) in metabolites driving predictions with Lasso (Suppl.Fig. 2B-D; Suppl.Table .2). Among the ten data modalities compared, the urine metabolome, profiled by 1 H-NMR spectroscopy was the weakest predictor of eGFRcr (Fig. 2A).

To pinpoint influential metabolites driving eGFR predictions, we averaged β-coefficients from Lasso models for each metabolite (Fig. 2C). Higher levels of the reno-protective 34 α-ketoacid 3-methyl-2-oxobutyrate, an intermediate of valine catabolism, the amino acid serine (possibly reflecting tubular reabsorption 35 ) and 6-bromotryptophan, linked to diminished CKD progression 36 drove eGFR predictions higher. Conversely, 5-methylthioribose an intermediary of the methionine salvage pathway 37, 38 , the marker of N-terminal protein acetylation N-acetylalanine 39 associated with incident CVD 40 , N,N,Ntrimethyl-alanylproline betaine (TMAP) a metabolite of unknown function associated with lower eGFR 41 , and hydroxyasparagine a proposed marker of fibrosis 42 , were the top metabolites driving lower eGFR predictions.

Building on a conceptual framework developed for aging clocks [11] [12] [13] , we defined personalised metabolomic Kidney Function Gaps (mKFGs) by subtracting from predicted eGFRcr values the corresponding calculated eGFRcr. Positive mKFG values denote higher predicted, whilst negative values imply lower predicted eGFR compared to the calculated eGFR. mKFGs, adjusted for baseline eGFR, were distributed around a mean of -0.1 ml/min/1.73m 2 (Fig. 2D) indicating a broadly equal number of participants with predicted improved or diminished eGFR. mKFGs correlated inversely but much less strongly with age compared to eGFRcr (ρ=-0.12, P=6.8x10 -7 versus ρ=-0.53, P<2.2x10 -16 , respectively; Suppl.Fig. 1C-D) indicating that mKFGs reflect the aging process to a lesser degree.

Collectively, our analyses indicate that the circulating metabolome captures most phenotypic information related to eGFRcr.

Sex-specific metabolic signatures of eGFRcr in men and women.

As there is a higher CKD incidence in women 43 but higher CVD mortality in men with CKD 43 , we next tested whether differences in CKM outcomes between sexes are reflected in the metabolome by training sex-stratified serum metabolomics models to predict eGFRcr. Models performed better in men (N=875) than those in women (N=848) in predicting eGFR (explained variance 0.63 versus 0.52, Spearman ρ=0.79 versus 0.73; Suppl.Figs. 3A-C). Discrete metabolic signatures drove predictions between men and women (Suppl.Figs. 3D-E). Of 55 metabolites common to both sexes (present in 80% of the models), 54 shared the direction of their β-coefficient, yet only 7 of the top 20 by absolute β-coefficient overlapped.

Common metabolites included the microbial metabolite γ-butyrobetaine 44 implicated in kidney fibrosis 28 , TMAP, 5-methylthioribose and 3-methyl-2-oxobutyrate, as in models trained with all participants. The thyroid hormone thyroxine associated with higher predicted eGFRcr only in women, suggesting possible divergent mechanisms underlying inter-organ crosstalk related to cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic health between sexes.

Metabolomic kidney function gaps (mKFGs) reflect cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic health better than eGFRcr.

To determine whether mKFGs inform on CKM health status, we tested their associations with biochemical variables and contrasted them to eGFRcr. We adjusted mKFGs for age, sex, country of recruitment and the corresponding eGFRcr to determine their prognostic value independently of underlying eGFRcr. This approach also accounts for the well-documented tendency of ML predictions to revert to the mean of the predicted variable 45, 46 , as previously applied for eGFRcr 11.

Like eGFRcr, mKFGs inversely associated with levels of pro-Atrial Natriuretic Peptide (proANP), indicative of cardiac stress 47 , C-peptide, a marker of endogenous insulin secretion reflecting compensatory hyperinsulinaemia in insulin-resistant individuals 48 , the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL6 and the marker of monocyte activation, sCD14 49 (Fig. 2E). Unlike eGFRcr, mKFGs correlated directly with haemoglobin, regulated by the kidney 50 and anticorrelated with incident diabetic nephropathy and biopsy-proven renal fibrosis 51 or higher Kidney Failure (KF) risk 52 . mKFGs also associated with higher levels of albumin, also linked to risk of incident CKD and eGFRcr decline 53 , potentially through an underlying liver-kidney axis 54 and anticorrelated with the Neutrophil-to-Lymphocyte ratio (NLR), a marker of systemic inflammation and cardiovascular mortality 55 . Similar patterns of association were observed with mKFGs from models trained in men (Suppl.Fig. 4A) or women (Suppl.Fig. 4B).

To enable external validation, we trained models predicting eGFR in MetaCardis with the 1,276 serum metabolites with available measurements in CLSA (common-model), an independent cohort representative of the Canadian adult population 29 (Suppl.Table .3) as input. We next used these models to predict eGFR in CLSA and observed a median eGFRcr explained variance of 0.43 for all participants (Fig. 2F), 0.54 for men (Suppl.Fig. 4C) and 0.36 in women (Suppl.Fig. 4C). This may reflect the documented divergence between eGFRcr measurements in women and actual kidney health 43.

Collectively, these analyses indicate that metabolome-derived kidney gaps are associated with additional aspects of CKM health not correlated with eGFRcr.

A physiology-guided feature selection strategy to optimise mKFGs improves their association with cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic health readouts

To extend the utility of mKFGs in reflecting CKM health status not captured by eGFRcr, we adopted a permutation-based approach recently applied to proteomic age clocks 11 . In the absence of classic biochemical readouts of kidney function (urinary Albumin Creatinine Ratio (uACR), urea, urate, cystatin C, calcium phosphate) in the non-CKD MetaCardis and CLSA cohorts, we sought metabolites that strengthen significant correlations of mKFGs with all the biochemical measures of CKM health not associated with eGFRcr (Fig. 2E; haemoglobin, albumin or NLR; "Methods"). To that end, we randomly permuted each metabolite present in at least 20% of our eGFR-predicting Lasso models (436/1,276 serum metabolites common between MetaCardis and CLSA to facilitate validation) in the held-out test group, predicted eGFRcr, computed new mKFGs and correlated them with haemoglobin, albumin or NLR (Fig. 3A). Metabolites that after permutation decreased the absolute correlation between mKFGs and the target variable were considered to strengthen their association. We discovered 66 serum metabolites, excluding drugs, that strengthened absolute associations of mKFGs with all three target measures (Fig. 3B&C). We hypothesised that these metabolites (Intersected (int) panel thereafter) could simultaneously capture aspects of CKM physiology reflected by these biochemical measures but not by eGFRcr alone and trained Lasso algorithms with the intersected panel to predict eGFRcr and compute gaps (mKFG int ).

From the intersected panel, after extracting models β-coefficients, the proposed fibrosis biomarker hydroxyasparagine 42 and 1-methylhistidine a metabolite reflecting dietary protein intake 56 were the top metabolites driving lower eGFR predictions. Conversely the androgen androstenediol (3beta, 17beta) monosulfate together with the amino acid tryptophan were the top metabolites predicting higher eGFR (Fig. 3D).

Intersected panel models, despite decreasing eGFRcr explained variance (Fig. 3E) increased the associations of gaps with corresponding target biochemical variables (Fig. 4A) compared to mKFG all , indicating that optimisation strengthened correlations with target biochemical measures. mKFG int additionally associated with lower urine albumin, plasma Insulin and glycated haemoglobin (Fig. 4B) and anti-correlated with standardised Odds Ratios (OR) of co-morbidities adversely affecting kidney health 2 (i.e., hypertension, T2D, CAD) or atrial fibrillation (AF), more strongly that mKFG all (Fig. 4C) suggesting superior performance in reflecting CKM status than eGFRcr or mKFG all . Associations of mKFG all and mKFG int with comorbidities remained significant after inclusion of serum albumin, haemoglobin and NLR in the models (Fig. 4D), demonstrating that intersected mKFGs capture information beyond their target biochemical variables.

Applying this strategy separately by sex identified 98 metabolites strengthening associations with all three biochemical measures in men (Suppl.Fig. 5A-B) and 46 in women after excluding drugs (Suppl.Fig. 6A-B). Although the intersected models reduced predicted eGFRcr explained variance (Suppl.Fig. 5C, men; Suppl.Fig. 6C, women) they associated more strongly with the target biochemical measures (Suppl.Fig. 5D, men; Suppl.Fig. 6D, women) and CKM readouts, including lower urine albumin in women, than mKFG all or eGFRcr (Suppl.Fig. 5F, men; Suppl.Fig. 6F, women). mKFG int anticorrelated with ORs of cardiometabolic disease prevalence in both sexes (Suppl.Fig. 5G, men; Suppl.Fig. 6G, women). As mKFGs in the MetaCardis cohort, mKFGs in CLSA, corrected for calculated eGFR distributed around 0 (Fig. 5C). mKFG int exhibited stronger absolute associations than mKFG all or eGFR with albumin, haemoglobin and NLR (Fig. 5D) demonstrating that physiology-guided optimisation in MetaCardis also strengthens associations in the CLSA cohort. mKFGs in CLSA, unlike eGFRcr, reflected cardiometabolic health correlating with lower systolic blood pressure, T2D duration, gynoid and android fat whilst associating with higher grip strength (a measure of reduced frailty 61 ; Fig. 5E) and lower ORs of co-morbidities (hypertension, T2D, MI) negatively impacting kidney function stronger than eGFRcr (Fig. 5F). Interestingly, mKFGs correlated with increased serum lipid measures (total, high-density lipoprotein and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol) probably reflecting the medical management of lipids in individuals with prevalent cardiometabolic disease. Sex-stratified CLSA models (men N=4,882; women N=5,076) reproduced the generalisable model patterns for eGFRcr explained variance, associations with CKM readouts or cardiometabolic disease prevalence at recruitment (Suppl.Figs. 7A-F, men; Suppl.Figs. 8A-F, women). Moreover, sex-optimised intersected panels (98 metabolites in men, 46 metabolites in women) more strongly inversely associated with cardiometabolic disease prevalence than the generalised intersected panel-derived models (66 metabolites) (Suppl.Fig. 7F, men; Suppl.Fig. 8F, women), suggesting they better represent sex-specific CKM health.

In those under the age of 74 (so FRS 24 could be computed; N=6,638) with baseline and 1 st followup (3 years) and eGFRcr available, mKFGs inversely associated with eGFRcr rate of decline whilst eGFR exhibited positive and FRS no significant associations (Fig. 6A) with similar trends observed for sexstratified analyses (Suppl.Fig. 9A, men; Suppl.Fig. 9B, women). We also tested associations with rapid kidney function decline 62 , which also independently associated with all-cause mortality in a non-CKD population 63 . mKFGs significantly anticorrelated with ORs of rapid eGFRcr decline, which survived FRS inclusion, and improved performance when included in models, whilst eGFRcr associated with increased ORs of rapid kidney decline (Fig. 6B). mKFGs additionally associated with lower ORs of incident CKD (Fig. 6C) with once more FRS inclusion to models not improving performance. We additionally investigated whether mKFGs can predict incident T2D, future CV events (MI, CVA) or unplanned hospitalization (predictive of frailty and mortality 64 ) in the CLSA cohort at the 3-year follow-up. mKFG int , unlike eGFRcr, associated with lower ORs of unplanned hospitalizations and of incident MI, improving on baseline models whilst exhibiting a trend towards lower incident T2D (Fig. 6D). Altogether, our work suggests that mKFGs associate with aspects of CKM health independently of conventional cardiovascular risk factors.

We next tested the relationship between mKFGs and all-cause mortality, the only outcome with time-to-event data in the CLSA cohort (373 deaths/9,205 participants at 6.5-year follow-up). CLSA participants in the high, medium and low tertiles of mKFG all or mKFG int after baseline calculated eGFRcr regression, exhibited divergent all-cause mortality risk after 6.5 years of follow-up (Fig. 7A&B). The highest cumulative incidence rate was observed in the "Low" mKFG all and mKFG int tertiles, suggestive of worse predicted eGFRcr (5.7% and 6.6% respectively) whilst those at the "Highest" mKFG all and mKFG int tertiles (indicative of better predicted eGFRcr) had 2-to 3-fold lower cumulative incidence rates (2.9% and 1.8% respectively). In Cox models also including eGFRcr, sex and age (Model 2; Fig. 7C) each SD of mKFG all or mKFG int conferred a near 20% lower Hazard Ratio (HR) of all-cause mortality and improved discrimination (C-statistic; ΔC = +0.004 and +0.016, respectively), model fit (Likelihood Ratio Test; LRTs, P<0.05), "goodness-of-fit" of the data (informativeness 65 ; by 121.2% and 154.4%, respectively), and patient reclassification (NRI: Net Reclassification Index 15 ; by 8.4% and 15.6%, respectively). The mKFG int association with lower all-cause mortality HR held true after adding FRS to covariates improving NRI by 27.2% (Model 3). This more stringent model ("Methods") consistently improved classification of individuals at higher risk of death for a wide range of False Positive Rates (Fig. 7D). Models including mKFG int were well-calibrated, with bootstrap-corrected calibration slopes comparable to the baseline models, indicating that the gains in discrimination and reclassification reflect added prognostic information rather than differential calibration (Suppl.Fig. 10). As a sensitivity test, applying the identical optimisation pipeline to 100 random triplets drawn from a pool of cardiovascular-and metabolicdisease-relevant biochemistry uncorrelated with both eGFRcr and mKFG all ("Methods") did not recapitulate mKFG int 's broader CKM performance in CLSA (Suppl.Fig. 11A-B). Moreover, mKFG int 's associations with CLSA cardiorenal outcomes broadly survived direct adjustment for Hb, albumin and NLR (Suppl.Fig. 12A-F) demonstrating that mKFG int is not merely a proxy for these variables. Additionally, to confirm that mKFG int is not simply a metabolome-based filtration estimate, we adjusted the CLSA models for a metabolome-derived eGFR computed from the validated metabolite GFR-estimating equation of Coresh et al 66 . mKFG int remained an independent predictor of future CKM events after this adjustment (Suppl.Table .5). As a further sensitivity test we confirmed that mKFG int retained positive incremental discrimination over the eGFRcr, age and sex baseline within strata of age, sex, diabetes status and baseline kidney function, with its prognostic effect strongest where creatinine-based eGFR is least informative (HR 0.59 per SD for eGFR ≥60 versus 0.82 for eGFR <60; mKFG int × eGFR interaction P=1.6×10⁻⁵; Suppl.Table.6). Finally, to confirm the reproducibility of the top metabolite selections, we trained Lasso models to predict eGFRcr de novo in CLSA using the common (1,276) metabolites. 35 of the 40 leading MetaCardis predictors (ranked by absolute β-coefficient) were also robustly selected in CLSA (≥20% of models; Suppl. Fig. 13). All main CLSA association and mortality results are summarised in Suppl.Table .7.

Collectively, we validated the utility of mKFGs derived from models trained in the MetaCardis cohort to detect future CKM outcomes in a geographically unrelated general population cohort. Crucially, mKFGs from models trained with the intersected panel of metabolites (mKFG int ) consistently performed better than models trained with all metabolites, eGFRcr, or FRS, indicating their potential clinical utility. mKFGs are predictive of future cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic events in participants of the German Chronic Kidney Disease cohort with mild-to-moderate CKD.

We next queried whether mKFGs can discriminate CKM disease trajectories in patients with predominately mild-to-moderate CKD of diverse aetiologies, participating in the GCKD study (N=5,113) 31 (Suppl.Table.8). To this end, we trained models in the MetaCardis cohort using all serum metabolites (1,152) or 60 metabolites (out of 66) of the intersected panel common between the three cohorts to predict eGFRcr and computed mKFGs in the GCKD study. Models trained with all metabolites explained 62.1% of eGFR variance in GCKD (Spearman ρ=0.79) as opposed to 51.4% (Spearman ρ=0.72) for intersected panel models (Fig. 8A-B). mKFGs inversely associated with proteinuria at recruitment more strongly than eGFRcr (Fig. 8C&Suppl. Fig. 14A).

We next leveraged the extensive longitudinal follow-up (up-to 8.5 years) of GCKD participants to query whether mKFGs computed on baseline metabolomics predict future CKM event incidence. We observed divergent cumulative incidence rate trajectories for all eight CKM events included in our analysis (3-point Major-Adverse Cardiovascular Events (MACE), 4-point MACE, acute kidney injury (AKI), all-cause mortality, CV mortality, KF, MI or stroke) in the "High" (higher predicted kidney function), median and "Low" (lower predicted kidney function) tertiles of mKFG all and mKFG int after regressing mKFGs on baseline calculated eGFR and using the residual. Cumulative incidence rates between the 'Low' and 'High' tertiles differed by 1.4-fold (stroke) to 3.6-fold (KF) for mKFG all , and were consistently better separated by the intersected panel, ranging from 1.6-fold (MI) to over 3-fold (CV mortality) for mKFG int (Fig. 8D-E).

To demonstrate that mKFGs do not merely represent improved GFR estimation, we subtracted from the model predictions eGFR calculated from serum cystatin C (eGFR cys ), which better estimates true (measured) GFR and future CV risk 3 but is used predominately for confirmatory clinical tests 62 and plotted cumulative incidence rates as above. We still broadly observed considerable separation in cumulative incidence rates particularly for mKFG int tertiles after eGFR cys regression (Suppl.Fig. 14B&C), consistent with our hypothesis that metabolome-derived mKFGs integrate signals broader than glomerular filtration relevant to kidney health.

Using Cox regression, we observed approximately 15-20% lower HR per SD of mKFG all for all monitored outcomes except for stroke and CV mortality in the most stringent model (Model 4). These models included age, sex, uACR, and FRS, except the AKI model that included both FRS and the Kidney Failure Risk Equation (KFRE) score 22, 23 as a surrogate of baseline renal risk and the KF model substituted KFRE for FRS. Maximum improvement in model informativeness ("goodness-of-fit") was observed for AKI (128%; Fig. 9A-D, Suppl.Fig. 14D-G, left panels and Suppl.Table .12) with an NRI of 20.6%. Models including mKFG int performed significantly better than baseline models or models containing mKFG all , associating with reduced HRs by 15-33% for all outcomes in Model 4 exhibiting maximum improvement in informativeness by 167% for AKI with an 30.6% NRI (Fig. 9A-D, left panels, Suppl.Fig. 14D-G). When eGFRcr was replaced by combined creatinine-cystatin C eGFR (eGFRcr-cys 67 , a more accurate estimate of glomerular filtration, in the fully-adjusted GCKD models, mKFG int retained significant incremental prognostic value for the broader CKM outcomes (Suppl.Table.9), indicating that it carries information beyond filtration. Using Model 4 to predict risk of all eight outcomes in GCKD participants for the 8.5year follow-up period ("Methods"), we consistently obtained improved classification of individuals at high risk of event incidence for a wide range of False Positive Rates (10-40%) with models including mKFG int outperforming those with all metabolites (Fig. 9A-D and Suppl.Fig. 14D-G, right panels). Across all eight outcomes, models with and without mKFG int were comparably well-calibrated (bootstrap-corrected calibration slopes 0.96 to 0.99; all eight outcomes in corresponding Source data; all-cause mortality in Suppl.Fig. 15). Sensitivity analyses confirmed that mKFG int provided significant incremental discrimination over the fully-adjusted Model 4 baseline at administratively right-censored shorter follow-up horizons of 3 and 5 years for all eight outcomes (8.5 years being the primary horizon itself; Suppl.Table .10), and across prespecified subgroups defined by age, T2D status, KDIGO categories 68 , and CKD aetiology for all eight outcomes, with no systematic decay in effect size across horizons or strata (Suppl.Table .11). Sexstratified analyses (sex-specific intersected panels: 91/98 metabolites in men, N=3,071; and 40/46 in women, N=2,042) reproduced these findings (Suppl.Fig. 16-17), with the largest discrimination gains for AKI in men (ΔC +0.027, NRI 32%) and MI in women (ΔC +0.042, NRI 54%). We determined that, in addition to a cohort representative of the general population, mKFGs could detect future CKM outcomes in a mild-to-moderately severe CKD population with intersected metabolite panels consistently outperforming mKFGs from all metabolites and significantly improving performance of models containing eGFRcr, uACR or established scores of CKM risk in both sexes.

Discussion

By harnessing the ability of ML algorithms to detect patterns in high dimensional data, we derived personalised metabolomic kidney function gaps (mKFGs) from ML-based eGFR predictions using circulating metabolites as input. mKFGs reliably distinguished baseline kidney and associated cardiometabolic health status and predicted future CKM disease trajectories across three independent cohorts of varying study designs and kidney disease burden totaling 16,794 participants. These cohorts were chosen to span the cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic disease spectrum, from a general population without overt CKD (CLSA) to an established CKD population at high cardiovascular risk (GCKD), allowing us to test whether a single baseline score predicts future CKM events across this range. Using a physiology-guided feature selection strategy, we optimised serum metabolite panels for men, women and all participants and captured CKM health status and risk more effectively than using all metabolites, creatinine-based eGFR and established risk scores only.

Despite extensive adjustment for multiple kidney health-related covariates (age, sex, eGFR, uACR), clinical risk scores (FRS, KFRE) and use of more precise method to estimate GFR (eGFR cys ), models including mKFGs improved classification of individuals across CKM trajectories. Anchoring our metabolomic prediction on eGFRcr leverages an already CKM-relevant starting point rather than refining pure glomerular filtration. The physiology-guided optimisation toward haemoglobin, albumin and NLR -CKM-axis variables uncorrelated with eGFRcr in MetaCardis -extends this CKM-sensitivity beyond what eGFRcr alone captures. These findings imply that mKFGs are not merely refined estimates of GFR -that is, a risk-enriched metabolomic deviation from creatinine-based kidney filtration -but rather represent, by integrating metabolic signals, a multifaceted score of kidney and associated cardiometabolic health independently of existing markers. Specifically, mKFG int inclusion, improved patient discrimination (from 0.012 (stroke) to 0.032 (all-cause mortality)) and reclassification (up to 38.5% for all-cause mortality) for cardiovascular outcomes comparable to those previously reported with proteome-based models 69, 70 underscoring the interplay between kidney and cardiovascular health. We additionally considerably improved patient discrimination for kidney-related outcomes (up to 0.036 C-statistic increase for all participants for AKI) leading to as much as a ~31% improvement in patient classification. Further highlighting their utility, mKFGs derived from women-focused panels demonstrated effect sizes often exceeding those from models including all participants or men only, underscoring their potential value in predicting CKM outcomes among women, where there is a clinical need to more accurately assess kidney status 10.

Our metabolite panels yielded candidate metabolites for future investigation and pointed to potential lifestyle or clinical interventions to mitigate CKM risks in a sex-specific manner. We identified in men, women and all participants the biomarker of meat consumption 1-methylhistidine 56 as one of the top serum metabolites of reduced predicted kidney function, suggesting that moderating meat intake may confer kidney health benefits, in line with a recent study 71 . In women, androstenediols were predictive of better kidney function, aligning with previous research on the relevance of sex hormone status on female cardiovascular health 72 , warranting further dedicated kidney-centred research.

Additionally, the α-keto acid 3-methyl-2-oxobutyrate associated with improved kidney function, suggesting that enhanced branched-chain amino acid catabolism could be renoprotective paralleling evidence from cardiac health 73.

Despite omics-based risk scores often improving patient classification, their adoption in clinical practice is often hindered by their complexity and costs. The optimised metabolomic panels we derived, by reducing the metabolomic space required to monitor CKM health by up to 40-fold combined with improvements in automated sample preparation and metabolite annotation could pave the way to overcome some of these limitations. A comparable path, from broad discovery to a compact clinically deployed test, has recently been realised in liver disease, where a serum panel of 12 lipids combined with routine clinical measures can prioritise patients at risk of steatohepatitis 74, 75 . Similarly, our work could serve as a paradigm for further development of selective metabolome-informed scores for clinical assessment of CKM health allowing for personalised risk stratification and early intervention. Early detection of individuals at higher CKM risk is particularly important, since new-generation anti-diabetic drugs (Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor analogs and Sodium-glucose Transport Protein 2 inhibitors) which have documented renoprotective properties [76] [77] [78] may help preserve kidney function if initiated early. Our findings could facilitate early detection of individuals at high risk of AKI, the most prevalent renal exacerbation event in patients with CKD, commonly leading to severe complications with high mortality 79 , allowing for targeted clinical vigilance and medication management.

Our study has some limitations. Our cohorts predominantly consisted of white participants albeit from varying geographical and environmental backgrounds (Europe and North America), questioning the generalisability of our findings across other ancestries. Additionally, we primarily relied on linear modelling to derive kidney function gaps and whilst a non-linear ML method in our training cohort did not confer appreciable benefits, non-linear ML could potentially further improve our approach particularly as computational power increases in the future. Untargeted metabolomics typically provide relative metabolite quantifications rather than absolute concentrations, preventing us from establishing universal reference concentrations. Devising targeted quantitative assays for our optimised metabolite panels, along with prospective validation, is an essential next step towards clinical application.

Importantly, the relationships we report between mKFGs and cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic health are observational associations and do not, in themselves, establish causal or physiological mechanisms. The biological interpretations we offer for individual metabolites and pathways are therefore hypothesisgenerating and warrant dedicated experimental validation. The lack of serial metabolomic measurements in our cohorts precluded direct assessment of whether mKFGs change over time in response to disease progression, lifestyle modification, or therapeutic intervention; this remains to be evaluated in cohorts or trials with serial metabolomic profiling. Additionally, urine metabolome data were not available across all three cohorts, so we could not test whether urinary metabolomics might 477 outperform or complement the serum metabolome as a readout of CKM health. This remains a 478 promising avenue for future work. Finally, like other omics-derived scores, our current panels rely on 479 high-throughput metabolomics platforms that are not yet widely accessible and currently limit 480 deployment in low-resource settings. Further reduction to compact, targeted assays, along with 481 validation in ancestrally diverse cohorts will be needed to broaden accessibility. 482

To conclude, we demonstrate that leveraging ML with circulating metabolite profiles can refine 483 estimates of CKM health and improve risk prediction of future CKM diseases beyond widely adopted 484 clinical markers. Our work sets the stage for developing noninvasive tests to monitor CKM disease risk 485 and informs future mechanistic and clinical studies aiming to preserve kidney function in both the 486 general and the CKD population. 487

Methods

Cohorts Description

MetaCardis description, study design and bio-sample collection MetaCardis (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT02059538) is a cross-sectional study comprising individuals ranging from metabolically healthy to metabolic syndrome and/or obesity through T2D and IHD, aged 18-75-years old recruited in Denmark, France and Germany between 2013 and 2015. Patients under care of the participating hospitals meeting the inclusion criteria of the study were invited to enroll. Ethical approvals were obtained from the Ethics Committee CPP Ile-de France, the Ethical Committees of the Capital Region of Denmark (H-3-2013-145), and Ethics Committee at the Medical Faculty at the University of Leipzig (047-13-28012013). Study participants provided written informed consent, and the study was undertaken according to the Helsinki Declaration-II guidelines. Study design, recruitment and exclusion criteria have been extensively described [25] [26] [27] 80 . Participants with severe kidney failure and/or on dialysis therapy, or with eGFR < 50 mL/min/1.73 m² (body surface area), were excluded, as specified in the registered MetaCardis protocol (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT02059538). Estimated GFR in the present analyses was calculated from serum creatinine using the CKD-EPI equation (eGFRcr). In the present study all participants with available eGFR measurements, serum metabolomics and microbiome composition were included in the analysis (N=1,723). In MetaCardis the following definitions for disease classification were used: hypertension, as defined by the American Heart Association 81 ; obesity, as defined by the World Health Organization 82 , metabolic syndrome, as defined by the International Diabetes Federation 83 , T2D as defined by the American Diabetes Association 84.

Within the MetaCardis ischaemic heart disease spectrum, cardiovascular phenotypes were defined as mutually exclusive categories per the registered protocol (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT02059538): acute coronary syndrome (ACS; STEMI or NSTE-ACS), chronic ischaemic heart disease (CIHD; stable coronary artery disease with LVEF ≥45% and no heart failure) and heart failure (HF; LVEF <45% or NYHA class II-IV, of ischaemic or non-ischaemic aetiology). CIHD and HF are thus distinguished by left-ventricular function and were not combined, as described 27 . Here, cardiometabolic disease is used as an umbrella term for all the above conditions. Biomaterial collection has been extensively described [26] [27] [28] 80 . Blood samples were collected in the morning after overnight fasting, and fecal samples were collected at home by participants, frozen immediately and transferred to study centres on dry ice within 48eh. All samples were stored at -80°C until use. Clinical history, medication and phenotypic information were acquired as described 26, 27 with standardised procedures across centres. Food intake was reported through a customized Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ), validated according to established practices by three web-based dietary recalls 85 for a subset of study participants (Ne=e324). Biochemical variables were measured in a single centre according to standard procedures 27 . Estimated GFR was calculated with the CKD-EPI formula without ethnicity adjustment 86.

Canadian Longitudinal Study on Aging (CLSA) cohort description study design and bio-sample collection

CLSA is a Canadian cohort that follows 51,338 women and men recruited between the ages of 45-85eyears recruited from 2010-2015 and followed-up longitudinally every 3 years 87 . All participants provided written informed consent. Additional information for available bio-clinical data and collection, recruitment and exclusion criteria, ethics governance and oversight and data availability can be found here www.clsa-elcv.ca. In this study we used data up to Follow-up 1 (FU1; approximately 3 years since recruitment), except for all-cause mortality where data were available also for Follow-up 2 (FU2; approximately 6 years since recruitment), which was also the only outcome with time-to-event data in CLSA. Incident disease was defined as disease reported in FU1 but not at baseline. Rapid kidney function decline was defined as an annual eGFR decline >5 ml/min/1.73 m². (here >15 ml/min/1.73 m² over the 3-year FU1 interval). The use of CLSA data for the purpose of this study was further approved by the Imperial Research Ethics Committee under application number 21IC7388. Here we used CLSA participants, regardless of ethnicity with available eGFR measurement (with the CKD-EPI formula without ethnicity adjustment 86 ) and serum metabolomics at recruitment (N=9,958).

German Chronic Kidney Disease (GCKD) cohort description study design and bio-sample collection

The German chronic kidney disease (GCKD) study is an ongoing prospective observational cohort that enrolled 5,217 patients with mainly CKD stage 3 between 2010 and 2012. Details of the study design have been published previously 31, 88 . Participants were recruited under nephrological care based on an eGFR of 30-60 mL/min/1.73 m² or an eGFR >60 mL/min/1.73 m² with overt proteinuria, defined as a urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio >300 mg/g or a urinary protein-to-creatinine ratio >500 mg/g. The study included adults aged 18-74 years. Detailed sociodemographic and clinical data were collected through standardised questionnaires and quality-controlled procedures. Biosamples collected at baseline were shipped frozen to a central biobank for storage at -80°C and were analyzed for laboratory parameters 89 . During follow-up of participants, incident events are adjudicated 89 . There are eight outcomes of interest herein: all-cause mortality, CV mortality, MI, stroke, 3-point Major Adverse Cardiovascular Events (MACE; defined as composite endpoint combining MI, stroke, CV death), 4-point MACE (defined as composite endpoint combining peripheral artery occlusive disease and related deaths in addition to 3-point MACE components), KF (defined as start of dialysis, kidney transplantation or death due to untreated KF) and AKI (based on Acute Kidney Injury Network criteria stage 3 per 2012 KDIGO guideline or temporary dialysis 90.

Machine Learning (ML) analysis

Data modalities

MetaCardis phenotypic variables were classed into ten groups (Supplementary Data 1), as previously described 28 . Briefly, biological parameters included biochemical and clinical serum laboratory tests, measured using standard enzymatic methods 27 . Serum creatinine and the derived CKD-EPI eGFRcr were excluded when bioclinical variables were used as predictors in Lasso models. Clinical parameters consisted of clinical history, clinical variables (BMI, systolic and diastolic blood pressure), anthropometric variables, and stool frequency and type. Demographic information included age, physical activity, educational and financial attainment, smoking, ethnicity and centre of recruitment. Drug variables reported intake of common medication, antibiotic courses in the last 5 years and total drugs per treatment category (antihypertensive, anti-diabetic, lipid-lowering) as described 26 . Nutritional parameters comprised habitual consumption of 37 food items, daily nutrients intake calculated and diet quality scores (alternative Healthy Eating (aHEI), Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH), Dietary Diversity (DDS) described in 85 . Clinical risk factors variables included age, sex, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, glycated haemoglobin levels, fasting cholesterol levels, smoking status and waist circumference as described 27, 28 . Serum metabolomics comprised the relative levels of 1,514 circulating metabolites, excluding creatinine, determined by UPLC-MS/MS, as described above. Urine metabolomics included absolute quantification of 47 urine metabolites derived from 1 H-NMR spectra, as described above. Microbiota variables included the abundance of 1,590 bacterial species present in at least 5% of MetaCardis participants after correction for microbial load and the first 10 principal components from a PCA of relative microbial gene abundances 28, 98 . Microbiome (Modules) group included abundance of 116 manually curated bacterial modules, as described above. The full model comprised all variables across the 10 data modalities (3,600 variables in total; Supplemental Data 1).

Categorical variables were converted into dummy variables using the caret library (v6.0.86).

Aggregated Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (Lasso) eGFRcr models and external validation

To estimate eGFRcr in MetaCardis participants (N=1,723) with input each of the 10 data modalities defined above, we built Lasso regression-based eGFR models using the R library glmnet (v4.1.6). In MetaCardis, the prediction target, eGFRcr, was approximately normally distributed (Suppl.Fig. 1A-B) and was therefore modelled untransformed. Overt CKD was an exclusion criterion in MetaCardis and we therefore postulated that training eGFR-predicting models in MetaCardis would capture kidney functional heterogeneity in the presence of common co-morbidities and would better generalise in independent cohorts. We employed bootstrap aggregation for model training and nested fivefold crossvalidation to optimise the L1 regularization parameter, λ. Initially, we split our MetaCardis cohort into 5 equal parts with 4/5 as the training set and 1/5 as the test (left-out) set and mean-centred and varianceadjusted (z-scored) the input variables. We next used fivefold cross-validation in the training set (80% of participants) to determine the optimal regularization parameter lambda (from 200 values ranging from 10 -2 to 10 1 ) for predicting eGFR. We used the optimal λ to train a Lasso regression model in the training set and predicted eGFR in the left-out group (20% of the cohort). This process was repeated for each of the other four equal parts of the cohort, resulting in a predicted eGFR value for each participant which was then used to calculate the coefficient of determination using the rsq function from yardstick (v0.0.7) and an averaged β-coefficient from five models in each training split for each input variable. To account for cohort heterogeneity the process was repeated 100 times and the mean predicted eGFR and βcoefficients from all ensemble models were used in all subsequent analyses. In separate analyses, to exclude chance-biased predictions, eGFR was randomly permuted in the left-out group and predicted with metabolomics as input in all three studied cohorts, as indicated.

For metabolomics, which was the data modality best explaining eGFRcr variance, we also trained models in MetaCardis with 1,276 metabolites common between MetaCardis and CLSA; and with 1,152 metabolites common between MetaCardis, CLSA and GCKD, to predict z-scored eGFR (to account for different eGFR distributions between cohorts). We then used these models to predict eGFR in CLSA (N = 9,958) or GCKD (N=5,113) participants with available baseline metabolomics, as applicable. Metabolite intensities were z-scored within each cohort separately prior to modelling; the MetaCardis-trained models were applied to identically z-scored CLSA and GCKD data, without further cross-platform rescaling and without information leakage between cohorts. To assess the robustness and reproducibility of the top metabolite selections, the eGFRcr-prediction Lasso ensemble (100 iterations, nested 5-fold cross-validation) was retrained de novo in CLSA on the 1,276 metabolites common to MetaCardis and CLSA, and per-metabolite selection frequencies were compared between the two cohort-specific ensembles. To uncover sex-specific metabolite panels relevant to kidney health we also performed model training as above exclusively in men or women participating in MetaCardis participants and predicted z-scored eGFR in males or females in CLSA or GCKD.

Metabolomics Kidney Function Gap (mKFG) calculation

To calculate personalized metabolomic kidney function gaps (mKFGs) for each participant of the three studies, conventional creatinine-based eGFRcr was subtracted from eGFR predictions of models trained in MetaCardis on serum metabolomics, as applicable. This is conceptually similar to proteomic ageing gaps 11 and multi-omic BMI signatures 18, 19 , defined as the numerical difference between a clinical reference variable and an omics-derived prediction of that variable. For association studies between mKFGs and biochemical variables or health outcomes gaps were adjusted with the corresponding conventional creatinine-based eGFRcr obtained at recruitment, in line with similar research with age gaps, to account for the well-documented tendency of ML algorithm predictions to revert to the mean of the predicted variable 45, 46 , including eGFR 11 . This was achieved either by including conventional eGFR as covariate in corresponding models, as appropriate, or by regressing eGFR from mKFGs by extracting residuals from a linear model with kidney gaps as the dependent and conventional creatinine-based eGFR as the independent variable. To allow for direct comparisons between models, kidney function gaps from each model were z-scored.

Physiology-guided feature selection strategy

To discover metabolites strengthening associations with cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic phenotypes we adapted the permutation feature importance (PFI) method 99 , as further refined by Oh and colleagues for health-relevant outcomes 11 . We did not seek to identify metabolites that after permutation decrease the accuracy of predicted eGFRcr but instead focused on permuted metabolites that modify association between kidney gaps (predicted minus eGFRcr) with biochemical variables indicative of CKM health. In the absence of a universally accepted score of kidney health and biochemical metrics of kidney function (uACR, urea, urate, cystatin C, calcium phosphate) in our non-CKD cohorts (MetaCardis and CLSA), we optimised our models in MetaCardis with serum haemoglobin, serum albumin or the Neutrophil-to-Lymphocyte ratio as the target variables. These variables were selected in a data-driven way due to their association with mKFGall but not eGFRcr (Fig. 2E). We reasoned that optimising our models towards these variables could integrate and reflect interlinked multi-organ information relevant to CKM health independently to that already contained in eGFRcr. A metabolite that after its permutation in the test set, kidney gaps using the resulting eGFR prediction after correcting for age, sex, creatinine-based eGFR and centre of recruitment, exhibited diminished Spearman ρ with the target variable than gaps without permutation, was deemed relevant in capturing associations between gaps and target variable. The feature importance (FI) score for each metabolite was calculated as the % difference between partial Spearman ρ between the target variable and the kidney gaps with or without permutation and was used to rank metabolites.

We applied this strategy to 436 metabolites (out of 1,276 in total) that were included with a non-zero value in a minimum of 20% of our eGFR-predicting Lasso models in MetaCardis, using the same cut-off as Oh and colleagues 11 and defined metabolite panels for haemoglobin, serum albumin and NLR. We also defined an intersected metabolomics panel with metabolites that strengthen mKFG associations with all three target variables, removing drugs. The intersected metabolite panels were then used to train Lasso models in MetaCardis as described above.

To test whether optimising mKFGs towards any cardiometabolic-health-relevant variable serendipitously improves associations of mKFGs with CKM outcomes, we applied the same physiology-guided optimisation pipeline (as described above) to 100 random three-anchor triplets sampled from a 17variable pool (HOMA.IR, FFA, CCL2, Eotaxine, HDL, TG, HbA1c, IL7, Insulin, IP10, ALT, MIF, MIP1, SDF1, sodium, VEGF, Monocytes) of cardiovascular-and metabolic-disease-relevant clinical biochemistry not significantly associated (FDR > 0.1) with either eGFRcr or mKFG all in MetaCardis. Microalbumin and total neutrophils/lymphocytes were excluded as direct kidney-damage and NLR-overlapping markers, respectively. Lasso models trained in MetaCardis were applied to CLSA to predict eGFR and generate negative-control mKFGs, with downstream analyses restricted to the 36 triplets whose eGFRcr predictions in CLSA achieved R² comperable to that achieved by models using all common metabolites between CLSA and MetaCardis (R² > 0.4). These negative-control mKFGs were tested against four future cardiorenal endpoints in CLSA (eGFRcr decline rate, incident CKD, incident MI, all-cause mortality), using the same model specifications as for mKFG int (described above).

XGBoost and SHapley additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis

We also predicted eGFR levels in all MetaCardis participants (N=1,723) with serum metabolomics as input using gradient-boosting decision trees based on the xgboost algorithm (v1.3.2.1) 100 , as we previously described 28 , with modifications. We used two nested fivefold cross-validations to optimise the xgboost hyperparameters (eta, max_depth, min_child weight, gamma, lambda, colsample_bytree; for additional information on model parameters please see the XGBoost documentation (https://xgboost.readthedocs.io/en/latest/index.html) in the training cohort independently of the test cohort, as described for Lasso above. Briefly, we used a hyperparameter grid search of 1350 parameter combinations with early stopping set to 10 to identify the optimal parameters in each training set, evaluating model outcomes using the root-mean-square error (RMSE) metric. After parameter optimisation, eGFR was predicted in the left-out group (20% of cohort) with a model trained with the optimal parameters in the training group (80% of cohort) and this was applied to the other four trainingtest set pairs. Like Lasso and to account for cohort heterogeneity we repeated the process for 20 times and averaged eGFR predictions.

To assign relative importance to metabolites driving XGBoost eGFR predictions we used SHAP values, as implemented in tree-based models 101 and recently reported by us 28 and others 98 . Metabolite SHAP values for each individual in the five left-out groups were extracted from corresponding XGBoost models trained on the remaining 80% participants, as described above, using SHAPforxgboost (0.1.0) and averaged over 20 iterations. For each metabolite relative importance was assigned by calculating the mean absolute SHAP value for each MetaCardis participant, as described 28 . For swarm plots depicting each individual participants SHAP values, metabolite relative levels were inverse-normalised using the RNOmni (v1.0.0) package.

Logistic regression

For logistic regressions for prevalent disease at recruitment or incident events without time-to-event data in MetaCardis or CLSA, we used base the R generalised linear model function (glm) with family set to binomial with mKFGs, eGFR or other input variables z-scored to allow for direct comparison between models. Age, sex and in the case of MetaCardis country of recruitment were also included as covariates in the models. In certain models we also included the Framingham Risk score, serum albumin, haemoglobin or NLR (all z-scored), as indicated in the text. Likelihood Ratio Tests (LRTs) between nested models with or without kidney gaps, were performed using the base R anova function using the Likelihood Ratio ("LR") test option throughout.

Cox proportional hazard models

To assess associations between mKFGs and all-cause mortality in CLSA, or 8 CKM events in GCKD with time-to-event data we used Cox proportional hazard models, as implemented in the survival library (v3.7.0) in R. For GCKD, competing events (i.e., death for other reasons than included in considered outcomes) were available and treated as censored observations (i.e., cause-specific analysis was conducted). In GCKD, study entry was defined as the time origin and the time scale was time since study entry; follow-up was restricted to the first 8.5 years after baseline. Time-to-event was defined as the interval from study entry to the event of interest. Participants who did not experience the event of interest were censored at the earliest of: date last known alive, date of death from causes unrelated to the event of interest, or 8.5 years after baseline. The censoring of deaths unrelated to the event of interest (competing events) in the analysis corresponds to a cause-specific analysis. To determine how covariates influence associations between mKFGs and CKM events we sequentially added covariates to the models that had this general structure: event riske≈emKFGe+eeGFR + covariates and compared them with nested models where only eGFR and the same covariates were included. To allow for direct model Hazard Ratio (HR) comparisons kidney gaps or eGFRcr were z-scored. In CLSA models included: Model 1: eGFRcr only; Model 2: eGFRcr, age and sex; Model 3: eGFRcr, age, sex and the Framingham 10year Risk score 24 , as an indicator of baseline cardiovascular risk. In GCKD, Models 1 and 2 were identical with Model 3 including eGFRcr, age, sex and uACR whilst Model 4 included in addition FRS and in the case of kidney outcomes the 5-year Kidney Failure Risk (KFRE) equation 22, 23 . C-statistics for each model along with 95% Confidence Intervals (CIs) were extracted from the models. Nested models with or without mKFGs, were compared using the base R anova function using the Likelihood Ratio ("LR") test option. Informativeness, as a measure of improvement in the "goodness-of-fit" of the data with models including kidney gaps in comparison to baseline models, was calculated as the ratio of the Wald test χ2 statistic of the two models as described in 65 . Model calibration was assessed using bootstrap-corrected calibration slopes (500 resamples) computed with the validate and calibrate functions of the rms package (v6.8-1) in R, where a slope of 1 indicates perfect agreement between predicted and observed risk. 5-year Kidney Failure Risk Equation

Five-year risk of progression of CKD to Kidney Failure (KF) within 5 years was estimated using Tangri's Kidney Failure Risk Equation (KFRE) implemented in the KFRE: Kidney Failure Risk Estimator package for Python (https://github.com/lshpaner/kfre?tab=readme-ov-file#references).
