4 Background and Objectives: Normative modeling expresses an individual’s brain measures as deviations from a large healthy reference and has been advanced as a route to precision imaging biomarkers in epilepsy, where structural abnormality is distributed rather than confined to the presumed focus. Whether this framework can inform clinical classification from clinically acquired T1-weighted and diffusion MRI is untested. We asked whether normative deviations discriminate between (1) epilepsy and functional seizures; (2) focal and generalized epilepsy; and (3) drug-resistant and drug-responsive epilepsy. Methods: We retrospectively studied patients with epilepsy or functional seizures without comorbid epilepsy who underwent 3-Tesla epilepsy-protocol MRI at the University of Pennsylvania. Morphometric deviation scores (cortical thickness, subcortical volume) were derived from CentileBrain (n>37,000 healthy references). White matter fractional anisotropy and mean diffusivity profiles were referenced to harmonized Human Connectome Project cohorts. Lobar composites, hemispheric asymmetry indices, and whole-tract means were compared with rank-sum tests; discrimination was summarized as area under the ROC curve (AUC) with bootstrap confidence intervals and Benjamini-Hochberg correction. Whole-brain deviation burden was examined as a secondary analysis. Minimum detectable effects were estimated by Monte Carlo simulation at 80% power (α=0.05).

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background and Objectives: Normative modeling expresses an individual's brain measures as deviations from a large healthy reference and has been advanced as a route to precision imaging biomarkers in epilepsy, where structural abnormality is distributed rather than confined to the presumed focus. Whether this framework can inform clinical classification from clinically acquired T1-weighted and diffusion MRI is untested. We asked whether normative deviations discriminate between (1) epilepsy and functional seizures; (2) focal and generalized epilepsy; and (3) drug-resistant and drug-responsive epilepsy. Methods: We retrospectively studied patients with epilepsy or functional seizures without comorbid epilepsy who underwent 3-Tesla epilepsy-protocol MRI at the University of Pennsylvania. Morphometric deviation scores (cortical thickness, subcortical volume) were derived from CentileBrain (n>37,000 healthy references). White matter fractional anisotropy and mean diffusivity profiles were referenced to harmonized Human Connectome Project cohorts. Lobar composites, hemispheric asymmetry indices, and whole-tract means were compared with rank-sum tests; discrimination was summarized as area under the ROC curve (AUC) with bootstrap confidence intervals and Benjamini-Hochberg correction. Whole-brain deviation burden was examined as a secondary analysis. Minimum detectable effects were estimated by Monte Carlo simulation at 80% power (α=0.05).

Results: We included 273 patients with epilepsy (203 focal, 37 generalized, 33 unclassified; 97 drug-resistant, 176 drug-responsive) and 32 with functional seizures. In the primary analyses, no regional or tract-level normative measure distinguished epilepsy from functional seizures, focal from generalized epilepsy, or drug-resistant from drug-responsive epilepsy; AUCs did not exceed 0.62, with upper confidence limits up to 0.72. In the secondary analysis, whole-brain morphometric deviation burden was modestly higher in epilepsy than in . functional seizures (AUC 0.66, 95% CI 0.57-0.74) but did not separate the within-epilepsy comparisons. The study could detect AUC ≥0.65 for epilepsy versus functional seizures, ≥0.64 for focal versus generalized, and ≥0.60 for drug-resistant versus drug-responsive epilepsy. Discussion: Univariate normative deviation summaries from clinical MRI do not currently distinguish these groups well enough to guide care. Whole-brain deviation burden, the only summary measure that differed after correction, was detectable at the group level but insufficient for individual-level classification. Progress may require network-targeted analysis of patient-specific deviations and multimodal integration.

Introduction

The evaluation and management of patients with suspected epilepsy involves a sequence of clinical decisions, each carrying diagnostic or prognostic uncertainty. The first is determination of whether the paroxysmal events are epileptic in origin. Functional seizures, also known as psychogenic nonepileptic seizures or events, are common, frequently mistaken for epilepsy, and a leading cause of misdiagnosis, delayed care, and unnecessary antiseizure medication (ASM) exposure. 1, 2 The second is classification of epilepsy as focal or generalized, which differ in presumed mechanism, treatment, and surgical candidacy. The third is identifying ASM resistance, as approximately one-third of patients are drug-resistant, defined by the International League Against Epilepsy as failure of two adequate trials of appropriately chosen and tolerated antiseizure medications to achieve sustained seizure freedom. 3, 4 While growing evidence suggests that earlier surgical treatment in patients with drug-resistant epilepsy improves seizure freedom outcomes, the clinical determination of drug resistance depends on sequential trials of ASMs and is reached at a median of 10 years after epilepsy diagnosis. [5] [6] [7] Objective biomarkers could inform any of these decisions, enabling earlier interventions and improved outcomes.

Quantitative analysis of brain MRI offers objective measures that may be relevant to each of these clinical classifications. From T1-weighted images, cortical thickness and subcortical volumes capture morphometric change, while diffusion-weighted imaging yields measures of white matter microstructure such as fractional anisotropy (FA) and mean diffusivity (MD). 8, 9 In epilepsy, structural abnormalities have been found to be widespread, extending beyond the presumed seizure focus, including distributed cortical thinning and subcortical volume loss, and altered white matter microstructure most pronounced in the corpus callosum, cingulum, and external capsule. 10, 11 Compared to generalized epilepsy, focal epilepsy showed more extensive white matter abnormalities and partly distinct regional patterns of gray matter change, alongside differences in structural network organization. 10, 12, 13 In functional seizures, the few studies comparing patients to healthy controls reported subtle, network-level differences. These included convergent cortical thinning in visual and sensorimotor cortex and hyperconnectivity across sensorimotor, default-mode, and attentional networks, though findings came from small, heterogeneous cohorts. 14, 15 Few studies have directly compared imaging between drug-resistant and drug-responsive epilepsy. In idiopathic generalized epilepsy, drug-resistant patients showed more widespread FA alterations in white matter networks. 16 Structural network analyses in temporal lobe epilepsy revealed differences in global network architecture between the two groups. 17, 18 A persistent challenge across these comparisons is the heterogeneity of epilepsy, which limits the interpretability of traditional group-level analyses. Case-control comparisons rely on population averages and may obscure meaningful individual-level variation in brain structure, a limitation compounded in focal epilepsy by uncertainty in epileptogenic network localization, particularly in cases without invasive monitoring or surgery. Normative modeling addresses this limitation by quantifying how much an individual deviates from a reference distribution matched by age and sex, rather than relying on group averages, yielding interpretable individual-level deviation scores. 19 This framework has recently been advanced as a route to precision MRI biomarkers in epilepsy, with potential for abnormality localization and individual-level prediction. 20 Here we ask whether normative modeling is ready to inform the clinical classifications providers rely on, using clinically acquired MRI, which at our tertiary epilepsy center includes 1-mm T1-weighted and 30-direction diffusion-weighted imaging. We applied normative models of cortical thickness, subcortical volume, and along-tract white matter microstructure to the scans from a consecutive tertiary-center cohort, and tested whether individual-level deviations can discriminate between epilepsy and functional seizures, between focal and generalized epilepsy, and between drug-resistant and drug-responsive epilepsy. Rather than asking whether group differences exist, we asked whether individuallevel deviations discriminate these groups strongly enough to be clinically useful. We also estimated the effect sizes this cohort could detect and the range of effects compatible with the data, so that negative findings could be interpreted as bounds.

Materials and methods

Patient Selection

This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Pennsylvania (protocol 835008) with a waiver of informed consent. Normative reference data were obtained from the Human Connectome Project Young Adult and Aging datasets under their respective data use agreements; participants in those studies provided written informed consent at the originating institutions.

Epilepsy-protocol 3-Tesla MRI brain scans with both T1-weighted and 30-direction diffusion-weighted sequences were obtained from patients who were seen at the Penn Epilepsy Center at the University of Pennsylvania from May 2022 to January 2025. Medical records were reviewed by a board-certified epileptologist (D.Z.) to determine each patient's clinical diagnosis and classification, based on the assessments of their treating epileptologists.

Patients with epilepsy and patients with functional seizures without comorbid epilepsy were included. Patients without a clear diagnosis of either, including those with functional seizures occurring alongside epilepsy, single unprovoked or acute symptomatic seizures, or events that were not clearly epileptic, were excluded. Among patients with epilepsy, those with prior brain surgery before the time of MRI, or without usable diffusion or morphometric data, were excluded.

Epilepsy was classified as focal, generalized, or unclassified according to the treating epileptologist's diagnosis. Among patients treated with ASM for at least one year, drug resistance was defined according to the International League Against Epilepsy as failure of two tolerated, appropriately chosen and used ASM schedules to achieve sustained seizure freedom, 4 assessed at the most recent follow-up. Patients were classified as drug-responsive if they had been seizure-free on ASM for at least 12 consecutive months, with seizure freedom continuing through the most recent follow-up. Patients whose treatment response could not be clearly determined, such as those with continued seizures after only a single medication trial, were excluded from the study (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Extracted clinical metadata included age at first seizure, epilepsy duration, history of convulsive seizures or status epilepticus, presence of an epileptogenic lesion on MRI, and presence of interictal epileptiform discharges on routine or ambulatory EEG. Epilepsy duration was calculated from the date of first seizure to the date of the MRI scan. Presence of an epileptogenic lesion was determined by the treating epileptologist.

Image Acquisition and Processing

MRI scans of the clinical cohort were acquired at the Hospital of the University of Pennsylvania on Siemens 3T scanners. T1-weighted images were acquired using a magnetization-prepared rapid gradient-echo (MPRAGE) sequence at 1.0 mm isotropic resolution. Diffusion-weighted images were acquired with a single-shell echo-planar imaging sequence comprising 30 diffusion-encoding directions at b = 1000 s/mm² and one nondiffusion-weighted (b = 0) volume, at a native resolution of 2.2 mm isotropic. A separate b = 0 volume with reversed phase-encoding direction was acquired to estimate the susceptibility-induced B0 field, and the resulting geometric distortions were corrected during preprocessing using FSL topup. 21 Cortical thickness and subcortical volumes were estimated from T1-weighted images using FreeSurfer (version 7.3.2) 22 with the Desikan-Killiany atlas, 23 yielding 68 cortical and 14 subcortical regions of interest. Diffusion-weighted images were preprocessed using QSIPrep (version 1.0.1) 24 and resampled to 1.25 mm isotropic resolution. White matter tract profiles were reconstructed using pyAFQ (version 2.1), 25 yielding FA and MD, sampled at 100 nodes along each of 16 bundles. The middle cerebellar peduncle was excluded, as brainstem tractography is prone to susceptibility-induced distortion and artifactual contamination by crossing fibers. 26

Normative Modeling and Data Harmonization

Deviation scores were derived separately for morphometry and tractometry, each expressing an individual's brain measures as a deviation from an age-and sex-matched healthy reference population (Fig. 1).

For morphometry, z-scores for cortical thickness and subcortical volume were obtained from CentileBrain, which used pretrained sex-specific models expressing each region as a standardized residual adjusting for age, sex, intracranial volume for subcortical volumes, and mean cortical thickness for cortical thickness. 27 These models were derived from over 37,000 healthy individuals aged 3-90. Cortical thickness and subcortical volumes were extracted with FreeSurfer 7.3.2, and the corresponding scanner vendor (Siemens) and FreeSurfer version were provided to CentileBrain as covariates.

For tractometry, z-scores for FA and MD along tracts were referenced to healthy subjects from the Human Connectome Project (HCP) Young Adult (ages 22-35, 1064 subjects) and Aging (ages 36-100+, 1365 subjects) datasets, 28, 29 preprocessed with the HCP minimal preprocessing pipelines. 30 Diffusion tensors were fit to the b = 1000 s/mm² shell for HCP Young Adult and to the b = 1500 s/mm² shell for HCP Aging, which has no b = 1000 shell. Missing left-fornix profiles (22 clinical and 46 HCP scans) were replaced with the pooled mean. Site-and protocol-related variance was removed with neuroCombat (version 0.2.12), 31 with acquisition site as the batch variable (six sites: HCP Young Adult, the four HCP Aging sites, and Penn), age and sex as protected covariates, and site parameters estimated from all scans without a reference batch. As deviations shared by all patients may be partly absorbed into site effects and harmonization can distort the normative age curve, we repeated all tractometry comparisons without harmonization and with site parameters estimated from reference subjects only (Supplementary Fig. 4). Following harmonization, each tract and metric was regressed on age and sex in the reference subjects only (the HCP cohorts and 26 syncope patients who underwent the same epilepsy-protocol imaging), and each patient's value was expressed as a standardized residual. Deviations for the 42 patients (14%) younger than 22 years of age (39 with epilepsy, 3 with functional seizures) relied on extrapolation below the HCP age range. The syncope reference patients had a median age of 33.4 years (IQR 26.0-48.6), versus 36.0 years (IQR 26.0-46.0) in the analyzed cohort.

Region-level measures were aggregated for statistical analyses. For morphometry, ROI z-scores were averaged (unweighted) within seven regional groups (frontal, temporal, parietal, occipital, insula, thalamus, and basal ganglia), combining cortical and subcortical structures where both contributed (hippocampus and amygdala within the temporal group; caudate, putamen, pallidum, and nucleus accumbens as basal ganglia; thalamus alone).

Composite z-scores retained their sign, so that negative values indicate lower thickness or volume than expected. A lobe-level asymmetry index was computed as the absolute difference between left and right hemisphere mean z-scores, without normalization by the bilateral sum, which is unstable when z-scores approach zero. For tractometry, left and right homologues of each paired tract were averaged node-by-node, and commissural tracts were used as reconstructed, yielding nine tract profiles (six paired bilateral tracts and three commissural tracts) of 100 nodes each. For each profile, a tract-level mean z-score summarized overall deviation, while the full node-wise profile was retained for along-tract analysis.

Statistical Analyses

Analyses were performed in Python using scipy and statsmodels. 32, 33 Group demographics were compared using Fisher's exact test for categorical variables and Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous variables. Three imaging comparisons were performed: epilepsy versus functional seizures, focal versus generalized epilepsy, and drug-resistant versus drug-responsive epilepsy. Because normative z-scores were derived from age-and sex-stratified reference models, no additional covariate adjustment was applied in the primary analyses.

Imaging features were analyzed at two scales: region/tract-level summary scores and node-wise along-tract profiles. Lobar composite z-scores, hemispheric asymmetry indices (|z left -z right |), and tract-level mean z-scores (FA and MD) were compared using Mann-Whitney U tests. Discriminability was quantified as the area under the curve (AUC = P(group_a > group_b)), reported as max(AUC, 1 -AUC) with the direction of each effect given separately, with 95% confidence intervals from 5,000 bootstrap resamples. Benjamini-Hochberg correction was applied within each feature family per comparison (lobar composite z-scores, asymmetry indices, and pooled FA+MD tract z-scores). Two secondary analyses were performed. First, whole-brain deviation burden was summarized per patient as the number of regions with |z|>2 and the mean |z| across the 82 morphometric regions, the 18 tract-level FA and MD z-scores, and the 3,000 node-level z-scores of the 15 bundles without left-right averaging, with correction across these six measures; the epilepsy versus functional seizures comparison was repeated in non-lesional epilepsy. Second, the mean hippocampal zscore and hippocampal asymmetry index were compared, with correction across the two measures. As a positive control, these measures were compared between patients with unilateral mesial temporal sclerosis and no other lesion and patients with non-lesional epilepsy, and the sclerotic hippocampus was compared with the contralateral side (Wilcoxon signed-rank test).

Along-tract differences in FA and MD were assessed using cluster-based permutation testing, without covariate adjustment. Group labels were randomly permuted 5,000 times; for each permutation, a t-statistic was computed at each node comparing groups, contiguous suprathreshold nodes (p<0.05) were grouped into clusters, and the maximum cluster mass was retained to form a null distribution specific to that tract. Observed clusters were tested against this tract-specific null, controlling the family-wise error rate across nodes within each tract. As all bilateral tracts were surveyed, along-tract findings were considered exploratory.

Minimum detectable effect sizes were estimated for each comparison by Monte Carlo simulation (3,000 iterations per candidate effect size), assuming normally distributed scores with equal variance and a location shift between groups, using each comparison's achieved sample sizes, 80% power, and a two-sided Mann-Whitney U test at α=0.05, both uncorrected and at α=0.05/18 (a single true effect within the largest feature family after correction).

Effects were expressed as the minimum detectable AUC.

Results

Demographic and clinical information

Demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. We identified 781 patients who underwent a clinical 3-Tesla epilepsy-protocol brain MRI at the University of Pennsylvania. After exclusions, the analyzed cohort comprised 273 patients with epilepsy and 32 with functional seizures without comorbid epilepsy (Supplementary Fig. 1). Within the epilepsy cohort, 203 (74%) had focal epilepsy, 37 (14%) generalized, and 33 (12%) unclassified; 176 (64%) were drug-responsive and 97 (36%) drug-resistant. Patients with epilepsy and functional seizures did not differ in age at MRI but differed in sex distribution (p<0.001; Table 1). Focal and generalized epilepsy differed in age at onset, age at MRI, history of convulsive seizures, and presence of an epileptogenic lesion (all p<0.05), but not in sex or status epilepticus history. Drug-responsive and drug-resistant patients differed in age at onset, epilepsy duration, and history of convulsive seizures (all p<0.05).

Epilepsy versus functional seizures

Lobar composite and asymmetry z-scores did not differ significantly between epilepsy and functional seizures after FDR correction (Fig. 2A-B). In tractometry, no tract differed at the whole-tract level (Fig. 2C-D), and along-tract analysis showed no significant clusters.

These results held without harmonization and with reference-estimated site parameters estimated from reference subjects only (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Focal versus generalized epilepsy

Lobe-level composite and asymmetry z-scores did not differ significantly between focal and generalized epilepsy after FDR correction (Fig. 3A-B). In tractometry, no tract differed significantly at the whole-tract level (Fig. 3C-D). Without harmonization, eight MD tracts separated the groups (AUC up to 0.72, q<0.05), but this reflected a miscalibrated reference age curve, as the b-value difference between HCP cohorts shifted the median MD z-score of reference subjects aged 36-50 to about -1.0, and focal and generalized epilepsy differed in age (Supplementary Fig. 4). No tract differed when age and sex were protected and site parameters were estimated from reference subjects only. Exploratory along-tract analysis revealed several differences: compared with generalized epilepsy, focal epilepsy showed lower FA in the central segment of the forceps minor (p=0.007), and higher MD in the mid-segment and posterior segments of the inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus (p=0.016 and 0.043, respectively), posterior arcuate fasciculus (p=0.022), and anterior forceps major (p=0.038) (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Drug-responsive versus drug-resistant epilepsy

Lobe-level composite and asymmetry z-scores did not differ significantly between drug-responsive and drug-resistant epilepsy after FDR correction (Fig. 4A-B). In tractometry, no tract differed significantly at the whole-tract level after FDR correction (Fig. 4C-D).

Exploratory along-tract analysis localized higher MD in drug-resistant epilepsy to the anterior cingulum (p=0.017), and lower FA in drug-resistant epilepsy to the mid-segment of the inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus (p=0.048) (Supplementary Fig. 3). In a sensitivity analysis with age and sex protected and site parameters estimated from reference subjects only, three tract differences reached significance at AUC ≈0.60 (cingulum MD, arcuate fasciculus FA, and inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus FA; Supplementary Fig. 4).

Secondary analyses

Whole-brain morphometric deviation burden was modestly higher in epilepsy than in functional seizures (median 7 vs 5 of 82 regions with |z|>2; AUC 0.66, 95% CI 0.57-0.74, q=0.010; mean |z| AUC 0.67, q=0.009; Supplementary Fig. 5A). When restricted to patients with non-lesional epilepsy, the difference from functional seizures was attenuated and did not survive correction (n=184 vs 32; regions with |z|>2 AUC 0.62, 95% CI 0.53-0.72, q=0.08; mean |z| AUC 0.64, 95% CI 0.54-0.74, q=0.07), and patients with lesional epilepsy had the highest burden (median 9 regions of interest). Burden did not differ between focal and generalized or drug-resistant and drug-responsive epilepsy, and tractometric burden did not differ in any comparison (Supplementary Table 1).

Hippocampal measures (mean z-score and asymmetry) did not discriminate any of the three comparisons (Supplementary Table 1). Among 12 patients with unilateral mesial temporal sclerosis and no other lesion (6 left, 6 right), the sclerotic hippocampus was smaller than the contralateral side (median difference -1.00; Wilcoxon p=0.007), by ≥3 standard deviations in 4 of 12 (Supplementary Fig. 5B). Hippocampal asymmetry in these patients was correspondingly greater than in non-lesional epilepsy (median 1.00 vs 0.48; AUC 0.72, 95% CI 0.55-0.88, q=0.02; Supplementary Fig. 5C), whereas the mean hippocampal z-score did not differ significantly.

Minimum detectable effect sizes

At 80% power and uncorrected α=0.05, the study could detect AUC ≥0.65 for epilepsy versus functional seizures, ≥0.64 for focal versus generalized, and ≥0.60 for drugresistant versus drug-responsive epilepsy (Table 2). For a single true effect within the largest correction family (18 tract features), these increased to 0.70, 0.69, and 0.64. The largest observed AUCs were 0.61 (95% CI 0.50-0.72), 0.62 (0.53-0.70), and 0.60 (0.53-0.67), respectively (Fig. 5; Supplementary Table 1).

Discussion

Normative modeling of clinical epilepsy-protocol MRI did not reliably discriminate between the clinical groups we tested. In the primary analyses, no regional or tract-level measure (lobar composite, hemispheric asymmetry, or whole-tract mean) distinguished epilepsy from functional seizures, focal from generalized epilepsy, or drug-resistant from drug-responsive epilepsy. In secondary analyses, neither hippocampal measure separated the groups, and the one significant difference was in whole-brain deviation burden, which was higher in epilepsy than in functional seizures (AUC 0.66) and partly reflected visible lesions (AUC 0.62 in non-lesional epilepsy), an effect detectable at the group level but insufficient for individual-level classification. Even the largest regional or tract-level effect compatible with these data (upper confidence limit 0.72) would not be actionable. Under a binormal model, an AUC of 0.72 corresponds to about 32% sensitivity at 90% specificity, whereas discrimination conventionally considered outstanding (AUC ≥0.90) 35 would be a reasonable minimum before such a marker could inform decisions such as withdrawing ASM. Within the precision of this cohort, univariate normative deviation summaries did not reach a level of discrimination that could inform clinical care.

The gap between group-level effects and individual-level discrimination has also been reported for normative modeling in other disorders. In schizophrenia, normative modeling of harmonized diffusion MRI in 601 patients and 512 controls produced larger group-level effects in deviation scores than in raw measures, yet summary deviation measures classified individuals at AUC below 0.63; only combining tracts and diffusion measures exceeded 0.70. 36 Our findings followed the same pattern: whole-brain deviation burden and hippocampal asymmetry differed at the group level, but neither discriminated individuals well. In temporal lobe epilepsy, supervised classification of multimodal normative deviations reached AUC 0.77 for distinguishing patients from disease controls and 0.64 for predicting postsurgical seizure freedom. 37 Together, these studies indicate that combining deviations across regions and modalities could improve on single summary scores, but that discrimination with multimodal models remains below the level required for clinical decision-making.

Structural correlates have been reported in both epilepsy and functional seizures, but they differ in extent and consistency. In functional seizures, findings have been subtle and variable across studies, including superior temporal and occipital cortical changes and reduced motor and premotor cortical thickness and volume. 38, 39 In epilepsy, large-scale analyses have established widespread cortical thinning and subcortical volume reduction, but the abnormalities shared by all epilepsy groups are modest against healthy controls: lower right thalamic volume (Cohen's d 0.24-0.73) and lower bilateral precentral cortical thickness (d 0.34-0.52), corresponding to AUCs of roughly 0.57-0.70 and 0.60-0.64. 10 These effects lie largely at or below this cohort's minimum detectable effect, and the expected separation from functional seizures, which carry their own structural deviations, is smaller than that from healthy controls, consistent with the weak separation observed here.

The hippocampal positive control illustrates the gap between group-level and individual-level effects. In mesial temporal lobe epilepsy with hippocampal sclerosis, group analyses report large reductions in ipsilateral hippocampal volume (d = 1.73-1.91, AUC ≈ 0.90). 10 Here, hippocampal asymmetry separated unilateral mesial temporal sclerosis from non-lesional epilepsy, but marked volume loss (≥3 SD) was present in only a third of patients. Hippocampal sclerosis is diagnosed from T2/FLAIR hyperintensity and loss of internal architecture as well as atrophy, and these features can occur without measurable volume loss on T1-weighted MRI. Even for this lesion, the large effect reported in group analyses did not translate into consistent individual-level deviation.

In the primary analyses, no summary-level measure separated the groups in either within-epilepsy comparison, and differences were confined to short along-tract segments. These findings were exploratory, although the focal-generalized differences were directionally consistent with a meta-analysis reporting more pronounced white matter change in focal epilepsy. 12 The segments were predominantly in long association and commissural pathways, which have been shown to be affected across epilepsy syndromes. 11 Such a shared signal could produce weak group-level differences while lacking the specificity to distinguish focal from generalized or drug-resistant from drug-responsive epilepsy. Because epileptogenic networks vary across patients, the deviation common to a clinical group is likely small, suggesting that classification may require multimodal features rather than further refinement of single-modality summary scores. Several limitations should be considered. This was a single-site, cross-sectional study, and clinical diagnosis, epilepsy type, and drug-response status were determined by retrospective review rather than independent adjudication or a uniform confirmatory protocol, so misclassification cannot be excluded. Because normative z-scores were already age-and sex-referenced, no further covariate adjustment was applied, leaving within-epilepsy comparisons unadjusted for clinical differences between groups. Epilepsy duration, for example, was longer in drug-resistant patients, possibly reflecting referral patterns, and could contribute to the diffusivity differences observed. Without presurgical evaluation, the epileptogenic zone could not be localized, and without seizure frequency data, imaging differences could not be separated from cumulative seizure burden. Excluding patients with prior brain surgery was necessary for methodological consistency but may have omitted the most severe cases.

The normative references introduced further limitations. CentileBrain model fit varied across regions (Supplementary Table 2), with the weakest fit in the medial occipital cortex (explained variance 0.08-0.14 for pericalcarine), and some composites deviated from zero in every group (e.g., occipital, thalamus, and basal ganglia; Figs. 2 3 4), probably reflecting site calibration. Because the same models were applied to all groups, such offsets should largely cancel in between-group comparisons, although age-dependent miscalibration could bias comparisons between groups that differ in age. CentileBrain also conditions regional cortical thickness on mean cortical thickness, so diffuse thinning would not be captured by the regional composites. The clinical diffusion protocol (single shell, 30 directions, b = 1000 s/mm², 2.2 mm) differed in angular and spatial resolution from the HCP reference acquisitions, 40 and HCP Aging, which lacks a b = 1000 s/mm² shell, was fit at b = 1500 s/mm 2 , which increases FA and decreases MD. Because this shift coincides with the age boundary between the two HCP cohorts, residual protocol bias after ComBat harmonization 31 could distort the normative age curve, an effect most relevant to the focal versus generalized comparison, in which groups differed in age (Supplementary Fig. 4). Finally, 30-direction diffusion imaging is not part of consensus epilepsy MRI protocols such as HARNESS-MRI 41 and is not acquired at all centers, limiting the applicability of the tractometry analyses.

Future work could focus on the networks implicated in each patient's epilepsy.

Normative modeling yields patient-specific, spatially distributed deviations, but groups here were compared one region or tract at a time, so deviations confined to a patient's epileptogenic network would be diluted by unaffected regions and by other patients' differently located deviations. Localization from semiology, EEG, or presurgical evaluation could restrict analyses to the presumed epileptogenic network and align deviations to the focus. Alternatively, clustering deviation patterns across patients could identify subtypes and the modalities best suited to stratify them. Finer spatial sampling may also help, as cortical deviations here were averaged within large parcels and lobes, and the reconstructed bundles omitted superficial white matter, where abnormalities in temporal lobe epilepsy are prominent. 42, 43 Finally, the diffusion tensor assumes Gaussian diffusion and cannot capture the non-Gaussian diffusion arising from tissue microstructure; in temporal lobe epilepsy, non-Gaussian measures, which require multi-shell acquisitions, showed greater abnormality than conventional diffusivity in association white matter. 44 In a consecutive cohort imaged on a clinical epilepsy protocol, normative modeling of structural and diffusion MRI could not classify epilepsy by type or treatment response and separated epilepsy from functional seizures only weakly, through whole-brain deviation burden. Upper confidence limits remained below the discrimination required for clinical use, indicating that any individual-level signal in univariate deviation summaries is too small to be clinically informative. Normative modeling remains a powerful framework for characterizing distributed abnormalities in epilepsy, but regional deviation scores from clinical MRI are not yet ready to guide these classifications, and network-targeted, multimodal approaches may be needed to achieve clinically useful discrimination.
