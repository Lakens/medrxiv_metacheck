Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. Abstract

The physician global assessment (PhGA) is a core outcome measure in juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) but is incompletely captured, even in clinical registries. We developed a large language model (LLM) approach that extracts explicitly documented PhGA from clinical narratives and infers a score where none is recorded. To our knowledge, this is the first study to address both tasks.

Methods: We linked charts from 306 children with JIA at one center to the Childhood Arthritis and Rheumatology Research Alliance (CARRA) Registry (2016-2024). A structured prompt was developed on 100 randomly sampled charts, then evaluated on 400 charts from patients not represented in the development set. Extraction used GPT-5.2 within a HIPAA-compliant Azure environment. Registryrecorded PhGA served as the reference standard, with discordances adjudicated by a pediatric rheumatologist blinded to both sources. Agreement was assessed by intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC(2,1)), exact match, and mean absolute error (MAE).

Results: Against registry values, ICC(2,1) was 0.87 (95% CI 0.85-0.89; n=400), with 70.5% exact agreement and MAE 0.363. Agreement was near-perfect for explicit extractions (n=190; ICC 0.99; 95% CI 0.98-0.99) and lower for inferred scores (n=210; ICC 0.72; 95% CI 0.64-0.78). Large discordances (≥2 points) occurred in 7.3% of cases. Against the adjudicated standard, overall ICC(2,1) was 0.96 (95% CI 0.95-0.97), with 77.5% exact agreement and MAE 0.200. All 14 explicit discordances reflected mismatch between the registry and the clinical note, rather than extraction errors (adjudicated ICC 1.00). For inferred scores, ICC rose to 0.90 (95% CI 0.87-0.92). Residual disagreement was concentrated at the inactive/minimally active boundary.

An LLM can reliably extract PhGA from clinical notes with high accuracy to support downstream clinical research, offering a scalable approach to mitigating missingness in patient registries.

INTRODUCTION

The physician's global assessment of disease activity (PhGA) is a physician assigned score that reflects a clinician's overall assessment of disease activity, with values ranging from 0 for no activity, to 10 for maximum activity. It is a core outcome measure in juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA), serving as a component of the American College of Rheumatology JIA response criteria for defining clinically inactive disease. 1 Despite its importance, PhGA, which should be assigned by the practitioner at or near the conclusion of a clinic visit, is not consistently recorded at every encounter and is incompletely captured in clinical registries, limiting the completeness of disease activity measures and the cohorts available for longitudinal analysis. In the Childhood Arthritis and Rheumatology Research Alliance (CARRA) Registry -the largest clinical registry for childhood rheumatic diseases in North America, PhGA was missing from 6% of visits at baseline, rising to 82% at the 36-month follow-up. 2 Nonetheless, PhGA scores are often documented in clinical notes, either explicitly or as reflected in the treating rheumatologist's description of the physical examination and overall clinical impression.

With the advent of large language models (LLMs), there is a new opportunity not only to extract explicitly documented values from clinical text, but also to infer values that are not directly stated in the clinical record. The latter requires integrating contextual information captured in the clinical narrative, a capability that is difficult to achieve with traditional natural language processing approaches. We hypothesized that frontier LLMs could be effective in retrospectively inferring PhGA scores from clinical narratives, processing the treating rheumatologist's history, physician examination, and impression/plan. Here, we developed an LLM-based approach to extract PhGA from clinical narratives, extracting explicitly documented PhGA values and inferring a score from the narrative where none is recorded. To our knowledge this is the first study to address both extraction and inference of PhGA, offering a practical route to reducing missingness in registry-based disease activity monitoring.

METHODS

Study cohort and dataset

The study cohort comprised 306 children with JIA seen at Boston Children's Hospital (BCH) who were also enrolled in the Childhood Arthritis and Rheumatology Research Alliance (CARRA) Registry (years 2016-2024) (Table 1). The Registry captures gold standard PhGA data reported by the treating pediatric rheumatologists. We extracted clinical notes from the electronic health record (EHR) at BCH and linked each note to its corresponding CARRA Registry visit. A random sample of 100 charts were selected for prompt development before evaluation on a 400-chart test set drawn from patients not represented in the development set to prevent data leakage. Subjects contributed a mean of 1.6 charts each (median 1, range 1-6).

This study was approved by the Boston Children's Hospital Institutional Review Board (IRB-P00030533).

Prompt development

A structured prompt template was developed to extract PhGA from clinical narratives. The prompt instructs the LLM to first identify a physician-documented numeric PhGA value when present in the note (i.e. "explicit" extraction), and if no explicit value can be detected, to derive PhGA from documented examination findings, disease activity narrative, and treatment context (i.e. "inferred" extraction). Inference of PhGA was guided by scoring rules based on joint count, site-specific findings (e.g., temporomandibular joint, dactylitis), and presenting symptoms. Treatment direction (e.g. escalation of therapy) and laboratory findings were applied as secondary modifiers. Output was returned in structured format including the extracted PhGA value, the extraction type (i.e. explicit vs inferred), a confidence rating (high, medium or low), supporting text (verbatim text quoted from the note as evidence) and rationale (a brief explanation of how the score was derived).

The template was iteratively refined across multiple versions based on chart-level error analysis in the development set, with the final version locked prior to evaluation on the test set. Iterations addressed specific error patterns identified through manual chart review. These included explicit handling of nearremission presentations with minor residual exam or symptom findings to avoid systematic under-scoring of near remission cases; recognition of extra-articular features in the absence of active joint (e.g. uveitis, psoriasis); recognition of additional PhGA label conventions for explicit value detection; and consideration of treatment direction as a modifier.

PhGA extraction and inference were performed using GPT-5.2 (OpenAI), the most advanced frontier model available within BCH's HIPAA-compliant Microsoft Azure Service environment at the time of the study. Processing was conducted within this institutionally governed infrastructure, ensuring that clinical note content did not leave institutional control. The reference standard was the CARRA registrydocumented PhGA, supplemented by blinded adjudication by a pediatric rheumatologist (MDN). Accordingly, all discordances of magnitude ≥2 between LLM-extracted and registry-documented values were reviewed by a pediatric rheumatologist masked to both registry-documented and LLM-extracted PhGA. All explicit-extraction discordances were manually reviewed regardless of magnitude. Analyses were stratified by extraction modes (explicit versus inferred) and by the LLM's self-reported confidence level. Primary agreement statistics were intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC(2,1); two-way randomeffects, single-measure, absolute agreement), mean absolute error (MAE), mean signed difference (i.e. measure of directional bias), and percentage exact/near match to the reference standard. Provider-level variability was examined as an exploratory sub-analysis.

Self-consistency evaluation

It is well-established that LLM outputs are not fully deterministic. 3 The same prompt and input can result in different output across independent runs. 4 Variation in model output has important implications for the repeatability of the extraction approach. We therefore conducted a separate analysis to quantify variability in chart-level scores across 3 independent runs on the test set, using the locked prompt with no modifications between runs and with all model parameters held constant. Agreement among runs was characterized by ICC(2,1) across runs, pairwise ICC(2,1) between each pair, the proportion of charts receiving an identical score across all runs, and the proportion with any disagreement in extraction type.

For each chart, we further computed the standard deviation of the extracted PhGA value across runs as a chart-level instability metric. This measure is independent of accuracy: a chart may be stably incorrect (low standard deviation, high error) or unstably correct. We conducted 2 exploratory comparisons using this metric: (a) whether chart-level instability is associated with LLM-reported extraction confidence; and (b) whether instability is associated with magnitude of disagreement from the reference standard.

Multi-run aggregation

Given the inherent stochasticity of LLM outputs, aggregation across runs has the potential to reduce error where variation is stochastic. 5 The median across runs was used as the aggregation method instead of the mean, since PhGA is recorded on a discrete 0.5-point grid and thus arithmetic averaging of discordant runs produces values that cannot exactly match any reference value. The median returns an observed value, and with three runs is unaffected by a single aberrant run.

Assessment of hallucination

Accuracy of the extracted PhGA value does not establish that the LLM's stated reasoning is faithful to the source note. An extraction may report a numerically correct score while citing a clinical finding that does not appear in the note, or referencing a scoring rule that does not exist in the prompt. We therefore conducted a separate assessment of extraction faithfulness. Accordingly, we assessed three potential categories of hallucination: (1) citation fabrication: one or more snippets in supporting text do not appear in the source note; (2) schema fabrication: rationale cites a rule that does not exist in the prompt; (3) rationale fabrication: rationale asserts a clinical finding that is not documented in the note, or that contradicts the note. An extraction was classified as containing a hallucination if it failed any of the three categories.

For citation fabrication, each snippet in supporting text was compared against the source note following Unicode normalization, harmonization of quotation-mark and dash variants, whitespace collapsing, and case-folding. A snippet was classified as grounded if it appeared as an exact normalized substring of the note. All flagged extractions underwent blinded manual review to determine whether they represented true hallucinations. In addition, an independent stratified random sample of 50 extractions (20 explicit, 30 inferred) underwent blinded manual review to provide an estimate for schema and clinical fabrications, and as an additional check on citation fabrication. The hallucination rate was reported, with 95% confidence intervals; where no events were observed, the upper bound of the 95% confidence interval is reported using the rule of three (3/n). 6

RESULTS

In the held-out test set, agreement between LLM-extracted and registry-documented PhGA was ICC(2,1) = 0.87 (95% CI 0.85-0.89). Extracted values matched the registry exactly in 70.5% of cases, with 81.8% falling within 0.5 points and 91.2% within 1.0 point (MAE 0.363). The mean signed difference was -0.012, indicating no net directional bias overall. Figure 1 shows the distribution of the difference between LLM-extracted and registry-documented PhGA scores (LLM minus registry). Figure 2 shows the mean (SD) of this difference across each registry-documented PhGA score. Corresponding development set results, which informed prompt iteration are summarized in Table 2.

Large discordances (|difference| ≥ 2) were uncommon (5% [n=5] of development set and 7.2% [n=29] of test set comparisons) and were concentrated in inferred cases with medium LLM-reported confidence. In the test set, 41 cases were adjudicated: all 14 explicit discordances (with |difference| ≥ 2) and 27 inferred discordances with |difference| ≥ 2. Applying adjudicated values, ICC(2,1) rose to 0.96 (95% CI 0.95-0.97), with exact agreement rising to 77.5% and MAE falling to 0.200 (Table 3).

Performance by extraction type

Performance differed substantially by extraction type. In the held-out test set, there were 190 and 210 explicit and inferred cases, respectively. Explicit extraction showed near-perfect raw agreement (ICC = 0.99, n=190, 92.6% exact match). All 14 discordant explicit cases were reviewed against the source note and confirmed to be discordances between the registry and clinical notes, rather than LLM extraction errors, yielding 100% exact agreement (ICC = 1.00) once corrected. Inferred extraction showed lower uncorrected agreement (ICC = 0.72, n=210, 50.5% exact match), rising to ICC = 0.90 (95% CI 0.87 -0.92) with the adjudicated corrections applied. One confirmed LLM error identified a specific, actionable limitation of the current prompt: a chart in which fever was present but attributable to a concurrent viral illness rather than active JIA.

Among inferred extractions, near-remission cases accounted for a substantial share of discordances. 38 of 104 inferred discordances (36.5%) differed by exactly 0.5 points, and the single most common pattern (n=20) was a registry value of 0 against an inferred value of 0.5.

Zero-inflation sensitivity analyses

PhGA is zero-inflated in this population, with 50.2% (n=201) charts having a PhGA score of 0 (Supplementary Figure S1). Uncorrected ICC(2,1) restricted to the active disease subset (i.e. PhGA > 0, n=199) was 0.91, higher than the corresponding unrestricted value (ICC 0.87). Because this analysis removes the zero-inflated portion of the distribution entirely rather than reweighting around it, it provides direct evidence that residual disagreement concentrates at the inactive/near-zero boundary rather than reflecting a general difficulty grading disease severity. This is corroborated by treating PhGA=0 versus PhGA>0 as a binary classification: with inactive disease (PhGA=0) as the positive class, sensitivity was 84.1% and specificity was 95.5%, and binary agreement was 89.8% (Cohen's κ = 0.80).

Extraction confidence

The LLM's self-reported confidence field was strongly associated with actual accuracy (Table 4). Highconfidence extractions (n=292) achieved ICC(2,1) = 0.93 (exact agreement=86.0%; MAE = 0.200) against registry values, compared with ICC(2,1) = 0.68 (28.7% exact agreement; MAE = 0.801) for medium-confidence extractions (n=108). No cases were rated as low-confidence.

Provider-level agreement

Charts were linked to one of 21 distinct providers. Of these, 13 had at least 10 notes in the test set, sufficient for a reasonably stable provider-level ICC estimate; the remaining providers (n=18 charts total) are reported descriptively only, given inadequate precision for individual ICC estimation at that sample size.

Among the 13 providers with n≥10, ICC against the registry-documented gold standard ranged from 0.79 to 0.98 (Supplementary Table S2). No consistent directional bias was observed across providers (mean signed difference ranged -0.14 to +0.28), and individual providers' largest discordances were distributed in both directions rather than reflecting a uniform pattern, arguing against a single systematic scoring bias tied to provider.

The lowest-agreement provider (ICC = 0.79, n=47) was examined directly via chart review. Notes from this provider were subjectively judged to be more frequently ambiguous in their description of joint findings than notes from higher-agreement providers, offering a plausible explanation for this provider's lower agreement.

Multi-run aggregation

Aggregating across runs had a negligible effect on agreement. Using the pre-specified median-of-three rule, ICC rose to 0.88 against the registry, with a median MAE of 0.350.

Self-consistency Assessment

Single-run repeatability was ICC(2,1) = 0.97 (95% CI 0.97-0.98) across the three runs (pairwise range 0.97-0.98), indicating that a single execution reproduces closely. Extractions were identical across all three runs for 309 (77.2%) charts; the aggregation rule therefore affects only the remaining 91. Medianof-three aggregation changed the extracted value relative to a single run on 7.8-10.2% of charts depending on the run (Supplementary Table S3).

Instability was almost entirely confined to the inferred extractions. Explicit extractions were identical across all three runs in 98.9% of cases (self-agreement ICC = 1.00), compared with 57.6% for inferred extractions (ICC = 0.94). Extraction method itself was unstable in a few cases: 7 charts (1.8%) were assigned a different extraction type in at least one of the three runs. Manual review of these charts found 3 causes. In 4 charts, the note documented a PhGA using a label in the prompt as high-confidence ("MD global", "Physician VAS"), yet the value was not recognized in at least one run. In one chart, the documented value used a malformed denominator ("1/8"). The remaining two involved ambiguous text: a value labeled only "MD" without indicating a global assessment, which 2 runs treated as explicit and one did not, and the use of non-standard acronym "GPA".

Cross-run instability was strongly associated with error. Using median-of-three values, charts stable across all three runs had an MAE of 0.233 and exact-match agreement of 81.9%, compared with 0.747 and 31.9% for charts with any between-run disagreement. Instability was likewise associated with modelreported confidence: 90.8% of high-confidence extractions were stable across all three runs versus 40.7% of medium-confidence extractions. Extraction confidence and cross-run instability therefore provide two complementary signals identifying extractions most likely to require manual review, both obtainable without reference to a gold standard. This association was not uniform: the single most unstable chart in the test set (values of 6.0, 2.0, and 5.0 across three runs against a registry value of 5.0) was assigned high confidence, indicating that confidence alone does not fully capture extraction reliability.

Assessment of hallucinations

Automated screening of all 400 test-set extractions identified no fabricated citations. Blinded manual review of 50 extractions (20 Explicit, 30 Inferred, randomly selected within each stratum) identified no instances of citation, schema, or clinical fabrication, giving an observed hallucination rate of 0% (onesided upper 95% bound 6.0% by the rule of three; 15.0% within the Explicit stratum and 10.0% within the Inferred stratum).

One reviewed extraction, while meeting all three pre-specified criteria, illustrated a distinct failure mode. The source note documented a swollen and tender second DIP joint but described the patient's remaining pain regions as of uncertain attribution, offered an alternative explanation for them (prior Osgood-Schlatter disease at the same site), and raised then withdrew the possibility of enthesitis. The extraction's rationale cited the documented findings and treatment escalation accurately without fabricating any content, but did not account for the clinician's stated uncertainty. The resulting score exceeded the reference value by one point.

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrates that an LLM can reliably recover PhGA from routine clinical notes, achieving agreement with an adjudicated reference standard that exceeds previously published inter-rater reliability for the measure. 7 To our knowledge, this is the first study to show that an LLM can not only extract explicitly documented PhGA values, but also in infer PhGA from clinical narratives when no score is recorded. The capability offers a scalable approach to recover missing PhGA data in clinical registries, expanding the availability of these measures for downstream clinical research.

Beyond recovering missing values, our study found that registry-recorded PhGA differed from the value documented in clinical notes in 14 of 190 explicit extractions (7.4%), pointing to possible errors in existing registry data. The differences ranged from -2 to +2 points. Discrepancies of this magnitude can be consequential: PhGA is a direct component of the clinical Juvenile Arthritis Disease Activity Score (cJADAS10), and based on the American College of Rheumatology 2021 cJADAS10 cutoffs, a 2-point difference can move a visit between disease activity states (e.g. across the inactive disease threshold of 2.5 in polyarticular JIA). 8 In our analysis, we treated the note as the gold standard where PhGA was explicitly documented, on the premise that it is a more direct record of the physician's assessment than the registry entry transcribed separately, typically by a research coordinator. However, it is possible that some discordances may reflect documentation error in the note, rather than transcription error in the registry. Reconciling these discrepances would be important beyond the goal of this study.

Among inferred extractions, near-remission cases accounted for a substantial share of discordances. This is likely partially attributable to the prompt design. During development of the prompt, we observed systematic under-scoring of near-remission cases, in which notes documenting remission alongside minor residual findings were assigned a score of 0. We therefore introduced rules assigning 0.5 where remission or inactive-disease language is accompanied by a single minor residual finding, and 1.0 where two or more are present. The 0-versus-0.5 discordances observed in the test set correspond closely to this presentation. Although these rules corrected an error pattern observed in the development dataset, they may have over-corrected in the test set. These rules may also not transfer at another institution, where near-remission findings may be documented differently. Testing in an external cohort with different physicians and documentation practices is needed to determine whether the half-point rules improve accuracy.

Given the well-documented concerns about hallucination in medical applications of LLMshttps://sciwheel.com/work/citation?ids=19242140,18138835&pre=&pre=&suf=&suf=&sa=0,0&dbf=0&dbf=0 the absence of detected fabrication in our study is reassuring. This finding may in part reflect the design of the extraction task. Hallucination is most often characterized in the context of open-ended generation, where an LLM generates assertions with no source against which they can be verified. 11 PhGA extraction is a tightly bounded task: the complete source document (i.e. clinical note) is provided in the context window, the output is short and constrained by a pre-specified schema, and the scoring rules are defined by a closed set of tables, ranges, and preconditions. Importantly, the output schema requires verbatim supporting text before a finding can be asserted, thus forcing the model to identify evidence in the source document rather than generate a claim and justify it afterward. These design features may reduce opportunities for unsupported content generation and are consistent with approaches shown to improve LLM reliability in other settings. 12, 13 Several specific improvements to the prompt are indicated for the next iteration. First, the scoring rules currently do not account for symptoms that may not be attributable to JIA. In one chart, this produced a high-severity score for fever caused by a concurrent viral illness. Second, in the explicit extractions, several labels (e.g. a bare "MD" preceding a fraction, and the acronym "GPA") were treated inconsistently across runs. These cases should be classified as ambiguous, routing to inference. Another issue relates to how documented uncertainty is handled. Our prompt specified how to weight documented findings but did not specify how to treat findings that the clinician had expressed uncertainty. Specifying this directly (e.g. instructing that findings the clinician does not attribute to JIA with reasonable confidence be excluded from scoring) may be a reasonable refinement for subsequent iterations.

Limitations and future directions

There are several limitations to our study. First, prompt behaviour may be model-dependent. We optimized our prompt template against a single model version (GPT 5.2). Its performance cannot be assumed to generalize to other models or on a subsequent version of the same model. Second, we conducted the evaluation at a single institution. Some prompt components were developed in response to patterns observed in our development data and may encode local documentation conventions, which may not generalize to other institutions. External validation is therefore an important next step. Some degree of site-specific adaptation, however, may be unavoidable, given differences in documentation conventions across institutions. However, we anticipate that the underlying scoring logic will require less adaptation, given that it is grounded in how JIA is assessed, rather than in the specific ways they are documented. Finally, clinician-adjudicated scores can only be derived from the content of the note, which may not be complete.

Our analysis points to several specific improvements to prioritize in the next prompt iteration. First, the scoring rules currently do not account for clinical manifestations that resemble JIA-related symptoms but not attributable to underlying JIA disease activity. In one case, the model incorrectly assigned a higher PhGA based on fever caused by a concurrent viral illness rather than JIA disease activity. Second, several non-conventional labels for PhGA (e.g. "MD" preceding a fraction, or the acronym "GPA") were treated inconsistently across runs. Such cases should be classified as ambiguous and routed to the inference pathway, rather than treated as definitive extractions. Another area for refinement is the handling of documented clinical uncertainty. Although the prompt specified how to weight documented findings, it did not explicitly address findings for which the clinician expressed uncertainty about their attribution to JIA. This could be addressed in subsequent iterations by specifying that findings not attributed to JIA by the clinician with reasonable confidence should be excluded from PhGA scoring.

CONCLUSION

Our findings demonstrate the potential of LLMs as a scalable approach to addressing gaps in the availability of PhGA for clinical research. With careful design and validation, these approaches could make clinical registries more complete and support their use in downstream research. s
