Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Introduction

Brain health in later life, encompassing cognitive function and mental wellbeing, is a growing global priority. Cognitive decline spans a spectrum from mild impairment to dementia, 16 while depression is characterised by persistent low mood, loss of interest, and impaired daily functioning. 17 Both carry substantial consequences for independence, quality of life, and mortality, share common risk factors, frequently co-occur, and may be bidirectionally related. 18, 19 The burden is shifting decisively toward the global south, and eastern sub-Saharan Africa is projected to experience one of the steepest increases in dementia cases worldwide by 2050. 20 In the Hai district studied here, dementia prevalence in adults aged 70 years and older rose from 6.4% in 2009-2010 to 8.9% in 2018-2019. 27 Prevention frameworks have so far concentrated on individually modifiable risk. The 2024 Lancet Commission identified 14 modifiable risk factors accounting for approximately 45% of dementia cases worldwide. 23 Recent work argues that this framework under-represents the realities of the global south, 21, 22 and shows that incorporating poverty, wealth shocks, income inequality, and HIV raises the potentially preventable fraction to as much as 65%. 24 There are corresponding calls to shift prevention from individually targeted behaviour change toward structural approaches, since behaviour-based prevention places the burden on individuals and may widen disparities when better-resourced groups benefit disproportionately. 25, 26 Poverty is among the most consequential of these structural determinants, and is increasingly understood as multidimensional rather than purely economic. 1 Individuals commonly experience overlapping social, material, and psychosocial deprivations that interact to shape health. 1, 2 This is reflected in the global Multidimensional Poverty Index adopted by the United Nations Development Programme, which assesses deprivation across health, education, and standard of living and classifies individuals as multidimensionally poor when they experience a weighted threshold of deprivations. 8, 9 Income-based measures alone capture neither the lived experience of poverty nor its consequences, whereas multidimensional measures help identify priority areas for intervention. 7 Older adults are particularly exposed. Disadvantage accumulates across the life course, so that limited schooling, poor living conditions, and food insecurity in early life compound into later life. 3 Older adults typically have fewer resources with which to respond to deprivation, 4 and are more likely to live with multimorbidity and functional decline. 5, 6 Sub-Saharan Africa carries a disproportionate share of this burden, with an estimated 556 million people affected, of whom 82% live in rural areas, 10 while experiencing some of the fastest demographic ageing globally. 13 Evidence directly linking multidimensional deprivation to brain health in African settings remains limited. A cross-sectional study in Soweto, South Africa, found that multidimensionally poor adults aged 50 years and older had 2.31 times the risk of dementia, 50 and work in rural Kenya has demonstrated a dose-response association between multidimensional poverty and depressive symptoms. 51 Neither examined cognition and depression together, and to our knowledge no study in East Africa has assessed multidimensional deprivation against both domains of brain health in the same older population.

This study assessed multidimensional deprivation in older adults in the Kilimanjaro region of northern Tanzania and examined its associations with depressive symptoms and cognitive function. By identifying which dimensions of deprivation carry the greatest association with each outcome, we aimed to inform the design of targeted, multisectoral interventions.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a cross-sectional analysis using data from the Akili Mali (Brain Wealth) study, a community-based study of older adults in the Hai district of Kilimanjaro region, northern Tanzania. Hai is a rural district on the slopes of Mount Kilimanjaro, with a population of 240 999 at the 2022 national census, 79.3% of whom live in rural areas. The majority of older residents are subsistence farmers and levels of illiteracy in this age group are high, reflecting historically limited access to schooling. 14 Dementia prevalence in this population rose over the decade to 2019, against declines reported in several high-income settings. 27 The study includes three rural communities stratified by geography and socioeconomic status, selected by the District Medical Office as typical exemplars of these strata: Narumu Tella, a highland community of relatively higher socioeconomic status; Orori, a neighbouring highland community of relatively lower socioeconomic status; and Sanya Station, a geographically and culturally distinct lowland community.

Participants and recruitment

A door-to-door census was conducted in each community by balozis, elected leaders of ten households who report to the village committee, recording year of birth and sex of all residents. Individuals born in 1965 or earlier were eligible, assuming a January birth date, and all were invited through door-to-door information delivered by members of the Akili Mali Lay Advisory Board, older residents of the participating communities who advise the study and are co-authors of this paper, together with the village committee. Those too frail to attend a study centre were offered a home visit, and those too unwell to participate were excluded and referred to services. 6 Eligible participants were aged 60 years or older, resident in one of the three study communities, and consented to participate. Where data were implausible, field teams returned to verify and correct entries, and years of birth were revised accordingly. All residents identified by the census as born in 1965 or earlier were invited to take part, and 1054 were confirmed as aged 60 years or older on verification of year of birth. Year of birth was not known for 13 participants, whose age is therefore an estimate.

Consent

All participants gave informed consent following a village information meeting and subsequent verbal and written information. For participants unable to read, information was read aloud by a study team member and consent was indicated using a thumbprint. Those lacking capacity to consent were included only with evidence of active willingness to participate and with consent provided by a close relative.

Multidimensional deprivation assessment

Multidimensional deprivation was measured using a Multidimensional Deprivation Index constructed with the Alkire-Foster counting methodology. 28 This framework is the most widely used and internationally recognised approach to multidimensional poverty measurement and underpins the global Multidimensional Poverty Index. 1, 8 The method identifies individuals experiencing overlapping deprivations across multiple domains of wellbeing. 29 Participants were assessed against a set of nine predefined indicators, each with a deprivation cutoff, and classified as deprived or not deprived on each. Indicators were coded in binary form (1 = deprived,0 = not deprived) and summed to give a deprivation count from 0 to 9. A normalised index score was computed as the mean of all binary indicators, producing a continuous measure between 0 and 1. Participants deprived on three or more indicators were 7 classified as multidimensionally deprived, following the convention adopted in the global index for a nine-indicator specification. 8, 28 Those with fewer than six valid indicators were not assigned a binary classification.

The dimensions used align with the global Multidimensional Poverty Index domains of education, health, and living standards. 8 Indicator selection was informed by the global index to facilitate comparison, while being adapted to reflect local context and available data. Within the education dimension, years of schooling and literacy capture formal attainment and current functional capability, respectively. We used an individual-level adaptation of the global index threshold of six years of schooling, and substituted literacy for school attendance to suit an older population. Within the health dimension, food insecurity and dietary diversity reflect short-term and structural aspects of food deprivation, both associated with adverse mental health and cognitive outcomes in later life. 30 The global index uses body-mass index to indicate undernutrition, but that measure has important limitations in older populations. Living standards were assessed with five indicators. Solid cooking fuel use and inadequate housing materials follow established global index cutoffs; household air pollution from solid fuel combustion remains one of the leading environmental risk factors globally and is concentrated in low-income settings. 31 Household crowding captures constrained living space, and absence of glass windows was used as a further marker of housing quality. Low perceived safety, although not a standard global index indicator, was included because perceived safety influences mobility, social participation, and mental health in older adults. 32 It was defined as a score at or below the sample 25th centile, a value of 7.0 or lower on the 1 to 10 scale, because the distribution was heavily skewed toward feeling safe. The full indicator set with cutoffs, derivation and source is given in online supplemental appendix 1.

Brain health assessment

Cognition was assessed using instruments with prior validation and normative data for Tanzania.

The Identification and Intervention for Dementia in Elderly Africans (IDEA) cognitive screen total score measured general cognition. It was developed for populations with low levels of formal education and, across Nigerian and Tanzanian cohorts, showed area under the receiver operating characteristic curve above 0.9 in all settings and no evidence of educational bias. 33 Two components were additionally analysed as full-scale scores because normative data were available: the CERAD ten-word list learning task, with minor local adaptation, 34 and one-minute categorical verbal fluency using animal naming.

Depressive symptoms were assessed using a previously established Swahili translation of the 15item Geriatric Depression Scale. 35 Although the scale is designed for self-administration, high levels of illiteracy and uncorrected near vision impairment meant that for most participants it was administered by a clinical research assistant. A score of 5 or more was classified as screenpositive depression. At this cutoff, pooled sensitivity across 43 studies is 0.89 and pooled specificity 0.77. 36 Most sociodemographic and general health measures were obtained by selfreport, including history of stroke, diabetes, HIV and treated hypertension, with the exception of body-mass index and blood pressure, which were measured at assessment. Uncontrolled hypertension was defined as a measured blood pressure in the hypertensive range, averaged over three readings taken at assessment, in a participant either not taking antihypertensive medication or remaining hypertensive despite treatment. The hypertensive range was taken as a systolic pressure of 140 mmHg or higher or a diastolic pressure of 90 mmHg or higher; mean systolic pressure was 140 mmHg or higher in 718 (97.4%) of the 737 participants so classified. Perceived social standing was measured with the MacArthur ladder, on which participants placed themselves on a ten-rung ladder relative to others in their village and to others in Tanzania.

Patient and public involvement

Older residents of the study communities were involved throughout. The Akili Mali Lay Advisory Board, whose members are drawn from the participating communities and are coauthors of this paper, contributed to the design of the study, advised on the acceptability and wording of the assessment measures, and delivered the door-to-door information that invited people to take part. Village committees and balozis supported recruitment and the community meetings at which the study was explained before consent was sought. Participants were not involved in the analysis or in drafting this manuscript. Findings will be returned to the communities through the Lay Advisory Board and the village committees.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were done in R version 4.3.3. Continuous variables are presented as mean (SD) and categorical variables as number (%). Comparisons between deprived and non-deprived participants used Welch two-sample t tests and Pearson χ² tests. Because the three communities were purposively selected to differ in geography and socioeconomic status, characteristics and outcomes were additionally compared across sites using one-way ANOVA and χ² tests, with all three pairwise contrasts examined and p values Bonferroni-corrected.

Before finalising the index, we assessed whether any indicators carried substantially overlapping information, which would risk double-counting a single underlying deprivation. Pairwise Pearson correlations were computed across all binary indicators and variance inflation factors were estimated by regressing each indicator on all others, with a value above 5 taken as indicating problematic multicollinearity. 40 10 Missingness ranged from under 1% to 13.3% across variables, being highest for verbal fluency and lowest for literacy at 0.2%, and reflecting assessment completion rather than any systematic pattern. Rather than restrict analyses to complete cases, 37 missing data were handled by multiple imputation by chained equations, generating 20 imputed datasets with ten iterations each. 38 Continuous variables were imputed by predictive mean matching and binary variables by logistic regression, with all exposure, outcome, and covariate variables in the imputation model.

Estimates were pooled using Rubin's rules. 39 Associations between deprivation and each brain health outcome were estimated with the normalised index score as the exposure, using linear regression for continuous outcomes and logistic regression for binary outcomes. Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 adjusted for age, sex, years of education, study community with Narumu Tella as reference, multimorbidity, tobacco use, alcohol use, body-mass index, and hospitalisation in the preceding three months.

Community was included as a fixed effect because the three sites were purposively selected to differ and therefore represent a structural rather than incidental source of variation. Each dimension sub-score was additionally modelled in place of the overall score, with identical adjustment.

Three prespecified sensitivity analyses were undertaken, each re-estimating every model with an alternative index: one excluding the educational deprivation indicator, compared against the standard index within an identical complete-case subsample, because years of education independently predicts cognitive test performance; one adding uncontrolled hypertension, which is used here as a marker of limited health-care access but is also an established vascular risk factor; and one weighting each dimension equally rather than each indicator equally, following the global index convention, since weighting is discretionary. Given the high prevalence of 11 screen-positive depression, depression models were additionally re-estimated using a more conservative GDS-15 threshold of 7 or more. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p value below 0.05.

Discussion

In this community-based study of 1054 older adults in rural northern Tanzania, multidimensional deprivation was the norm rather than the exception, with more than four in five participants meeting the threshold for multidimensional poverty. Higher index scores predicted greater depressive symptom burden, markedly increased odds of screen-positive depression, and poorer performance on global cognitive screening and verbal fluency, after adjustment for age, sex, education, community, comorbidity, and health behaviours. Three findings merit particular attention: the depression association strengthened rather than attenuated on adjustment; deprivation tracked measured cognition but bore no relationship to participants' own reports of difficulty; and the most deprived community had the best brain health outcomes, inverting the individual gradient.

The magnitude of the depression association is substantial, corresponding to an odds ratio of 1.37 for each additional deprivation and to a 17.2-fold difference in odds across the full range of the index after full adjustment. That the association strengthened on adjustment requires explanation rather than dismissal. Sanya Station combined the highest material deprivation with by far the lowest depression prevalence, so in unadjusted analysis this community-level pattern operated against the individual-level association and suppressed it; only when community entered the model did the underlying relationship emerge fully. This is substantive rather than technical, because it shows that aggregate comparisons across communities can obscure, and even reverse, relationships operating within them, a caution directly relevant to how population-level deprivation data are read in low-resource settings. That living standards deprivation was the strongest predictor of depressive symptoms, ahead of health and education, suggests that the immediate material conditions of daily life may bear more directly on mood in later life than educational history.

Our observed prevalence of screen-positive depression, at 69.3%, is high and warrants careful framing. Prevalence estimates for late-life depression in sub-Saharan Africa vary by an order of magnitude according to ascertainment method, with the Geriatric Depression Scale consistently yielding higher pooled prevalence than structured diagnostic interviews. 15, 41 At the cutoff used here, pooled specificity of 0.77 implies that a meaningful proportion of those screening positive would not meet diagnostic criteria. 36 Regional comparison is instructive: in neighbouring Moshi district a community study using the same instrument reported depressive symptoms in 44.4%, 52 whereas in Hai district structured DSM-IV assessment yielded 21.2%. 53 Our figure should therefore be read as symptom burden captured by a screening instrument rather than the prevalence of major depressive disorder. Applying a more conservative threshold of 7 or more reduced prevalence to 46.7% and strengthened rather than weakened the association with deprivation (odds ratio 18.52, 95% CI 8.24 to 41.66; p<0.001), indicating that the finding is not an artefact of a permissive cutoff.

Associations with global cognitive screening and verbal fluency were robust to full adjustment, whereas those with CERAD list learning and delayed recall were not. Verbal fluency draws on 20 lexical access, executive control, and processing speed, and may be a more sensitive marker of early or diffuse cognitive change than verbal learning and recall, which can show floor effects in populations with limited formal schooling. That the memory associations attenuated specifically after adjustment for age, education, and community suggests these variables, rather than deprivation itself, account for the crude differences initially observed. Education emerged as the dominant deprivation dimension for cognition, consistent with cognitive reserve theory. 42 This raises a methodological concern, since educational deprivation contributes to the index while years of education independently predicts cognitive test performance. Removing the education indicator from the composite left the associations with global cognition and verbal fluency essentially unchanged, indicating that the relationship is not reducible to educational attainment, and the IDEA screen itself showed no evidence of educational bias across Nigerian and Tanzanian cohorts. 33 The absence of any association with self-reported cognitive impairment, set against consistent associations with objective performance, carries practical weight, because it indicates that subjective cognitive complaint is a poor proxy for measured cognition in this setting and that screening approaches relying on self-report in low-resource contexts may systematically fail to identify those most affected.

The most theoretically challenging finding is the inversion between material deprivation and brain health at community level. Sanya Station was the most deprived community overall and on most individual indicators, yet had the lowest depression prevalence and the strongest cognitive performance, while Orori, intermediate in overall material deprivation, had the worst outcomes on most measures and performed significantly worse than its immediate highland neighbour Narumu Tella on both depression and cognition. This pattern resists explanation through absolute material deprivation alone but is consistent with relative deprivation theory, which 21 holds that health is shaped not only by absolute resources but by an individual's standing relative to a salient reference group, with adverse comparison generating psychosocial stress. 43 Two pathways are conventionally proposed, one material and one psychosocial, the latter operating through chronic stress and cumulative allostatic load. 44 Our measure of perceived social standing bears directly on this reading: within the the village (Spearman r -0.35, p<0.001) and, more weakly, nationally (r -0.08, p=0.029), so the ladder does track measured deprivation at the individual level. Across communities, however, perceived standing was statistically indistinguishable: village ladder means were 3.22 in Narumu Tella, 3.20 in Orori, and 3.31 in Sanya Station (p=0.78), and national ladder means were 3.94, 3.79, and 3.91 respectively (p=0.79), even though measured deprivation differed significantly between every pair of communities. That older adults in the most materially deprived community rate their social position no lower than those in the least deprived is itself consistent with a relative rather than absolute standard of comparison: if people judge their position chiefly against neighbours rather than against the country, perceived standing will be approximately equalised even where objective circumstances diverge. The salient comparison for Orori residents is Narumu Tella, an adjacent community selected precisely because it is better off, whereas Sanya Station is geographically and culturally distinct from both highland communities, so its residents' reference group is likely internal to a community in which deprivation is widely shared. Sanya Station also differs in topography, natural resources and ethnic composition, being the only community with a substantial Maasai population, and these differences may bear on both outlook and assessment.

Language is the most immediate concern, since all instruments were administered in Swahili, but it does not appear to explain the pattern: confidence speaking Swahili did not differ across the three communities (96.3%, 96.3% and 93.0% respectively; p=0.11), and Sanya Station residents were the most likely of the three to give Swahili as their preferred language (87.4% versus 79.2% and 49.0%; p<0.001), so any language disadvantage would work against the better performance observed there. We emphasise that the ladder captures self-rated position rather than explicit comparison with a specified reference group, so this interpretation remains hypothesisgenerating. It nonetheless converges with evidence that structural inequality shapes brain outcomes beyond individual characteristics, including multi-country work in which income inequality predicted brain dynamics more strongly than individual demographic or cognitive factors. 45 An alternative and complementary possibility is that factors operating at individual, social and community level in Sanya Station attenuate the effect of material deprivation. Social cohesion, shared cultural identity, intergenerational support, and dietary or activity differences between lowland and highland settings are plausible candidates, but were not measured here. 46, 47 These findings speak to a growing argument that dementia and brain health prevention frameworks must extend beyond individual behaviour change to address structural determinants. 25, 26 Our data are consistent with the argument that the 2024 Lancet Commission framework under-represents the realities of the global south, 23, 24 and extend the small African evidence base, which has linked multidimensional poverty to dementia in South Africa and to depressive symptoms in Kenya, by examining cognition and depression together in the same East African population, a region projected to experience one of the steepest global increases in dementia cases by 2050. 20 That living standards deprivation predicted depression most strongly while educational deprivation predicted cognition most strongly suggests that different dimensions of poverty may call for different responses, and the balance is likely to differ by setting: individual behaviour change may carry most of the preventable fraction where living conditions and health care are already secure, whereas in lower-income settings structural measures such as access to care, housing and food security plausibly come first, with behaviour change adding to rather than substituting for them. We also note that 737 participants (71.9%) had uncontrolled hypertension, at once a marker of limited health-care access and an established vascular risk factor for cognitive decline, and one for which prevention pathways are well described. 23, 48, 54 Our sensitivity analysis confirmed that this dual role does not drive our findings, but the figure itself is a substantial and directly addressable gap.

This study has several strengths, including a large community-based sample with high participation, assessment instruments validated for the local setting, a theoretically grounded deprivation measure, principled handling of missing data, and prespecified sensitivity analyses addressing the main structural concerns with the composite. Several limitations must be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design precludes inference about temporal ordering or causal direction, and depression and cognitive impairment may plausibly contribute to deprivation as well as result from it. 18 Deprivation was measured at a single time point, so we cannot distinguish chronic from recently acquired disadvantage, a meaningful limitation given that cumulative life-course disadvantage is central to the conceptual model motivating this work. 3 Both the GDS-15 and the IDEA are screening instruments rather than diagnostic assessments.

Perceived social standing was measured only as self-rated position on the MacArthur ladder, which does not identify the reference group against which participants compare themselves, so relative deprivation could not be tested directly. Several index indicators, notably food insecurity and perceived safety, are self-reported as is the depression measure, raising the possibility of common method bias; 49 the convergence of the depression finding with objectively measured cognitive outcomes offers partial reassurance but cannot exclude this. Several standard global index indicators, including sanitation, drinking water, asset ownership, and child mortality, could not be included because the corresponding data were unavailable, so our index is an adapted rather than complete implementation. Residential history would help in interpreting the community-level contrast, but the item was administered to only a small subset of participants and almost entirely in one community, so it could not be compared across sites. Year of birth was not known for a small number of participants, whose age was estimated, and a small number of recorded ages at the extreme upper end of the distribution should be treated as approximate.

One finding remains unexplained: net of deprivation and other covariates, more years of education was associated with modestly higher depressive symptom scores, which runs counter to the typically protective association reported in the literature. This may reflect heightened awareness of relative disadvantage, differential item interpretation, or residual confounding, and merits dedicated investigation. Finally, the three communities lie within a single district of one Tanzanian region and were purposively selected as exemplars of highland and lowland, higher and lower socioeconomic status settings, so the community-level inversion we observed is specific to this configuration and should not be assumed to be generalisable without replication.

Conclusion

Multidimensional deprivation was highly prevalent among older adults in rural northern

Tanzania and was independently associated with greater depressive symptom burden and poorer objectively measured cognition. Different dimensions of deprivation predicted different outcomes, which suggests that anti-poverty interventions targeting brain health may need to be dimension-specific rather than undifferentiated. The inversion of this relationship at the community level, where the most materially deprived community showed the best outcomes while rating its social position no lower than the least deprived, indicates that absolute deprivation alone cannot explain the distribution of brain health and points toward relative 25 position within a salient reference group, and toward unmeasured resilience factors in the least materially advantaged community, as candidate explanations meriting dedicated study. These findings support calls to broaden dementia and brain health prevention frameworks beyond individual behaviour change to encompass the structural conditions in which older adults live.
