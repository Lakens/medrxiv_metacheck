To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Text Sentence
p = 0.61 Mixed teams did not differ from male-only teams after adjustment (-7%, p = 0.61).

For metascientific articles demonstrating the rate of misinterpretations of non-significant results is high, see:

Aczel B, Palfi B, Szollosi A, Kovacs M, Szaszi B, Szecsi P, Zrubka M, Gronau Q, van den Bergh D, Wagenmakers E (2018). “Quantifying Support for the Null Hypothesis in Psychology: An Empirical Investigation.” Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science, 1(3), 357–366. doi:10.1177/2515245918773742.

Murphy S, Merz R, Reimann L, Fernández A (2025). “Nonsignificance misinterpreted as an effect’s absence in psychology: Prevalence and temporal analyses.” Royal Society Open Science, 12(3), 242167. doi:10.1098/rsos.242167.

For educational material on preventing the misinterpretation of p values, see Improving Your Statistical Inferences.

About the personal-information flags. These are pattern matches, not confirmed disclosures, and they are tuned to over-report rather than miss something. Expect false positives:

  • Column names are matched against identifying words in English and other European languages (email, telefoonnummer, geboortedatum, …). A column called phone_number flags whether or not it holds one, and an empty or already-anonymised column flags on its name alone.
  • Values are matched against the shape of an identifier. An IP address pattern also matches a version string or a dotted numeric code; a card-number pattern requires both a real issuer prefix and a checksum, but a long numeric ID can still coincide.
  • Free text is flagged when a column holds long, mostly-distinct prose — the shape open-ended answers take. Most such columns contain no names at all.

A flag means look at this column. Where the data is already de-identified (an ip column reading Anonymized, a name column the authors emptied), no action is needed.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background. Bacteriophage therapy is moving from compassionate use toward regulated clinical use, as antimicrobial resistance (AMR) grows. Who pays for phage therapy research, and where the money goes, has not been measured globally.

Methods. We searched Dimensions on 13 February 2024 and screened 1,521 grant records. We analysed the 802 grants with a recorded funding amount that started in 1999-2023 (USD 318.6 million). We estimated log-linear growth, measured concentration with the Herfindahl-Hirschman index (HHI) and Gini coefficient, and counted shared grants fractionally across recipient institutions. Topics came from latent Dirichlet allocation, and team gender from first names. Sensitivity analyses added the 272 relevant grants without an amount, merged yearly records into projects, and varied key definitions.

Results. Grants grew by 12.2% a year and recorded funding by 16.6% a year (14.1% after inflation), yet the field received only about USD 13 million a year. US funders provided 47.7% of funding and one institute, NIAID, 21.0%, rising to 23.3% in 2016-2023. The top 10% of recipient institutions received 53.4%. Low-and lower-middle-income countries received 0.33% of recorded funding, and only 7 grants linked Global North and Global South recipients. Antibiotic-resistance research rose from 3% to about a quarter of funding. Female-only teams received grants 19% smaller than male-only teams after adjustment (95% CI -32 to -2), and 84% of researchers held a single grant. Counting grants rather than dollars, the Global South received 17.5%, because several of its funders report no amounts. The headline results held in 37 of 40 sensitivity checks.

Phage therapy research funding is growing fast from a small base, but it rests on a narrow set of funders and countries and rarely reaches the countries most affected by AMR. Broader funding, and fuller reporting of national investments, will matter as phage therapy enters regulated clinical use.

1. Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance is one of the leading causes of death worldwide. In 2019, bacterial AMR was associated with an estimated 4.95 million deaths, and the burden was highest in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia [1]. As new antibiotics reach patients slowly, interest has returned to bacteriophage therapy: the use of viruses that infect and kill specific bacteria [2, 3]. Phages were used clinically before the antibiotic era and never stopped being used in Georgia, Poland and Russia. They are now being tested again in compassionate-use cases and clinical trials in Europe, North America and Australia [3, 4].

The field is moving from individual cases toward regulated products. Belgium allows phages to be prepared as magistral medicines [5], and in October 2025 the European Medicines Agency published its first draft guideline on the quality of phage therapy medicinal products [6]. In 2026, the US National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases (NIAID) funded three Centers for Accelerating Phage Therapy, with USD 6 million in their first year [7]. As phage therapy becomes a product rather than a last resort, who pays for the underlying research shapes which infections, which patients and which countries it will serve.

Yet no study has measured research funding for phage therapy at the global, grant level. Funding analyses exist for AMR [8, 9] and for infectious disease research in general [10], but they do not separate phage therapy. We previously mapped the phage therapy patent landscape, which measured what comes out of the field as intellectual property [11]. This study measures what goes into it. Together, the two studies follow the field from money to inventions.

We ask four questions about 25 years of grants . How much money has gone into phage therapy research, and how fast has it grown? Who provides it, and how concentrated is it? Where does it go, by country and income group? And what, and who, gets funded, by research topic and by the gender of research teams? Because funding databases record some grants without an amount, we also test how far each answer depends on that gap.

2. Methods

>Methods

2.1 Data and screening

We searched the Dimensions grants database [12] on 13 February 2024. The query combined phage terms in titles (bacteriophage, phage, phagotherapy and related forms) with therapy terms in titles or abstracts (therapy, treatment, antibacterial, antimicrobial, cocktail, prophylaxis and related forms); the full query is in the Supplementary Methods. The search returned 1,521 records. Titles and abstracts were screened for therapeutic use of phages, which excluded 391 records and left 1,130 relevant grants (Figure 6). Of these, 312 had no funding amount in Dimensions, and 16 of the remaining 818 started outside 1999-2023. The analytic sample is the 802 grants with a recorded amount that started in 1999-2023. Dollar-based results therefore describe recorded funding. The 272 relevant grants without an amount that started in 1999-2023 were kept for sensitivity analyses (Section 2.4).

Amounts are in US dollars as converted by Dimensions. Each grant is dated by its start year. Because Dimensions indexes grants with a lag, 2023 is incomplete; it is shown in figures but excluded from trend estimates.

2.2 Classifications

Funders and recipient institutions were assigned a country from Dimensions' standardised organisation records. Each country was given a World Bank income group (fiscal year 2024) and a Global North or Global South label from one reference table, used for both funders and recipients [13, 14]. The table is published with the code. It documents the cases where sources disagree: Russia, Georgia and Ukraine are labelled Global North, Singapore Global South, and Taiwan, which the World Bank does not list separately, high income. The European Commission and the European Research Council are coded as funder country "European Union".

A grant with several recipient institutions gives each an equal share of its amount and of the grant (fractional counting), so country and income-group totals add up to the whole. A grant is called domestic when every recipient institution is in the funder's country.

2.3 Analyses

Growth. Annual growth was estimated with a log-linear regression of yearly grant counts and USD totals on year, over 1999-2022, and reported with 95% confidence intervals. Growth is also reported in constant 2023 dollars, deflated by the US consumer price index (CPI-U) by start year.

Concentration.

We computed the HHI (the sum of squared percentage shares, 0-10,000) for funders, funder countries and recipient countries. Above 2,500 is usually called highly concentrated. For recipient institutions we report the Gini coefficient and the share received by the top 10%. US and NIAID shares are reported for 1999-2008, 2009-2015 and 2016-2023.

Topics. Titles, abstracts and keywords (English translations) were lemmatised with spaCy (nouns, adjectives and verbs). Words on the WordCloud and NLTK stopword lists were removed. A term-count matrix kept terms in at least 50 grants and at most 75% of them. We fitted latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA) models [15] with 2 to 12 topics and chose k = 5, where c_v coherence [16] was highest. Each grant was assigned its most probable topic. Four topics were interpretable and the fifth held a single grant, which we report as unclassified. Refitting with five other random seeds kept 51-68% of grants in the same topic, so topic results are reported as aggregate trends.

Gender.

The gender of named researchers was inferred from first names with Gender API, with manual checks of ambiguous names [17]. Grants were placed in four exclusive groups: male only, female only, mixed, and gender unknown (no named researcher, or none whose gender could be inferred). Grant sizes were compared with Mann-Whitney tests. The main model is an ordinary least squares regression of log(amount) on team group, log team size, log grant length, start year, funder (funders with fewer than 10 grants pooled) and topic, with HC1 robust standard errors.

Engagement.

Researchers, institutions and funders were labelled by their activity across years, as in our patent study [11]: newcomer, continuant, terminator or transient. Because these labels depend on whether a grant counts in its start year only or in every year it runs, our main measure of retention is the share of researchers who held a single grant.

2.4 Sensitivity analyses

We tested whether each headline result depends on the analytic sample or on a definition. First, the 272 relevant grants without an amount were classified by award type (research grant, training award or capacity funding) from their programme names. They were added in count-based analyses, and their amounts were imputed from priced grants of the same funder, funder country or award type, at the 25th, 50th and 75th percentiles. Second, yearly records of the same project were merged. Third, we varied the timing of funding (spread over grant years), the income group (at grant start), the definition of the Global South (OECD Development Assistance Committee list), the treatment of NIAID (excluded), the dollar basis (constant 2023 dollars) and the topic assignment (topic probabilities from a refitted model). Finally, the gender model was re-estimated per year of grant, without funders in Japan and China, where namebased inference is less reliable, and without training awards.

2.5 Reproducibility

All analyses were run in Python (pandas, statsmodels, scikit-learn, gensim, spaCy). Seven notebooks rebuild every table and figure, and every number quoted in this paper, from the raw export with one script. A clean rebuild reproduced all 43 output tables byte for byte. The code is openly available (see Data availability).

3. Results

>Results

3.1 Growing, but small

The 802 grants came from 105 funders in 25 countries and went to 525 institutions in 39 countries.

Together they recorded USD 318.6 million over 25 years, or about USD 13 million a year. The median grant was USD 180,000 (interquartile range 40,400-428,116).

Funding grew fast from this small base. Grants grew by 12.2% a year (95% CI 10.0-14.5) and recorded funding by 16.6% a year (12.6-20.7) over 1999-2022 (Figure 1, Supplementary Table S2). In constant 2023 dollars, funding grew by 14.1% a year (10.2-18.2). Two thirds of all funding (USD 213.0 million, 502 grants) started in 2016-2023, against USD 29.1 million (102 grants) in 1999-2008 (Table 1). Yearly totals were uneven, with peaks in 2009 and 2018, and reached about USD 30-39 million a year in 2018-2022.

3.2 Concentrated in one country and one institute

US funders provided 47.7% of recorded funding, and one institute, NIAID, provided 21.0% (Table 2, Funding was highly concentrated by country: the HHI across funder countries was 2,501, above the usual threshold of 2,500. Without NIAID, it fell to 1,508 and the US share to 33.8%, while growth was almost unchanged (16.2% a year; Supplementary Table S27). Across recipient institutions, the top 10% received 53.4% of funding (Gini 0.68; Figure 5b). The largest single recipient, Intralytix in the United States, received 2.7%.

3.3 Unequal geography

Funding stayed almost entirely within high-income countries. Funders in high-income countries provided 98.3% of recorded funding, and institutions in high-income countries received 97.8% (Figure 4, Supplementary Table S4). Low-and lower-middle-income countries received 0.33%. Global South funders provided USD 5.6 million (1.7%), and Global South recipients received USD 6.7 million (2.1%), 73% of it in China (Supplementary Table S11).

Most funding also stayed within each funder's own country. Grants whose recipients were all in the funder's country accounted for 88.7% of funding (Supplementary Table S12). The European Commission was the largest cross-border funder, with 19 grants and USD 14.6 million. Collaboration across the income divide was rare. Of the 135 grants with more than one recipient institution, 39 involved more than one country, and only 7 linked Global North and Global South recipients (Supplementary Table S26).

3.4 What, and who, gets funded

Four research topics emerged: infectious disease control (280 grants; 35.9% of funding), bacterial genetics and mechanisms (237; 28.8%), antibiotic resistance strategies (151; 22.4%), and immunotherapy and targeted treatments (133; 12.9%) (Figure 2, Supplementary Table S37). Their balance shifted over time. Antibiotic-resistance research received 3% of funding in 1999-2005 and 5% in 2006-2010, then about a quarter from 2011 onward (28%, 25% and 27% in the three later periods). Immunotherapy fell from half of the small early funding to about 10%. Funders specialised. NIAID placed 58% of its grants in antibiotic resistance, the National Natural Science Foundation of China 44% in immunotherapy, and the US National Cancer Institute 83% in immunotherapy (Supplementary Figure S3).

Of 1,073 named researchers, 373 were inferred as women and 696 as men; women made up about a third of named researchers each year from 2011 to 2022 (Supplementary Figure S6). Male-only teams held 446 grants (USD 179.0 million), female-only teams 201 (USD 57.4 million) and mixed teams 95 (USD 41.6 million). A further 60 grants (USD 40.7 million) had no researcher of known gender (Table 3, Figure 3). After adjustment for team size, grant length, start year, funder and topic, female-only teams received grants 19% smaller than male-only teams (95% CI -32 to -2; p = 0.026). Per year of grant, the gap was 22% (-35 to -6). Mixed teams did not differ from male-only teams after adjustment (-7%, p = 0.61).

Most researchers appeared once. Of the 1,073 named researchers, 83.9% held a single grant. Among the 525 recipient institutions, 61% appeared in only one year (Supplementary Figures S8-S9).

3.5 Robustness

The 272 relevant grants without an amount were not a random subset. Training awards made up 43% of them (studentships, scholarships and theses), against 8% of priced grants, and most came from funders in Brazil, Russia and Belgium (Supplementary Table S30). Counting grants rather than dollars, the Global South received 17.5% of the 1,074 relevant grants, against 11.8% of the 802 priced ones (Supplementary Table S31). With imputed amounts, its share of funding rose from 2.1% to 2.6-5.5% (Supplementary Table S32).

The other headline results held. Low-and lower-middle-income countries stayed at or below 0.6% of funding and 1.4% of grants. Growth stayed at 16-17% a year, the US share at 41-48% and NIAID's at 17-21%. The gender gap held in every subset (-19% to -22%). Merging yearly records into 771 projects changed no result by more than one point. Overall, 3 of 40 checks changed a conclusion: the two countbased Global South measures, and the year-based "transient" label, which falls from 82% to 3.5% when every year of a grant counts (Supplementary Figure S12). For this reason we report retention as the share of researchers holding a single grant, which does not depend on that choice.

4. Discussion

>Discussion

4.1 Main findings

Over 25 years, recorded funding for phage therapy research grew by about 17% a year. It remained small, about USD 13 million a year on average. It is concentrated in one country and one institute: US funders provide nearly half of it and NIAID a fifth, a share that is rising. It rarely leaves high-income countries, and low-and lower-middle-income countries receive a third of one percent. Antibiotic resistance has become a central topic. Female-only teams receive smaller grants, and most researchers hold a single grant.

4.2 A mismatch between burden and funding

The countries where AMR kills most people are almost absent from this funding. In 2019, the highest death rates attributable to AMR were in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia [1]. In our data, low-and lower-middle-income countries received 0.33% of recorded funding and 1.4% of grants, and Uganda and Tanzania were the only recipient countries in sub-Saharan Africa outside South Africa. This pattern is not unique to phage therapy: investment in infectious disease research also follows the location of funders rather than of disease [10]. It matters more for phages than for most drugs, however. Phages are specific to bacterial strains, so phage banks and cocktails must be built from local isolates, and production close to patients is part of the appeal of the approach [3, 4]. A field funded almost entirely in high-income countries may build its libraries and its evidence around their pathogens.

The Global South is not inactive. Counting grants rather than dollars, it holds 17.5% of relevant grants, mainly because funders in Brazil report no amounts to Dimensions; Russian funders do the same. Data visibility is therefore part of the inequity. Global funding trackers, and analyses such as ours, can only measure what funders report, and they under-count national investment in exactly the regions that most need to be seen.

4.3 Fragility

A field that draws nearly half its funding from one country, and a fifth from one institute, depends on that institute's priorities and budget. NIAID's support has been decisive: it funds 58% of its phage grants in antibiotic resistance and, in 2026, launched three Centers for Accelerating Phage Therapy [7]. The same dependence is a risk. By April 2026, the US Department of Health and Human Services had terminated 1,392 NIH awards before completion, against fewer than six from 2012 to January 2025 [18]. Our sensitivity analysis shows that growth does not hinge on NIAID alone (16.2% a year without it), but the field's centre of gravity does.

4.4 A thin pipeline

Most researchers (84%) appear on a single grant, and most institutions in a single year. Clinical trials, manufacturing to good manufacturing practice and regulatory dossiers need teams that stay in the field for years. A workforce that turns over this quickly may slow translation more than the level of funding does.

Longer awards, programme grants and fellowships that keep phage researchers in the field would address this directly.

4.5 Gender

Women made up about a third of named researchers, and female-only teams received grants about a fifth smaller than male-only teams, after accounting for funder, grant length and topic. This matches findings in other fields [19, 20]. Mixed teams' higher average grants disappeared after the same adjustment, so our data do not support a funding advantage for mixed teams; the unadjusted difference was accounted for by funder and grant length. Name-based inference is binary and less reliable for some languages, but the gap held when funders in Japan and China were excluded.

4.6 Implications

Four steps follow from these results: 1.

North-South partnerships and shared funding pools, so that phage research and trials include the countries with the highest AMR burden. Only 7 of 802 grants linked the two today.

2. Funding for phage banks and production capacity in low-and middle-income countries, since phage therapy depends on local isolates and local manufacture.

3. Tracking phage therapy separately in AMR funding dashboards, and asking national funders

to report award amounts, so that investment outside the major Western funders becomes visible. 4. Monitoring gender in phage therapy funding, including grant size and not only grant counts.

4.7 Limitations

First, dollar results cover the 802 grants with a recorded amount. Of the 1,074 relevant grants that started in 1999-2023, 272 had no amount; 43% of these were training awards, and most came from funders in Brazil, Russia and Belgium. Count-based and imputation analyses show which results depend on this gap.

Second, Dimensions covers NIH, European and Japanese funders well but many national funders poorly, including those of Georgia, home of the Eliava Institute, and Russia, both long-standing centres of phage therapy. Third, private, philanthropic and venture funding is largely out of scope, although companies such as Intralytix appear as grant recipients. Fourth, 2023 is incomplete because of indexing lag, and amounts are nominal US dollars converted by Dimensions; constant-dollar growth was similar. Fifth, gender was inferred from first names and is binary. Sixth, LDA topics are approximate: assignments varied between model runs, so we report topic trends rather than precise shares. Seventh, all results are descriptive associations; in particular, the gender model adjusts for observed differences between grants but cannot identify why they differ. Finally, we do not link grants to outputs such as publications, patents or trials; that link is the natural next step from our patent study [11].

5. Conclusion

Recorded funding for phage therapy research grew by about 17% a year over 25 years, but from a small base and on a narrow footing. Nearly half comes from US funders and a fifth from one institute, and almost none reaches the low-and lower-middle-income countries that carry the heaviest burden of antimicrobial resistance. As phage therapy moves toward regulated clinical use, broadening who funds it, where the money goes and who holds the grants will shape whom it serves. Making every funder's investment visible is the first step.
