Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) is an important predictive biomarker for poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitor (PARPi) therapy in ovarian cancer (OC). However, real-world data on HRD prevalence and assay performance in Indian patients are limited. This study evaluated commercial HRD assays, then developed an in-house HRD assay and characterized HRD and BRCA alterations in a large real-world Indian OC cohort.

Methods: This retrospective study included 4,163 formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) OC samples tested between 2021 and 2025. HRD and BRCA status were initially assessed using the AmoyDx HRD Focus Panel (n=1,097) and SOPHiA DDM HRD Solution (n=470). Subsequently, 170 representative samples were used for the development and validation of an in-house HRD assay (MG_HRD), with external concordance assessed using the Myriad myChoice CDx assay in a subset of samples. Following validation, MG_HRD was implemented in routine clinical practice and applied to an independent cohort of 2,596 OC samples. HRD prevalence, BRCA mutation status, and clinicopathological associations were evaluated in all cohorts.

Results: Among 4,163 ovarian cancer samples, HRD positivity was observed in 52.4% of cases, and pathogenic/likely pathogenic BRCA1/2 variants were identified in 23.6% of cases. Notably, 28.8% of tumors were BRCA wild-type but HRD-positive. BRCA-mutated tumors demonstrated significantly higher HRD genomic instability scores than BRCA wild-type tumors (p<0.001). HRD positivity was significantly enriched in high grade serous ovarian carcinomas. Remarkably, the BRCA1 c.68_69del (p.Glu23ValfsTer17) founder mutation was detected in 14.2% (97/685) of cases among the pathogenic variants in our study. The MG_HRD assay showed excellent concordance with commercial assays, achieving 97.1% overall agreement, 98.3% negative percent agreement (NPA), and 96.4% positive percent agreement (PPA), with complete concordance observed in the externally evaluated Myriad subset.

Conclusions: This is the first Indian study to develop, validate, and implement an in-house HRD assay and evaluate HRD across three testing platforms in a large real-world OC cohort. The high prevalence of BRCA wild-type/HRD-positive tumors highlight the importance of comprehensive HRD testing, whereas the strong performance of MG_HRD supports its use as a reliable and cost-effective alternative for routine clinical practice.

1. Introduction

Homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) is a significant genomic instability phenotype resulting from impaired repair of DNA double-strand breaks through the homologous recombination repair (HRR) pathway 1 . This deficiency results in the accumulation of genomic scars, including loss of heterozygosity (LOH), telomeric allelic imbalance (TAI), and large-scale state transitions (LST), which together contribute to tumorigenesis 2 . HRD is highly prevalent in ovarian cancer (OC), particularly in high-grade serous carcinoma (HGSC), which is a common histological subtype, comprising 70% of OC cases 2, 3 . HRD has become an important predictive biomarker for response to platinum-based chemotherapy and poly (ADPribose) polymerase inhibitors (PARPi) in OC. PARPi functions through the principle of synthetic lethality, where the inhibition of single-strand DNA repair results in the accumulation of DNA damage, which is lethal in HR-deficient tumors 4.

Approximately 50% of HGSC cases exhibit HRD, either due to pathogenic BRCA1/2 mutations [BRCA1/2_mut], which constitutes approximately 20-25% or through genomic scar markers such as LOH, TAI, and LST 3 . The HRD genomic scar score is calculated based on the quantification of these genomic scars 1 . While BRCA1/2 mutation testing remains a cornerstone of molecular diagnostics, it is increasingly recognized that a significant proportion of HRDpositive tumors are BRCA1/2 wild-type (BRCA1/2_wt). This highlights the need for comprehensive testing strategies that integrate both BRCA1/2 mutation analysis and genomic scar score-based HRD assessment 5 . Next-generation sequencing (NGS) technology plays a crucial role in identifying patients for targeted therapeutics by enabling comprehensive analysis of the genomic scars and molecular alterations that drive ovarian cancer 6.

Several commercial and laboratory-developed NGS-based assays are currently available for HRD testing. Among these, the Myriad MyChoice CDx assay (Myriad Genetics, Salt Lake City, UT, USA) is a Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved companion diagnostic HRD assay for PARP inhibitor therapy. This assay integrates BRCA1/2 mutation status with a genomic instability score (GIS) derived from the combined score of LOH, TAI, and LST, with a predefined cutoff of ≥42 to define HRD positivity 7, 8 . Despite its clear predictive value, the widespread adoption of this assay faces challenges because of its high cost, long turnaround time, and limited accessibility due to centralized testing 6.

To overcome the limitations of the Myriad MyChoice CDx assay, several alternative commercial HRD diagnostic assays have been developed, including the AmoyDx HRD Focus Panel, SOPHiA DDM HRD Solution, FoundationOne CDx, Leuven HRD, and SeqOne HRD assays. Although these platforms are designed with the common objective of determining HRD status, they differ considerably in assay design, including gene content, single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) backbone, sequencing methodologies and bioinformatic algorithms 7, 9, 10.

The lack of harmonized methodologies highlights the need for scalable, reproducible, and locally deployable diagnostic solutions for HRD assessment in ovarian cancer 3, 6 . Each of these HRD assays uses different genomic instability metrics and positivity thresholds for HRD classification 7 . For example, the AmoyDx HRD Focus Panel uses a Genomic Scar Score (GSS) threshold of ≥50; the SOPHiA DDM HRD Solution applies a Genomic Integrity Index (GII) threshold of ≥0; FoundationOne CDx primarily relies on genomic loss of heterozygosity (gLOH) with a cutoff of ≥16; Leuven HRD employs a GIS derived from combined genomic scar markers with a cutoff of >56; and SeqOne HRD uses a proprietary genomic instability threshold 7 . Among these commercial decentralized NGS-based assays, AmoyDx and SOPHiA provide the most practical and reliable alternatives by enabling in-house HRD assessment using standardized analytical workflows. Their high concordance with established reference assays supports their implementation in routine clinical HRD testing [10] [11] [12] [13] . Therefore, in this study, we chose to perform these two commercial assays in our MedGenome laboratory along with our in-house developed HRD assay, designated as the MedGenome HRD (MG_HRD) assay, to evaluate the HRD status in OC samples.

In this retrospective study, we validated the analytical performance and feasibility of the MG_HRD assay by comparing it with two commercially available HRD assays: AmoyDx and SOPHiA. A limited subset of samples underwent external benchmarking using the Myriad myChoice CDx assay. As mentioned above, the HRD metrics of all these assays are different. Hence, to ensure consistency across platforms, these metrics were collectively referred to as the HRD genomic score (HRD_GS) in this study. The primary objectives of this study were to assess cross-platform agreement using concordance metrics and to evaluate the analytical performance of the MG_HRD assay. The secondary objectives were to determine the prevalence of HRD, alterations in BRCA1/2, and the status of HRD_GS across the three assays. Furthermore, we investigated the association between HRD status with tumor content, and histological subtypes across a large, independent ovarian cancer cohort for all three assays.

Through this comprehensive evaluation, we aimed to investigate the applicability and scalability of MG_HRD testing for the molecular diagnosis of ovarian cancer.

2. Methodology:

>Methodology:

2.1. Study Design

In this study, a large independent clinical cohort of 4163 patients with OC was analyzed for HRD testing using three distinct assays. These included two commercially available assays (AmoyDx, SOPHiA) and one internally developed assay (MG_HRD). Among these, 1097 patients (26.4%) were assessed using the AmoyDx (assay 1), 470 patients (11.3%) using the SOPHiA (assay 2), and 2596 patients (62.4%) using the MG_HRD (assay 3). A small subset of samples was analyzed using the SOPHiA assay (n = 14), which yielded inconclusive HRD results. Therefore, these samples were excluded from downstream analysis, resulting in 470 evaluable samples for the SOPHiA assay [Figure 1A].

The analytical performance of the MG_HRD assay was assessed using an internal validation cohort of 170 OC samples. These samples were selected from the prevalence cohort of 1567 samples with known HRD results from the AmoyDx [157/1097] and SOPHiA [13/470] assays. Additionally, commercially available reference DNA samples (n = 10) with known HRD_GS and BRCA1/2 mutation statuses were included in the validation process to optimize the analytical workflow of the assay. The reference standards comprised NA12878 [GIAB] (n=1), SeraSeq (n=4), AmoyDx control (n=1), EMQN (n=2), CAP (n=1), and Horizon HRD (n=1). The MG_HRD validation cohort (n=170) was stratified into four groups based on the status of HRD_GS and BRCA1/2 mutations: BRCA1/2_mut/HRD_GS+(n=46), BRCA1/2_mut/HRD_GS-(n=14), BRCA1/2_wt/HRD_GS+(n=52), and

BRCA1/2_wt/HRD_GS-(n=58).

To further evaluate the concordance of the assays with an external reference assay, five samples from the validation cohort were evaluated using the FDA-approved Myriad myChoice CDx Assay. To represent the molecular subgroups as defined above, four samples were chosen at random, ensuring that one sample was taken from each subgroup. Additionally, one sample that failed the AmoyDx library quality control (QC) criteria was included because of its high tumor cellularity. This QC-failed sample was analyzed using the MG_HRD assay to assess analytical performance and was exclusively included in the external validation. However, this sample was not considered a part of the assay validation or overall prevalence cohort [Figure 1B].

2.2. Processing of FFPE Tumor Tissue Samples and DNA extraction

FFPE ovarian tumor tissue samples underwent initial histopathological assessment following hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Samples with a tumor content (TC) of ≥30% were preferentially selected for HRD testing based on the minimum TC requirement of the AmoyDx and SOPHiA assays.

Cases with TC <30% were also included, provided that TC was ≥20%, consistent with the minimum requirement of the in-house MG_HRD assay. In cases where the TC was insufficient, an additional FFPE block from the same patient was reviewed.

Subsequently, tissue sections of 10 µm thickness were sliced using a microtome, and the volume of the FFPE curls was adjusted based on the TC present in each block. The selected sections were then deparaffinized prior to molecular processing.

Genomic DNA was extracted from FFPE tissue sections using the QIAamp DNA FFPE Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Cat No. 56404, Germantown, MD, USA) as per the manufacturer's protocol. The concentration and purity of the isolated DNA were assessed via fluorometric quantification using Qubit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), while the fragment size distribution was evaluated using the TapeStation system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

2.3. HRD testing 2.3.a. AmoyDx HRD Focus Panel HRD profiling of assay1 samples [n=1097] was performed using the AmoyDx HRD Focus Panel (AmoyDx, Xiamen, China), according to the manufacturer's guidelines [Version: B1.0] 14 . Genomic DNA extracted from FFPE tumor tissue (100-200 ng) was used for library preparation based on Halo-shape Annealing and Defer-Ligation Enrichment (HANDLE) technology, followed by target enrichment, library amplification, purification, and quantification. Libraries were sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA). Sequencing reads were aligned with the human reference genome (hg19). Variants were called to identify P/LP single nucleotide variants (SNVs), small insertions and deletions (InDels), and splice site mutations in the BRCA1/2 genes.

In addition to mutation detection, the assay quantifies genomic instability using approximately 24,000 genome-wide SNP markers, enabling the inference of allele-specific copy number alterations through normalized read depth and B-allele frequency data 6, 8.

Bioinformatic data analysis was conducted through the AmoyDx NGS Data Analysis System (ANDAS) (Amoy Diagnostics Co., Ltd.) 14 . The Genomic Scar Score (GSS) was calculated using a machine-learning (ML) algorithm that integrates chromosomal copy-number alterations (CNAs) based on their length, type, and location 6 . The predefined HRD GSS threshold for positivity was set at GSS ≥50. Samples with a GSS ≥50, and/or those harboring pathogenic BRCA1/2 variants were classified as HRD-positive.

2.3.b. SOPHiA DDM TM HRD Solution

HRD profiling of assay2 samples [n=470] was performed using the SOPHiA HRD™ solution (SOPHiA GENETICS, Lausanne, Switzerland) according to the manufacturer's instructions 15 . This assay integrates targeted NGS of multi-HRR genes with low-pass whole genome sequencing (LP-WGS) for a genome-wide assessment of genomic instability. Genomic DNA extracted from FFPE tumor tissue (50-100 ng) was used for library preparation using the SOPHiA GENETICS™ DNA Library Prep Kit III. Library quality and quantity were assessed using Qubit fluorometry (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and an Agilent Fragment Analyzer™ [Agilent Technologies], followed by sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform.

The targeted HRR panel enabled the detection of SNVs, InDels, and CNVs, including alterations in BRCA1/2, whereas LP-WGS was performed at low coverage to capture genome-wide copy number profiles for genomic instability assessment 6 . Sequencing data were processed using the SOPHiA DDM™ platform, aligned to the human reference genome (hg19). Genome-wide coverage profiles were normalized and analyzed using a deep neural network-based algorithm to derive the Genomic Integrity Index (GII). The GII quantifies chromosomal aberration patterns, and the predefined positive threshold was GII ≥0. Samples exceeding the GII threshold and tumors with pathogenic BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutations were classified as HRD-positive. Samples not meeting predefined QC criteria for DNA input, sequencing depth, BRCA coverage, or inconclusive HRD score were excluded from the analysis.

2.3.c. MG_HRD Assay

2.3.c. MG_HRD Assay

i. Design of MG_HRD Panel:

To enable HRD assessment using our indigenous assay, we designed a custom panel with dual functionality: (a) genome-wide detection of copy number alterations using strategically distributed SNP markers, (b) identification of pathogenic variants in BRCA1/2 and other homologous recombination repair (HRR) genes. The panel incorporates genome-wide SNP biallelic markers selected to ensure broad representation across diverse ethnic populations, together with a targeted gene panel comprising key HRR-associated genes and CCNE1. The targeted panel includes 17 genes: ATM, BARD1, BRCA1, BRCA2, BRIP1, CDK12, CHEK1, CHEK2, FANCL, FGFR1, PALB2, PPP2R2A, RAD51B, RAD51C, RAD51D, RAD54L, and

CCNE1.

The genome-wide SNP backbone was selected from well-curated population reference databases, including the 1000 Genomes Project, GenomeAsia, gnomAD, and our internally curated SAS-ATLAS database, which comprises data from over 100000 samples.

SNP selection was performed by adapting the methodology described by Timms et al 16 . Briefly, candidate SNPs were filtered to retain markers with a minor allele frequency (MAF) greater than 5%, low linkage disequilibrium and exclusion from regions associated with segmental duplications, problematic genomic regions, repetitive elements and low-mappability sequences. Using this strategy, approximately 46,000 genome-wide SNP markers were prioritized for panel design. The shortlisted SNPs were subsequently evaluated for probe design, and the optimal probes were selected based on simulated hybridization performance.

The probes for both SNPs and HRR targets were densely and differentially tiled (3X-10X) for better capturing in the poor quality FFPE.

The coding regions (CDS) of BRCA1/2 and other HRR genes were selected based on latest MANE (Matched Annotation from NCBI and EBI) and GENCODE gene models. Our design also covered 20 bases upstream and downstream of the exon-intron boundaries for the identification of splice site disease causing variants.

ii. Sample preparation and sequencing:

Approximately 50-100 ng of DNA was used for targeted library preparation using a custom hybrid capture kit (Twist Library Prep Kit EF with AMP Mix cat#101058 Twist Bioscience, USA). Library quality was checked using a Qubit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and TapeStation (Agilent, USA).

Libraries that met the quality standards were quantified, loaded at a concentration of 120 pM, and sequenced as 2 × 150 bp paired-end reads on an Illumina Novaseq X plus. The sequencing process adhered to the manufacturer's protocol, employing the NovaSeq X Series 25B Reagent Kit 300Cyc (Cat No. 20104706), and achieved an average sequencing depth of approximately 250x for the analysis of somatic mutations.

iii. Analysis of sequencing data:

The sequencing data undergoes an optimized workflow to determine the HRD status.

Initially, this workflow assesses the quality of the reads and trims them as needed. After trimming, the reads were aligned to the hg38 reference genome and collapsed based on Unique Molecular Identifier (UMI) information to generate a consensus BAM file. Variant calling was then performed on this consensus BAM file for all targeted regions of HRR genes. Deleterious mutations were identified using a well-established mutation prioritization process and annotated with the comprehensive mutation annotation tool, VariMAT. This tool annotates mutations across various population and disease databases.

This assay evaluates the HRD status using a combined approach based on the genome aberration (GA) score, which is a quantitative measure of genomic alterations derived from high-quality ~46K SNP markers and clinically significant variants from the BRCA1/2 gene.

This assay identifies high-quality SNVs, InDels, and splice site mutations and estimates allelespecific copy numbers based on their bi-allele frequency and coverage. The GA score (GAS) was calculated by considering different levels of copy number changes with a positivity threshold of ≥40. A sample is classified as HRD-positive if P/LP variants were detected in the BRCA1/2 genes and/or the GAS ≥40. Additionally, this assay determines the copy number status of CCNE1, which was considered amplified if its copy number was ≥7.

2.4.Variant Classification

In the evaluation of HRD in ovarian cancer, current evidence suggests that only pathogenic or likely pathogenic (P/LP) variants in the BRCA1/2 genes hold clinical significance. Consequently, although the SOPHiA and MG_HRD assays include multiple genes in the homologous recombination repair (HRR) panel, this study focused exclusively on BRCA1/2 variants for HRD interpretation. The identified somatic BRCA1/2 variants were classified according to the ASCO-CAP and AMP guidelines, categorizing them as pathogenic, likely pathogenic, or variants of uncertain significance (VUS) 17 . Nonsense, frameshift insertions/deletions, in-frame and canonical splice site variants classified as P/LP were grouped as pathogenic. These pathogenic variants were further verified using the ClinVar, Human Gene Mutation Database (HGMD) and BRCA Exchange databases, and only clinically significant variants were considered for downstream HRD status analyses. Variants of uncertain significance (VUS) in BRCA1/2 genes were classified as negative for BRCA status within this HRD assessment framework.

In this study, we did not conduct germline testing; therefore, the BRCA1/2 mutations identified could be of either germline or somatic origin. Mutations in BRCA1/2 with a variant allele frequency (VAF) exceeding 40% were considered to infer the probability of germline origin.

2.5. Statistical Data Analysis and Visualization

Statistical data analysis was done by using Python (version 3.13.3) 18 . Python libraries, such as NumPy 19 , Pandas 20 , and SciPy 21 , were used for the statistical analysis of both descriptive and categorical analysis 22 . Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the distributions of sequencing QC parameters and HRD scores using the means, medians, and interquartile ranges (IQR). The Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare HRD scores between the BRCA1/2_mut and BRCA1/2_wt groups. Associations between HRD status and categorical clinicopathological variables, including tumor content and histological classification, were assessed using the chi-square test. Results were considered statistically significant if p < 0.05. Matplotlib 23 and Seaborn 24 Python libraries were used for the data visualization. All pathogenic BRCA1/2 variants were illustrated using a lollipop plot generated via cBioPortal with the genome build hg38.

3.2. Development and Analytical Validation of the MG_HRD Assay

To evaluate analytical performance, the MG_HRD assay was validated using reference standards and clinical samples with known HRD status of AmoyDx [n=157] and SOPHiA [n=13] assays. Higher concordance was observed between AmoyDx/SOPHiA and MG_HRD assays for these 170 OC samples, yielding 96.39% Positive Percentage Agreement (PPA), 98.3% Negative Percent Agreement (NPA), and 97.05% Overall Percent Agreement (OPA). Of the 170 samples, 165 had the same HRD status, whereas only five were discordant.

Among these five discordant cases, all were concordant with BRCA1/2_wt results, while only discordant at HRD_GS values. Of these four discordant cases, two had borderline scores near the respective assay thresholds (GSS: 50.1, GAS: 32; GSS:66.1, GAS: 39). This may reflect differences in assay-specific scoring algorithms, score calculation methods, and positivity cutoffs. Limit of detection (LoD) and precision of the MG_HRD assay:

Using FFPE samples, the assay was evaluated across a range of tumor content proportions (10-90%). Reliable detection of changes was achieved in samples containing as little as 20% tumor content. HRD scores remained consistent across varying DNA input amounts (200, 100, 50, and 30 ng), with reduced consistency observed only at 10 ng. Based on these findings, 30 ng was established as the optimal minimum DNA input requirement for the assay. The limit of detection (LoD) demonstrated 100% concordance at a 1% alternate allele frequency. The MG_HRD assay was evaluated using the same set of samples across multiple Illumina sequencing platforms, with different flow cell types, and by different operators on separate days. The MG_HRD assay results demonstrated 100% concordance across all testing conditions, indicating excellent reproducibility and robustness.

3.3. Sequencing performance Across the Platforms

Analysis of QC metrics revealed overall a good sequencing performance for all the three different assays. In the AmoyDx cohort, median alignment rate was 98.9% (IQR: 98.6-99.2%) and median effective depth of BRCA and SNP was 2498× and 472× respectively. The SOPHiA cohort achieved the high target coverage with a median BRCA coverage and panel coverage percentile of 100% and 99.9%, respectively. The median percentage of total fragmentation and whole-genome fragments were 15.0% and 70.4%, respectively. In the MG_HRD cohort, median coverage values for BRCA1 and BRCA2 were 100.0% and 95.3%, respectively, with a median backbone coverage of 90.5%. This assay obtained a median panel depth of 251x and 80% on target reads, allowing for adequate coverage to accurately assess both BRCA1/2 genes and genome-wide backbone regions. Despite differences in platformspecific QC metrics and sequencing designs, all assays consistently indicated greater coverage and robust sequencing quality for both BRCA and HRD analysis.

3.4. Distribution of HRD parameters

The HRD parameters included BRCA1/2 mutations and HRD_GS. Across all three assays, approximately half of the tumors were classified as HRD-positive (BRCA1/2 mutation and/or a positive HRD_GS). The HRD-positivity ranges from 44.7% (210/470) with SOPHiA to 54.8% (601/1097) with AmoyDx and 52.8% (1370/2596) with MG_HRD [Figure 2A].

Overall, 52.4% (2181/4163) of tumors were HRD-positive. Among the HRD-positive subgroups, BRCA1/2_wt/HRD_GS-positive tumors constituted the largest subgroup overall (28.8%), with frequencies of 31.6%, 20.9%, and 29.0% for AmoyDx, SOPHiA, and MG_HRD assay, respectively. 4. Discussion:

HRD testing in ovarian cancer has emerged as a crucial predictive biomarker, particularly for guiding therapeutic strategies involving PARP inhibitors. The FDA-approved CDx Myriad myChoice test is frequently employed for this purpose; however, for Indian patients it is costly, necessitates stringent quality standards, and involves prolonged result turnaround times, thereby limiting accessibility for patients 6, 25 . Therefore, the development of more affordable and reliable in-house HRD assays are needed, which can be implemented in standard molecular laboratories that could enhance patient access, reduce costs, and expedite treatment decision-making. Various studies have described laboratory-developed HRD assays using different gene panels, HRD score thresholds, bioinformatic pipelines, and sequencing platforms 3, 13 . Consistent with this, the recent AMP/ACCC/CAP joint consensus recommendations identified substantial heterogeneity in current commercial and laboratorydeveloped HRD assays regarding biomarker selection, scoring methods, validation strategies, and reporting practices 26 . These findings emphasize the need for rigorously validated and standardized in-house HRD assays to facilitate reliable clinical implementation and broader adoption in routine practice.

In this retrospective study, we present our experience of implementing an in-house developed HRD testing in our diagnostic laboratory. We assessed the feasibility, reliability and reproducibility of our MG_HRD assay by comparing results from the commercially available diagnostic assays [AmoyDx and SOPHiA] along with the reference assay, Myriad MyChoice CDx. We observed that the MG_HRD assay showed a complete concordance (100%) rate of HRD status with the AmoyDx assay among four samples and the outsourced Myriad myChoice CDx assay among five samples, which is similar to the reported studies 12, 27 . High concordance was observed for HRD_GS status, BRCA1/2 mutation status, and overall HRD classification, defined by the combined assessment of HRD_GS and BRCA1/2 alterations. This result demonstrates that the MG_HRD assay can be used as a reliable and potentially more accessible alternative to the Myriad myChoice CDx for HRD assessment in ovarian cancer patients.

Previous studies have demonstrated a high level of agreement between different HRD assays and the Myriad assay. Fumagalli C et al. reported that AmoyDx HRD Focus Panel showed an overall percent agreement (OPA) of 87.8% (65/74 tumors) compared with MyChoice CDx 3 . Similarly, Wehn AK et al. reported an OPA of up to 96% (n=198), with a PPA of 95.2% and an NPA of 96.8% for Illumina's TruSight Oncology 500 compared to MyChoice CDx 28 . The ENGOT European HRD initiative further developed the "Leuven" HRD assay as an academic alternative, demonstrating 91% overall agreement, with PPA and NPA of 94% and 86%, respectively, in 468 ovarian cancer samples from the PAOLA-1/ENGOT-ov25 trial 29 . The concordance of HRD status between the standardized Myriad assay (custom panel) and MyChoice® CDx test was 97.1% (499/514), according to Denkert et al 30 . In comparison, our MG_HRD assay showed the same concordance rate with an OPA of 97.1% (165/170), similar to the study conducted by Denkert et al 30 . A comparatively higher concordance of PPA (96.4 %) and NPA (98.3 %) was observed in the MG_HRD assay than in other studies 3, 28, 29 . These findings highlight the strong performance of the MG_HRD assay and its high degree of agreement with orthogonal platforms, including the AmoyDx HRD Focus and SOPHiA HRD assays, supporting its reliability for clinical implementation.

Our study indicated that the proportion of HRD-positive ovarian cancer cases ranged from 45% to 55% across the three evaluated assays. Specifically, the percentages were 44.7% for SOPHiA, 52.8% for MG_HRD and 54.8% for AmoyDx. These findings are consistent with previously published data. For instance, Guarischi-Sousa et al. reported HRD positivity rates of 45.9% and 65% using SOPHiA and AmoyDx assays, respectively 6.

Similarly, Fumagalli et al. observed 48.8% HRD-positive tumors using the AmoyDx assay, while data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) indicated an approximate HRD prevalence of 50% among high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma (HGSC) cases 3, 31 . The observed variability in HRD positivity across studies may be attributed to differences in assay methodologies and the thresholds used to define genomic instability. The proportions of HRD_GS-positive cases identified by AmoyDx, SOPHiA, and MG_HRD assays were 47.9%, 36.6%, and 46%, respectively, in our total study cohort. These results are slightly higher than those reported by Pepe et al. where HRD score positivity ranged between 30% to 45% for both AmoyDx and SOPHiA platforms 11 . Additionally, Romey et al. and Denkert et al. reported GIS positivity rates of 40.5% and 38.1% using the Myriad and standardized Myriad assays, respectively 30, 32 . Regardless of the HRD_GS status, we observed consistent frequencies of pathogenic BRCA1/2 mutations across all three assays ranging from 23.2 % -23.8% with an overall frequency of 23.6%. This aligns closely with the 22% prevalence reported by Landen et al study 33.

Our study further revealed that tumors with BRCA1/2 mutations had significantly elevated HRD genomic scores compared to those with BRCA1/2_wt (p < 0.0001), consistent with previous reports 32, 34, 35 . The median HRD scores of BRCA1/2 mutated versus wild-type tumors were 90.3 vs. 22.2 for AmoyDx, 3.0 vs. -4.1 for SOPHiA, and 57.0 vs. 28.0 for the MG_HRD assay, respectively. Similarly, Morgan et al. and Romey et al. reported higher median GIS for Myriad assay in BRCA1/2 mutated versus wild-type tumors (62 vs. 28; p < 0.0001) 32, 34 . The observed variation in absolute median HRD scores across the assays indicates that the HRD scores are assay-specific and should not be directly compared between the platforms. Although these assays differ in scoring algorithms, genomic metrics, and assayspecific thresholds, they converge on the same biological concept that BRCA mutated tumors consistently harbour a higher genomic scar burden than the wild-type tumors.

A substantial proportion of tumors in our cohort were BRCA1/2_wt but HRD_GSpositive (28.8% overall), with frequencies ranging from 20.9% with SOPHiA, 31.6% with AmoyDx, and 29.0% with MG_HRD. This distribution is broadly consistent with previous studies, including 30% reported by Yuan et al 36 using AmoyDx, 23% by Rojo et al 37 using SOPHiA, 26.2% by Romey et al 32 using Myriad, and 30.2% by Denkert et al 30 using a standardized Myriad HRD assay. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the frequency of this molecular subgroup of our MG_HRD assay is on par with the Myriad assay 32 . This observation highlights that a considerable subset of patients with HRD_GS positivity would be missed by BRCA testing alone, yet they could potentially benefit from treatment with PARPi.

Comparative analysis of BRCA1/2 mutation spectra revealed a consistent predominance of truncating variants in both genes, indicating that loss-of-function is the primary mechanism of gene inactivation. Several recurrent variants were identified in the BRCA1/2 genes; among these, the BRCA1 c.68_69del (p.Glu23ValfsTer17) variant was the most prevalent pathogenic variant, with a frequency of 14% [97/693] detected in ovarian cancer. This hotspot mutation is also known as "185delAG" and commonly known as the "Ashkenazi Jewish population founder mutation". This variant has also been widely reported across diverse Asian populations 38 , including the Indian population in breast and ovarian carcinoma cases. Singh et al. reported this germline variant in 3.4% (34/1,010) of patients with breast and ovarian cancer, while John et al. reported it in 24.5% of all pathogenic BRCA1 variants [29/181] in a cohort of 921 breast and ovarian cancer cases through germline testing 39,40 . Likewise, Parween et al. observed this variant in 7.2% (8/110) of pathogenic BRCA1 variants among patients with gynaecologic cancers, encompassing both somatic and germline tests 41 . To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to report the BRCA1 c.68_69del mutation exclusively in Indian patients with ovarian carcinoma using somatic BRCA testing methods. Collectively, these findings reinforce the prevalence of the BRCA1 c.

68_69del variant in the Indian population and highlight its clinical significance as an important target for routine BRCA testing and genetic risk assessment.

Tumor cellularity is an important pre-analytical factor that can influence HRD assessment. Although a TC of approximately 30% is generally preferable for a reliable analysis, HRD testing has also been successfully performed in samples with lower TC. Romey et al.

reported successful HRD analysis in 61.8% (264/427) of samples with TC<30% 32.

Furthermore, several clinically validated assays, including FoundationOne® CDx 42 , Caris HRD, SeqOne HRD solution, and CancerPrecision® (CeGaT), permit testing in samples with TC as low as 20% 10 . In our study, 512 samples with TC <30% remained evaluable for HRD classification, although HRD positivity was lower in this group than in samples with higher TC across all three assays. This finding suggests that TC may influence HRD detection while supporting the feasibility of HRD assessment in specimens with TC ≥20%. These observations are particularly relevant in real-world settings, where limited tumor material and variable tumor cellularity may otherwise restrict access to HRD testing.

HGSC was the predominant subtype in our cohort, with a significantly higher frequency than non-HGSC, consistent with prior reports. Overall, 82.5% of cases were HGSC, of which 60.2% were HRD-positive, aligning with studies demonstrating the strong association of HRD with HGSC 43 . Although in our study HRD was enriched in HGSC, it was also observed across non-HGSC subtypes. In low-grade serous carcinoma, we detected lower HRD positivity [3.8%] compared to 15% reported by Cunningham et al 44 . In clear cell carcinoma, HRD prevalence varied widely across studies, with 28% reported by Cunningham et al., 2.3% by Liu et al., while our study showed an intermediate rate of 13.7% (16/117) 44,45 . Carcinosarcoma exhibited a higher HRD frequency in our cohort (52.9%, 9/17) compared with the 25% reported by Denkert et al. This difference may reflect methodological variations between the studies, as our HRD classification incorporated both pathogenic BRCA mutations and genomic scar positivity, whereas Denkert et al. defined HRD positivity based on only the genomic instability score 30 . Similarly, the endometrioid carcinoma showed a higher HRD prevalence in our study (26.7%, 35/131) compared to 19% [5/26] reported by Pierson et al 46 . In contrast, mucinous carcinoma demonstrated a very low HRD positivity (2.2%), but no supporting evidence was found. Therefore, the presence of HRD in these less common histological subtypes underscores the importance of HRD profiling across these ovarian cancer subtypes, especially when they exhaust their standard therapeutic options.

CCNE1 amplification represents an alternative oncogenic pathway associated with homologous recombination proficiency and reduced sensitivity to PARP inhibitors 7, 45 . The prevalence of CCNE1 amplification in our cohort was 7.0% (181/2596) using the MG_HRD assay, which is comparable to the 6.4% (290/4509) reported by Rojo et al. 37
