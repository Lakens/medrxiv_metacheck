Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 8 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP), the age-associated expansion of blood cell clones carrying acquired driver mutations, remains less well characterized in young adults, across genetic ancestries, and in clinical populations. We analyzed whole-exome sequencing and longitudinal electronic health record (EHR) data from 59,598 adults in the Mount Sinai Million Health Discoveries Program, an urban, multi-ancestry hospital-based cohort that includes substantial representation of African (~35%) and Admixed American genetic ancestry (~15%) and young adults aged 18-39 years (~30%). After sample QC, separate analyses were performed in discovery (n = 28,051) and validation (n = 26,938) cohorts defined by sequencing platform and combined by meta-analysis.

CHIP was detected in 8.5% of participants in each cohort and in 4.0% of young adults, with DNMT3A, TET2, ASXL1, and PPM1D the most frequently mutated genes. Prevalence and clone size increased with age across ancestry groups. After adjusting for age, participants of African ancestry had lower CHIP prevalence than those of European ancestry in both cohorts (discovery: OR=0.86, 95% CI = 0.78-0.95, p=0.002; validation: OR=0.84, 95% CI = 0.75-0.93, p=0.001). Participants of Admixed American ancestry showed a similar trend.

Higher systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) was associated with CHIP after adjusting for age, sex and ancestry in both cohorts (pooled OR=1.30, 95% CI = 1.15-1.48, p=4.78e-5). Heterozygosity at an HLA class II locus HLA-DPA1 was associated with 7% lower odds of overall CHIP (pooled OR=0.93, 95% CI = 0.87-0.99, p=0.03). Driver-stratified analyses revealed distinct HLA class II nominal association patterns for DNMT3A-and TET2-mutant CHIP. Particularly, HLA-DQB1 heterozygosity associated with lower odds of TET2-mutant CHIP (pooled OR=0.84, 95% CI = 0.70-1.00, p=0.05), HLA-DQA1 and HLA-DOB heterozygosity associated with higher odds of DNMT3A-mutant CHIP (pooled OR=1.19, 95% CI = 1.00-1.42, p=0.05; pooled OR=1.12, 95% CI = 1.01-1.25, p=0.03 respectively). Longitudinal EHR analyses revealed CHIP associations with several incident diseases that varied by driver gene and clone size. The broadest pattern of associations involved high-risk CHIP genes and encompassed hematologic malignancies, solid tumors, cardiovascular outcomes, hypertension, and COPD. High-VAF clones were the main drivers of these associations. Among adults aged ≥40 years, phenome-wide analyses identified a Bonferroni-significant association with incident myeloproliferative disease and nominal associations with incident pancytopenia, anemia, cardiomegaly, and chronic renal failure. Young-adult CHIP was nominally associated with incident nodular goiter and esophageal disease. These findings characterize CHIP across adulthood and genetic ancestry and reveal inherited immune variation and systemic inflammation as correlates in a hospital-based population.

Introduction

Clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) is an aging-associated expansion of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cell clones carrying acquired leukemogenic mutations without clinical evidence of a hematologic malignancy 1 . The World Health Organization (WHO) defines CHIP using a variant allele fraction (VAF) threshold of at least 2% 2 . CHIP is uncommon in early adulthood but affects 10-20% of adults over 70 years 3 . Most carriers harbor a single mutation, commonly in the epigenetic regulators DNMT3A, TET2, and ASXL1. CHIP has been associated with a 10-to 13-fold increased risk of hematologic malignancy, a nearly 2-fold increased risk of coronary heart disease and elevated all-cause mortality 1, [4] [5] [6] . These risks vary according to the underlying driver mutation and clone size.

Large sequencing studies, including the UK Biobank, Trans-Omics for Precision Medicine (TOPMed), and the All of Us Research Program have substantially expanded our understanding of the genetic and clinical landscape of CHIP [7] [8] [9] . However, estimates of CHIP prevalence, driver composition, and clinical associations may vary with cohort ascertainment, age distribution, genetic ancestry, and sequencing technology 7, 9, 10 . Younger adults remain underrepresented in biobank-scale sequencing studies, which are weighted toward middle-aged and older participants. This limited representation, together with the low prevalence of CHIP detectable by standard biobank sequencing before age 40, leaves its mutational spectrum and clinical correlates in younger adults comparatively poorly characterized 4, 9.

Beyond differences in population representation, inherited genetic variation may contribute to heterogeneity in CHIP. Although its defining mutations are acquired, population-scale studies have identified common and rare germline susceptibility variants, including associations with specific CHIP drivers 7, 11 . Inherited variation in the highly polymorphic human leukocyte antigen (HLA) region may influence immune recognition of mutant clones through differences in antigen presentation. HLA heterozygosity may broaden the repertoire of presented peptides; class I heterozygosity has been associated with improved outcomes following immune-checkpoint blockade 12 and class II heterozygosity with reduced lung cancer risk among smokers 13 . While recent UK Biobank study found no consistent association between predicted binding of mutant peptides to HLA molecules and mutation prevalence or clone size 14 , it only focused on 40 recurrent hotspot variants, did not include TET2, and evaluated only classical HLA loci.

Alongside potential differences in immune recognition, systemic inflammation may impact the expansion and biological effects of mutant clones. Experimental studies support a bidirectional relationship in which CHIP-associated mutations alter inflammatory signaling, while inflammatory stress can confer a selective advantage to mutant cells [15] [16] [17] . For example, TET2 deficiency enhances NLRP3 inflammasome-dependent IL-1β signaling, whereas inflammatory stress promotes the survival and expansion of TET2-mutant clones 15, 16 . These effects may vary by driver gene 17 . Composite indices derived from routine laboratory measurements offer a scalable approach to assessing the inflammatory correlates of CHIP. The systemic immuneinflammation index (SII), integrates neutrophil, lymphocyte and platelet counts, whereas the INFLA-score combines C-reactive protein with cellular measures of low-grade inflammation [18] [19] [20].

Large hospital-based cohorts linked to longitudinal electronic health records (EHRs) enable the examination of CHIP across a broad age range, multiple genetic ancestry groups, and a wide spectrum of clinical conditions. These data also support phenome-wide association studies (PheWAS) to characterize clinical correlates across adulthood. As both CHIP prevalence and disease burden increase strongly with age, age-stratified analyses may identify clinical associations that would be obscured in a pooled population.

Here, we analyzed whole-exome sequencing (WES) and longitudinal EHR data from ~60,000 adults in the Mount Sinai Million (MSM) Health Discoveries Program, an urban hospital-based cohort with substantial representation of African (~35%) and Admixed American genetic ancestry (~15%) and young adults aged 18-39 years (~30%). Participants were divided into discovery and validation cohorts to account for differences in sequencing platforms. We characterized the CHIP mutational landscape across age and genetic ancestry groups, including a focused analysis of adults aged 18-39 years; examined inherited susceptibility, with particular emphasis on HLA class I and class II heterozygosity; evaluated associations with systemic immune-inflammation index SII; and performed outcome-specific association analyses and age-stratified PheWAS. Together, these analyses characterize the demographic, immune, and clinical heterogeneity of CHIP across adulthood in an urban, multi-ancestry, EHR-linked hospital population.

Methods

Study design and population.

The Mount Sinai Million (MSM) Health Discoveries Program (https://mountsinaimillion.org) is a health system-wide precision medicine initiative designed to enroll up to one million patients receiving care across the Mount Sinai Health System (MSHS). MSM builds on the BioMe Biobank 21 , an electronic health record (EHR)-linked biobank established in 2006 whose enrollment concluded in 2022. Both programs link genomic and biospecimen data with longitudinal EHR-derived clinical information. As MSHS serves diverse communities throughout New York City, the combined resource includes participants spanning a broad range of genetic ancestries and clinical conditions. At the time of this analysis, approximately 300,000 participants were enrolled, and genomic data were available for 59,598 participants.

The discovery cohort comprised 31,250 participants enrolled through BioMe before 2022. Whole-exome sequencing (WES) was performed using the IDT xGen capture panel (Integrated DNA Technologies, Inc, Coralville, IA) and an Illumina v4 HiSeq 2500 platform. Genome-wide genotyping was performed using the Infinium Global Screening Array (GSA). The validation cohort comprised 28,348 participants enrolled through MSM beginning in 2022, and genotyped using both genotype-by-sequencing (GxS) and a Twist targeted sequencing panel (Twist Bioscience, San Francisco, CA). Analyses were conducted separately in the discovery and validation cohorts to account for differences in the genomic platforms and sequencing depth, with cohort-specific results combined using meta-analysis where specified. The mean depth of coverage is ~36x 22 for the discovery cohort and ~45x for the validation cohort which could lead to differences in variant detection and CHIP prevalence between cohorts. Participants in the discovery cohort were enrolled earlier, with a median follow-up of 8.9 years, versus the validation cohort at 3.8 years. Follow-up time was defined as the time interval, in years, from specimen collection to the latest available diagnosis encounter for each participant.

The Program for the Protection of Human Subjects (PPHS) at the Icahn School of Medicine at

Mount Sinai determined that this study met the criteria for exempt human-subjects research (IRB no. 20-03271).

Participant selection and sample quality control (QC).

Of the 59,598 participants with WES data, 587 were excluded during sample-level quality control (QC) due to discordance between genetically inferred and reported sex, estimated contamination >5%, <80% of targeted exome bases achieving >20× coverage, or unresolved duplicates. After sample QC, 59,011 participants remained (30,784 in the discovery cohort and 28,227 in the validation cohort). We then excluded participants with missing phenotype or EHR data, duplicate or conflicting phenotype records, or age <18 years at biospecimen collection. We also excluded participants with a diagnosis of a lymphoid, hematopoietic, or related tissue neoplasm recorded before or within six months after biospecimen collection (ICD-10: C81-C96, D45-D47, Z85.6 and Z85.7; ICD-9: 200-208, 238.4-238.7, V10.6 and V10.7). This six-month window was used to reduce the likelihood that variants arising from an existing or recently diagnosed hematologic malignancy would be classified as clonal hematopoiesis. The final study population comprised 54,989 participants: 28,051 in the discovery cohort and 26,938 in the validation cohort. Participant demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Note that participants with baseline cytopenias were included in the primary analysis. Clonal cytopenia of undetermined significance (CCUS) was not clinically adjudicated, as no dedicated ICD-9-CM/ICD-10-CM code was available for its identification.

Sex and ancestry.

We used the genetically inferred sex and ancestry estimated from the WES data by Regeneron in the analyses. Briefly, genetic ancestry was estimated using principal component analysis (PCA) of common SNPs shared with the HapMap3 reference panel 23 . PCs were computed using the reference samples, and study samples were projected onto them. Kernel density estimates of each reference population in PC1 vs PC2 and PC3 vs PC4 were used to assign each sample to matching reference populations, which were then mapped into five genetic ancestry groups: African (AFR), Admixed American (AMR), European (EUR), South Asian (SAS), and East Asian (EAS). Samples that matched no reference population, or an ambiguous combination of populations, were labeled Unknown.

CHIP variant calling and classification.

Somatic single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) and small insertions and deletions were called from aligned CRAM files using our previously described pipeline 24, 25 , which implements GATK Mutect2 [4.2.0.0] in tumor-only mode with the GRCh38 reference genome. To exclude likely germline calls and sequencing artifacts, we provided to Mutect2 an external reference of germline variants from gnomAD and a Panel Of Normals (PON). For the gnomAD germline resource, we used the GATK-provided somatic-hg38_af-onlygnomad.hg38.vcf.gz resource. We generated separate PONs for the discovery and validation cohorts, each using WES data from 100 participants aged ≤25 years, an age group in which clonal hematopoiesis is expected to be uncommon. To minimize potential confounding from conditions associated with CHIP, the PON did not include individuals with neoplasms, HIV and inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). Finally, high-confidence somatic mutations were identified by applying Mutect2's orientation bias and PASS filters.

Candidate variants were restricted to a curated set of myeloid CHIP-associated variants across 76 prespecified driver genes, and required to have a variant allele fraction (VAF) ≥2% (Supplementary Table S1). Additional filters to remove likely artifacts required a minimum read depth ≥20; ≥3 reads supporting the mutant allele; and at least one forward-and one reversestrand read supporting both the reference and alternate alleles. Somatic variants were excluded if their gnomAD 26 population allele frequency was ≥0.1% overall or in any ancestry-specific population. To reduce potential germline contamination, we filtered candidate CHIP variants using ancestry-specific allele frequencies rather than relying solely on overall population frequencies. This excluded variants such as TET2 rs568009712, which was enriched among MSM participants of South Asian ancestry, showed elevated VAFs, and had an allele frequency of 0.4% in the South Asian population in gnomAD 26 . Variants with an observed allele frequency >1% in the cohort were also excluded, unless they had previously been implicated in hematologic malignancy. Variants were annotated for predicted pathogenicity using Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion (CADD) score (dbNSFP version 4.2a), and those with a Phredscaled CADD score <10 were excluded. U2AF1 required additional handling because the GRCh38 reference genome contains an incorrect duplication of the U2AF1 gene locus on chromosome 21 that impairs read mapping. U2AF1 CHIP variants were recovered using a custom pileup region script 8 . All CHIP variants that passed the filters are listed in Supplementary Table S2. Participants with at least one CHIP variant were classified as CHIP carriers; all other participants were classified as non-carriers. Clonal cytopenia of undetermined significance (CCUS) was not assessed separately.

Clone size definitions.

For each CHIP mutation, clone size was defined using mutation-level VAF. Mutations with VAF <10% were classified as small clones, and those with VAF ≥10% as large clones. Participant-level clone size analyses therefore use the maximum observed VAF for those individuals with multiple CHIP mutations.

HLA typing and zygosity classification.

To examine potential associations between HLA zygosity and CHIP, we inferred HLA class I and class II alleles for each participant from WES CRAM files using HLA-HD [1.7.1] at up to three-field (six-digit) resolution 27 . For zygosity analyses, allele calls were collapsed to two-field (four-digit) resolution. At each locus, participants carrying two distinct alleles were classified as heterozygous, whereas those carrying two identical alleles were classified as homozygous.

Participants heterozygous at all three classical HLA class I loci (HLA-A, HLA-B, HLA-C) were classified as fully class I heterozygous; participants homozygous at one or more loci were classified as non-fully class I heterozygous. Participants heterozygous at each of five classical HLA class II loci (HLA-DRB1, -DQB1, -DQA1, -DPB1, and -DPA1) were classified as fully class II heterozygous; participants homozygous at one or more loci were classified as non-fully class II heterozygous. We also evaluated locus-specific zygosity at HLA-DRA and the non-classical HLA class II loci (HLA-DMA, HLA-DMB, HLA-DOA, and HLA-DOB). Participants with missing or ambiguous calls (~0-25% depending on the loci, Supplementary Table S3) were excluded from analyses of the corresponding locus; composite class I and class II analyses required complete calls at all constituent loci.

Systemic immune-inflammation index.

Given the reported association between inflammation and CHIP 28 , we considered two blood-based indices of systemic inflammation. The systemic Immune-Inflammation Index (SII) is a composite marker that integrates innate inflammatory responses and adaptive immune function, calculated from peripheral blood cell counts as platelet count × neutrophil count / lymphocyte count, with higher values indicating greater systemic inflammation. We also considered the INFLA-score as a complementary measure of low-grade systemic inflammation because it combines C-reactive protein (CRP) with cellular inflammatory markers 19 . However, CRP measurements were available for only a limited subset of participants. Analyses therefore focused on SII, which could be calculated for a larger proportion of participants from available blood cell counts. Laboratory measurements were obtained from MSM health records and were subjected to data cleaning and harmonization, including unit standardization and removal of implausible, nonmeasurement, or sentinel values. The empirical distribution of each laboratory variable was subsequently examined to identify distributional gaps and extreme observations. These observations were evaluated alongside clinically plausible ranges to derive each measurement specific admissible value ranges: platelet count: 0-2,500 K/µL, neutrophils absolute count: 0-100 K/µL, lymphocyte absolute count: 0-300 K/µL. As part of the lab data quality control, we further excluded measurements that were recorded on at least three consecutive days as they could be indicative of acute clinical conditions or an inpatient monitoring. We also excluded multiple measurements that were available on the same day, which could reflect inpatient monitoring or repeat testing. For each participant, we selected the complete blood count obtained closest to the biospecimen collection date within a one-year window. To ensure that the SII was calculated from a single blood draw, all three components (platelet, neutrophil, and lymphocyte counts) were required to be available on the same date.

Replication of previously reported germline variant associations with CHIP risk.

In a recent study, Kessler et al. 29 conducted common and rare variant associations in the UK Biobank cohort, comprising 25,657 CHIP cases and 342,869 controls of European ancestry. The study identified 57 independent common (minor allele frequency (MAF) > 0.5%) germline variants across 24 loci, as well as a rare germline frameshift variant in the CHEK2 gene associated with the risk of acquiring CHIP. More recently, a multi-ancestry meta-analysis of six large-scale cohorts (UKB, All of Us Research Program, TOPMed, Vanderbilt University Biobank, Mass General Brigham Biobank, and Mexico City Prospective Study) including 53,274 CHIP cases and 922,528 controls identified 31 genome-wide significant loci harboring 2,572 variants 10 . We assessed whether these 2,593 previously reported germline variant associations with CHIP risk were replicated in our cohort.

Disease phenotypes.

To evaluate the association between CHIP and disease phenotypes, we mapped EHR diagnoses coded using ICD-9 and ICD-10 diagnosis codes to disease phenotypes previously associated with CHIP 9 . Specifically, we evaluated i) hematological neoplasms, including all hematological neoplasms, myeloid malignancies, myeloproliferative neoplasms, myelodysplastic syndromes, acute myeloid leukemias, and lymphoid leukemias; ii) solid tumors, including non-hematological malignant neoplasms and cancers of the breast, prostate, lung, colorectum, melanoma, kidney, head and neck, brain, esophagus, stomach, liver, and thyroid; and iii) other conditions previously reported to be associated with CHIP, including cardiovascular disease (myocardial infarction, stroke, ischemic stroke, coronary artery disease, heart failure, atrial fibrillation, and all cardiovascular diseases), type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, obesity, rheumatoid arthritis, Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, chronic kidney disease, gout, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), HIV, and inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). We evaluated disease associations using two frameworks: (i) prevalent diseases at baseline and (ii) incident diseases occurring after biospecimen collection. The ICD-9/10 codes used to define each disease phenotype are provided in Supplementary Table S4.

For prevalent disease analysis, participants were considered cases if the corresponding diagnosis code was recorded on at least two distinct encounter dates before or on the biospecimen collection date. For incident disease analysis, participants with prevalent disease at baseline were excluded. Incident cases were defined as participants with the corresponding diagnosis code recorded on at least two distinct encounter dates after biospecimen collection. In both analyses, participants without a qualifying diagnosis code for the corresponding disease before or after biospecimen collection were considered controls. We also performed additional stratified analyses of disease incidence to evaluate associations with specific CHIP subgroups, including high-and low-VAF (<10%) CHIP, mutations in the most frequently observed CHIP genes (DNMT3A and TET2), mutations in high-risk CHIP genes (TP53, ASXL1, JAK2, U2AF1, SF3B1, SRSF2, IDH1 and IDH2), and CHIP among young (18-39 years) and older adults (≥40 years).

EHR phenotype construction and phenome-wide association study (PheWAS).

To characterize the clinical phenome associated with CHIP in this hospital-based cohort, we performed a phenome-wide association study (PheWAS) using the PheWAS R package (version 0.99.6.1) 30 . Clinical diagnoses were derived from longitudinal EHR data using ICD-9 and ICD-10 diagnosis codes and mapped to ~1,800 phecodes using the createPhenotypes function. While mapping, phecode exclusions and sex-specific restrictions were applied. The incidence phenotype definitions included diagnoses recorded after biospecimen collection without a corresponding diagnosis documented before the biospecimen collection date. A participant was classified as a case for a phenotype when the corresponding phecode was documented on at least two distinct encounter dates. Participants without evidence of the phenotype were considered as controls; those with a single qualifying encounter were omitted from the analysis of that phenotype. Sex-specific phecodes were evaluated only among participants of the applicable sex. Participants were stratified according to age at biospecimen collection as young adults (18-39 years) or older adults (≥40 years). This stratification was used to examine marked age-related differences in both CHIP prevalence and overall disease burden.

Statistical analysis.

General approach and demographic variables. Analyses were performed in R. Unless otherwise specified, models were fitted separately in the discovery and validation cohorts. Effect estimates are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and two-sided p-values. In addition to overall CHIP, gene-specific analyses were performed for the four most prevalent CHIP driver genes: DNMT3A, TET2, ASXL1, and PPM1D. Age was modeled continuously in years; sex (male/female), smoking status (yes/no), and genetic ancestry were modeled categorically. Genetic ancestry was categorized into the three predominant genetic ancestry groups in this cohort, EUR, AFR, and AMR, in addition to Other, with EUR as the reference group. Associations of age, sex, smoking status, and genetic ancestry with CHIP were first evaluated using univariable logistic regression, with CHIP carrier status as the binary outcome (carrier/non-carrier). Covariates significantly associated with CHIP status in univariable analyses were then included in a multivariable logistic regression model, with CHIP status as the binary outcome. Statistical significance was defined as p ≤ 0.05.

Clone size analysis. To account for potential differences in clone size distributions across genetic ancestry groups, among CHIP carriers, the association between age and clone size was evaluated separately within each major genetic ancestry group (EUR, AFR, and AMR) and cohort, resulting in six independent analyses. Logistic regression models used clone size (VAF≥10% vs. <10%) as the binary outcome and age in years as the predictor. The six estimates were then combined using random-effects meta-analysis with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation and the Hartung-Knapp adjustment.

HLA zygosity analysis. To evaluate associations between HLA zygosity and CHIP, we used separate logistic regression models for each HLA locus and for the composite class I and class II measures. Locus-specific models compared heterozygous and homozygous participants; composite models compared fully heterozygous with non-fully heterozygous participants. Overall CHIP status was the binary outcome (carrier vs. non-carrier), and models were adjusted for age, sex and genetic ancestry. We performed additional analyses using DNMT3A-mutant and TET2-mutant CHIP as separate outcomes, comparing carriers of each mutation with participants without CHIP. Cohort-specific estimates were combined using inverse-variance weighted fixed-effects meta-analysis using the metafor R package 31 . To assess whether HLA associations were consistent across sequencing platforms and ancestry groups, we calculated Cochran's (Q) and (I² ) to assess and quantify whether variation among effect estimates exceeds that expected by chance. An (I² ) of 0% indicates no detectable heterogeneity, whereas higher values indicate progressively greater differences in effect estimates across groups. As a secondary analysis, estimates from the six cohort-by-ancestry strata were combined using random-effects meta-analysis to assess the consistency of associations across genetic ancestry groups.

Systemic immune-inflammation index analysis. The association between SII and CHIP was evaluated using logistic regression with CHIP carrier status as the binary outcome, and log10transformed SII as the predictor. Models were adjusted for age, sex and genetic ancestry, and restricted to participants with SII data. Estimates were obtained separately for discovery and validation cohorts and combined by inverse-variance-weighted fixed-effect meta-analysis. Additional analyses were performed to evaluate associations between SII and CHIP by clone size and individual CHIP driver genes.

Germline variant association analysis. Associations between previously reported germline variants and CHIP risk were evaluated using logistic regression, with CHIP carrier status as the binary outcome and genotype dosage, defined as the number of effect alleles (0, 1, or 2), as the predictor under an additive genetic model. Models were adjusted for age, sex, and genetic ancestry. Analyses were performed separately in the discovery and validation cohorts.

Disease phenotype analyses. Associations between CHIP carrier status and disease phenotypes were evaluated separately for prevalent and incident disease. For prevalent disease, we used a logistic regression model with disease status as the outcome and CHIP carrier status as the primary predictor. For incident disease, we used a Cox proportional hazards regression model to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs, with time from biospecimen collection to the first qualifying diagnosis as the time-to-event measure. All models were adjusted for age, sex, and genetic ancestry. For stratified analyses of incident disease, the same Cox proportional hazards framework was applied. Analyses were restricted to disease phenotypes with at least two CHIP carriers among individuals with the disease. Effect estimates were obtained separately in the discovery and validation cohorts and pooled using inversevariance-weighted fixed-effect meta-analysis. To account for multiple testing, we also report pvalues adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure.

PheWAS. For each phecode, logistic regression models used phecode case-control status as the binary outcome and CHIP carrier status as the primary predictor, adjusting for age, sex, and genetic ancestry. Models were fitted separately in the discovery and validation cohorts and within each age stratum (18-39 years and ≥ 40 years), based on age at biospecimen collection. Only phecodes represented by at least 20 cases or 20 controls were tested, with an additional requirement of at least 5 cases among CHIP carriers. Within each age stratum, cohort-specific estimates for phecodes tested in both cohorts were combined by inverse-variance weighted fixed-effects meta-analysis using the metafor R package 31 . Prior to meta-analysis, we excluded phecodes for which the ratio of the cohort-specific variances (squared standard errors) exceeded 100 to limit the influence of unstable effect estimates. To account for multiple testing, statistical significance was determined using a Bonferroni-corrected threshold, which was applied separately within each age stratum across the meta-analyzed phecodes. Associations that did not meet the multiple-testing-corrected significance threshold but with nominal p ≤ 0.05 were considered exploratory.

Results

Study design overview.

We characterized CHIP prevalence, mutational spectrum, and clone size (VAF) across age and genetic ancestry groups in the Mount Sinai Million (MSM) Health Discoveries Program cohort (Figure 1). Subset analyses focused on young adults (18-39 years), as well as CHIP subgroups defined by clone size and driver gene. We also evaluated inherited genetic correlates of CHIP, including HLA heterozygosity and previously reported common and rare germline variants, and assessed associations with blood-based inflammatory index. Finally, we integrated genomic data with longitudinal electronic health records (EHRs) to conduct age-stratified (18-39 years and ≥40 years) phenome-wide association studies (PheWAS) using clinical phenotypes derived from ICD-9 and ICD-10-codes.

Cohort characteristics.

Of 59,598 MSM participants, 59,011 passed sample QC and 54,989 remained after subsequent exclusions. These participants were then divided into discovery (n = 28,051) and validation (n = 26,938) cohorts based on sequencing methodology (see Methods). Demographic characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Participants in the discovery cohort were aged 18-82 years (median: 52 years), with major genetic ancestry groups 36% European (EUR), 41% African (AFR), and 12% Admixed American (AMR). Participants in the validation cohort were aged 18-87 years (median: 51 years), and 38%, 28%, and 18% were assigned EUR, AFR, and AMR genetic ancestry, respectively. Across the combined study population, 37% were of EUR, 35% of AFR, and 15% of AMR genetic ancestry. Ancestry-stratified analyses focused on these three major groups.

CHIP mutational landscape in MSM cohort. We identified 2,647 CHIP mutations among 2,386 participants (8.5%) in the discovery cohort, and 2,629 CHIP mutations among 2,284 individuals (8.5%) in the validation cohort. Across both cohorts and the three major ancestry groups, around 90% of CHIP carriers harbored a single mutation (Figure 2A-B). Nonsynonymous and stopgain mutations were the predominant mutation types (Figure 2C-D). The most frequently mutated genes were DNMT3A, TET2, ASXL1 and PPM1D (Figure 2E-F), consistent with the established CHIP mutational landscape.

Young adult (age 18-39 years) CHIP carriers in the MSM cohort. Unlike population-based biobanks, which tend to include predominantly middle-aged and older participants, the MSM cohort included 15,974 (~30%) participants aged 18-39 years (7,337 in the discovery cohort; 8,637 in the validation cohort). Of these, 646 (4.0%) were CHIP carriers, including 383 (5.2%) in the discovery cohort and 263 (3.0%) in the validation cohort. The median age of CHIP carriers was 31 years in both cohorts. The majority of young adult CHIP carriers harbored a single mutation (Supplementary Figure S1). As in the overall cohort, nonsynonymous and stop-gain mutations were the predominant mutation types. The most frequently mutated genes were TET2, DNMT3A, and CUX1. Mutation characteristics and ancestry distributions among young adults are shown in Supplementary Figure S1. In comparison, older adults (age ≥40 years) had higher CHIP prevalence in both cohorts (9.7% vs. 5.2% in the discovery cohort; 11.0% vs. 3.0% in the validation cohort), with the top mutated genes broadly similar (Supplementary Table S5).

CHIP prevalence increased with age and varied by genetic ancestry. CHIP prevalence increased with age in both cohorts and within each of the three major genetic ancestry groups (Figure 3). Prevalence also differed across ancestry groups. In the discovery cohort, CHIP was detected in 9.7% of EUR, 8.0% of AFR, and 8.2% of AMR participants; the corresponding prevalences in the validation cohort were 10.5%, 8.0%, and 6.6%, respectively. For young adults, CHIP prevalence among EUR, AFR, and AMR participants was 5.3%, 5.0%, and 6.0% in the discovery cohort, and 2.8%, 2.4% and 3.4%, in the validation cohort respectively.

We first evaluated the associations between individual demographic variables and CHIP using univariable regression (Table 2). CHIP was significantly associated with age and genetic ancestry, but not with sex or smoking status. In multivariable analyses adjusting for genetic ancestry, each additional year of age was associated with higher odds of CHIP in both cohorts (discovery: OR=1.030, 95% CI = 1.027-1.033, p <2e-16; validation: OR=1.051, 95% CI = 1.048-1.054, p <2e-16). After adjusting for age, AFR participants had lower odds of CHIP than EUR participants in both cohorts (discovery: OR=0.86, 95% CI = 0.78-0.95, p=0.002; validation: OR=0.84, 95% CI = 0.75-0.93, p=0.001). AMR ancestry was associated with lower estimated odds of CHIP, although the association did not reach statistical significance in the discovery cohort (discovery: OR=0.88, 95% CI = 0.76-1.01, p=0.08; validation: OR=0.72, 95% CI = 0.63-0.82, p=8.47e-07). Complete results are provided in Table 2.

At the driver gene level, focused analyses of the four most frequently mutated genes DNMT3A, TET2, ASXL1, and PPM1D revealed that their prevalence increased with age after adjusting for genetic ancestry in both cohorts (Supplementary Table S6, Supplementary Figure S2). Similar to overall CHIP, TET2-and ASXL1-mutant CHIP were less prevalent among AFR than EUR participants. Associations among AMR participants were directionally similar but did not consistently reach statistical significance across cohorts.

Clone size showed age-associated patterns across cohorts and ancestry groups. We assessed clone size using VAF. The majority of CHIP mutations had a VAF below 10% (Figure 4A-B), but older participants generally had larger clones across both cohorts (Figure 4C-H). We then assessed the association between age and the presence of large clones (VAF≥10%) separately by cohort, modeling age per 1-year increase and adjusting for genetic ancestry and sex. Older age was associated with higher odds of a large clone in the discovery cohort (OR=1.04, 95% CI = 1.03-1.04, p<2e-16), with a consistent trend in the validation cohort (OR=1.01, 95% CI = 1.00-1.01, p=0.06). In driver gene level analyses (Supplementary Table S7), older age was associated with higher odds of large clones in ASXL1 in both cohorts (discovery: OR=1.08, 95% CI = 1.03-1.13, p=0.003; validation: OR=1.04, 95% CI = 1.00-1.08, p=0.04). For DNMT3A and TET2, the associations were significant in the discovery cohort, and trended in the same direction in the validation cohort. PPM1D showed a similar trend but was not significantly associated with large clones in either cohort.

In a secondary analysis across cohort-by-ancestry strata, in the discovery cohort, increasing age was associated with higher odds of a large clone among EUR (OR=1.04, 95% CI=1.03-1.05, p<2e-16), AFR (OR=1.04, 95% CI=1.03-1.05, p=8.54e-13) and AMR participants (OR=1.03, 95% CI=1.01-1.05, p=0.001). In the validation cohort, the association remained significant among EUR individuals (OR=1.01, 95% CI=1.00-1.02, p=0.002), but did not reach statistical significance among AFR or AMR participants (Supplementary Table S7). A randomeffects meta-analysis of the cohort-and ancestry-specific estimates yielded a pooled OR of 1.02 (95% CI = 1.00-1.04, p=0.05) with substantial heterogeneity (I² = 91%, p<0.001).

Inherited immune variation and systemic inflammation in CHIP.

We examined inherited HLA variation and systemic inflammation as immune-related correlates of CHIP. We assessed HLA variation through allele heterozygosity, and systemic inflammation through the systemic immune-inflammation index (SII). Analyses were performed separately in the discovery and validation cohorts, adjusting for age, sex and ancestry, and cohort-specific estimates were combined using fixed-effect meta-analysis.

Certain HLA heterozygosities showed locus-, clone-size and driver gene-specific associations with CHIP.

To better understand the potential immunogenomic drivers of CHIP, we evaluated associations between HLA allele heterozygosity and CHIP prevalence, adjusting for age, sex and ancestry separately in the discovery and validation cohorts, and combined cohort-specific estimates using fixed-effect meta-analysis (Figure 5). The proportions of individuals heterozygous at each HLA locus are provided in Supplementary Table S3. At a nominal (p≤0.05) threshold, HLA-DPA1 heterozygosity was associated with 7% lower odds of overall CHIP (pooled OR=0.93, 95% CI = 0.87-0.99, p=0.03; Figure 5A). Point estimates indicated higher odds for HLA-DQA1 heterozygosity (pooled OR=1.10, 95% CI = 1.00-1.22, p=0.05), and lower odds for HLA-DQB1 heterozygosity (pooled OR=0.92, 95% CI = 0.84-1.02, p=0.10). No HLA class I locus or aggregate class I or class II heterozygosity measure was associated with overall CHIP at the p ≤ 0.05 threshold.

Among CHIP carriers, we additionally performed an exploratory analysis comparing maximum VAF ≥20% versus <20% to examine HLA associations with larger clones. A 20% threshold has been used in myeloid malignancy risk stratification, including the clonal hematopoiesis risk score 32 . We observed that HLA-C heterozygosity was nominally associated with higher odds of a maximum VAF ≥20% versus <20% (pooled OR=1.52, 95% CI = 1.19-1.94, p=0.001).

Driver gene-specific analyses revealed distinct HLA associations for DNMT3A-and TET2mutant CHIP. Heterozygosity at both HLA-DQA1 (pooled OR=1.19, 95% CI = 1.00-1.42, p=0.05) and HLA-DOB (pooled OR=1.12, 95% CI = 1.01-1.25, p=0.03) was associated with higher odds of DNMT3A-mutant CHIP (Figure 5B). DNMT3A R882 hotspot mutations were identified in 64 participants in the discovery cohort and 109 in the validation cohort. Excluding these carriers yielded similar effect estimates for HLA-DQA1 heterozygosity (pooled OR=1.19, 95% CI=0.99-1.42, p=0.07) and HLA-DOB heterozygosity (pooled OR=1.12, 95% CI=1.00-1.25, p=0.050), while R882-specific estimates were positive but imprecise. In contrast, HLA-DQB1 heterozygosity associated with lower odds of TET2-mutant CHIP (pooled OR=0.84, 95% CI = 0.70-1.00, p=0.05; Figure 5C). Note that while HLA-DQB1 effect estimates were opposite in direction in the DNMT3A-and TET2-mutant CHIP analyses, only the TET2 estimates reached the statistical threshold (Supplementary Table S8). Collectively, these findings suggest that associations between HLA class II zygosity and CHIP show different patterns according to the underlying driver mutation.

Ancestry-stratified HLA analyses.

To examine the HLA associations within genetic ancestry groups, we performed separate analyses among EUR, AFR and AMR participants in each cohort. Within each ancestry group, we combined the discovery and validation estimates using fixed-effect meta-analysis to obtain ancestry-specific pooled ORs. Among participants of European genetic ancestry, HLA-DQA1 heterozygosity showed higher estimated odds of overall CHIP (pooled OR=1.15, 95% CI = 0.98-1.36, p=0.08) and was associated with higher odds of DNMT3A-mutant CHIP (pooled OR=1.49, 95% CI = 1.11-2.01, p=0.008). Among participants of African genetic ancestry, HLA-DMA heterozygosity was associated with higher odds of TET2mutant CHIP (pooled OR=1.49, 95% CI = 1.16-1.91, p=0.002), with concordant estimates in both cohorts. A comparable association was not observed in the AMR ancestry groups. Additional ancestry-stratified and less common driver-specific analyses are summarized in Supplementary Table S8.

Secondary HLA meta-analysis across cohort-by-ancestry strata.

We then combined all six cohort-by-ancestry estimates: EUR, AFR, and AMR within the discovery and validation cohorts, to obtain a second overall estimate and assess between-stratum heterogeneity. This analysis complemented the primary ancestry-adjusted analysis by pooling associations estimated separately within ancestry groups. For DNMT3A-specific HLA-DQA1 heterozygosity, the pooled OR was 1.21 (95% CI = 0.97-1.51, p=0.074), with minimal estimated between-stratum heterogeneity (I² =0.54%, Q-test p = 0.52). Estimates for HLA-DOB heterozygosity and DNMT3A-mutant CHIP were directionally consistent across ancestry groups (pooled OR=1.14, 95% CI = 1.04-1.25, p=0.02; I² =0%; no detectable between-stratum heterogeneity). Estimates for HLA-DQB1 heterozygosity and TET2-mutant CHIP were also directionally consistent across cohorts and ancestry groups (pooled OR=0.84, 95% CI = 0.71-1.00, p=0.06). Collectively, these findings identify locus-specific HLA associations with overall CHIP, clone size, and driverdefined CHIP subtypes and suggest that these associations may also vary across genetic ancestry groups.

CHIP and systemic immune-inflammation index (SII).

We next examined systemic inflammation as a phenotypic correlate of CHIP. Eligible laboratory measurements for SII calculation were available for 67.6% of participants in the discovery cohort and 57.0% in the validation cohort. Higher log10-transformed SII was associated with increased odds of CHIP in both the discovery (OR=1.25, 95% CI = 1.07-1.48, p=0.005) and validation (OR=1.39, 95% CI = 1.12-1.71, p=0.002) cohorts (Figure 6, Supplementary Table S9). The pooled OR was 1.30 (95% CI =1.15-1.48, p=4.78e-5).

In gene-specific analyses, higher SII was associated with increased odds of TET2-mutant CHIP (pooled OR=1.40, 95% CI = 1.09-1.79, p=0.01) and PPM1D-mutant CHIP (pooled OR=2.33, 95% CI = 1.25-4.33, p=0.01). Age-stratified analyses showed similar associations among younger adults aged 18-39 years (pooled OR=1.58, 95% CI = 1.11-2.24, p=0.01) and older adults aged ≥40 years (pooled OR=1.21, 95% CI = 1.05-1.39, p=0.01). Associations were also observed for both low-VAF CHIP (pooled OR=1.25, 95% CI = 1.06-1.46, p=0.01) and high-VAF CHIP (pooled OR=1.35, 95% CI = 1.11-1.65, p=0.002).

Inherited genetic determinants of CHIP. Of the 2,593 common variants previously identified 10, 29 as associated with CHIP risk in GWAS analyses, 2,474 were present in our cohort. Overall, 74 variants (Supplementary Table S10) were nominally significant in both cohorts including the TERT variant (rs7705526-A, 5:1285859:C:A) (discovery: OR=1.09, 95% CI = 1.02-1.16, p=0.01; validation: OR=1.25, 95% CI = 1.17-1.34, p=8.66e-11), that had the strongest association from the previous study 10.

Associations between CHIP and prevalent and incident disease outcomes vary by clone size and underlying driver genes.

We evaluated associations between CHIP and prevalent and incident outcomes across three categories: hematologic neoplasms, solid tumors, and other conditions previously linked to CHIP, including cardiovascular disease. We estimated ORs for prevalent disease (Supplementary Table S11) and hazard ratios (HRs) for incident disease (Figure 7, Supplementary Table S11).

Hematologic neoplasms. CHIP was associated with higher incidence of myeloid malignancies (pooled HR=3.54, 95% CI = 2.46-5.09, p=1.16e-11), myeloproliferative neoplasms (pooled HR=3.64, 95% CI = 2.06-6.44, p=8.97e-6), and myelodysplastic syndromes (pooled HR=3.70, 95% CI = 2.20-6.23, p=8.54e-07). These associations were generally higher for CHIP with large clones, including myeloid malignancies (pooled HR=5.97, 95% CI = 3.91-9.12, p<2e-16); myeloproliferative neoplasms, (pooled HR=6.61, 95% CI = 3.41-12.82, p=2.19e-8); and myelodysplastic syndromes (pooled HR=5.63, 95% CI = 3.06-10.35, p=2.71e-08). The associations were highest for CHIP involving high-risk genes: myeloid malignancies (pooled HR=15.71, 95% CI = 9.59-25.74, p<2e-16), myeloproliferative neoplasms (pooled HR=24.16, 95% CI =11.50-50.74, p<2e-16), and myelodysplastic syndromes (pooled HR=14.44, 95% CI = 7.23-28.84, p=4.01e-14). Among young adults, CHIP showed a nominal association with incident hematologic malignancies overall. At gene level, TET2-mutant CHIP was significantly associated with myeloid malignancies (pooled HR=4.06, 95% CI = 2.21-7.45, p=6.45e-6). Solid tumors. For solid tumors, we generally observed positive associations across several incident and prevalent analyses for colorectal, liver, esophageal, lung, stomach, head and neck and kidney cancers. The association between CHIP involving high-risk genes and incident liver cancer remained statistically significant after FDR correction (pooled HR=3.00, 95% CI = 1.52 -5.92, p=0.002), whereas the other solid-tumor associations did not.

Cardiovascular and other diseases. Cardiovascular associations were observed primarily for CHIP with large clones (VAF ≥10%), TET2-mutant CHIP, and CHIP involving high-risk genes. Particularly, TET2-mutant CHIP was associated with heart failure. CHIP involving high-risk genes was associated with higher HRs of ischemic stroke (pooled HR=2.05, 95% CI = 1.30-3.24, p=0.002), stroke (pooled HR = 1.86, 95% CI = 1.18-2.93, p=0.007), and COPD (pooled HR=1.63, 95% CI = 1.09-2.44, p=0.01) and a lower hazard of hypertension (pooled HR=0.70, 95% CI = 0.53-0.93, p=0.02). Stratification by clone size suggested that associations were predominantly driven by high-VAF clones (VAF≥10%) rather than low-VAF clones. Complete results by clone size and driver gene are provided in Supplementary Table S11.

CHIP exhibited age-stratified associations with clinical phenotypes in PheWAS.

To characterize clinical associations more broadly, we performed PheWAS separately among young adults aged 18-39 years (discovery cohort: n=7,337; validation cohort: n=8,637) and adults aged ≥40 years (discovery: n=20,714; validation: n=18,301). Analyses included incident diagnoses documented after biospecimen collection for sequencing. Associations were estimated separately in the discovery and validation cohorts and combined by fixed-effect metaanalysis (Figure 8, Supplementary Table S12).

Among adults aged ≥40 years, 622 phecodes were included in the meta-analysis. The strongest association was with myeloproliferative disease (phecode 200; OR=3.32, 95% CI = 2.21-5.00, p=7.79e-9), which remained significant after Bonferroni correction. Additional nominal associations were observed across hematopoietic and neoplastic phenotypes, including diseases of white blood cells (phecode 288; OR=1.48, 95% CI = 1.12-1.94, p=0.005), pancytopenia (phecode 284.1; OR=1.82, 95% CI = 1.20-2.75, p=0.005), other anemias (phecode 285; OR=1.20, 95% CI = 1.05-1.36, p=0.006), and iron deficiency anemia (phecode 280; OR=1.23, 95% CI = 1.05-1.45, p=0.010). Nominal associations were also observed with benign neoplasms of connective and other soft tissue (phecode 215; OR=1.72, 95% CI = 1.21-2.44, p=0.002), chemotherapy (phecode 197; OR=1.29, 95% CI = 1.09-1.53, p=0.004), and leukemia (phecode 204; OR=1.56, 95% CI = 1.10-2.21, p=0.012).

Outside these categories, nominal associations included cardiomegaly (OR=1.73, 95% CI = 1.19-2.50, p=0.004), chronic renal failure (OR=1.28, 95% CI = 1.07-1.53, p=0.008), and disorders resulting from impaired renal function (OR=1.72, 95% CI = 1.14-2.61, p=0.010). Additional associations were observed with dermatologic conditions, including dyschromia and vitiligo (OR=0.73, 95% CI = 0.59-0.91, p=0.005), as well as disorders of the sense organs, including light-headedness and vertigo (OR=1.21, 95% CI = 1.06-1.37, p=0.005) and hypermetropia (OR=1.85, 95% CI = 1.17-2.92, p=0.009).

Among young adults, only 54 phecodes met the eligibility thresholds for minimum number of participants in both cohorts (see Methods). The strongest observed association was with nontoxic nodular goiter (phecode 241; OR=2.02, 95% CI = 1.20-3.41, p=0.009). Additional associations included several digestive phenotypes, particularly diseases of esophagus (phecode: 530, OR=1.42, 95% CI = 1.03-1.97, p=0.03) including esophagitis, gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), and related diseases. CHIP was also negatively associated with anxiety disorders (phecode: 300, OR=0.67, 95% CI = 0.46-0.97, p=0.03). Other associations included noninflammatory female genital disorders (OR = 1.42, 95% CI = 1.03-1.97, p = 0.03) and cardiac dysrhythmias (OR = 1.39, 95% CI = 1.00-1.93, p = 0.050) (Supplementary Table S12). Together, these findings identify clinical phenotypes associated with CHIP within each age stratum.

Among older adults, associations were predominantly hematologic and neoplastic phenotypes, with additional associations involving genitourinary and dermatologic disorders. In contrast, among younger adults, the nominal associations were predominantly nonhematologic. Given the limited number of phecodes that could be evaluated in younger adults, we also examined phenotypes that showed suggestive associations (p ≤ 0.05) in both age groups. Nontoxic nodular goiter was the only overlapping nominal association in both age groups, captured under related phecodes (young adults: phecode 241; OR = 2.02, 95% CI = 1.20-3.41, p = 0.009, older adults: phecode 241.2; OR = 1.41, 95% CI = 1.05-1.89, p = 0.02). Complete association results are provided in Supplementary Table S12.

Discussion

In this study, we characterized CHIP in adults from the Mount Sinai Million Health Discoveries Program (MSM), a multi-ancestry, EHR-linked hospital cohort spanning adulthood. Substantial representation of African and Admixed American genetic ancestry and adults younger than 40 years enabled examination of CHIP in populations less well-represented in large sequencing studies. Linking genomic data with longitudinal EHRs, we examined the CHIP mutational landscape alongside inherited HLA variation, systemic inflammation, and clinical outcomes. Associations with lower CHIP prevalence among participants of African ancestry, and higher systemic immune-inflammatory index (SII) were observed in both discovery and validation cohorts. Together, these analyses extend the demographic, immune, and clinical characterization of CHIP across adulthood.

CHIP prevalence increased with age in both discovery and validation cohorts. After adjusting for age, participants of African (AFR) ancestry exhibited lower odds of CHIP than participants of European ancestry in both cohorts, and those of Admixed American (AMR) ancestry trended similarly. The latter pattern is consistent with findings from the Mexico City Prospective Study, in which clonal hematopoiesis prevalence was lower than in the UK Biobank, and increased with the proportion of European ancestry within the Mexican cohort 33 . Our findings extend these population-based observations to a clinically ascertained hospital cohort and highlight the importance of ancestry representation when characterizing CHIP prevalence.

Uniquely, the MSM cohort included 15,974 adults aged 18-39 years, an age group that is underrepresented in population biobanks. Their inclusion provided an opportunity to characterize CHIP in early adulthood. CHIP was detected in 646 young adults, corresponding to a combined prevalence of 4.0%. Most young carriers harbored a single mutation, with TET2, DNMT3A, and CUX1 among the most frequently mutated genes. Young-adult prevalence differed between the discovery and validation cohorts, at 5.2% and 3.0%, respectively, compared with ~1-3% observed in other cohorts 8, 10 . Our results suggest that young-adults with CHIP are a subgroup that requires future longitudinal studies of clone persistence, growth, and clinical consequences.

We then asked whether HLA-mediated immunosurveillance impacts CHIP. A recent UK Biobank study found no consistent association between predicted MHC binding of recurrent CH-derived peptides and CH prevalence or clone size, and no protective association of greater HLA diversity 14 . However, that study focused on 40 recurrent variants in 11 genes, excluding TET2, and its heterozygosity analysis evaluated classical HLA loci in participants of European genetic ancestry. Here, we asked whether these findings extend to a broader mutation spectrum and to nonclassical class II loci involved in peptide editing. Towards this end, we examined HLA zygosity across classical and nonclassical loci in the multi-ancestry MSM cohort, evaluating associations with overall CHIP, clone size, and driver-defined subtypes.

Our exploratory analyses identified nominal associations involving several HLA class II loci. HLA-DPA1 heterozygosity was associated with lower odds of overall CHIP, whereas HLA-DQA1 and HLA-DOB heterozygosity was associated with higher odds of DNMT3A-mutant CHIP. These loci encompass both peptide-presenting molecules and regulators of peptide editing. HLA class II molecules are expressed primarily by antigen-presenting cells, where HLA-DQ and HLA-DP present peptides to CD4 T-cells, while HLA-DM facilitates peptide exchange, and HLA-DO regulates HLA-DM activity, helping shape repertoire of presented peptides. These findings span both classical peptide-presenting molecules and nonclassical regulators of peptide editing [34] [35] [36].

As the HLA genotype is inherited, it predates CHIP and can play a role in determining which peptides derived from an acquired mutant clone can be presented to T-cells. The observed differences in the direction of some HLA class II effect estimates between DNMT3A-and TET2mutant CHIP suggest that HLA variation may drive the selection of clones carrying these mutations. Mutations in DNMT3A and TET2 can generate distinct but partly overlapping epigenetic and transcriptional states [37] [38] [39] : DNMT3A catalyzes de novo DNA methylation, whereas TET2 oxidizes 5-methylcytosine as part of the demethylation pathway. These states could alter antigen-processing pathways or the repertoire of available peptides, potentially affecting how mutant clones are recognized within a given HLA background.

A recent experimental study in AML has shown that the DNMT3A-R882H mutation generates an HLA-DQ-restricted neoantigen recognized by a CD4 T-cell clone through specific DQA1/DQB1 combinations 40 . In this study, HLA-DOB heterozygosity was associated with higher odds of DNMT3A-mutant CHIP in both primary and secondary meta-analyses. The similar effect estimates after excluding R882 mutations suggest that the observed DNMT3A-HLA associations may extend beyond this hotspot, motivating investigation of immune recognition across a broader DNMT3A mutation spectrum. Together with our HLA-DQA1 finding, these observations support further investigation of HLA-DQ presentation and class II peptide editing in the recognition and persistence of DNMT3A-mutant clones. Allele-and haplotype-level analyses could clarify whether these associations reflect zygosity itself or particular HLA backgrounds. The exploratory association between HLA-DMA heterozygosity and TET2-mutant CHIP among participants of African genetic ancestry also motivates studying whether these relationships differ across HLA allele and haplotype backgrounds.

Among CHIP carriers, HLA-C heterozygosity was nominally associated with higher odds of a maximum VAF≥20% versus <20%. HLA-C is a class I molecule that participates in both adaptive and innate immune recognition: it presents peptides to CD8+ T-cells and serves as a ligand for killer-cell immunoglobulin-like receptors (KIRs), which regulate natural killer (NK) cell activity [41] [42] [43] . These dual functions suggest examining both T-cell and NK-cell pathways in relation to clone size. Longitudinal studies incorporating HLA-C alleles and KIR genotypes could help determine whether these immune interactions relate to subsequent clone persistence or expansion.

Independent replication and comparisons between driver-defined groups in larger cohorts will be needed to establish the specificity of these exploratory patterns. Allele-and haplotype-level analyses could clarify whether the associations reflect heterozygosity itself or particular HLA backgrounds, while functional studies could test their consequences for antigen presentation and immune recognition.

We also observed higher systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) values associated with CHIP in both cohorts. Because SII is derived from neutrophil, lymphocyte, and platelet counts, it may reflect both systemic inflammation and changes in hematopoietic output. Experimental studies, particularly in models of TET2-deficient hematopoiesis, support a bidirectional relationship between CHIP and inflammation: CHIP-associated mutations can promote inflammatory signaling, while inflammatory stress can favor the survival and expansion of mutant clones 15, 16 . Although our findings are consistent with this relationship, they cannot distinguish whether inflammation precedes clonal expansion, results from altered hematopoiesis, or reflects shared clinical factors. SII may also be influenced by acute illness, medication use, and comorbid conditions 18, 44, 45 . When blood counts and DNA samples are collected at different times, SII may not reflect the inflammatory state at CHIP assessment. Repeated blood counts and sequencing would help clarify how changes in systemic inflammation relate to clone acquisition and expansion over time.

The targeted disease analyses and age-stratified PheWAS identified the clearest clinical associations with hematologic disease. Incident myeloid malignancies, myeloproliferative neoplasms, and myelodysplastic syndromes were associated with CHIP, with higher estimated hazard ratios in analyses of large clones and high-risk driver genes. Myeloproliferative disease also emerged as the strongest PheWAS association among adults aged ≥40 years and remained significant after correction for multiple testing. These findings emphasize the importance of considering clone size and driver mutations when examining the clinical correlates of CHIP. Although participants with hematologic malignancies diagnosed before or within six months after biospecimen collection were excluded, occult or prodromal disease at sampling cannot be fully ruled out.

Associations beyond hematologic disease were more limited after correcting for multiple testing. The targeted analyses nevertheless identified cardiovascular associations in selected CHIP subgroups, including nominal stroke associations involving high-risk genes (TP53, ASXL1, JAK2, U2AF1, SF3B1, SRSF2, IDH1 and IDH2). Thus, the absence of cardiovascular phenotypes among the leading PheWAS findings should be considered alongside the targeted analyses. Differences in phenotype definitions, statistical models, and multiple-testing burden may contribute to the different patterns observed across these analyses. Similarly, nominal associations with anemia and renal phenotypes may reflect several processes, including hematopoietic changes, comorbid disease, and clinical ascertainment. The associations with chronic kidney disease are consistent with emerging human and experimental evidence linking CHIP to kidney-function decline, impaired recovery from acute kidney injury, and renal inflammation and fibrosis 46, 47.

The young-adult PheWAS findings were exploratory. Only 54 phecodes met the eligibility criteria in both cohorts, compared with 622 among adults aged ≥40 years, limiting direct comparison of the clinical patterns across age groups. The nominal associations reported in younger adults provide candidates for further investigation. Evaluating shared phenotypes in larger young-adult populations, with tests of differences by age, would help distinguish age-related variation from differences in statistical power and disease frequency.

Several limitations should be considered. WES at the sequencing depths used here has limited sensitivity for small clones, and differences in capture methods and sequencing coverage may affect comparisons between our discovery and validation cohorts despite using the same variant calling pipeline and thresholds. Tumor-only variant calling may retain residual germline variants or technical artifacts despite population-frequency filters and cohort-specific panels of normals. A single sequencing time point precludes direct measurement of clone growth. HLA alleles were inferred from WES, and locus-level heterozygosity cannot distinguish effects attributable to specific alleles, class II α-β pairings, or linked haplotypes. Testing multiple HLA loci, driverspecific outcomes, VAF thresholds, and ancestry strata creates a substantial multiple-testing burden. Associations supported only at nominal significance should therefore be considered exploratory and require independent replication. Broad genetic ancestry categories may obscure within-group diversity, and incomplete smoking information limits adjustment for this potential confounder. SII was available only for a subset of participants, which may introduce selection related to laboratory testing. It may also reflect hematopoietic output and concurrent clinical conditions in addition to inflammation. Statistical power was lower in young adults, limiting interpretation of age-stratified findings. Finally, hospital-based recruitment and EHRderived phenotypes are subject to selection, encounter-frequency, and diagnostic misclassification biases. Diagnoses recorded after biospecimen collection establish the order of recorded events but may not identify the biological onset of disease, and the diagnostic exclusion window may not fully eliminate occult hematologic disease at sampling.

Overall, this study extends the characterization of CHIP across adulthood, and establishes MSM as a resource for studying CHIP in a multi-ancestry hospital population, including younger adults. The findings reproduce the established relationships of CHIP prevalence and clone size with age, identify ancestry-associated differences in prevalence, and characterize inflammatory and clinical correlates of CHIP. Exploratory HLA associations suggest that inherited HLA variation and systemic inflammation represent complementary components of the host immune context associated with CHIP. The locus-, clone size-, driver-, and ancestry-dependent HLA patterns are consistent with a model in which immune selection contributes to CHIP heterogeneity rather than exerting a uniform effect across clones. Longitudinal clone tracking, HLA haplotype analyses across ancestry groups, functional antigen-presentation studies, and targeted clinical outcome analyses will clarify the biological relevance of these associations and their implications for clonal evolution and disease risk.
