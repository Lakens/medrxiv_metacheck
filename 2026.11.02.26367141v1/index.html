What we found

We found 2 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

The double burden of malnutrition (DBM), characterised by coexisting undernutrition and overnutrition, remains a public health concern among adolescents especially in low-and middle-income countries.

Adolescents in Nepal are nutritionally vulnerable, yet evidence on the coexistence of stunting and overweight/obesity is limited. This study assessed the prevalence and associated factors of DBM among school-going adolescents in urban Kathmandu Valley, Nepal.

A school-based cross-sectional study was conducted to assess factors associated with the double burden of malnutrition among adolescents in Kathmandu Valley, Nepal from 25 th April, 2022 up to 25 th July, 2025.

A multi-stage cluster random sampling technique, using the Probability Proportional to Size method, was employed to ensure the selection of the desired number of participants. Students from grades 8 to 10 were randomly selected for face-to-face interviews and anthropometric measurements (height and weight were collected to calculate Body Mass Index (BMI)-for-age Z-scores (BAZ)), using tools adapted to the local context. Bivariate and multivariable models examined the associations between the dependent and independent variables.

This study revealed that 15.3% of the students were stunted, 5.5% were underweight, 16.9% were overweight/obese, and 3% of students with the coexistence of DBM.

In the fully adjusted model, the odds of stunting were significantly higher among adolescents aged 15-19 years (aOR = 2.03, 95% CI: 1.35-3.06), salty-snack consumers (aOR = 1.53, 95% CI: 1.00-2.36), those with low nutritional knowledge (aOR = 3.17, 95% CI: 2.17-4.66), and low diet quality (aOR = 7.27, 95% CI: 4.96-10.65). Conversely, fatty-food consumption was associated with lower odds of stunting (aOR = 0.65, 95% CI: 0.42-1.00). For overweight/obesity, older adolescents had significantly lower odds (aOR = 0.49, 95% CI: 0.35-0.67). Regarding the double burden of malnutrition (DBM), low nutritional knowledge (aOR = 2.16, 95% CI: 0.99-4.71) and low diet quality (aOR = 5.27, 95% CI: 2.42-11.48) were significantly associated with higher odds.

The double burden of malnutrition exists among adolescents in urban Kathmandu Valley, highlighting a critical public health challenge. Socio-demographic, dietary, and behavioral factors significantly influence its prevalence. Comprehensive school-and community-based interventions are crucial for addressing this dual nutritional burden.

Introduction

Over the past three decades, malnutrition and its various forms have emerged as a significant challenge to the growing population drawing attention from the global health community 1 . The double burden of malnutrition (DBM) refers to the coexistence of undernutrition (such as stunting, underweight) and overnutrition (overweight or obesity) within the same individual, household, or population level, which is common in low and middle-income countries (LMICs). In adolescents, this often reflects the combined impact of poor dietary quality, rapid nutrition transition, epigenetics, and lifestyle changes during a critical period of growth and development 2 3.

Globally, an estimated 390 million children and adolescents aged 5-19 years are overweight, including 160 million living with obesity, while approximately 190 million experience underweight, defined as a BMI-for-age more than two standard deviations below the reference median. In 2022, around 149 million children under 5 years were stunted, and 37 million were overweight or obese. Nearly half of all deaths among children under 5 are attributed to undernutrition, with the majority occurring in low-and middleincome countries 3 . The prevalence of overweight and obesity among children and adolescents, aged 5-19 years has increased rapidly from 4% in 1975 to 18% in 2016 4 . Adding to this, a systematic review revealed the prevalence of greater than 14% for overweight/obesity in adolescents 5 . One in three persons residing in Low-and middle-income countries (LMICs) is affected by the DBM 6 7 , resulting in the global nutrition transition from childhood up to adolescence 8 . A study conducted in 57 LMICs reported that 10.2% of adolescents were stunted, 5.5% underweight, 21.4% overweight/obese 1 . By the 2010s, approximately 38 % of the 126 LMICs experienced DBM with a notably high prevalence in sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, East Asia, and the Pacific 5 . Further, the evidence revealed that 2% of school going adolescents in South Asian countries showed the prevalence of concurrent stunting and overnutrition 9.

The DBM was mostly prevalent among mother-child pairs, children under five, and women of reproductive age 10 . Additionally, the prevalence of DBM among mother-child pairs was 6.6% in Nepal 11 affecting overall health system, future economic growth, and human well-being 8 12 . With around a quarter of the population (24%) are adolescents in Nepal 13 ,the Global School-Based Student Health Survey (GSHS) Nepal 2015 report shows that 6.7% of adolescent students were overweight, while 0.6% were obese 12.

Adding to this, the Nepal National Micronutrient Survey (NMSS), 2016, showed that 32.0% of the boys and 32.1% of the girls among adolescents were stunted 14 . The prevalence of overweight ranged from 4-5% among both boys and girls, whilst obesity was below one percent among both sexes 15 . Addressing the nutrition needs of adolescents is essential to break the vicious cycle of intergenerational malnutrition, and chronic diseases 16.

Different anthropometric indicators provide insight into nutritional deficiencies. Stunting is widely recognized as a marker of chronic undernutrition, reflecting long-term health consequences, while obesity indicates excess nutrition and an increased risk of non-communicable diseases such as type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease 17 18 . Wasting is more indicative of acute malnutrition driven by recent dietary intake and illness 3 . Adolescent's higher nutritional needs, their rapid physical and physiological development puts them at a higher risk of malnutrition 11 . Additionally, the DBM poses several long-term complications, especially if developed during childhood and adolescence, as it increases the risk of diverse chronic non-communicable diseases (NCDs), whose development is closely associated with dietary habits and lifestyle factors 5 . With a lower mortality rate than any other age group and a relatively low morbidity rate, adolescents are generally not a priority for public health interventions 10 . By 2030, an estimated 150 million individuals aged 10-19 years worldwide are expected to be living with obesity 19 . In children and adolescents, obesity is a chronic health condition arising from a multifaceted interaction of physiological, psychological, environmental, socioeconomic, and genetic influences 20.

The growing burden of DBM among adolescents is largely attributed to the nutrition transition-shift in dietary habits and lifestyles driven by urbanization and economic development. It includes increased consumption of ultra-processed foods high in sugar, salt, trans-fats, and calories, alongside decreased intake of nutrient dense foods and reduced physical activity. It has led to shift in nutritional outcomes from undernutrition to overnutrition and now double burden of under and over nutrition 11 18 . These ultraprocessed foods are likely to be cheaper and easily available but of poor nutritional quality, exacerbating both under and over nutrition risks of malnutrition 18 . Rapid economic transformation, urbanization, cultural expectations for body size and sedentary behavior are some of the key drivers of DBM in this population 21.

Government of Nepal has implemented various plans, policies, and programs to reduce adolescent malnutrition like Multi-Sector Nutrition Plan II 22 , and school health nutrition program 23 . However, many efforts often prioritize focus on undernutrition among children under five and women of reproductive age, leaving adolescents relatively underserved. There are also implementation challenges, as the limited cross sector collaboration, insufficient data on adolescent malnutrition, particularly regarding DBM.

Despite the growing burden and significant long-term health risks of the DBM, research among adolescents in Nepal remains limited. Existing research primarily addresses household or population level DBM or focuses on younger children and mother 24 25 . To address this gap, this study aims to assess the prevalence and determinants of DBM-defined as concurrent stunting and overweight -among adolescents in urban Kathmandu Valley, contributing to provide evidence needed to improve the adolescent nutrition policy and programing in Nepal.

Materials and Methods

Study design, sites, and participants

A school-based cross-sectional study design was used to assess the factors associated with DBM among adolescents in Kathmandu Valley, Nepal. Kathmandu Valley is the central lower part standing at 1,425 meters (4,675 ft) above sea level. It consists of three districts (Kathmandu, Lalitpur, and Bhaktapur) of Bagmati Province and is the most developed as well as the largest urban agglomeration of Nepal 26 with a total population of 6, 116,866 27 and a total area of 933.73 square kilometers (360.52 sq mi), with an annual population growth rate of 0.97% 27.

The study was conducted in schools of Kathmandu, Lalitpur, and Bhaktapur districts from 25 th April, 2022 up to 25 th July, 2025. There were 1,362 schools in Kathmandu, 475 in Lalitpur, and 344 in Bhaktapur.

Among the total schools, 616 schools were community schools, and the remaining 1,565 schools were institutional 28 . Adolescent students, both male and female, aged 10-19 years studying in the selected schools of Kathmandu Valley were the study population for this study. Details on study sites have been presented on the map (Fig. 1). The map was created using ArcGIS Pro (version 3.4). The base files of the administrative provinces and districts of Nepal were obtained from the Government of Nepal, Ministry of Land Management, and Natural Earth, which are publicly available without restrictions (http://dos.gov.np/nepal-map).

Although most students in grades 8-10 typically fall between the ages of 12 and 17, the study included all enrolled students aged 10-19 to align with the WHO definition of adolescence and to capture any students who may have started school earlier or experienced grade repetition. This broader age range allowed for a comprehensive assessment of nutritional status across the full spectrum of adolescence. Both males and females aged 10-19 years studying grade 8-10 class and those who gave consent to participate in the study were included in the study, whereas adolescents who cannot be traced during the data collection stage and who are severely ill at the time of data collection were excluded.

Sample size and sampling technique

The sample was determined using a single proportional formula with a conservative prevalence of 0.5 considering key indicators. Taking the standard normal deviation (Z) at 95% confidence level, and 4% margin of error (d), the minimum sample size was calculated using the formula: Sample size (n0) = Z2 ×p (1-p)/d2, where Z = 1.96 (for 95% confidence), p = 0.5 (conservative estimate), and d = 0.04. This yielded a calculated sample size (n₀) of 600. After adjusting for a 10% non-response rate and a design effect of 2, the final total sample size was 1,320. A multi-stage random sampling technique was used to select participants for this study. First, three wards from each district (Kathmandu, Lalitpur, Bhaktapur) were randomly selected. Next, all secondary schools (grades 8-10) within these wards were listed, and eight schools were chosen to ensure representation from both public and private institutions. Specifically, one public school was selected from Kathmandu, two public and two private schools from Lalitpur, and two public and one private school from Bhaktapur. In the third stage, students from grades 8-10 in these schools were selected using Probability Proportional to Size (PPS) sampling. Finally, simple random sampling was employed to select students from the list of eligible adolescents in the chosen schools.

Data collection tools and techniques

Data collection tool

Semi-structured Questionnaire: The socio-demographic information was collected using a semistructured questionnaire adapted from the global school-based student health survey (GSHS) questionnaire 12.

Behavior factors: The questionnaire on smoking, alcohol, drug abuse, and sedentary behavior was also adapted from the GSHS questionnaires 12 . These questions asked if the participants had consumed any alcohol, drugs, or smoking in the last 30 days and the frequency of consumption 12.

Physical activity:

The physical activity questionnaire was adapted from the Nepalese version of the GSHS questionnaire mode 12.

Dietary Habit: Dietary habits were assessed by adopting GSHS questionnaire modules developed by the WHO 12 . The information was collected to assess the dietary habits of adolescents on consumption of different types of foods (fruits consumption per day, vegetables consumption, food with high fats), frequency of junk food consumption, carbonated soft drinks (Coke, Pepsi, Sprite), fast food (Momo; a popular Nepali dumpling, Pizza; a traditional Nepali recipe made of rice flour etc.) consumed in last 30 days from the date of data collection.

Nutritional knowledge:

Nutritional knowledge level was determined based on the eighteen questions (Q1-Q18). A nutrition questionnaire developed in Polish conditions and education based on the Polish Food Guide Pyramid was adapted and contextualised into Nepalese terms 29 . The score '1' was given for correct answers, and a '0' was given for wrong or unknown answers and missing data. Respondents were divided into three nutritional knowledge levels: lowest (0-4 points), moderately low (5-7 points), and higher (8-10 points). This tool has already been utilized and implemented previously in Nepal 30.

Diet quality: Diet quality was assessed through 11 food-frequency questions focused on both healthy (e.g., fruits, dairy, vegetables) and unhealthy (e.g., soft drinks, fast food) items. Two indices were generated: the pro-Healthy Diet Index (pHDI) and non-Healthy Diet Index (nHDI), based on consumption frequency.

Final diet quality was categorised as low, moderate, or high following established scoring cut-offs on the basis of usual food frequency consumption within the last 12 months 31 and has already been implemented in previous research in Nepal with similar settings 30.

Anthropometric measurement:

Height was measured using a stadiometer to the nearest 1cm, whereas participants' weight was measured using the Seca weighing scale to the nearest 0.1kg.

Data collection techniques

The questionnaire was developed, translated into Nepali, and validated by language experts. Back translation was done before pre-testing, which involved 10% of the study sample and was subsequently adjusted. Face-to-face interviews and anthropometric measurements were conducted using adapted tools in the local context. Research assistants were trained in data collection, sampling, and ethical guidelines.

Piloting ensured data consistency, with field supervisors overseeing the process. Semi-structured questionnaires gathered socio-demographic, behavioral, and nutritional data. Apart from Face-to-face interviews, an anthropometric and nutrition survey was conducted to assess the adolescents' nutritional status utilizing the height-for-age (HAZ) and BMI-for-age (BMIZ) indices as Zscores 31 . Validity and reliability were ensured using a random sampling process at each stage to ensure representative participants and was maintained throughout data collection, entry, and analysis.

Study variables

Dependent variables

The primary dependent variables were undernutrition (stunting and underweight), overnutrition (overweight and obesity), and the DBM, coexistence of undernutrition (stunting) and overnutrition (overweight/obesity).

Stunting was defined as height-for-age Z score (HAZ) < -2SD, and underweight was defined as BMI-forage Z score (BAZ) < -2SD 32 . Overweight was defined as BAZ > +1SD and obesity as BAZ > +2SD, following WHO growth reference standards 2006 for children and adolescents aged 5-19 years 33.

Anthropometric z-scores (HAZ and BAZ) were calculated using the WHO AnthroPlus software 34.

Overweight and obesity were grouped together in analysis due to the relatively low prevalence of obesity alone, which would have limited statistical power for separate modelling. This approach is commonly used in similar studies examining adolescent nutritional status in LMICs 9 35 . The double burden of malnutrition was operationalized as the concurrent presence of stunting (HAZ < -2SD) and overweight/obesity (BAZ > +1SD) 36 . For analysis, this co-occurrence was analyzed as a binary outcome, coded as "Yes" if both stunting and overweight/obesity were present and "No" if this combination was absent. This approach, adapted from prior study 37 , allowed us to clearly distinguish adolescents experiencing DBM from those with only one or neither form of malnutrition.

Independent variables

The independent variables were categorized into three domains: socio-demographic factors (age, sex, grade, caste, religion, family type, parents' education level, and parents' occupation), behavioral factors (physical activity, number of physical education class days, and sedentary behavior), and dietary patterns (intake of seasonal fruits and vegetables, carbonated drinks, fast food, salty food, and fatty food, along with nutritional knowledge and overall diet quality). These variables were included based on their established associations with adolescent nutritional status in previous literature and their biological plausibility in influencing stunting, underweight, overweight/obesity, and the double burden of malnutrition 38 39 . Variables were categorized, and in some cases merged, to ensure meaningful interpretation and address sparse data in individual categories, thereby improving the robustness of statistical analysis.

Data management and analysis

The collected data were entered into EpiData version 3.0 by four trained data entry professionals and data was analyzed using Stata/MP version 14.1 (StataCorp LP, College Station, Texas). Frequency, and percentage were calculated using descriptive statistics. Although BMI-for-age and height-for-age z-scores were collected as continuous variables, we categorized them using WHO cut-offs to define underweight, stunting, overweight, and obesity. The co-occurrence was treated as a binary outcome ("Yes" if both stunting and overweight/obesity were present, "No" otherwise) to ensure clear classification and comparability with previous literatures 40 41 . Using a binary outcome also facilitates binary logistic regression analysis, allowing for the estimation of odds ratios for factors associated with DBM, and provides a straightforward interpretation for public health planning and policy. Multivariable logistic regressions assessed the association between predictors and outcome variables. Three adjusted models were constructed to evaluate the associations. Model 1 was adjusted for demographic factors, Model 2 for behavioral and dietary factors, and Model 3 for demographic, behavioral, and dietary factors. All behavioral and dietary pattern variables were included regardless of their bivariate significance, as they are biologically and theoretically important contributors to adolescent nutritional status and help control for potential confounding. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF) to prevent statistical bias, and model fit was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. The mean value of VIF < 10 was the cut-off point 43 . Crude odds ratio (cOR) and adjusted odds ratio (aOR) with 95% CIs were calculated to assess the presence and strength of association, with a p < 0.05 used for the determination of statistical significance.

Ethical aspects

Approval for this study was sought from the Nepal Health Research Council (Protocol registration number: 144\2022p). In addition, we obtained formal written permission letters from each participating school to conduct the study. Before data collection, permission was obtained from schools, written informed consent from guardians, and written assent from participants. The study's purpose was clearly explained to all. No personal identifying information was collected, participation was voluntary, and confidentiality was strictly maintained throughout the study.

Patient and Public involvement

Patient and public weren't involved in design, implementation, reporting and dissemination plans of this research.

Results

The findings revealed that 15.3% of the students were stunted, 5.5% were underweight, and 16.9% were overweight/obese. Additionally, the coexistence of DBM was observed in 3.0% of the students (Table 1). In terms of socio-demographic characteristics, male adolescents slightly outnumbered females (50.3% vs. 49.7%). More than half (54.9%) were aged 15-19 years, and the majority were from disadvantaged ethnic groups (60.5%). The majority (86.8%) identified as Hindu.

Regarding physical activity, 71.6% of adolescents led a sedentary lifestyle, although a similar proportion (71.22%) reported walking to and from school. In terms of dietary habits, more than half consumed seasonal fruits (64.43%) and vegetables (79.31%). A significant majority reported never consuming carbonated drinks (71.83%), while a little over half reported the same for fast food (55.82%) and fatty foods (55.36%).

More than two-thirds had high nutritional knowledge (69.03%), and a substantial majority had a highquality diet (79%), yet almost one-fourth (24.47%) had experienced hunger due to food shortages (Table 1).

Discussion

The present study provides evidence on the burden and correlates of malnutrition among urban schoolgoing adolescents in the Kathmandu Valley of Nepal, highlighting the coexistence of undernutrition and overweight/obesity within the same population. Overall, 15.3% of adolescents were stunted, 5.5% were underweight, 16.9% were overweight/obese, and 3.0% experienced the coexistence of the DBM. This study explored the prevalence of DBM and its determinants among school-going adolescents within the Kathmandu Valley of Nepal and found a DBM prevalence of 3.0%, which is less than half of what has been observed in the secondary data analysis of the Nepal Demographic and Health Survey, 2016 13 and study of Sunuwar et al. 44 Although there are no known primary studies specifically assessing DBM among adolescents in Nepal, comparable findings have been observed in a large scale analysis of 57 LMICs adolescents in South Asian countries' DBM where the prevalence of concurrent stunting and overweight-DBM was 2% 9 , which is tenfold of what was observed among Brazilian adolescents where it was only 0.2% 16 . This slightly higher prevalence could be attributed to dietary shifts, sedentary lifestyle and socioeconomic changes specific to the Kathmandu Valley, Nepal 45.

A particularly important finding of this study was the strong association between age and stunting.

Adolescents aged 15-19 years had approximately twice the odds of being stunted compared with those aged 10-14 years (AOR = 2.03, 95% CI: 1.35-3.06; p<0.001). This finding is consistent with evidence from South Asian adolescents showing a higher prevalence of stunting during late adolescence 46, 47 and with findings from Ethiopia reporting greater odds of stunting among older adolescents 16 . The higher odds observed among older adolescents should, however, not necessarily be interpreted as indicating that older age itself causes stunting. Stunting reflects cumulative growth faltering and may therefore represent nutritional disadvantages experienced earlier in the life course. In addition, differences in pubertal timing and the increased nutritional requirements associated with adolescent growth may influence anthropometric status during this period. The finding consequently highlights the importance of addressing nutritional vulnerability before and throughout adolescence rather than viewing adolescent stunting solely as a problem emerging during late adolescence.

The strongest modifiable correlates of stunting in the fully adjusted model were observed to be diet quality, and nutritional knowledge. Adolescents with low diet quality had more than seven times higher odds of stunting than those with high diet quality (AOR = 7.27, 95% CI: 4.96-10.65; p<0.001), representing the strongest association identified in the stunting model. Similarly, adolescents with low nutritional knowledge had more than three times higher odds of stunting (AOR = 3.17, 95% CI: 2.17-4.66; p<0.001). These findings are consistent with evidence from Nepal and other settings indicating an association between inadequate nutritional knowledge, poorer dietary practices, and adolescent undernutrition 48 49 . Dietary quality is particularly important during adolescence because rapid growth increases requirements for energy, protein, and micronutrients. Diets characterized by limited diversity or inadequate nutritional quality may therefore be insufficient to support optimal linear growth. Previous evidence has similarly linked greater dietary diversity and improved diet quality with better nutritional outcomes among adolescents 50 51 52.

The strong association between nutritional knowledge and stunting may reflect the potential role of knowledge in shaping food choices and dietary practices. Adolescents with greater nutrition knowledge may be more likely to recognize nutrient-dense foods and adopt healthier dietary practices 53 . However, knowledge alone is unlikely to determine nutritional status. The ability to translate knowledge into dietary behavior may depend on household food availability and affordability, parental practices, school food environments, and broader socioeconomic conditions. Thus, the observed association supports an integrated approach in which nutrition education is combined with improvements in the food environment and access to diverse and nutritious foods.

Consumption of salty snacks was also associated with higher odds of stunting in the fully adjusted model (AOR = 1.53, 95% CI: 1.00-2.36; p=0.031). Although the magnitude of this association was substantially smaller than that observed for diet quality and nutritional knowledge, it may indicate the influence of broader dietary patterns among adolescents. Frequent consumption of energy-dense, nutrient-poor snack foods may displace nutrient-dense foods within the diet and may therefore be associated with poorer overall dietary adequacy. This finding also reinforces the importance of considering the adolescent food environment, particularly the availability and consumption of commercially prepared snack foods around schools. However, because the present study did not establish temporality, the observed association should be interpreted as correlational rather than causal.

An inverse association was also observed between fatty-food consumption and stunting (AOR = 0.65, 95% CI: 0.42-1.00; p=0.049). This finding should be interpreted cautiously. The category of "fatty foods" may include foods with substantially different nutritional profiles, ranging from nutrient-rich sources of dietary fat to fried or processed foods. Consequently, the observed inverse association may reflect differences in the types of foods included in this category, overall dietary patterns, or residual confounding rather than a protective effect of fatty-food consumption itself. A study done among school age children in North Lebanon found the association of double burden of malnutrition with unhealthy dietary behavior 54 . Further research using more detailed dietary assessment would be required to clarify this relationship.

In contrast to the strong associations observed for stunting, the fully adjusted analysis of body-weight status identified considerably fewer independent correlates. The prevalence of overweight/obesity was 16.9%, demonstrating that excess weight is also an important nutritional concern among adolescents in the Kathmandu Valley. The observed age-related pattern is broadly consistent with evidence from China and South Asian populations indicating differences in overweight/obesity prevalence across adolescent age groups 9 55 . Such differences may reflect changes in growth, physical activity, dietary behavior, and body composition during adolescence. Nevertheless, interpretation of the regression finding should remain closely aligned with the exact coding of the dependent variable and the final adjusted model. Importantly, the relatively high prevalence of overweight/obesity alongside stunting in the same population illustrates the coexistence of different forms of nutritional vulnerability during adolescence.

The finding of a 3.0% prevalence of DBM is particularly important because it demonstrates that undernutrition and excess weight are not necessarily separate public health problems within this population.

Rather, they may coexist within the same nutritional and social environment. The fully adjusted model identified low diet quality as the strongest correlate of DBM. Adolescents with low diet quality had more than five times higher odds of experiencing the DBM compared with those with high diet quality (AOR = 5.27, 95% CI: 2.42-11.48; p<0.001). Low nutritional knowledge was also associated with approximately two-fold higher odds of DBM (AOR = 2.16, 95% CI: 0.99-4.71; p=0.041), consistent with the study done among adolescents in Zimbabwe 56 . Together, these findings suggest that the double burden may be linked to common underlying dietary and knowledge-related factors rather than representing two entirely independent nutritional problems.

The strong association between poor diet quality and DBM is particularly relevant in the context of the nutrition transition. A diet may provide sufficient or excessive energy while remaining inadequate in essential nutrients, particularly when consumption of energy-dense foods occurs alongside inadequate dietary diversity. Such dietary patterns may create conditions in which undernutrition and excess weight coexist. The present findings therefore support a broader conceptualization of adolescent nutrition in which the quality and composition of the diet are considered alongside total energy intake. Further, the association between low nutritional knowledge and DBM suggests that adolescents' understanding of healthy nutrition may be relevant to the emerging double burden. However, as with stunting, nutrition knowledge should not be considered an isolated determinant. Adolescents make food choices within environments shaped by household resources, food prices, availability, marketing, school food environments, and prevailing social norms.

An integrated strategy combining nutrition education with supportive school and household food environments may therefore be more appropriate for addressing the multiple forms of malnutrition observed in this population.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish temporality or causal relationships between dietary, behavioral, and socioeconomic factors and nutritional outcomes. In particular, reverse causality cannot be excluded, as existing nutritional status may itself influence dietary practices and behaviors. Second, dietary and behavioral information was collected using interviewer-administered questionnaires and may therefore be subject to recall and social desirability bias. Third, the study was restricted to urban, school-going adolescents in the Kathmandu Valley, limiting the generalizability of the findings to adolescents who are out of school and those living in rural or geographically different areas of Nepal. In addition, the use of fixed anthropometric classifications across a heterogeneous adolescent age range may not fully capture individual variation in pubertal growth and may contribute to some degree of nutritional-status misclassification.

Despite these limitations, the study has several strengths. It included a relatively large sample and used a multistage cluster sampling approach with probability proportional to size, which strengthens the representativeness of the sampled school-going adolescent population within the study setting.

Standardized anthropometric measurements based on WHO growth references also enhance comparability with other studies. Furthermore, the inclusion of demographic, behavioral, dietary, and nutritionalknowledge variables allowed examination of multiple potential correlates of adolescent malnutrition within a common analytical framework. Multivariable logistic regression enabled adjustment for several measured covariates, thereby reducing the potential influence of measured confounding.

Conclusion

Overall, this study highlights the complexity of adolescent malnutrition in urban Nepal. While stunting and overweight/obesity remain important and seemingly contrasting nutritional concerns, the coexistence of these conditions demonstrates the emergence of a broader double burden. The particularly strong associations of low diet quality and low nutritional knowledge with stunting and DBM suggest that improving the quality of adolescents' diets, together with creating supportive environments that enable healthier food choices, may be central to addressing this challenge. Longitudinal and nationally representative studies are needed to further clarify the pathways linking dietary practices, socioeconomic conditions, pubertal development, and nutritional outcomes across adolescence and to evaluate integrated interventions capable of addressing multiple forms of malnutrition simultaneously.
