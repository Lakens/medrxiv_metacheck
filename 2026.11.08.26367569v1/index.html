Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Full text

Abstract

Objective. To examine temporal trends in in-hospital mortality among patients hospitalized with heart failure, ischemic heart disease, and cerebrovascular disease in Suriname from 2013 to 2022, and to assess differences according to age, sex, and ethnicity.

Conducted a retrospective hospital-based observational study using routinely collected discharge data from three major public hospitals in Suriname. These hospitals accounted for approximately 71.8% of national hospital admissions. Admissions with a primary diagnosis of HF (ICD-10 I50), IHD (I20-I25), or Stroke (I60-I69) were included. The primary outcome was inhospital mortality. Separate multivariable logistic regression models were fitted for each disease, adjusting for calendar year, age, sex, and ethnicity.

Results. Among 84,691 admissions, 42,301 were IHD, 24,584 for Stroke, and 17,806 for HF. Inhospital mortality declined significantly across all three conditions, with adjusted odds decreasing by 14% annually for HF (aOR 0.858; 95% CI 0.841-0.875), 6% for IHD (aOR 0.941; 95% CI 0.914-0.969), and 5% for Stroke (aOR 0.947; 95% CI 0.931-0.963). Increasing age was associated with higher mortality in IHD and Stroke. Female sex was associated with lower mortality in HF (aOR 0.835; 95% CI 0.744-0.937). Ethnic disparities were most pronounced for Stroke, with higher mortality among Maroon, Creole, Javanese, and Other groups than Hindustani patients.

In-hospital mortality declined across cardiovascular conditions, but improvements were heterogeneous. Pronounced ethnic inequalities in Stroke mortality and age-related gradients highlight the need for equity-focused cardiovascular surveillance and provide a baseline for evaluating future health-system and prevention initiatives in Suriname.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases remain the leading cause of death globally, accounting for approximately 17.9 million deaths annually. Although age-standardized cardiovascular mortality has declined in many high-income countries, the absolute burden continues to increase in low-and middleincome countries (LMICs), driven by population growth, aging, and persistent exposure to preventable risk factors (1) (2) (3) The Caribbean Transition. The Caribbean region faces a distinct epidemiologic profile. Rapid urbanization, dietary shifts toward processed foods, declining physical activity, and rising obesity prevalence have driven accelerating non-communicable disease burden (4-7). Unlike high-income countries with established cardiovascular prevention systems, many Caribbean nations are navigating the early phase of cardiovascular transition-where disease incidence is rising faster than healthcare system capacity can accommodate (8). Additionally, the Caribbean's multiethnic populations (South Asian, African, indigenous, and mixed ancestry) have historically exhibited differential cardiovascular risk, yet longitudinal data on whether health systems are reducing this inequity remain scarce (9).

Policy Context: The HEARTS Initiative as a Timely Response

Suriname exemplifies Caribbean health system challenges. Primary healthcare is delivered through fragmented public (Regional Health Services, Medical Mission) and private sectors, with no unified electronic health record, variable resource allocation, and limited real-time surveillance of acute cardiovascular outcomes (10). Like many Caribbean nations, Suriname faces chronic shortages in cardiovascular specialists, inconsistent access to evidence-based medications, and competing priorities across a resource-constrained system (7).

In December 2021, Suriname's Ministry of Health officially adopted the HEARTS in the Americas Initiative-a WHO-endorsed primary healthcare strengthening program targeting hypertension detection and control (11). HEARTS focuses on six core components: healthy-lifestyle counseling, evidence-based treatment protocols, access to essential medicines, risk-based management, team-based care, and systems monitoring and evaluation (12, 13). Operational implementation began in 2023, with the goal of achieving population-level blood pressure control rates ≥50% by 2025 (14) . However, the baseline health system performance on acute cardiovascular mortalitythe ultimate indicator of prevention effectiveness-had not been systematically evaluated. Baseline data are essential for (1) establishing targets against which to measure HEARTS impact, (2) identifying which population groups are at highest risk and require targeted interventions, (3) determining whether HEARTS should be coupled with acute care strengthening strategies, and (4) planning cardiovascular service capacity over the next decade.

This study therefore evaluates health system performance on cardiovascular mortality in the immediate pre-implementation period (2013-2022), providing a baseline against which to measure HEARTS impact and identifying equity gaps that prevention alone may not resolve.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a retrospective hospital-based observational cohort study using routinely collected hospital discharge data from three major public hospitals in Suriname: Academic Hospital Paramaribo, 's Lands Hospital, and Mungra Medical Center. The study period extended from January 1, 2013, through December 31, 2022. Together, the participating hospitals accounted for approximately 71.8% of national hospital admissions and provide secondary and tertiary care to populations from different geographic areas of Suriname. The study was based on routinely collected administrative hospital data. The unit of analysis was the hospital admission rather than the individual patient.

Study population

All hospital admissions with a primary discharge diagnosis of Hearts Failure (HF), Ischemic Hearts Disease (IHD), or Stroke during the study period were eligible for inclusion. HF was identified using ICD-10 code I50, IHD using codes I20-I25, and Stroke using codes I60-I69. The diagnostic categories were analyzed separately because they represent clinically heterogeneous conditions with different patterns of presentation and prognosis.

Diagnostic classification

IHD (I20-I25) included ischemic heart disease diagnoses recorded in the hospital discharge data, including angina, acute myocardial infarction, and other ischemic heart disease diagnoses. Stroke (I60-I69) included cerebrovascular diagnoses recorded in the discharge data, including hemorrhagic and ischemic cerebrovascular conditions, transient ischemic attack, and late effects of cerebrovascular disease. HF (I50) included heart failure diagnoses recorded in the discharge data, including acute and chronic heart failure presentations. The administrative nature of the dataset means that the clinical specificity of individual diagnoses was dependent on the quality and completeness of ICD-10 coding.

Outcome

The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality, defined as death occurring during the index hospitalization. For descriptive analyses, the crude case-fatality proportion was calculated as the number of admissions resulting in death divided by the total number of admissions within each diagnostic and demographic category. In-hospital mortality reflects short-term mortality among hospitalized patients and should not be interpreted as population-level mortality, disease-specific incidence, or long-term survival.

Demographic variables

The principal demographic variables were age, sex, and ethnicity. Age was analyzed as a continuous variable in years. Sex was categorized as male or female according to the hospital administrative record. Ethnicity was categorized into five population groups routinely recorded in hospital administrative data: Hindustani, Maroon, Creole, Javanese, and Other. Hindustani patients were used as the reference category in regression models. Ethnicity was treated as a social and population characteristic rather than a biological determinant. The categories may reflect differences in social, cultural, geographic, and structural circumstances. Because ethnicity was obtained from routinely collected administrative records, misclassification or inconsistencies in classification cannot be excluded.

Length of hospital stay

Length of hospital stay (LOS) was measured in days. Because LOS is determined after admission and may be influenced by disease severity, treatment, discharge practices, and death during hospitalization, it was not included as a primary explanatory variable in the main mortality models. Its association with mortality was examined separately. This distinction is important because early death may itself result in a shorter observed hospital stay, creating potential reverse causation and survivor bias.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize admissions according to diagnosis, sex, and ethnicity. Crude case-fatality proportions were calculated overall and by ethnic group. Temporal trends in in-hospital mortality were examined using separate multivariable logistic regression models for HF, IHD, and Stroke. In each model, in-hospital mortality was the binary outcome. Calendar year was modeled continuously. The resulting adjusted odds ratio represents the change in the odds of in-hospital mortality associated with each additional calendar year. An aOR below 1.00 therefore indicates declining odds of mortality over time; the approximate annual percentage decline in odds was calculated as (1 -aOR) × 100.

Age, sex, and ethnicity were included as covariates. Male sex and Hindustani ethnicity were used as reference categories. Interaction terms between calendar year and demographic variables were evaluated to assess whether temporal mortality trends differed across demographic groups. Interaction terms were retained when statistically significant and when they contributed meaningfully to model fit, according to the prespecified criteria in the analytical framework.

Model fit was assessed using McFadden and Nagelkerke pseudo-R² statistics. The generally low explanatory power of the models was considered when interpreting the findings because the administrative dataset did not contain several clinically important determinants of mortality, including disease severity, comorbidities, vital signs, laboratory findings, imaging findings, treatment, and time to presentation.

All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics and JASP. Statistical tests were two-sided, with p<0.05 considered statistically significant. Gemini AI was used to create figures.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with applicable national regulations and institutional requirements governing the use of routinely collected health data. Ethical oversight was provided by the relevant national review body under approval letter CMWO-14, dated March 25, 2026. The study used retrospectively collected, anonymized hospital data, and the requirement for informed consent was waived in accordance with applicable national legislation and institutional policy.

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

Between 2013 and 2022, 84,691 hospital admissions for Stroke, IHD, and HF were included in the analysis (Table 1). IHD accounted for half of all admissions (50.0%), followed by Stroke (29.0%) and HF (21.0%). The cohort was slightly male-predominant (51.8%). Hindustani patients comprised the largest ethnic group (52.8%), followed by Creole (19.4%), Other (12.7%), Javanese (10.3%), and Maroon patients (4.9%).

The annual number of admissions increased markedly over the study period, more than doubling between 2013 and 2022. This finding indicates a substantial increase in the volume of hospitalizations for these major cardiovascular conditions over time, although changes in population structure, healthcare utilization, referral patterns, and access to hospital care may also have contributed to the observed increase.

Patients admitted with Stroke and HF were older on average than those admitted with IHD, while length of stay was longest among patients with Stroke. In absolute terms, the highest number of in-hospital deaths occurred among patients admitted with Stroke, followed by HF and IHD.

Hospital Admission Volume and Capacity Implications

The volume of hospital admissions for cardiovascular conditions increased markedly over the study period. Annual admissions to the three participating hospitals increased from approximately 5,600 in 2013 to 12,400 in 2022, representing a 121% increase over 10 years. This escalation occurred alongside modest population growth (approximately 1.4% annually) and suggests that the increase in admissions reflects both demographic change and rising age-specific disease incidence (15). Projected forward, if historical admission trends continue unchanged, the three participating hospitals would need to accommodate approximately 24,800 annual cardiovascular admissions by 2030-more than double the baseline. This scale of demand would place substantial pressure on emergency department, cardiology, neurology, and intensive care capacities. The concurrent improvement in in-hospital mortality (Table 2) demonstrates that the health system has managed quality-of-care improvements despite rising volume. However, the combination of rising demand and fixed infrastructure suggests that this dual improvement (more patients + lower mortality) may not be sustainable indefinitely without investment in capacity expansion. These trends underscore the urgency of primary prevention. The HEARTS Initiative's goal of reducing hypertension prevalence and improving hypertension control represents a direct pathway to reducing the future incidence of acute cardiovascular events, thereby slowing the rate of demand escalation on inpatient services.

Crude case fatality rates

Crude case fatality rates (CFRs) differed across disease categories and ethnic groups (Table 2). Overall, the CFR was highest among patients admitted with IHD (9.74%), followed by HF (7.33%) and Stroke (7.09%). Substantial ethnic variation was observed within each disease category (Table 1). Among patients with Stroke, the highest CFR was observed among Javanese patients (9.78%), compared with 5.84% among Hindustani patients. A similar pattern was observed for HF, for which Javanese patients had the highest CFR (9.32%), whereas the CFR was lowest among Hindustani patients (6.84%). For IHD, Creole patients had the highest CFR (11.30%), while patients classified as Other had the lowest CFR (6.99%). Hindustani patients consistently had relatively low CFRs across all three disease categories, with rates of 5.84% for Stroke, 6.84% for HF, and 10.00% for IHD.

These crude findings demonstrate that both the overall risk of in-hospital mortality and the magnitude of ethnic differences varied by cardiovascular condition, providing a rationale for examining diagnosis-specific temporal trends and adjusted associations in the multivariable analyses.

Discussion

Principal findings

This study examined ten years of hospital-based cardiovascular mortality data from three major hospitals in Suriname and identified three principal findings. First, in-hospital mortality declined significantly between 2013 and 2022 for HF, IHD, and Stroke, although the magnitude of decline differed substantially between conditions. Second, age and sex were associated with mortality in disease-specific patterns: age was associated with higher mortality in IHD and Stroke, whereas female sex was associated with lower mortality in HF. Third, ethnic inequalities were particularly pronounced for Stroke. Compared with Hindustani patients, all other major ethnic categories had significantly higher adjusted odds of in-hospital mortality. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that overall cardiovascular mortality trends do not represent a homogeneous phenomenon. Improvements occurred across conditions, but the distribution of mortality differed according to disease and population group.

Declining mortality across cardiovascular conditions

The decline in in-hospital mortality across all three conditions represents an important temporal finding. However, the observational nature of the study means that the reduction cannot be attributed to a particular treatment, policy, or health-system intervention. Several mechanisms may plausibly contribute to changes in hospital mortality over time, including changes in clinical management, access to care, referral patterns, diagnostic practices, patient characteristics, admission thresholds, and coding practices (16). Because detailed clinical data were unavailable, these mechanisms cannot be distinguished in the present analysis. The different magnitude of decline across conditions is nevertheless informative. HF showed the steepest decline, whereas IHD and Stroke showed more modest reductions (17). This heterogeneity suggests that cardiovascular mortality should be monitored at the disease level rather than through a single aggregate indicator. The finding is particularly relevant for public-health surveillance because an overall improvement could conceal areas in which progress is slower.

Increasing hospital volume alongside declining mortality

The increase in annual admissions is another important feature of the observed period. Admissions to the participating hospitals more than doubled between 2013 and 2022. This finding should not be interpreted as evidence of increasing disease incidence because hospital admissions are influenced by multiple factors beyond disease occurrence. Population ageing, changes in healthcare-seeking behavior, referral patterns, accessibility, and admission practices may all contribute (18). Nevertheless, the combination of increasing hospital volume and declining inhospital mortality indicates that the burden placed on hospitals increased substantially while short-term outcomes improved. From a health-system planning perspective, this emphasizes the importance of considering both volume and outcomes. Monitoring mortality alone may underestimate pressure on hospital services, whereas monitoring admissions alone does not capture whether outcomes are improving. The increasing volume also strengthens the rationale for prevention and early management of cardiovascular risk factors (16) (17) (18) (19). However, the present study cannot quantify the contribution of prevention to the observed admission trends.

Age-related inequalities

The strong association between increasing age and mortality among patients hospitalized with IHD is consistent with the vulnerability of older patients to adverse cardiovascular outcomes (18) (19) (20).

A similar, although smaller, association was observed for Stroke. These findings may reflect greater comorbidity, more severe disease, physiological vulnerability, or differences in presentation and treatment among older patients (2, 21). However, the available data do not allow these mechanisms to be evaluated.

The absence of an independent association between age and HF mortality is noteworthy. HF is clinically heterogeneous, and the administrative data did not permit differentiation by phenotype, severity, or etiology (22). The lack of association should therefore not be interpreted as evidence that age is clinically unimportant in the presence of other risk factors in HF. From a public-health perspective, the age gradients observed for IHD and Stroke reinforce the importance of prevention, risk-factor management, and appropriate access to care among older adults.

Sex differences were condition-specific

Female sex was associated with lower odds of in-hospital mortality among patients hospitalized with HF, while no statistically significant sex differences were observed for IHD or Stroke. The finding does not imply that sex is unimportant for cardiovascular outcomes in IHD or Stroke. Rather, it indicates that after adjustment for the variables available in this dataset, sex was not independently associated with in-hospital mortality in those two disease categories (23). Potential explanations for sex-specific differences may include differences in age, disease phenotype, comorbidity, presentation, healthcare utilization, or treatment (22) (23) (24) (25). These factors could not be adequately assessed in the current administrative dataset. The results therefore support continued sex-disaggregated cardiovascular surveillance rather than assuming that temporal improvements or mortality risks are equivalent between men and women.

Ethnic inequities in cardiovascular mortality

The most important health-equity finding was the pronounced ethnic gradient in Stroke mortality. Compared with Hindustani patients, mortality was significantly higher among Maroon, Creole, Javanese, and Other ethnic groups. The association was strongest among Javanese patients, whose adjusted odds of in-hospital mortality were approximately 1.8 times those of Hindustani patients. Importantly, ethnicity should not be interpreted as a biological cause of these differences. Ethnic categories are social and population classifications that may capture differences in socioeconomic circumstances, geographic location, healthcare access, healthseeking behavior, risk-factor exposure, continuity of care, or other structural determinants (26, 27). The present study cannot determine which of these mechanisms explains the observed disparities. The appropriate interpretation is therefore that ethnicity identifies a population-level inequity signal that warrants further investigation.

Why the ethnic gradient may be particularly pronounced for cerebrovascular disease

The stronger ethnic gradient observed for Stroke than for HF or IHD is potentially important. Cerebrovascular outcomes can be influenced by processes occurring before hospital admission, including recognition of neurological symptoms, decision-making about seeking care, transportation, geographic accessibility, and time to hospital presentation. Once patients reach hospital, outcomes may also depend on diagnostic capacity, early neurological assessment, access to imaging, specialized expertise, and subsequent management. These factors provide plausible pathways through which structural and healthcare inequalities could contribute to differences in mortality (28). However, none of these mechanisms was measured directly in the present study. The findings therefore should not be interpreted as demonstrating that ethnic differences in Stroke mortality are caused by delayed presentation, unequal access to stroke services, or differences in quality of care. Instead, the observed pattern identifies a priority for future research. This distinction is particularly important because the ethnic gradient was broader for Stroke than for HF. The consistency of the association across four non-reference ethnic groups suggests that the finding is unlikely to be explained solely by a difference between one specific ethnic group and the reference population.

Implications for public health and health-system planning

The findings have several implications for cardiovascular health policy in Suriname. First, cardiovascular surveillance should routinely report outcomes by disease, age, sex, and ethnicity. Aggregate mortality measures alone may conceal important inequalities. Second, the ethnic disparities observed for Stroke should be investigated using data capable of examining the pathways between population characteristics and outcomes. These should include, where feasible, time from symptom onset to presentation, geographic accessibility, emergency transport, disease severity, diagnostic investigations, treatment, and hospital-level characteristics. Third, the disease-specific differences in temporal trends suggest that cardiovascular policy should not rely exclusively on aggregate cardiovascular indicators. HF, IHD, and Stroke may require different approaches to prevention, early recognition, referral, and care. Fourth, the substantial increase in hospital admission volume highlights the importance of strengthening prevention and risk-factor control. Hypertension is particularly relevant because it is a major modifiable risk factor for both ischemic and cerebrovascular disease. Finally, the findings provide a useful preimplementation reference point for Suriname's cardiovascular prevention efforts, including the HEARTS initiative. HEARTS implementation began after the study period; therefore, the present study cannot evaluate its effectiveness. Future monitoring can nevertheless assess whether improvements in cardiovascular outcomes continue and whether those improvements are equitably distributed across population groups.

Strengths

This study has several strengths. First, it included 84,691 hospital admissions over a ten-year period, providing a substantial longitudinal dataset in a setting where population-based cardiovascular surveillance is limited. Second, the participating hospitals represented approximately 71.8% of national hospital admissions, providing broad coverage of hospital-based cardiovascular care in Suriname. Third, the analysis examined HF, IHD, and Stroke separately. This allowed identification of disease-specific temporal and demographic patterns that would have been obscured by combining heterogeneous cardiovascular conditions. Fourth, ethnicity was explicitly incorporated into the analysis, allowing identification of population-level differences that would not have been apparent from overall mortality estimates. Fifth, multivariable logistic regression allowed adjustment for age, sex, and ethnicity when examining temporal trends and mortality differences.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, this was a hospital-based study. Although the participating hospitals account for a large proportion of national admissions, the findings cannot be interpreted as estimates of population-level cardiovascular mortality or incidence. Second, the unit of analysis was the hospital admission rather than the individual patient. Patients with multiple admissions could therefore contribute more than one observation. If repeated admissions from the same individuals were present, conventional logistic regression may underestimate uncertainty. Third, diagnoses were based on routinely collected ICD-10 administrative data. Misclassification is possible, and changes in coding practices over time could influence observed temporal trends. Fourth, the dataset lacked several important clinical variables, including disease severity, comorbidities, vital signs, laboratory results, imaging findings, treatment, and time from symptom onset to hospital presentation. Consequently, the models had limited explanatory power and residual confounding is likely. Fifth, in-hospital mortality captures deaths occurring during hospitalization only. The study therefore cannot assess post-discharge mortality, long-term survival, or total population mortality. Sixth, ethnicity was obtained from routinely collected administrative categories. Misclassification is possible, and broad categories may conceal substantial heterogeneity within population groups. Seventh, the observational design prevents causal inference. In particular, the observed decline in mortality over time cannot be attributed to specific changes in clinical care, health policy, prevention programs, or other interventions. Finally, LOS was not used as a primary explanatory variable because it is determined after admission and can be affected by the outcome itself. Associations between LOS and mortality should therefore not be interpreted as causal effects.

Future research

The findings provide several directions for further research. First, future studies should examine whether ethnic differences in Stroke mortality are associated with differences in risk-factor control, disease severity, time to presentation, geographic accessibility, emergency transport, diagnostic assessment, or treatment. Second, linkage of hospital records with primary care, emergency medical services, mortality registries, geographic information, and socioeconomic data could provide a more complete assessment of the pathways underlying cardiovascular inequalities. Third, future analyses should examine whether the observed temporal decline in mortality was similar across ethnic and sex groups. Such analyses would directly address whether improvements in cardiovascular outcomes have been equitable. Fourth, incorporation of clinical variables into hospital databases would permit more robust risk adjustment and facilitate monitoring of risk-adjusted mortality over time. Finally, the 2013-2022 data provide a useful preimplementation reference period for subsequent evaluation of cardiovascular prevention and health-system initiatives, including HEARTS. Future studies should assess both overall effects and distributional effects across population groups.

Conclusion

In-hospital mortality among patients hospitalized with HF, IHD, and Stroke declined significantly in Suriname between 2013 and 2022, but the magnitude and distribution of improvement differed across cardiovascular conditions and population groups. HF showed the steepest temporal decline, whereas IHD and Stroke demonstrated more modest improvements. Age was associated with higher mortality in IHD and Stroke, while female sex was associated with lower mortality in HF. The most prominent inequality was observed for Stroke, for which all major non-reference ethnic groups had higher adjusted odds of in-hospital mortality than Hindustani patients. These differences should not be interpreted as biological effects of ethnicity, but as an important population-level signal of potential social, structural, geographic, or healthcare-related inequalities. The findings demonstrate the importance of monitoring cardiovascular outcomes not only by disease and over time, but also across population groups. Strengthening routine surveillance and investigating the mechanisms underlying ethnic differences should be priorities for cardiovascular public health in Suriname. The 2013-2022 findings also provide a relevant preimplementation baseline against which future cardiovascular prevention and health-system initiatives can be evaluated.
