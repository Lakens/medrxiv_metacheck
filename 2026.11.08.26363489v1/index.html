How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. Introduction

Diabetic retinopathy (DR), age-related macular degeneration (AMD), and glaucoma are among the leading causes of blindness in the United States, collectively aecting over 20 million Americans. [1] Due to the high volume nature of ophthalmology and widespread use of in-clinic imaging, articial intelligence (AI) is poised to augment ophthalmic clinical care, including disease diagnosis and predicting disease progression and forecasting. Specif-ically, there exist several high-performing models for diagnosis of posterior segment disease such as DR, glaucoma, and AMD using fundus photographs, optical coherence tomography (OCT), and more recently optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA). More recently, there has been growing interest in whether deep learning can identify latent disease-associated information or systemic data from imaging modalities not traditionally used for standard-of-care diagnosis. [2, 3] External eye photographs represent a unique opportunity for AI-based analysis of periocular and orbital biomarkers associated with intraocular disease and systemic health status.

Prior work using external eye photographs has focused largely on identifying eyelid measurements and oculofacial disease [4, 5], strabismus screening[6], and various systemic laboratory abnormalities and diabetic retinopathy. [2, 3] Deep learning applied to external eye photographs can estimate circulating biomarkers such as glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) and blood lipids. [3, 2] Elevated HbA1c is an independent risk factor for vision-threatening diabetic retinopathy, [7] and systemic changes in lipid metabolism have been linked to AMD, to disease stage, and to specic retinal imaging features. [8, 9, 10] We therefore hypothesised that external eye photographs may carry phenotypic signal associated with posterior-segment disease, including vision-threatening DR and advanced AMD, reecting shared systemic and periocular correlates, even though these posterior structures are not directly imaged. Recent studies have also applied deep learning to anterior segment OCT and corneal topography to predict demographic characteristics such as age and sex. [11, 12] Other studies have explored external eye features associated with systemic biomarkers, including HbA1c and haemoglobin, [13] and conjunctival vascular changes in diabetes. [14] A well-known issue with ne-tuning AI models for diagnostic prediction is variable performance across important dataset characteristics, such as race and sex. This is particularly concerning in healthcare because DL models that perform variably across populations, especially underrepresented groups, have the potential to propagate disparities. [15] For example, prior work has demonstrated that DL models tend to misclassify skin diseases such as melanoma in patients with darker skin tones. [16] Similar biases have been exposed in DL models predicting heart failure hospitalisation length [17] and postpartum depression. [18] To address these concerns, methods to mitigate bias beyond simple dataset augmentation have been explored to optimise models more fairly across demographic groups, collectively termed fairness training. [19] There currently are few proof-of-concept studies implementing fairness training for DL models in ophthalmology. [20, 21] These studies evaluated models on fundus photographs using fairness training to ne-tune foundation models and convolutional neural network architectures. However, there remains a paucity of literature describing how fairness methods could improve DL model performance on external eye photographs. There also exists a gap in knowledge regarding how fairness training could optimise current stateof-the-art models such as vision transformers (ViT) [22] and vision-language models (VLMs).

Discussion In proof-of-concept study, we assessed a spectrum of deep learning model architectures and fairness training methods for detecting vision-threatening DR, advanced AMD, and glaucoma from external eye photographs. Our study has three key ndings: (1) DL models demonstrated high performance in identifying vision-threatening DR and AMD from external eye photographs; (2) VLMs such as LLaVA performed comparably to the best conventional architectures, with no statistically signicant superiority; and (3) fairness training methods improved both overall performance and subgroup performance for DR across various demographic groups, in single training runs and with the caveats on minority-group case counts discussed below.

Our rst key nding was that DL models demonstrated high performance in identifying vision-threatening DR and AMD from external eye photographs. These ndings underscore the potential for AI-assisted biomarker exploration for posterior segment disease, and that external eye photographs may hold useful data for stratifying disease vs non-disease states. There are several possible explanations for the predictive ability of these models. First, DL models may detect subtle features of the fundus visible within corneal reections. Second, external structural changes associated with systemic conditions (e.g., diabetic cornea changes, age-related periocular changes, diabetic cataracts) may increase pre-test probability for posterior segment diseases. Saliency maps for both DR and AMD frequently highlighted the pupil and the red reex, as well as the irisscleral junction, and less frequently the adnexal features. We therefore investigated whether the signal depends on a pharmacologically dilated pupil. In the 857 test images with an undi-lated pupil, discriminatory power persisted for both vision-threatening DR and advanced AMD, although with lower point estimates and wide condence intervals (Supplementary Table S17). Previous studies of external eye photographs have found that predictions of DR and systemic biomarkers generalised from undilated to dilated pupils and relied predominantly on the central region of the image (sclera, iris and pupil) rather than the eyelids. [3] These observations argue against the claim that the signal consists exclusively of a low-resolution image of the fundus through a dilated pupil. However, we cannot rule out the possibility that the red reex contributes part of the signal. To resolve this issue, an explicit experiment involving pupil masking and subsequent retraining is needed.

Consistent with prior work showing that external eye photographs encode systemic biomarkers,[2, 3] all DR models, and the leading AMD and glaucoma models, captured signal signicantly beyond a demographics-only baseline. Because this baseline already conditioned on age, race, and ethnicity, the image-specic increment cannot be attributed to these demographic factors alone. This increase was greatest for DR, for which a plausible biological mechanism has been proposed. External eye photographs have been shown to encode information about glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c),[2, 3] and elevated HbA1c is itself an independent risk factor for vision-threatening DR (odds ratio ≈1•33 per 1% increase),[7] so the strong DR signal beyond demographic variables may arise from glycaemia-related oculomic features rather than from demographic information. A comparable, though more tentative, rationale may apply to AMD. Alterations in systemic lipid metabolism have been reported in AMD and linked to disease stage,[8, 10, 9, 32] and external eye photographs can encode circulating markers including blood lipids,[3] so the modest but signicant AMD signal beyond demographics may likewise reect genuine oculomic correlates. We nonetheless present these mechanisms as plausible hypotheses rather than established explanations, and acknowledge that periocular changes correlated with age or other systemic factors may also contribute. However, it is important to acknowledge that these saliency heat maps do not necessarily explain biological mechanisms for disease classication, but rather give external insight into important pixel-level data that informed its decision. Therefore, a key limitation of our study is that there remains no obviously biological explanations for which external eye photographs may classify posterior segment disease besides the pixel-level information shown and the above hypotheses. Future work on pupil masking and the red reex, certain adnexal or extraocular features, or possibly segmentation of the external vascular system may facilitate better understanding of how these models work in predicting posterior segment disease. Ultimately, these ndings support the growing body of oculomics literature demonstrating the eye as a window to systemic health.[ 33] Of note, it is important to acknowledge that external eye photographbased models did not outperform fundus photograph-based models; as such, the goal of our study was not to demonstrate that our methodology can or should serve as a screening tool for eye disease, but rather to demonstrate the proof-of-concept utility of using external eye photographs for posterior segment disease classication. Prior DL models for vision-threatening DR from fundus photographs achieved AUCs of 9697%,[34, 35] and glaucoma models achieved AUCs of 8291%.[ 36, 37] Although the image models significantly exceeded a demographics-only baseline for glaucoma (ViT +18•9%, 95% CI 16•321•2), absolute discrimination remained modest (AUC 6774%). This ceiling most plausibly reects a fundamental constraint of external-eye imaging rather than a shortfall of model capacity or training data: glaucoma is dened by damage to the optic nerve head and retinal nerve bre layer (posterior structures not visible in external photographs), and its reference standard depends on functional visual-eld testing, which need not have any external ocular correlate. This contrasts with diabetic retinopathy and advanced AMD, whose systemic and microvascular determinants (glycaemic and vascular status in DR; ageing-and lipid-related changes in AMD) may leave detectable signatures in the external eye and periocular tissues. Glaucoma may simply lack a comparably strong, externally expressed biomarker. Our second key nding is that the VLM performed at least as well as the strongest conventional architectures, without statistically signicant superiority. LLaVA achieved the highest AUC for DR (85•4% vs 82•6% for ViT), whereas for advanced AMD a convolutional network (EcientNet, 86•3%) was numerically highest and LLaVA (84•8%) and ViT (84•7%) were comparable, with overlapping 95% condence intervals across the leading models. This pattern is consistent with prior work reporting comparable performance of VLMs to transformers on healthcare tasks,[38] and high performance of ne-tuned LLaVA on chest X-ray classication.[ 39] A potential explanation may lie in the optimisation of vision-based tasks using LLaVA's vision-language connector,[26] which facilitates ecient knowledge transfer from diverse training sources. Our models performed comparably to those published by Babenko et al (AUC 0•790•86), despite their larger training cohort.[ 3] Future work is needed to explore applications of VLMs in other imaging diagnostic applications, as well as novel use cases including image report generation and dierential disease generation, especially when used in a multimodal manner (text and imaging). Our third key nding is that fairness training generally improved overall performance and reduced demographic disparities for DRin single training runs; these comparisons are subject to the caveats on run-to-run variability and minority-group case counts noted in the Limitations. Using ViT as the baseline, most fairness optimisation methods demonstrated comparable or improved ES-AUC compared to baseline training, particularly for race-based equity. These ndings were less pronounced for AMD, likely due to signicant demographic imbalance in our dataset (87•8% of patients with a positive nding for advanced AMD were White, while in the entire cohort there were only 4 Black patients and 9 Asian patients with a positive AMD nding). The divergent behaviour of race-versus ethnicity-based metrics across DR and AMD (Supplementary Tables S1S2) likely reects diering subgroup case mixes and sample sizes: ethnicity was dominated by a large non-Hispanic majority with a small other stratum, whereas racial disparities were larger and more systematically reduced by fairness training for DR than for AMD. These results highlight the interplay between data imbalance and algorithmic fairness and the need to account for both dataset and methodological components of model ne-tuning. Because compilation of representative datasets can be resource-intensive, fairness training methods may oer a complementary approach to promote equitable screening and decrease AI bias against underrepresented demographic groups. We additionally demonstrated similar improvements with FairLoRA in VLMs for DR. To our knowledge, this is a novel nding within AI-based imaging diagnostics and future work should explore how fairness training could potentially improve VLM performance for disease diagnosis within other ophthalmic diseases and other medical specialties as well. Although our experiments were conducted on external eye photographs which are not and should not be used for disease screening with currently existing models, application of these methods for existing AI model applications for posterior segment disease classication may improve model performance and decrease demographic bias, therefore potentially improving model generalization. Our study has several limitations. First, this was a retrospective study conducted at a single academic center with a predominantly White patient population, which may limit generalisability. Furthermore, 98•7% of the images were acquired on Topcon platforms, so performance on other devices remains unestablished (Supplementary Tables S14S16). External validation using independent datasets with more diverse populations and acquisition devices is therefore required prior to clinical deployment. Second, disease labels were derived from ICD codes and clinical documentation, which may introduce label noise and misclassication. The comparison groups were dened according to the target outcome within the clinical population and were not matched for concomitant ocular or systemic diseases. Therefore, outcome-associated features that are not specic to the target disease, such as cataract or previous ocular surgery, may contribute to the learned signal. Third, we performed binary classication, whereas ophthalmic diseases exist along a clinical spectrum that may dierentially aect demographic groups. Fourth, subgroup analysesparticularly for AMDare limited by small sample sizes in underrepresented racial groups, necessitating cautious interpretation of subgroup-specic performance. Our cohort was 80•1% White, with small minority strata (Black 4•1%, Asian 3•4%, Hispanic 5•6%). Subgroup AUCs in these groups carried very wide condence intervals, so this imbalance imposes a ceiling on what algorithmic fairness methods can achieve, and apparent fairness gains must be interpreted cautiously and cannot substitute for representative data collection. The overall cohort included only nine Asian patients and four Black patients with advanced AMD. Therefore, the corresponding subgroup AUCs and race-based ES-AUC for AMD should be interpreted with considerable caution, and no claim is made regarding improvements in racial equity for AMD. The observed improvements in race-based ES-AUC for VTDR should likewise be regarded as exploratory ndings that require independent replication in larger and more diverse cohorts. Each model conguration was trained once; we did not repeat training across random seeds, so run-to-run variability is not captured by the bootstrap condence intervals, which quantify sampling variability of the test set only. Dierences of a few AUC points between methods, such as those observed for sex and ethnicity, may lie within seed-to-seed variation, whereas the race ES-AUC changes for DR (1319 percentage points for adversarial debiasing, FSCL and FIS) are larger than is typically attributable to seed noise but still derive from small minority-group case counts. Fifth, our study used external eye photographs captured on fundus-camera devices during routine clinical care, and did not assess performance of external photographs acquired by other modalities such as smartphones. While the goal of our proof-of-concept study was to demonstrate feasibility of predicting posterior segment disease, we acknowledge that the performance of our models is insucient for clinical deployment and more work is needed to improve model performance and potentially evaluate on more easily acquired external eye photographs, such as smartphone photographs if the goal is to utilize external photography-based screening for ophthalmic disease. As a real-world retrospective cohort, the racial and ethnic distribution of our dataset is inherently imbalanced and reects local patient demographics, referral patterns, and structural access to care. Unlike curated benchmark datasets, clinical datasets cannot be articially balanced without altering disease prevalence and compromising external validity. Accordingly, it is important to acknowledge that our objective was not to enforce demographic balance, but rather to evaluate model performance and mitigate demographic disparities under realistic clinical data distributions. We demonstrate that state-of-the-art DL models and VLMs can identify vision-threatening DR and advanced AMD from external eye photographs, signal that signicantly exceeds a demographics-only baseline, thereby contributing to the growing eld of oculomics; the ne-tuned VLM performed comparably to, but not better than, the strongest conventional architectures. We further show that fairness training can improve both overall and demographic-specic performance for DR. These external eye models are a proof-of-concept and are not intended to replace standard-of-care fundus photography or to function as a screening tool in their current form; rather, they establish the feasibility of oculomic analysis of external eye photographs and provide fairness methods that are transferable to other ophthalmic and medical AI applications. Getting closer to clinical readiness would require external and prospective validation in diverse, multi-site populations and acquisition on widely available devices such as smartphones, and patient stakeholders to advance equitable and cost-eective access to care.

Dataset and Demographics

External eye photographs were obtained from patients who underwent clinical examination at the Massachusetts Eye and Ear Inrmary (MEEI) up until December 2024. The photographs were initially stored with fundus images in a mixed dataset comprising approximately one million images. All images were acquired using a variety of ophthalmic imaging platforms, including Clarus, Visucam, and Visucam NM/FA (Carl Zeiss Meditec); Maestro and Topcon color retinal imaging cameras (Topcon); Silverstone (Optos); and RetCam (Natus Medical). The decision to obtain photographs from a specic machine was determined based on clinician preference and if imaging was felt indicated for documentation purposes or diagnostic assistance. Ophthalmic photographers captured photographs as part of routine clinical care. There was no specic protocol noted for image acquisition, and image resolution, formatting, and metadata varied by the machine used (median resolution 2672×3000 pixels; median eld of view 50 • ; stored in JPEG format; 95•7% of images acquired after pharmacological dilation). Device groups were derived from the device labels contained in the image le names and represent broad imaging platforms rather than conrmed individual camera models. Images labelled Topcon accounted for 98•7% (40,174 of 40,687) of images, with the remainder consisting of images labelled Zeiss (130), RetCam (17) or Optos (2) and unlabelled images (364) (Supplementary Table S14). Patients of all age groups were included; age was dened as the patient's age at the time the rst image for the study was acquired. We employed a three-step procedure to identify external eye photographs. First, 3,000 randomly sampled images were manually reviewed and categorized into three classes: (1) external eye photographs, (2) fundus images, and (3) unusable images. Unusable images were dened as overexposed images resulting in a completely white image, test images of the device, and images of the room without a human subject. Second, these annotated images were used to train a previously developed deep learning classier based on the EcientNet architecture, which was subsequently applied to identify candidate external eye photographs. Third, four trained medical re-searchers independently reviewed all candidate images and classied them as dilated external photographs, undilated external photographs, non-external photographs, unsure, or unusable. Images classied as "unsure" by a reviewer were subsequently assessed by a senior grader for nal adjudication. Unusable images were external eye photographs (not fundus images) that were unsuitable for analysis; reasons for unusability included fully closed eyelids, a non-visible iris, previous keratoprosthesis (KPro) surgery, the presence of corneal uorescein dye, and acquisition under a non-standard illumination lter. This process resulted in a nal dataset of 40,687 external eye photographs from 9,878 unique patients. Demographic variables including sex, race, ethnicity, and primary language were extracted from the electronic health record. Self-reported sex was dened as male and female. Race and ethnicity were self-reported by patients at registration. Race categories included Asian, Black or African American, White, and Other (including multiple races and unknown). Ethnicity was categorised as Hispanic, non-Hispanic, or unknown. Disease Denitions Disease labels were obtained from clinical diagnoses and the electronic health record as follows: AMD diagnosis was based on International Classication of Diseases (ICD-10) codes. Subjects were categorised into four classes: normal, early AMD, intermediate AMD, and advanced AMD (including both geographic atrophy and neovascular AMD). For this study, we performed binary classication of advanced AMD versus all other categories. ICD-10 codes have been assigned by treating ophthalmologist and DR severity followed the International Clinical Diabetic Retinopathy Severity Scale. DR diagnosis was based on ICD-10 codes. Subjects were classied as having non-vision-threatening DR (no DR, mild non-proliferative DR, or moderate non-proliferative DR) or vision-threatening DR (severe non-proliferative DR or proliferative DR). For both DR and AMD, the comparison group therefore comprised all other patients in the cohort, including those with less severe stages of the same disease and those with other ocular diagnoses, rather than healthy controls. Comparison groups were not matched for concomitant ocular disease, because the aim was to detect advanced disease within a real-world clinical population rather than to isolate disease-specic features under a matched case-control design. Fairness Optimisation Methods

We evaluated ve fairness-enhancing methods: 3. Fair supervised contrastive loss (FSCL) addresses the tendency of supervised contrastive learning to encode sensitive-attribute information.

FSCL mitigates this by restricting the negative set to samples that share the same sensitive attribute but belong to a dierent target class, so that the representation is not encouraged to separate classes in a way that relies on the sensitive attribute. [29] 4. Fair identity scaling (FIS) is a fairness approach tailored to medical AI, which views fairness as both a group-level and identity-level problem. It proposes an identity-level scaling mechanism that downweights patients the model already performs well on, while emphasizing patients with poorer generalization, who often belong to minority groups. Further-ables ne-tuning with a reduced number of trainable parameters by adding trainable low-rank parameters to the pre-trained weights. Specically, for a pre-trained weight matrix W 0 ∈ R m×n , the ne-tuned weight is written as where A ∈ R m×r and B ∈ R r×n , with small rank r.

The resulting low-rank update admits an equivalent formulation via singular value decomposition (SVD), where U ∈ R m×r and V ∈ R n×r are the left and right singular vector matrices and S ∈ R r×r is a diagonal singular value matrix; the singular vectors capture global structural directions across the embedding space, while the singular values control the relative importance of each direction.

FairLoRA extends this framework to incorporate group fairness by disentangling shared and group-specic components of the low-rank update. For a demographic group g, FairLoRA denes the LoRA weights as where U and V are shared across all demographic groups (capturing intergroup relationships) and S g is a group-specic singular value matrix (preserving intra-group distinctiveness).

In this study, we applied FairLoRA to LLaVA-7B with LoRA rank r = 8 and scaling factor α = 16 to evaluate the impact of fairness-aware ne-tuning on VLM performance.
