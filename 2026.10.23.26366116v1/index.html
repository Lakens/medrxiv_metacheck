To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 41 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Abstract

Anorexia nervosa (AN) is a severe psychiatric disorder with high relapse rates despite successful weight restoration. Altered reward processing and an imbalance between goal-directed and habitual control within the striatum have been proposed to contribute to the persistence of maladaptive behaviours, but the striatal mechanisms underlying recovery and relapse remain unclear. Here, we combined cross-species structural MRI, including cross-sectional analyses in mice and longitudinal neuroimaging and clinical assessments in patients with AN, to investigate striatal alterations associated with recovery. Male mice with deletion of the vesicular acetylcholine transporter (VAChTcKO; n = 9), a model of maladaptive eating behaviour, showed reduced dorsolateral striatal volume compared with wild-type mice (n=9). At baseline, women with AN (n = 71) showed reduced striatal volumes compared with healthy controls (n = 30). 1 NOTE: This preprint reports new research that has not been certified by peer review and should not be used to guide clinical practice.

Longitudinally, patients with AN (n = 41) and healthy controls (n = 28) were reassessed, revealing substantial recovery of striatal volumes following weight restoration in AN. Posterior-anterior putamen gradient changes differed according to subsequent remission status, with changes in the right putamen gradient predicting relapse. Striatal gradient changes were also associated with goal-directed and habitual behaviour during the slip-of-action task, response consistency during delay discounting, and circulating ghrelin and desacyl ghrelin concentrations. Introduction

Anorexia nervosa (AN) is a severe psychiatric disorder characterized by persistent restriction of energy intake, intense fear of gaining weight, cognitive rigidity, and disturbances in body image perception 1, 2 . AN affects approximately 1-4% of women 3 and is associated with one of the highest mortality rates among psychiatric disorders 4 . Current treatment strategies primarily aim to restore body weight and alleviate eating disorders-related symptoms 5 . Despite successful weight restoration during treatment, relapse remains frequent, with nearly half of patients relapsing within the year following hospitalization [6] [7] [8] [9] . Weight restoration does not necessarily indicate sustained clinical recovery, and the neurobiological changes associated with subsequent remission or relapse remain poorly understood 10 . Identifying neurobiological markers that distinguish sustained recovery from vulnerability to relapse therefore represents a major unmet clinical need 11.

One proposed contributor to the persistence of AN is the development of maladaptive behaviours that become increasingly automatic and resistant to change. Repeated behaviours such as dietary restriction, excessive exercise and body checking may progressively become less sensitive to their consequences and more strongly driven by learned stimulus-response associations [12] [13] [14] . This has motivated the hypothesis that an imbalance between goal-directed and habitual control contributes to symptom persistence and relapse in AN [15] [16] [17] . However, evidence for increased habitual responding in AN remains mixed, suggesting that restrictive behaviours may reflect both persistent goal-directed pursuit of weight-related outcomes and increasingly automatic action patterns, rather than a simple shift from goal-directed to habitual control. 18, 19 . These processes may be further shaped by altered reward and punishment sensitivity, as restrictive behaviours may be reinforced by anticipated weight-related outcomes and the avoidance of perceived adverse consequences of eating 20, 21 . 2 These complementary behavioural systems are supported by partially segregated corticostriatal circuits involved in reward valuation, reinforcement learning and action selection 22 . Goal-directed actions remain sensitive to the expected value of their outcomes, whereas habitual actions become less dependent on outcome value and more strongly influenced by learned stimulus-response associations. Across species, rodent dorsomedial and dorsolateral striatal territories broadly correspond to associative and sensorimotor corticostriatal territories involving the human caudate and putamen, respectively, although these anatomical and functional correspondences are not one-to-one 23, 24 . Striatal dopamine and acetylcholine signalling contribute to corticostriatal plasticity and to the balance between flexible, habitual and compulsive action patterns relevant to eating pathology 25, 26 . Consistent with this framework, mice lacking the vesicular acetylcholine transporter (VAChT) in striatal cholinergic interneurons show impaired behavioural flexibility, enhanced habit formation and increased susceptibility to binge-like and anorexia-like behaviours [27] [28] [29] . Together, these findings implicate striatal cholinergic signalling in behavioural flexibility and habit formation, providing a rationale for examining striatal morphology across experimental and clinical contexts.

Human neuroimaging studies have implicated corticostriatal circuits in AN, particularly through alterations in functional activation and structural or functional connectivity 30, 31 . Previous neuroimaging studies have also linked changes in anterior cingulate morphology and prefrontal activation to subsequent clinical outcomes in AN, but the prognostic relevance of spatially heterogeneous striatal reorganization remains largely unexplored [32] [33] [34] [35] . Cross-sectional and longitudinal structural MRI studies have also demonstrated widespread gray matter alterations in AN, including changes in striatal morphology and volume, with substantial but often incomplete normalization following weight restoration 36, 37 . However, conventional regional volumetric analyses provide limited information about whether structural changes are spatially homogeneous within the striatum or preferentially affect territories associated with distinct corticostriatal circuits. Characterizing such spatial heterogeneity may therefore offer additional insight into striatal reorganization during recovery.

The striatum exhibits spatial gradients in anatomical organization, functional specialization and corticostriatal connectivity 38, 39 . These gradients encompass posterior sensorimotor territories associated with habitual action control and more anterior associative territories implicated in flexible, goal-directed behaviour 40, 41 . Examining the spatial distribution of gray matter volume (GMV) along this axis may reveal heterogeneous patterns of structural change that are obscured by whole-region measurements 42, 43 . Although morphometric gradients do not directly measure the functional balance between goal-directed and habitual systems, they may provide an anatomically informed description of changes across striatal territories. Whether these structural patterns reorganize during weight restoration in AN, distinguish subsequent remission outcomes, or relate to behavioural alterations remains unknown.

3

To examine the behavioural relevance of striatal reorganization, measures of action control and reward-related decision-making offer complementary perspectives. The Slip-of-Action Task (SOAT) assesses sensitivity to outcome devaluation, providing an experimental measure relevant to goal-directed and habitual action control [44] [45] [46] . Delay discounting task, by contrast, characterizes preferences between immediate and delayed rewards, providing a complementary measure of reward-related decision-making and cognitive control, both of which have been implicated in AN [47] [48] [49] [50] [51] . Beyond these behavioural processes, metabolic signals may influence reward-related decision-making. Ghrelin, an orexigenic hormone implicated in energy homeostasis and reward-related processes 52, 53 , can modulate dopaminergic signalling 54 . Notably, previous work in AN has identified an altered association between circulating desacyl-ghrelin and delay discounting 55 , while desacyl-ghrelin has also been associated with food-cue-related caudate activation 56 . Together, these findings motivate exploratory examination of behavioural and metabolic correlates of striatal morphology, without presupposing a direct mechanistic relationship.

In this study, we combined two complementary cohorts, comprising a mouse model with disrupted striatal cholinergic signalling (VAChTcKO) and a human cohort of patients with AN and healthy controls, using cross-sectional structural MRI in mice and cross-sectional and longitudinal MRI in humans. We investigated whether (i) striatal GMV and its posterior-anterior spatial distribution change during weight restoration in AN, (ii) these structural changes are associated with subsequent sustained remission or relapse, and (iii) they relate to behavioural and circulating ghrelin measures, examining whether striatal reorganization during weight restoration was associated with subsequent clinical outcomes.

Delay discounting was assessed by estimating two parameters: the discount rate (k), reflecting sensitivity to delayed rewards, and inverse temperature (m), reflecting response consistency 64 (see more in methods). None of the DDT parameters showed significant longitudinal change from V1 to V2 (all P > 0.23) (Figure S3). Nevertheless, exploratory analyses (n = 40) suggested an association between longitudinal striatal reorganization and DDT performance, with greater increases in the left putamen posterior-anterior gradient associated with increases in m (β = 0.40, P = 0.0126; FDR-P = 0.101). A similar but weaker positive association was observed for the right putamen gradient (β = 0.31, P = 0.0508; FDR-P = 0.203), whereas associations involving the discount rate k were weaker overall. None of these associations survived FDR correction.

Discussion

In the present study, In the present study, we investigated dorsal striatal organization in anorexia nervosa using a cross-species structural imaging framework, combining analyses in VAChTcKO mice with longitudinal neuroimaging in patients with AN and healthy controls. We characterized structural changes during weight restoration using conventional morphometric measures and posterior-anterior GMV gradients and examined their behavioural, endocrine and clinical associations. Our principal finding is that dorsal striatal changes during weight restoration are spatially heterogeneous, with longitudinal putamen gradient alterations associated with subsequent remission status.

The cross-species findings reveal partially corresponding patterns of structural alterations in sensorimotor striatal territories, despite the distinct biological and clinical contexts of the mouse model and human AN. In VAChTcKO mice, structural alterations were observed in the dorsolateral striatum, whereas the dorsomedial striatum remained relatively preserved. A comparable pattern emerged in humans, with more pronounced alterations in the putamen than in the caudate. Recent experimental evidence linking transitions toward habitual responding to changes in dorsolateral striatal activity 66 provides a potential functional context for these anatomical observations. Previous longitudinal MRI studies have shown widespread gray matter reductions in anorexia nervosa with substantial recovery following weight restoration 67 . However, our findings reveal a more complex pattern within the dorsal striatum: whereas caudate GMV increased relatively consistently, the putamen exhibited posterior reductions alongside anterior increases. This spatial heterogeneity is compatible with the organization of the striatum along continuous anatomical and functional gradients 38, 41 . Functional differentiation between posterior and anterior putaminal territories has also been demonstrated in Parkinson's disease, although its underlying pathology differs fundamentally from AN 68 . Importantly, posterior-anterior gradients captured longitudinal spatial changes beyond those observed using conventional ROI-based measures, suggesting that spatially resolved morphometry may provide complementary information to whole-region volumetric analyses.

The clinical relevance of this spatial reorganization was further supported by its association with subsequent remission status. Patients who later relapsed exhibited greater increases in posterior-anterior putamen gradients during weight restoration than those who achieved sustained remission, particularly in the right hemisphere. Although exploratory logistic analyses suggested potential prognostic value, these associations did not survive FDR correction, and their discriminative performance was assessed in-sample. Together, these findings suggest that structural changes accompanying weight restoration may reflect processes associated with longer-term clinical outcomes, highlighting a potential distinction between nutritional restoration and sustained recovery.

The behavioural associations provide functional context for these structural changes. Established corticostriatal models suggest that the caudate preferentially contributes to flexible goal-directed behaviour, whereas the putamen is more strongly implicated in stimulus-response habits 33 . Exploratory analyses revealed opposite directional associations between caudate and putamen gradient changes and SOAT performance. Caudate changes showed positive associations with goal-directed and negative associations with habitual responding, whereas putaminal changes showed the reverse pattern. Exploratory delay-discounting analyses also revealed nominal associations between putamen gradient changes and response consistency, suggesting a possible relationship with reward-related decision-making processes 69 . Although these patterns are compatible with the proposed functional specialization of dorsal striatal subregions, none survived correction for multiple comparisons, and they do not establish a functional dissociation or imply that structural gradients directly measure behavioural control or reward processing.

The relationship between striatal gradients and ghrelin-related measures provides a potential metabolic context for dorsal striatal reorganization during weight restoration. The distinct 17 patterns observed for acyl-ghrelin and desacyl-ghrelin suggest that different aspects of ghrelin signalling may be associated with striatal structural changes. Previous evidence implicating ghrelin in dopaminergic reward processing and corticostriatal plasticity 70, 71 as well as AN pathophysiology 72 , offers a possible biological framework for these findings. However, these associations did not survive correction for multiple comparisons, and further research is needed to determine whether ghrelin signalling contributes to striatal remodeling or merely covaries with nutritional recovery.

Collectively, these findings highlight the potential clinical relevance of spatially resolved dorsal striatal organization in AN. Together with the cross-species anatomical observations and exploratory behavioural and metabolic associations, they raise new questions about the distinct contributions of caudate and putamen remodeling to sustained recovery and relapse vulnerability, warranting further investigation in larger longitudinal cohorts.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, remission analyses were based on a relatively modest longitudinal sample, limiting statistical power for subgroup analyses. Second, the human cohort included only female patients, whereas the experimental mouse cohort consisted exclusively of males. This sex difference may influence the interpretation of cross-species comparisons and should be considered in future studies involving larger, sex-balanced cohorts to assess the generalizability of the findings. Third, although posterior-anterior gradient measures differentiated remission groups and showed the strongest associations with behavioural and metabolic measures, most behavioural and hormonal correlations did not survive correction for multiple comparisons and should therefore be considered exploratory. Fourth, only two MRI time points were available, preventing characterization of the temporal dynamics of striatal remodeling throughout recovery. Fifth, conventional VBM and DBM analyses were used to characterize regional volume changes and tissue deformation but were not predictive of clinical outcome. Finally, the predictive analyses were based exclusively on posterior-anterior gradient measures, which require validation in larger independent longitudinal cohorts before their clinical utility can be established.

Participants

Participants were recruited at GHU Paris Psychiatrie et Neurosciences (Sainte-Anne Hospital, Paris) as neuroimaging part of the longitudinal REMANO study of anorexia nervosa (http://clinicaltrials.gov/show/NCT04560517 Duriez et al., 2024) 57 . Female patients meeting DSM-5 criteria for anorexia nervosa underwent MRI, clinical evaluation, and neurocognitive assessment at baseline (V1). Approximately 4 months after admission, following weight restoration (BMI > 18.5), patients underwent repeat clinical and neurocognitive assessments at discharge (V2), with a subset completing a second MRI acquisition. Clinical outcome was assessed approximately 6 months after hospitalization (V3) based on BMI, allowing patients to be classified as having achieved sustained remission or relapse. Healthy age-matched female controls underwent the same baseline MRI, clinical, and neurocognitive assessments, with a subset completing a second MRI acquisition at follow-up. Participants with neurological disorders, MRI contraindications, pregnancy, or inability to provide informed consent were excluded.

Clinical, behavioural and hormonal measures for the human study

Participants underwent a comprehensive clinical, behavioural, neuropsychological, and metabolic assessment. Clinical variables included BMI, duration of illness, and AN subtype. Eating-disorder psychopathology was assessed using the Eating Disorder Inventory-2 (EDI-2) 58 (Table1), comprising eleven subscales (Drive for Thinness, Bulimia, Body Dissatisfaction, Ineffectiveness, Perfectionism, Interoceptive Awareness, Interpersonal Distrust, Maturity Fears, Asceticism, Impulse Regulation, and Social Insecurity). Anxiety and depressive symptoms were assessed using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) 59 , and obsessive-compulsive symptoms using the Yale-Brown Obsessive-Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS) 74 (Supplementary Table 3,4,5). Reward-based decision-making and goal-directed/habitual control were assessed using the DDT and SOAT, respectively, as detailed below. Circulating ghrelin concentrations were measured as peripheral metabolic markers implicated in appetite regulation and reward processing 52, 75.

MRI acquisitions for animal study

For brain perfusion, mice were anaesthetized with ketamine (100 mg•kg⁻¹) and xylazine (10 mg•kg⁻¹) diluted in saline, perfused with 30 mL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing heparin (1 μL•mL⁻¹) and 2 mM ProHance® at a flow rate of 1 mL•min⁻¹, followed by 30 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) containing 2 mM ProHance® at the same flow rate. After dissection, brains were post-fixed overnight in 4% PFA with 2 mM ProHance® at 4°C, then transferred to PBS containing 0.02% sodium azide and 2 mM ProHance® until imaging. MRI data were acquired on a Bruker 7T 30-cm bore scanner with AVANCE electronics using a three-dimensional FLASH sequence (70 μm isotropic resolution).

20

MRI acquisitions for human study

Human MRI data were acquired on a 3T Vantage Galan 3T/XGO system (Canon Medical Systems Corporation, Otawara, Japan) equipped with a 32-channel head coil at the Groupe Hospitalier Universitaire (GHU) Paris Psychiatrie & Neurosciences. Structural imaging consisted of a three-dimensional T1-weighted MPRAGE sequence (TR = 6.5 ms, TE = 3 ms, TI = 950 ms, flip angle = 9°, FOV = 256 × 256 mm², acquisition matrix = 256 × 256, 160 sagittal slices, slice thickness = 1 mm, shot interval = 2500 ms, voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm³).

MRI processing and morphometric analysis

MRI data were converted from DICOM to NIfTI format and organized according to the standard Brain Imaging Data Structure (BIDS) specification using HeuDiConv 76, 77 . Structural 3D T1 images were processed using the Computational Anatomy Toolbox (CAT12, version 12.9) implemented in SPM12, running under MATLAB R2018b 63, 78 . Preprocessing included denoising, bias-field correction, affine registration, probabilistic tissue segmentation into gray matter (GM), white matter (WM), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) using tissue probability maps, followed by adaptive maximum a posteriori segmentation with partial volume estimation 79, 80 . Images were spatially normalized to Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) standard space using Geodesic Shooting registration 81.

Given the longitudinal study design, T1-weighted images acquired at baseline (V1) and follow-up (V2) were additionally processed using the CAT12 longitudinal pipeline. The neuroplasticity model was selected because MRI sessions were separated by approximately four months and relatively subtle structural changes were expected.

Structural MRI data were retained after successful CAT12 processing and visual confirmation of adequate segmentation, with no major artefacts, gross mis-segmentation, or incomplete brain coverage. CAT12 image-quality metrics, including IQR, NCR, ICR, and image-boundary measures, were additionally reviewed to identify scans requiring further visual inspection.

Morphometric analysis

ROI-based analyses were performed using the default CAT12 probabilistic atlas (Neuromorphometrics) and unsmoothed modulated GM maps to preserve regional anatomical specificity. Total intracranial volume (TIV) was estimated as the sum of GM, WM, and CSF volumes in native space and included as a covariate in all analyses.

For voxel-wise analyses, modulated GM maps were smoothed with an 8-mm full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) Gaussian kernel. Significant clusters were anatomically localized using the Harvard-Oxford probabilistic atlas.

21

Posterior-anterior gradient analysis

To investigate the observed spatial heterogeneity, reflected by a posterior-anterior gradient within the regions of interest, we adopted the approach proposed by Drori et al. (2022) 41 for characterizing microstructural gradients of the human striatum, building on the broader framework of macroscale brain gradients described by Bernhardt et al. (2022) 39 . Inspired by their method, we subdivided the caudate and putamen into seven consecutive segments with the same number of voxels and the same distance along their main anatomical axis. For each structure, regions of interest were derived from the Harvard-Oxford atlas, and singular value decomposition (SVD) 82 was used to identify the principal spatial axis, corresponding to the posterior-anterior direction. GMV was then extracted separately for each of the seven segments. To summarize the posterior-anterior gradient profile into a single metric, we fitted a polynomial function to the GMV values across the seven segments for each subject. The fitted polynomial provided an individual quantitative description of the posterior-anterior gray matter gradient, allowing us to quantify and compare the spatial distribution of GMV within the caudate and putamen between groups and across sessions.

Deformation-based morphometry

To complement the voxel-based morphometry (VBM) analyses, deformation-based morphometry (DBM) 61 was performed by CAT12 to quantify voxel-wise tissue expansion and contraction from the deformation fields generated during nonlinear spatial normalization. Unlike VBM, which relies on tissue segmentation, DBM directly measures local anatomical deformations by analysing the Jacobian determinant of the nonlinear deformation field, allowing the detection of regional expansion and contraction independent of tissue classification 83 . For both human and mouse MRI data, Jacobian determinant maps were computed from the nonlinear registration fields and used as voxel-wise measures of local volumetric change. For the mouse MRI data, Jacobian maps were smoothed with a 200-μm Gaussian kernel before statistical analysis [83] [84] [85] (Figure S2, S3).

The Delay Discounting Task (DDT)

The reward based decision making was assessed by Delay Discounting Task (DDT) 64 . Participants made repeated choices between a smaller reward available sooner ("Smaller Sooner", SS) and a larger reward available after a longer delay ("Larger Later", LL). These choices were used to estimate the temporal discount rate (k) and inverse temperature parameter (m). Lower k values indicate weaker discounting of delayed rewards, reflecting a greater relative valuation of larger delayed rewards. Higher m values indicate greater consistency in choosing the option with the higher estimated subjective value. Further details are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

22

Slip-of-action Task (SOAT)

Goal-directed and habitual control were assessed using the Slip-of-Action Task (SOAT) 44, 45 as a part of instrumental learning task. During this task, specific outcomes were devalued, and participants were instructed to respond only to stimuli associated with still-valued outcomes while withholding responses to stimuli associated with devalued outcomes. Because previously learned stimulus-response associations remained unchanged, successful performance required the use of action-outcome knowledge. Responses to valued outcomes indexed goal-directed control, whereas responses to devalued outcomes ("slips of action") indexed habitual responding.

Blood sampling and ELISA Immuno-Assays

Blood was collected at each visit after an overnight fast on BD Vacutainer tubes treated with 15% EDTA and Aprotinin 250 HIU 71 . After collection, blood was kept at 4°C on ice then centrifuged within 2h (1000 g for 10 min at 4°C). Plasma samples were aliquoted and one aliquot was immediately acidified with HCl (final concentration of 0.1N) since acidification is known to preserve ghrelin stability. Samples were then stored at -80°C until the assay.

Plasma levels of ghrelin and desacyl ghrelin were measured using specific EIA (ghrelin: catalog no. A05106; desacyl ghrelin: catalog no. A05119; Bertin Bioreagents, Montigny-le-Bretonneux, France). All used samples came from acidified aliquot. External quality control of the same human plasma was used in all assays to ensure inter-assay stability. Coefficients of variation for human acyl and desacyl ghrelin were between 8-9% and 10-16% for intra-and inter-assay respectively.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed in Python (version 3.12.3). ROI-based analyses were conducted using the Statsmodels library (version 0.14.6) 86 . For cross-sectional comparisons, ordinary least squares (OLS) regression models were fitted to assess group differences while adjusting for relevant covariates, including age and total intracranial volume (TIV) 87 . For longitudinal analyses, linear mixed-effects models (MixedLM) were used to account for repeated measurements within participants, with subject-specific random intercepts and fixed effects for group, session, and their interaction.

For voxel-wise analyses, group-level statistical analyses were performed using the general linear model (GLM) framework implemented in Nilearn (version 0.10.0) 88 . Multiple-comparison correction was performed using the false discovery rate (FDR) procedure with a significance threshold of q < 0.05 86 . Significant clusters were anatomically localized using the Harvard-Oxford probabilistic atlas to facilitate interpretation of voxel-wise findings.

Depending on data normality, group comparisons were performed using independent-samples t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests, whereas within-subject comparisons were assessed using paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, as appropriate. These analyses were performed using SciPy (version 1.17.0) 89 . FDR correction was applied to families of ROI-based statistical tests.

To evaluate the predictive value of neuroimaging biomarkers, logistic regression models were implemented using Scikit-learn (version 1.8.0) 90 . Model performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) together with classification accuracy.

Neuroimaging results were visualized using Nilearn (version 0.13.1) 88 , including statistical maps projected onto MNI standard templates and region-of-interest representations. Additional statistical plots and figures were generated using Matplotlib (version 3.10.8) 91 and Seaborn (version 0.13.2) 92.
