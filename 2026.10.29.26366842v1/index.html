Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). Abstract

Background: DNA methylation (DNAm) signatures of major depressive disorder (MDD) may be related to psychosocial, environmental, biological, and lifestyle risks. We tested a blood-based DNAm depression score (MD-MS) based on an existing EWAS (N = 24,754) in N = 731 individuals for predicting 8 clinical and 16 MDD risk factors and whether different MDD trajectories mapped to changes in MD-MS over six years. Methods: We calculated the MD-MS based on the top 15 strongest, epigenome-wide significant depression associated sites from the largest EWAS to date in EPICv2profiled blood samples from N = 366 NESDA participants at baseline and N = 365 at six-year followup (FU6). Sixty percent of participants obtained a lifetime MDD diagnosis before or during the study timeframe. Linear mixed effect models were used for all analyses. Results: The MD-MS was elevated in lifetime MDD vs. controls (β=0.137, p=0.012). Lower social activity (β=0.051, p=0.043), CRP (β=0.068, p=0.006), triglycerides (β=0.055, p=0.036) and fasting glucose (β=0.059, p=0.025) showed positive associations with MD-MS, while higher neighborhood air pollution levels showed a negative association (β=-0.054, p=0.031). Adjustment for psychosocial variables (social activity, childhood trauma, partner status) attenuated the MD-MS association with lifetime MDD by 17.5% (β=0.114, p=0.051). Longitudinally, individuals with chronic MDD exhibited consistently higher MD-MS compared to controls (β = 0.295, p = 0.001). Conclusions: The MD-MS captures an epigenetic signature of lifetime exposure to MDD. The significant influences of psychosocial and biological factors on this association, although of limited effect size, warrant further research on potentially actionable mechanisms against aberrant depression-related DNAm patterns.

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is one of the most urgent health challenges worldwide, and its partially biological underpinnings are increasingly recognized (1, 2). While individual genetic variation influences personal MDD risk (3), epigenetic factors such as DNA methylation (DNAm) likely integrate MDD-associated risk exposures and behaviors into the organism with the potential to alter gene expression (4, 5). Such changes, measurable in blood immune cells, often correlate with DNAm levels in brain tissue (6). Independently of central nervous system mechanisms, blood immune cell DNAm changes may further predispose to cardiometabolic or other comorbidities in the context of MDD (7) (8) (9) (10). They could occur before clinical MDD onset, as suggested in Shen et al., 2022 (11), or also arise as a consequence of MDD, potentially due to altered lifestyle behaviors or pharmacological treatment (12) (13) (14) (15). Certain DNAm signatures may be indicative of disease outcomes and responsive to specific MDD treatments, but conclusive mechanistic understanding is currently lacking (16) (17) (18) (19). Previous epidemiological studies have identified several psychosocial (20) (21) (22), environmental (23, 24), biological (25) (26) (27) and lifestyle factors (4, 12, 28, 29) which were associated to the presence of MDD, but few have specifically investigated whether they may be involved in shaping the epigenetic signature of MDD in blood (30).

As part of the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium (PGC), Shen et al., 2025 previously conducted a large epigenome-wide association study (EWAS) on major depression (MD), encompassing 24,754 blood samples of individuals of European ancestry from 18 cohorts (31). Of note, training samples included mostly self-reported MD but also clinically diagnosed MDD. From this work, an MD methylation score (MD-MS) was derived, shown to be elevated in individuals with a lifetime history of MD in an independent medical record dataset of N = 8,876 individuals. However, the psychosocial, environmental, biological and lifestyle factors driving elevated MD-MS in psychiatrically healthy and depressed populations, and how such epigenetic risk evolves longitudinally within individuals, remain unknown. Here, we leverage Illumina EPICv2 DNA methylation data from the Netherlands Study of Depression and Anxiety (NESDA (32)), involving a well-characterized sample including healthy controls and individuals with clinically diagnosed MDD, to examine the application of the MD-MS for 1.) detecting lifetime MDD, 2.) capturing a broad range of clinical, psychosocial, environmental, biological, and lifestyle exposures, and 3.) reflecting six-year MDD trajectories and thereby changes in symptomatology. Together, we aim to characterize the responsiveness of the MD-MS blood DNAm signature to MDD symptom changes and time-varying external exposures to better understand the importance of epigenetic factors to eventually improve MDD treatment practice.

Methods

Study design and participants Data are from the Netherlands Study of Depression and Anxiety (NESDA), an ongoing multi-center, observational, naturalistic and longitudinal cohort study examining the course and consequences of depressive and anxiety disorders. A detailed description of the study rationale, design and methods is given previously (32). Briefly, 2,981 adults (18-65 years old) with or without a diagnosis of depression and/or anxiety disorder were recruited from community samples, primary care practices, and mental health organizations between 2004 and 2007. Detailed assessments were repeated after one, two, four, and six years of follow-up. Exclusion criteria were being diagnosed with a current clinically overt psychiatric disorder other than depression and anxiety as well as lacking proficiency in Dutch. Ethical approval was obtained centrally from the Ethical Review Board of the Vrije Universiteit Medical Centre (reference number 2003/183) in Amsterdam, the Netherlands, as well as from the Ethics Review Boards of the other participating research centers. All participants provided written informed consent.

Selection criteria

To be included in this study, healthy controls from NESDA had to have an IDS-SR score < 14 at baseline. Further, only samples with sufficient volumes (> 30 µl) and concentration (> 50 ng/µl) of blood DNA were selected and participants without follow-up blood measurement at FU6 were omitted. Samples were further selected to have a balanced/normal distribution of age, sex, education and childhood trauma. Samples were also chosen to maximize the overlap with other available data modalities within NESDA. Some samples from the healthy control and remitted group were selected at random. Inclusion criteria for depression at baseline were (i) the presence of a DSM-IV MDD or dysthymia diagnosis (or both) in the past 6 months at baseline, established using the structure Composite International Diagnostic Interview AND (ii) confirmation of depressive symptoms in the month prior to baseline either by the CIDI or life chart interview AND (iii) availability of 2, 4 but in any case 6-year follow-up data on DSM-IV diagnosis and depressive symptoms (6-month recency).

Disease course trajectories

For this subsample of N = 731, we selected persons based on MDD status as determined using the DSM-IV based CIDI psychiatric interview that was conducted at baseline and again after 2, 4 and 6 years of follow-up. Three specific MDD trajectories were deliberately represented among the chosen samples: New onset MDD (healthy at baseline, fulfilling MDD diagnosis during one of the NESDA FU waves over 6 years), remitted MDD (depressed at T0, but gets better at 2 years or 4 years and stays better at 6 years, stays better for at least 2 years) and chronic MDD (diagnosed with MDD at both baseline and follow-up, no remission in-between). The healthy trajectory refers to people with no clinical diagnosis of MDD from baseline to FU6 (40% of samples). Lifetime MDD refers to people who have experienced a clinical diagnosis of MDD at any point in their life, including those with a current diagnosis. Hence, this study can be described as an observational, trajectory-based, two-wave longitudinal design with repeated measures. Bisulfite conversion, DNA methylation measurement, quality control and data-preprocessing Bisulfite conversion was performed at Erasmus MC Rotterdam. For this, 500 ng of genomic DNA underwent bisulfite conversion using the EZ-96 DNA Methylation Kit (Zymo Research Corporation, Irvine, CA, USA). Epigenotyping was performed on Illumina Infinium EPICv2 BeadChips (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) on the iScan TM platform in 2025. Baseline and FU6 data were preprocessed together in R. M-values were corrected for sample plate using the ComBat function from the sva R package to account for potential batch effects. For details see Suppl. Information.

Calculation of major depression methylation score EWAS summary statistics have been taken from https://doi.org/10.7488/ds/7929. The MD-MS is a weighted sum between EWAS-derived effect sizes and each of the epigenome-wide significant 15 CpG sites' respective M value. For details see Suppl. Information.

Clinical assessments

The MDD diagnoses and age of illness onset, and anxiety diagnoses were ascertained using structured CIDI interviews. MDD symptom severity was determined using the Inventory of Depressive Symptomatology (IDS-SR) (33). Anxiety symptoms were assessed using the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI-SR) (34). Antidepressant use was determined from unstructured interviews and ATC coded. Chronicity (% of past years spent depressed) was determined with a life chart interview (35).

Other exposure variables In total, 16 factors from four conceptual exposure domains (psychosocial, environmental, biological and lifestyle) were selected based on their relevance for MDD. Of note, we did not observe concerning levels of multicollinearity across our chosen determinants (max. Spearman's r = 0.56 for neighborhood safety ~ neighborhood air pollution, Fig. S1). For detailed information on psychosocial, environmental, biological, and lifestyle variables, which were available for both timepoints, please see Suppl. Information. For missingness in the chosen variables see Table S1.

Statistical analysis All statistical analyses have been conducted in RStudio v2024.12.0 (R v4.4.2) unless specifically stated otherwise. Model covariates were age, age 2 , sex and cell type proportions. For a full list of used R packages and detailed descriptions of Cohen's d and AUC calculation and all model specifications see Suppl. Information.

Results

Major depression methylation score and lifetime MDD diagnosis First, we calculated the major depression methylation score (MD-MS) in our cohort of controls and people with lifetime major depressive disorder (MDD) (Table 1, Table S2). While we use the term "lifetime MDD" throughout this article, many individuals in our lifetime MDD sample actually had a current MDD diagnosis (43% of samples), i.e. an active MDD episode at time of blood sampling, while a recent or past MDD diagnosis was less common and mostly refers to people with remitted MDD at FU6 (17% of samples). Among new onset samples, 74% showed MDD remission before FU6. The duration of current MDD episodes before baseline assessments was different for chronic MDD and remitted MDD groups at baseline (Wilcoxon rank sum test for percentage of time spent depressed in previous 4-5 years: median chronic = 43%, median remitted = 25%, p = 4.42x10 -6 ). Longitudinal depressive symptom severity over the follow-up period is depicted in Fig. S2.

The MD-MS was significantly increased in people with a lifetime MDD diagnosis compared to people who never had any MDD diagnosis (Cohen's d = 0.21 [0.044, 0.374], β = 0.137 [0.03, 0.244], p = 0.012, Figure 1A). In a logistic regression model, we achieved discriminatory power of case versus control of AUC = 0.57 [95%CI: 0.53-0.61] (Figure 1B). Furthermore, we tested the association with overall symptom severity and antidepressant use. The association between current MD-MS and current symptom severity was not significant (β=0.009, p=0.716). The association between current antidepressant use and current MD-MS was comparable to that of lifetime MDD (Cohen's d = 0.23 [0.039, 0.415], β = 0.149 [0.026, 0.272], p = 0.017) (Table 2). In a sensitivity analysis, we focused on subjects with current MDD and examined further clinical correlated exposures in Table 2. None of the clinical phenotypes were associated with MD-MS in this subsample, including depression severity (β = -0.027, p = 0.478) and current antidepressant use (β = 0.045, p = 0.553). No other clinical phenotype showed significant correlations to the MD-MS in neither full nor MDD subsample (MDD age of onset or chronicity, current anxiety diagnosis or symptoms, lifetime anxiety diagnosis). Altogether, we validated the PGC MD-MS within the independent NESDA samples and observed small group-and individual-level discriminatory effects with specificity for the lifetime MDD phenotype and current antidepressant use.

Exposures underlying the association between MD-MS and a lifetime MDD diagnosis Next, we aimed to understand whether the observed association between current MD-MS and lifetime MDD diagnosis could be partially explained by other factors, such as psychosocial, environmental, lifestyle or biological stressors which have been found to influence MDD. First, we assessed the univariate associations of these variables with the current MD-MS (N max =731; N may vary due to data missingness, Table S3; highest is 6% missingness in neighbourhood socioeconomic status). In the psychosocial domain, we observed a positive association between MD-MS and current low social activity (β = 0.051, p = 0.043, Figure 2A). In the environmental domain, we observed a significant negative association between MD-MS and higher current neighborhood air pollution (β = -0.054, p = 0.031, Figure 2B). In the biological domain, we observed three significant positive associations between MD-MS and current CRP (β = 0.068, p = 0.006), triglycerides (β = 0.055, p = 0.036), and fasting glucose levels (β = 0.059, p = 0.025, Figure 2C). In the lifestyle domain, we observed no significant associations between MD-MS and the assessed MDD-risk factors (Figure 2D). We found in full-case analyses (N=654-722) that the association between current MD-MS and lifetime MDD was decreased by 17.5%, 9.4% and 4.9% when adjusting the regression coefficient of lifetime MDD for the psychosocial, environmental or biological domain variables, respectively (Table S4).

We further performed sensitivity analysis using genetic principal components for the main analyses in 660 samples with genetic data, which suggested little influence of ancestry on MD-MS differences (Fig. S3). Interestingly, in a separate cross-sectional covariate-only model, we observed highly significant influences on MD-MS of the chosen covariates age (β = 12.28, p = 1.89x10 -28 ), age 2 (β = -4.59, p = 9.26x10 -7 ), B-cells (β = -0.616, p = 6.32x10 -69 ), CD4T cells (β = -0.564, p = 3.30x10 -61 ), CD8T cells (β = -0.519, p = 6.96x10 -42 ), NK cells (β = -0.211, p = 8.80x10 -8 ), and neutrophils (β = 0.647, p = 9.87x10 -98 ). The influences of sex (β = -0.085, p = 0.361) and AHRR cg-probe DNA methylation percentage as a proxy for smoke exposure (β = 0.094, p = 0.031) were small in comparison (Fig. S4). Since smoking was part of the main analyses shown in Figure 2, we decided to not model it as a covariate but as a variable of interest. To summarize, psychosocial factors, environment, and immuno-metabolic factors partially contributed to the association between current MD-MS and lifetime MDD.

The six-year longitudinal trajectories of MD-MS Finally, we modeled six-year longitudinal changes of the MD-MS, in relation to distinct disease courses and symptom severity changes. Across samples, we analyzed control participants, chronic MDD, remitted MDD, and new onset MDD (Figure 3A, Table 3). First, we saw that the overall effect of visit was significant (β = 0.193, p = 2.44x10 -6 , Table S5), indicating an increase in MD-MS in all groups over the course of six years. Further, there was a significant overall effect of chronic MDD course (β = 0.295, p = 0.001, Table S5). When adding an interaction in the model between visit and group, neither chronic (β = -0.047, p = 0.631), new onset (β = 0.028, p = 0.792) nor remitted MDD groups (β = 0.081, p = 0.343,) presented with different slopes (visit-by-group effects) compared to the control group (Figure 3B, Table S5). Pairwise comparisons per timepoint showed that estimated marginal means for MD-MS were consistently elevated in chronic MDD compared to controls at baseline (β = 0.319, p = 0.002) and FU6 (β = 0.272, p = 0.008). Chronic MDD had elevated MD-MS compared to remitted MDD at baseline (β = 0.222, p = 0.027), but not at FU6 (β = 0.094, p = 0.350). Remitted MDD did not differ from controls at baseline (β = 0.097, p = 0.270), but showed elevated MD-MS at FU6 (β = 0.178, p = 0.044) (Figure 3B, Table S6). When we decomposed IDS-SR for between-and within-person variance in a second longitudinal LMEM adjusted for baseline age 2 , sex and person-mean decomposed cell type proportions, we resolved significance for the (between) person-mean symptom severity (β = 0.081, p = 0.010), but not for longitudinal within-person changes (β = -0.026, p = 0.178, Table S7). In conclusion, these findings suggest that MD-MS differences at baseline remained stable, except for the remitted group. The MD-MS was consistently elevated in chronic disease course over six years. MD-MS increased over time in the whole study population.

Discussion

In this study, we validated the most recent PGC methylation score for major depression (MD-MS, ( 31)) in predicting lifetime major depressive disorder (MDD) in an independent dataset from the NESDA cohort including N = 731 repeated blood DNA methylation measurements at two timepoints six years apart. In particular, we were interested in other factors underlying MD-MS apart from lifetime MDD exposure, and the longitudinal 6-year change of the score in relation to different MDD disease courses. For this, we assessed 8 clinical and 16 exposome factors and their association to the MD-MS in controls and people with lifetime MDD. A higher MD-MS was associated with lower social activity and higher CRP, triglyceride and fasting glucose levels, but MD-MS was lower for high neighborhood air pollution. Longitudinally, the MD-MS increased over the course of six years, independent of MDD trajectory, although people with chronic MDD showed consistently elevated MD-MS over time compared to all other participants.

In our study, people with lifetime MDD had on average higher MD-MS than healthy controls, with low effect size and AUC (Cohen's d = 0.21, AUC = 0.57) comparable to other cohorts using different MD-MS conceptualizations (4, 12, 31) or other biological markers associated with MDD (36) (37) (38). We achieved slightly higher effect sizes and AUC compared to the original publication by Shen et al. (Cohen's d = 0.13, AUC = 0.53) in N = 8,876 individuals potentially due to different case definitions (31). In their testing sample, Shen et al. defined cases as people with ≥ 1 general practitioner (GP) diagnosis of major depression in their electronic health record; controls were defined as people without such entry. In contrast, cases in NESDA were people recruited from GP offices but also secondary mental health care institutions with a clinical diagnosis of MDD based on a psychiatric CIDI interview and included chronic high severity MDD cases, whereas controls reported an IDS score below 14 (i.e., low symptom severity) in addition to the absence of a lifetime MDD diagnosis.

The MD-MS was associated to lifetime MDD but not to current depressive symptom severity (IDS-SR). This might suggest that the epigenetic embedding of depression in the blood methylome is a reflection of stable underlying risk traits or a lasting mark of exposure to clinical depression and its lifestyle and behavioral correlates rather than a dynamic state marker of current disease activity. Clinically, the MD-MS for lifetime MDD was comparatively specific for the phenotype it was trained on, with only current antidepressant use showing similar effect sizes between cases and controls. In analyses focusing on MDD cases, clinical features such as current depressive symptoms, age of MDD onset, time spent depressed before assessments, current anxiety symptoms, current anxiety diagnosis and lifetime anxiety diagnosis did not show significant associations with the MD-MS.

When examining the impact of psychosocial, environmental, biological or lifestyle variables in the relationship between lifetime MDD and MD-MS, the psychosocial domain resulted in the highest reduction in association estimates (-17.5% vs. -9.4% for environmental and -4.9% for biological factors). However, it is important to mention that, unlike current social activity, current partner status and past childhood trauma were not significantly associated with MD-MS in separate models. Current social activity is a summed score of five self-report items from a questionnaire asking about the current frequency of social free time activities such as going to a bar, museum, restaurant, association, amusement park, cinema, club, or sports activity outside of home (from "almost never" to "a couple of times per week"). To rule out that alcohol intake or physical activity was the underlying factor beneath the association of MD-MS and social activity we calculated their correlations and found r of -0.24 and 0.19, respectively (Fig. S1), suggesting that it is more likely that decreased interpersonal contact contributed to the association between social activity and MD-MS. Social stress and its influence on DNA methylation changes have been studied extensively in rodents, primates and humans (39). For example, social isolation and living alone was associated with accelerated blood epigenetic age in men (40). Further, in a recent epigenome-wide association study in U.S. middle-aged twins, 25 suggestive CpG sites from blood were proposed to be linked to self-reported loneliness (41). Among the most studied physiological responses to social stress is increased cortisol production (42) (43) (44). However, studies have so far provided only early evidence regarding associations between cortisol exposure and blood DNA methylation (45, 46), leaving the biological mechanism underlying the association between MD-MS and social factors to be investigated in more detail.

It is noteworthy that CRP, triglyceride and fasting glucose levels showed significant positive associations to the MD-MS while these were uncorrelated to lifetime MDD itself (r between -0.02 and 0.06). This may warrant follow-up research on the potentially different interpretations of the MD-MS in non-depressed people and how it might relate to MDD vulnerability. Interestingly, we did not find an association for IL-6 and MD-MS as was earlier shown in Shen et al. using data from a communitybased birth cohort (N = 875) (31). Further, we observed that both current neighborhood air pollution and current smoking were associated to a lower MD-MS, a correlation which reached statistical significance for air pollution but not for smoking. The fact that both factors decreased the MD-MS, rather than increased it, as could be expected due to their status as risk factors for MDD (23, 24, (47) (48) (49), is challenging to explain mechanistically and may simply be a spurious association observed due to selection (collider) bias in our sample with enrichment of MDD cases (50).

To our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate elevated MD-MS scores in individuals with a clinical diagnosis of MDD compared with healthy controls and to examine these differences longitudinally across two time points six years apart in a large well-characterized clinical sample. This study is therefore also the first comprehensive investigation of 24 risk factors associated with MDD contributing to the MD-MS. Despite these important strengths, several limitations are noted. First, we analyze MDD-related DNAm signatures in whole blood, whose concordance with brain DNAm is debated and may vary between brain regions (51, 52). Second, due to the restriction of our analysis to the top 15 CpGs with epigenome-wide significance, we may have optimized the detection of lifetime MDD versus healthy samples at the cost of detecting associations to lifestyle or environmental factors, which may affect CpG sites at less stringent P-value thresholds. Third, we cannot exclude the influence of other sources of heterogeneity, including differences in sample characteristics (e.g., ethnicity) and technical or methodological factors (e.g., sample freezer time), which may have affected our findings and their generalizability. Finally, we did not correct the reported P-values for multiple testing and therefore underline that the presented results should be seen as descriptive, exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than providing mechanistic explanations. It would be of interest to validate these findings in a targeted way in larger cohorts with comparable data (55).

Further research is needed to better understand whether people with MDD scoring high on MD-MS show different treatment responses or outcomes than people with "low DNAm burden". Finally, we assume that DNAm marks linked to MDD in blood could provide further explanations for comorbidities of MDD with metabolic or cardiovascular health conditions (7), but this needs to be tested in larger cohorts with an enrichment for such conditions. Ultimately, the potential for reversibility of MDD-related DNAm marks and how this affects physical and mental health should be assessed to better understand the clinical relevance of blood DNAm in MDD.

Conclusions

Overall, our study confirms that blood DNAm signatures are a promising molecular readout of the biological embedding of MDD and its associated risk factors. However, its exact place in the etiology of the disorder remains unclear. Our results suggest that social activity and immuno-metabolic mechanisms may partially explain the epigenetic signature of lifetime MDD and could therefore be considered potential targets for its modulation. High DNAm-burden for MDD may further be indicative of chronic MDD courses, although this observation warrants further replication in people with MDD with similar symptom severity and chronicity.
