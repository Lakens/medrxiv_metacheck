Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Introduction

History of VNS Cervical vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) is an established neuromodulation therapy that involves implanting a small pulse generator in the chest to deliver electrical impulses to the left (typically) vagus nerve. The vagus nerve is mixed with a preponderance of sensory fibers. It connects various organs to the brainstem, with outputs into the parasympathetic system and somatic sensory system and plays a crucial role in modulating immune responses through the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway (Mandalaneni & Rayi, 2025). By modulating vagal activity, VNS has shown efficacy in reducing seizures in epilepsy and alleviating symptoms of treatment-resistant depression (TRD). Despite nearly three decades of clinical use, questions persist regarding the role of stigma, ethical concerns, and barriers to access in the uptake of VNS across various mental health conditions. VNS received U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval for epilepsy in 1997 and for Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) in 2005. Its mechanism of action involves altering neuronal excitability through sustained vagal modulation, likely involving polysynaptic actions at multiple brain sites (Vonck & Larsen, 2018). Over 125,000 devices have been implanted for epilepsy and approximately 5,000 for depression, underscoring a notable discrepancy (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?OANaUf. While it offers long-term symptom relief, its adoption in psychiatric conditions is relatively limited, highlighting potential barriers beyond clinical efficacy (Aaronson et al., 2022; O'Reardon et al., 2006) Characteristics of VNS VNS is unique in the field of neurostimulation. Unlike deep brain stimulation (DBS), which penetrates the cranium, VNS surgery is minimally invasive because the surgery is shorter, quicker, and does not involve the brain. Additionally, while transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) generally involves multiple weeks of on-site sessions, VNS offers a more prolonged and sustained course of treatment. Once implanted, a VNS battery can last anywhere from five to 15 years (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?ug8FyJ. VNS has several advantages, including the less invasive nature of surgery, and demonstrated long-term efficacy, particularly in reducing seizure frequency in epilepsy patients and improving symptoms of treatment-resistant depression, sometimes outperforming pharmaceutical interventions (Aaronson et al., 2017; Batson et al., 2022). Though common side effects include voice alterations, difficulty swallowing, and shortness of breath, treatment is generally regarded as safe. Side effects resulting from programming can often be reduced through changes in stimulation parameters. Many patients experience delayed but meaningful benefits, with seizure or depressive symptom relief often becoming apparent over months to years (Aaronson et al., 2022; O'Reardon et al., 2006).

Approximately 60% of epilepsy patients and 40-60% of TRD patients respond to VNS within 2 years (Aaronson et al., 2022; Englot et al., 2016; O'Reardon et al., 2006).

Stigma considerations

VNS sits at the intersection of treatments for both "physical" (epilepsy) and "mental" (depression) conditions, both of which carry distinct forms of stigma (Kwon et al., 2022; Pescosolido et al., 2021; Schnyder et al., 2017). While epilepsy has historically been shrouded in myths of demonic possession, depression remains burdened by the misconception that individuals are personally responsible for their condition, highlighting how stigma manifests in distinct yet equally harmful ways for both disorders. Stigma is both an attitudinal barrier and an ethical concern that significantly impacts medical technology uptake. Mental illness stigma can decrease treatment-seeking and exacerbate social isolation.

Similarly, stigma can lead to fear and feeling of rejection that cause lower levels of treatment seeking in epilepsy which has been linked to lower quality of life and increased seizure frequency (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?lPUFvO Kwon et al., 2022; Mayor et al., 2022). The progress made in reducing stigma around epilepsy may partially account for the lag in adoption of VNS within the mental health field.

Ethical considerations

Ethical questions around informed consent and patient autonomy arise for both depression and epilepsy with some individuals presenting with impaired decision-making capacity. Treatments commonly used to treat these diseases can improve aspects of cognition relevant to decision making for some, but others can experience short-or long-term impairments to decision-making capacity (Goering et al., 2017 (Goering et al., , 2021;; Hendriks et al., 2019; Jotterand et al., 2010; Kwan & Brodie, 2001; Novak et al., 2022; Rock et al., 2014). The need for adequate informed consent processes has been a pillar of medical ethics to date. In addition to treatment decision-making, adequately informing patients of the delayed effects is a central topic. Access concerns relating to travel and resource limitations also raise justice issues: VNS is typically offered at academic medical centers, leaving patients in rural areas or states lacking such centers at a disadvantage (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?lOwnca.

Given that VNS requires frequent in-person visits during the first year to fine-tune the device, exploring how to address barriers to make VNS more accessible is a priority.

Stakeholder considerations

Healthcare decision-making is a two-way process that involves clinicians and patients. As gatekeepers of this implantable neuromodulation therapy, clinicians significantly influence patient access through their knowledge, biases, and willingness to present or refer to VNS as an option. Meanwhile, patients with limited exposure to new VNS research may harbor misconceptions or concerns regarding invasiveness and safety, further shaping decisions (Cabrera et al., 2023).

Study rationale

Despite VNS's clinical longevity, the interplay of stigma, ethical concerns, and limited accessibility remains understudied. As VNS expands to treat conditions such as rheumatoid arthritishttps://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?THZOQv heart failure, fibromyalgia and other chronic medical conditions, (Lerman et al., 2025) , a deeper examination of stakeholder perceptions will guide ethical, practical, and policy-related discussions, ultimately informing better adoption and informed consent practices. Of particular interest is how stigma does or does not develop in the context of disease about which one does not have significant personal knowledge. This study aims to understand stakeholders' perceptions of VNS through reports of healthcare barriers, ethical considerations, perceived acceptability, and knowledge level.

Data Analysis

We conducted a data-driven thematic analysis using Dedoose (Braun & and Clarke, 2006). Two independent coders used the interview guide to inform an initial codebook. As interviews were conducted, new codes were added to reflect emergent concepts. After at least six interviews, data collection continued until two consecutive interviews yielded no new codes, which was our operational definition of thematic saturation. At that point the coders refined the codebook by merging overlapping codes and reclassifying codes that had few occurrences, generally less than 5 occurrences in the set of interviews, into broader thematic categories when appropriate.

Each transcript was first coded by the person who conducted the interview using the original codebook, then re-coded by the non-interviewing coder using the revised codebook to reduce interviewer bias. Both coders jointly coded three transcripts (approximately 15% of the dataset) to assess agreement and refine coding decisions; fewer than three discrepancies per transcript emerged during this process, all of which were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Themes were initially developed independently by each coder and then consolidated collaboratively. The final thematic structure was organized into overarching domains that reflected stakeholder perceptions of VNS. Questionnaire data were exported to RStudio for descriptive and inferential statistical analysis (e.g., t-tests) and integrated into Dedoose to allow for mixed-methods comparisons.

Finally, qualitative themes were compared with quantitative findings to generate a comprehensive understanding of stakeholder perspectives.

Among the 19 interview participants in the final analysis, 70% were Depression stakeholders, and 30% were Epilepsy stakeholders. The mean duration of illness was 27 years (SD = 3.74) for Depression and 5 years (SD = 4.58) for Epilepsy. All eight Depression patients had tried medication, therapy, and light therapy; 63% (n=5) had undergone ECT, 50% (n=4) had tried TMS, 37% (n=3) had utilized residential treatment, and 25% (n=2) had tried Ketamine. The eight clinicians practiced in diverse settings, with 50% working in Academic Health Centers, 25% in Private Practice, 12.5% in Tertiary Care Centers, and 12.5% in Community Practices.

Results

In the following sections, we present our findings on (1) how VNS acceptability and access differ by disease group (epilepsy vs. depression), (2) how clinicians' and patients' perspectives converge or diverge, and (3) whether and why participants would support VNS for new conditions (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis, heart disease). The focus of the result section is on interview data from Phase 2. We integrate quantitative results from Phase 2 (e.g., stigma scales, barrier frequencies) and qualitative insights (focus group/interview quotes) to illustrate these findings. Quotes have been cleaned of typos and other utterances for clarity and fluidity.

Thematic Findings Access and Insurance

One of the most prominent themes was insurance coverage and overall access to VNS. Seventeen discussed access as a key issue, and sixteen of the nineteen interview participants mentioned insurance issues at least once. Depression participants more frequently ranked the cost of treatment as their top barrier, while epilepsy participants focused on side effects or disease minimization. An analysis of overall barrier rankings (p = 0.003) revealed that Clinicians and Patients differ in prioritizing obstacles to receiving VNS. Stigma

Stigma emerged as a critical theme, with quantitative scores showing Depression participants reporting significantly higher experiences of blame or unkindness and scoring higher across stigma questions. In particular, they were substantially more likely to agree with the statement "Some people acted as though it was my fault I have this illness" (p = 0.001) and "Because of my illness people were unkind to me" (p=0.008) after adjusting for multiple comparisons. In total, 13 of 19 participants brought up themes of stigma, spanning all subgroups.

"I am very vocal and open about my mental health struggles, but… people are taught that it's such a shameful thing to have mental health… [it's] not your fault." -Depression Patient Both patients and clinicians noted a cultural bias where psychiatric conditions are sometimes minimized. One Depression participant explained: "I feel like other people might judge you more if you say we're doing it for depression… I think some people for depression would probably be like, 'What are you so sad about that you need an implant?'" -Depression Support Person "Hey, I have epilepsy, and I have this device implanted, there's not really cultural stigma around that. Now, if I say I have depression, I have a device implanted. People are like, "What are you doing?" So there's still stigma around mental health issues, and especially something as big as a surgery." -Depression Clinician Depression patients and their clinicians often felt Depression was not taken as seriously as Epilepsy and worried about being judged for considering an implantable device: "I mean, the epilepsy… it can really be dangerous.

Depression, I have a hard time seeing that the same as a physical condition… just 'Oh, pull yourself up by your bootstraps.'" -Depression Patient "People take epilepsy more seriously; it seems more real." -Depression Clinician Epilepsy patients noted stigma, too, but from a more "medical/physical illness" lens. They sensed less pushback on having a device implanted for a recognized neurological disorder. Clinicians echoed patients' observations regarding mental health conditions being viewed as "less legitimate" or "willed away": "Some think of depression as a choice… that it should be easy to treat if you do psychotherapy or yoga…" -Depression Clinician Participants illustrated the internal and external stigmas associated with seeking a surgical treatment for depression: ": "You know, it's easier if a patient with refractory epilepsy comes to you [for VNS].

But [Depression] patients that have had hundreds of ECT [treatments] and you suddenly say VNS and they say 'whoa, whoa, whoa, I'm not that depressed. What are you talking about? No, no, no surgery, you're crazy.'" -Depression Clinician "I don't think that depression really needs any devices put into your body … I think it's more of you having to deal with it yourself." -Epilepsy Patient Interestingly, a participant's skepticism about VNS for arthritis underscores how new or emerging indications may also confront doubts and stigma about whether a condition is deemed "worthy" of implantable interventions. "I have chronic pain that I deal with and … another reason for participating in [REVEAL] is to see what happens with [pain] as a secondary thing. And when I was talking with the surgeon, he was like "Chronic pain is off the table . Get it out of your head that that's going to improve". So that's why I don't think that [VNS is appropriate for] arthritis." -Depression Patient However, any surgical implant for "invisible" conditions, including RA and HF, could still face residual skepticism, although participants largely believed mental health carried the greatest stigma burden.

Invasiveness and Treatment Order

Nearly all participants acknowledged that VNS is surgically invasive, yet less invasive than penetrating brain surgeries (e.g., DBS, cingulotomy). Both clinicians and patients made the comparison of VNS to a pacemaker, citing its placement in the chest rather than the skull.

"It's certainly invasive… but pacemakers are invasive too." -Depression Patient

"I use the analogy to a pacemaker because most people kind of understand that that goes under the skin and is there in the chest wall." -Epilepsy Clinician "It doesn't seem terribly invasive... sometimes I think drugs are more invasive." -Epilepsy Patient "[Regarding brain surgery] That's like messing with the mainframe, and you just don't mess with the mainframe." -Depression Patient Epilepsy participants often embraced earlier VNS use if seizures were uncontrolled. In contrast, Depression participants noted it "happens very late" in their treatment journey, especially if standard therapies (medications, therapy, TMS, ECT) fail. In our sample the individuals had lived with Depression for an average of 27 years, compared to Epilepsy with an average of 5 years.: "VNS happens very late in the [Depression] patient journey… it's less likely to be as helpful as it is for epilepsy." -Depression Clinician "If I tried 2 or 3 medications …and they're not really seizure free, then we have that conversation [of pursuing surgery]." -Epilepsy Clinician Participants also stressed a treatment order, with VNS typically introduced after several failed medication trials.

There was a general belief that "the level of the treatment must match the level of disease" -Depression Clinician, meaning that the invasiveness of VNS must be relational to the disease it is treating. Participants believed earlier VNS could be advantageous if the disease was irreversible (e.g., RA joint damage), but still felt less-invasive options should be tried first:

You can't turn back the clock on RA, you can only slow it down." -Depression Patient "It shouldn't be the first mode of approach… but it's the insurance company that decides that." -Depression Patient "If medications aren't working… VNS seems like a worthwhile thing to try." -Depression Patient VNS is also potentially more manageable for families and support persons to accept compared to an invasive brain surgery like DBS or a cingulotomy: "My experience is most caregivers don't want [their loved ones] to have brain surgery because they are disabled. And it's really hard to do the work-up. So that's why we end up using the VNS mostly." -Epilepsy Clinician "If they benefit from epilepsy surgery, we go with [that]… if not, then we go with… VNS, because it's been around the longest." -Epilepsy Clinician Clinicians emphasized physical surgical risk: "If you go by invasiveness… medication is less invasive than VNS, because it's not surgery." -Epilepsy Clinician "It's an implanted device… You can have some risk of infections with the surgery, but then it works." -Depression Clinician Patients had a diverse view of invasiveness of VNS compared to other treatments, mentioning that medications and therapy carry their own types of invasiveness. Patients saw VNS as invasive, but sometimes saw ECT as more invasive given personal experience with long term side effects "Some medications can have long lasting impacts … I think most medications wouldn't be as invasive, but maybe some with longer term impacts would be." -Depression Patient "VNS is more physically invasive, but therapy uncovers emotional things that can't be undone..." -Depression Patient "My only parlor trick is my memory… with ECT, that's a big thing you're probably losing." -Depression Patient Moreover, no one saw VNS as a standalone therapy; they expected it to be paired with medications, therapy, or other adjuncts. "It's sort of like they're complementary… you need multiple modalities." -Depression Clinician Quality of Life and Appropriateness of VNS Of the 19 interviewees, nine were asked to consider HF, and 10 were asked to consider RA. For treatmentresistant Depression or uncontrolled Epilepsy, it might be justified; otherwise, a less-invasive approach should be prioritized. "With depression, you are implanting in people who are considered treatment resistant… Are you taking advantage of someone's desperation?" -Depression Clinician "There's such a wide range of how severe you have epilepsy or depression that you'd have to make your decision off of the severity of those before going ahead with it." -Epilepsy Patient Capacity to Consent surfaced repeatedly (14/19 interviews), especially for severe Depression, where cognition or desperation might impair fully informed decisions. Overall, while patients often based their views on perceived disease severity and personal empathy, clinicians underscored the scientific rationale for whether VNS should be offered in the first place.

Hope

Despite financial and logistical hurdles, a strong undercurrent of hope emerged throughout the interviews. Many living with longstanding or treatment-resistant conditions expressed optimism for at least partial relief: "VNS is giving me a little more hope that I can live like this [waves hand], instead of this [nosedive motion]… I would almost do anything to not live that way." -Depression Patient "And when I heard about it being used for depression, I was like, wow, that -that would really be cool." -Depression Patient "They've gotten to this point … that they've exhausted from trying so many different things and kind of. Not getting the results that they need to …live a life worth living …This is another hopeful option." -Depression Patient However, for some Depression participants these hopes were guarded, shaped by prior disappointments with ECT, TMS, or multiple medications:

"I don't put a lot of hope into thinking, 'This is the one'… if it works, it works, and if it doesn't, it doesn't." -Depression Patient "So, that's kind of my mindset is, it's not so much hopelessness, but it's also not putting all your eggs in one basket, you know." -Depression Patient

Epilepsy participants valued the possibility of fewer seizures and better daily functioning without the same feeling of guarded hope.

"If it were a case where … someone was really struggling with it … on a daily basis. Then, yes, I mean [I'd] definitely want to have [VNS]." -Epilepsy Patient Patients described seeking therapy that could improve their life, in a way making "life worth living," while clinicians embraced the notion of hope but emphasized keeping realistic expectations due to the gradual time-to-response. Hope extended to HF/RA, where some participants reasoned that if standard approaches fail, VNS might "offer another hopeful option," especially when QOL is severely compromised.

Discussion

Collectively, these interwoven themes reveal how stakeholders navigate VNS across multiple conditions. While Depression and Epilepsy remain the primary contexts for approved VNS, participants indicated cautious openness toward HF/RA if robust data supports its efficacy and the disease severely impairs daily functioning. Overall, the tension between financial/insurance barriers, mental health stigma, and the potential for symptom relief shaped stakeholder views on whether and how VNS should be adopted. These findings set the stage for a deeper discussion on the implications for clinical practice, policy, and future research.

Our study highlights how stakeholders perceive and adopt VNS across Epilepsy, Depression, and potential new indications (HF/RA). While participants generally recognized VNS's therapeutic value, they also voiced concerns about financial barriers, directly attributed to insurance coverage policies and delayed coverage approval, as well as social stigma, particularly regarding mental health. At the same time, a strong theme of hope prevailed, with many participants viewing VNS as an opportunity for significant improvement in QOL, providing a robust body of evidence supporting its use.

Our findings are consistent with literature suggesting that neuromodulation for psychiatric conditions lags behind more "physical" indications due to greater stigma (Cabrera et al., 2023). In our sample the stigma associated with VNS is rooted in the disease it is treating instead of the treatment itself. Individuals were generally open to VNS as a treatment but their opinions changed based on the disease it was treating. Knowledge of VNS did not differ between the stakeholder groups, reinforcing the notion that differences in attitudes towards VNS is not due to a knowledge deficit. For instance, although Epilepsy also carries some stigma, Depression participants faced higher levels of shame, blame, and hesitancy to undergo an implantable intervention. Within our sample it was unclear whether this stigma was self stigma, externalized stigma, or a mix of the two. This disparity mirrors the broader pattern of device adoption seen with deep brain stimulation (DBS): around 300,000 patients have received DBS implants for Parkinson's disease, while only 300 implants have been performed for obsessive-compulsive disorder (Mocking et al., 2021). Such stark differences reflect how mental health stigma can hinder acceptance and coverage of surgical neuromodulation, and even the funding of research for different diagnoses, despite comparable clinical promise.

The role of insurance coverage emerged as a powerful determinant of VNS adoption. Patients who lacked coverage, often Depression patients, reported waiting months or relying on research studies to access VNS, underscoring the need for cost-effectiveness data to persuade insurers. These results extend prior observations that lack of coverage disproportionately hinders the adoption of invasive therapies for mental health compared to epilepsy or movement disorders (Mocking et al., 2021). Policymakers and insurers might benefit from systematically evaluating the long-term cost savings of VNS, particularly for TRD and emerging conditions.

Going forward, researchers must collect robust, high-quality data that demonstrate VNS's safety, efficacy, and cost-benefit advantage for new indications. Such evidence could help streamline insurance approval pathways, reducing out-of-pocket costs, and broadening patient access. Additionally, efficacy and cost-benefit must be viewed in relation to the disease. As our population attested, Epilepsy is easier to get approval for because the outcome is quantitative; You can observe a countable reduction in seizure numbers from VNS. With Depression, untreated or poorly managed depression is associated with increased risk for substance abuse, chronic conditions, such as cardiovascular disease, and premature mortality (Remes et al., 2021) https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?YPLAld However, it is harder to get at these endpoints because TRD trials are often focused on changes in subjective clinical ratings of patient symptoms. A prime example of this in practice is the RECOVER trial of VNS for TRD. In this trial, VNS for TRD was assessed for QOL benefits through 6 outcome surveys and was deemed to be superior to no stimulation, but this was only true for three of the six surveys (Rush et al., 2024). For outcomes that are less objective, it is harder to identify and prove efficacy of a treatment.

Understanding the unique stigma of a disease is especially crucial for HF and RA, where VNS remains experimental. It is unclear how either disease group would receive VNS as a treatment and what stigma may emerge, and how it will impact uptake. We know that treatment disparities can be rooted in the disease it is treating. For example, the comparatively lower uptake of VNS for Depression mimics the historical pattern seen in DBS for OCD vs. Parkinson's Disease. Clinicians, advocacy groups, and policy initiatives should jointly tackle mental health stigma, ensuring that implantable interventions are not dismissed as "too invasive" for psychiatric disorders, and proactively consider what role stigma may play in VNS for future indications. Educational efforts targeting clinicians and patients, highlighting the physical underpinnings of TRD and the efficacy of VNS, may erode the biases currently dissuading many from considering this option.

Participants consistently stressed that "the level of treatment must match the level of disease." Clarifying where VNS fits the treatment trajectory, especially for new indications, would help integrate it into standard practice. Integrating VNS earlier for irreversible or highly debilitating conditions might prevent years of suffering, provided patients are fully informed and clinically appropriate.

Ethical concerns about the capacity to consent were especially salient for severely depressed individuals. Formal assessments of decision-making capacity and structured consent protocols can minimize the risk of inadvertently taking advantage of patient desperation. However, the due diligence of minimizing risk should be balanced with the time and effort required of potential surgical candidates.

As shown by our study population, individuals considering VNS have often lived with their disease for years, if not decades, having tried numerous other treatments. The consent process should match the capacity to consent of the individual instead of lumping all individuals into a group of high vulnerability. Considerations for how current treatment regimens may be impacting capacity to consent should be explored, acknowledging that fluctuation in consent ability can be influenced by both the disease progression and the medications being used to treat it. Educating patients and potential proxies on the long timeline to VNS response is likewise essential for realistic expectations and adherence. Multiple types of neuromodulation (TMS, DBS, VNS) have some level of delayed response, making the emphasis on time to response central to consent discussions.

Travel distance and reliance on academic centers limit uptake of Neuromodulation treatments, including VNS. Exploring decentralized models such as telemedicine follow-ups, remote programming, training of residents to expand clinician access, or closer collaboration with community clinics may help expand access. An example of this model is the Abbott NeuroSphere Virtual Clinic for neurostimulation devices which allows for routine device checks, programing adjustments, and initiation of new settings through a real time visit done via video call (https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?2E1fgd.) Early data shows that remote care can be consistently and reliably delivered through remote care platforms (Silburn et al., 2022). Providing special training for non-academic clinicians to manage and program VNS would similarly reduce the burden on patients living far from major medical centers.

Limitations

Several factors constrain the generalizability of our findings. First, only one support person ultimately participated, limiting our ability to draw robust conclusions about caregivers' perspectives. This was in part due to low enrollment in the Epilepsy arm and the lack of identified support persons from our Depression participants. The reliance on recruitment from a parent study inherently limited the pace of enrollment. For example, in December 2024 when recruitment for this study was ending, there were 26 depression enrollments in contrast to 2 epilepsy enrollments. Additionally, in asking Depression patients to identify a support person of theirs who they could recommend to the study, most people replied that they felt they didn't have anyone who provided significant support. It is unclear if this is due to stigma surrounding the disease, lack of support experienced by people living with depression, social isolation, or other factors.

In our case, relying on a neuroethics supplement is what allowed us to get this study funded. Limited flexibility in the timing of supplements can create vulnerabilities. If the parent study faces delayed IRB approval, study stoppage due to audits, or slow/low enrollment, it can be difficult for the neuroethics supplement to adjust with limited funds and timing.

Snowy Minnesota winter weather further complicated focus group enrollment and attendance, reducing the number of focus groups we were able to run and limiting participation to only 3 persons per group, which is smaller than normal for focus groups. Our sample may be biased toward individuals already somewhat familiar or open to VNS, especially those in the parent research pipeline. Working within the single site of the parent study also inherently limited the diversity of perspectives given the homogeneity of our clinic sample. Lastly, while we collected insights on HF/RA, these were hypothetical vignettes; actual experiences might differ once VNS is formally tested or approved for those conditions.

Conclusion

To address disparities in VNS uptake within Depression and Epilepsy and reduce disparities for future indications, large, multi-site research is needed to explore VNS's adoption in diverse socioeconomic and geographic settings. Emphasizing telehealth integration to minimize travel burdens and conducting a cost-benefit analysis comparing the long-term costs of treating conditions with standard treatments versus VNS could be particularly impactful. This type of analysis can help determine when VNS becomes a financially viable option for insurance approval. Early focus on robust data and costeffectiveness may ease the path to insurance coverage and bolster confidence among clinicians, payers, and patients.

VNS is widely recognized by clinicians and patients in our sample for its potential to improve quality of life. Yet, it remains underutilized for mental health indications due to stigma, insurance barriers, and ethical concerns regarding the capacity to consent. As VNS expands to new indications like HF and RA, these challenges must be addressed proactively through rigorous efficacy studies, educational campaigns to reduce stigma, and policies promoting equitable access to realize the therapy's benefit fully. Our findings illuminate the complex interplay between clinical, social, and financial factors that shape whether and how VNS is adopted, offering a foundation for more inclusive and evidenceinformed approaches to neuromodulation.
