Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. Full text

Abstract

Although rare non-coding variants are likely involved in psychiatric disorder susceptibility, their contribution is poorly understood. MicroRNAs (miRNAs) regulate gene expression, and targets in the brain include genes involved in neuronal development and synaptic plasticity. Because single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) can alter miRNA processing and target specificity, this can have disproportionate downstream impact on multiple genes affecting pathways that regulate biological processes, making them compelling candidates for mediating psychiatric disorder risk.

We investigated rare miRNA variants in whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data from 25 pedigrees with diagnoses including schizophrenia, psychosis, bipolar disorder, depression, OCD, and substance use disorder. Evidence of segregation with schizophrenia and related psychotic disorders was identified for five rare miRNA variants (two seed-region, one mature-region, and two precursor-region variants), including a SNV in the mature-region of miR-4794, segregating strongly in two unrelated pedigrees and identified in a parent-offspring pair with schizophrenia from a third pedigree. One of these two pedigrees also segregates a seed-region SNV in miR-4423-3p, predicted to significantly impact expression-regulation, causing the loss of synaptic gene-targets associated with psychiatric disorders. Collectively, targets of three of the miRNAs are enriched for schizophrenia rare variant risk genes (p-value: 0.00027): six SCHEMA genes were targeted by miR-96 and four genes by miR-4423-3p.

While further understanding of miRNA function and validation is required, this study demonstrates the value of integrating structural modelling, target-shift prediction, and pathway enrichment to interpret non-coding variants. This highlights the utility of WGS for identifying and investigating rare non-coding variation in psychiatric disorder susceptibility, supplementing exome-based approaches.

Introduction

Psychiatric disorders including schizophrenia (SCZ), bipolar disorder (BD), and major depressive disorder (MDD), are substantially heritable (1), with most cases having a complex aetiology, involving the interplay of genetic and environmental risk (2). Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have revealed hundreds of common genetic risk variants across psychiatric disorders, individually with small effects and collectively explaining a fraction of estimated heritability (e.g. 23% for SCZ) (3) (4) (5).

Studies of rare variants have identified single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and copy number variants (CNVs) that confer larger effects on disorder risk, as either de novo or inherited events (6) (7) (8), but individually these are carried by a small fraction of the total patient population.

Large-scale genomic studies indicate that many psychiatric disorders share a substantial proportion of their genetic architecture, with genes harbouring both common and rare variants being associated with multiple diagnoses (9). This shared architecture implies that genetic factors have pleiotropic effects that may be mediated through their impact on neurodevelopmental and/or regulatory processes (10). Most research has focussed on protein-coding variants, as their likely functional impact is easier to interpret than non-coding variants (5, 11). By contrast, the contribution of higher-level regulatory variation is relatively under-explored (6). Here we investigated the role of miRNA variation on gene regulatory mechanisms. MicroRNAs (miRNAs) represent a major class of post-transcriptional regulators with critical roles in neuronal development, synaptic plasticity, and circuit refinement (12). miRNA genes are transcribed as primary transcripts that are processed into precursor hairpins by the microprocessor complex, followed by dicer-mediated cleavage to generate mature miRNA strands (13). These short non-coding RNAs (~22 nucleotides on average) function by binding partially complementary sequences within target messenger RNAs (mRNAs), leading to translational repression or transcript destabilisation (Figure 1). Variants occurring within different regions of the miRNA hairpin can disrupt distinct stages of this process (14). Variation within the seed region is considered the most functionally impactful, as this can alter target recognition and binding (15). Even a single nucleotide substitution within the seed region can result in the loss of canonical targets while simultaneously creating novel target interactions, substantially altering the miRNA regulatory network. Variants elsewhere within the mature miRNA may also influence target binding affinity or strand selection without completely redefining target specificity. Outside the mature miRNA sequence variation can modify downstream gene regulation by disrupting Drosha or Dicer processing, altering secondary structure stability, or affecting miRNA abundance or half-life.

Because individual miRNAs can regulate hundreds of transcripts sequence variation affecting any stage of miRNA biogenesis or function has the potential to disrupt entire gene regulatory networks (16). In the brain, miRNAs are particularly abundant and dynamically regulated, with well-established roles in neurogenesis, axon guidance, and synapse remodelling (17).

Emerging data support a role for miRNAs and miRNA dysregulation in neuropsychiatric disorders including SCZ (18) (19) (20), MDD (21), BD (22), substance use disorder (SUD) (23), anxiety (24), autism (25), Tourette syndrome (26), post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) (27) and obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD) (28). An exemplar is miR-137, which regulates synaptic function by regulating synaptogenesis, synaptic ultrastructure and synapse function (29) and has been recognized as a crucial controller in a gene expression network implicated in neurodevelopmental processes associated with SCZ (30). miRNAs have also been shown to have the potential to be used as biomarkers (31), with miR-34a and miR-181b repeatedly being observed as overrepresented in the blood of people suffering from psychotic disorders (32). Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in miRNA binding sites have been found to be enriched in GWAS summary statistics for SCZ, BD, MDD and anorexia nervosa, particularly for synaptic gene sets for SCZ and BD GWAS (33). SNPs in miRNA-mRNA targets in genes within dopaminergic, glutamatergic and GABAergic systems have also been associated with increased risk of SCZ (34, 35). Currently miRNA variants are poorly characterised, in part due to limitations in standard variant annotation frameworks and the lack of robust predictive tools comparable to those used for protein-coding genes (36). A recent study by Brusati et al. (2022) used an in silico pipeline to investigate the potential impact of rare (MAF<0.01) miRNA variants in whole genome sequencing (WGS) data from a cohort of unrelated (sporadic) amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (sALS) patients (37).

Extended pedigrees provide a powerful framework for investigating rare, high-impact genetic variation. Unlike population-based case-control designs, family-based studies enable the identification of variants that segregate with traits and allow for the assessment of inheritance patterns within a controlled genetic background (38, 39).

Such designs have proven valuable in identifying rare coding mutations in psychiatric and neurodevelopmental disorders (8,40-44) but have been applied less frequently to non-coding elements such as miRNA genes. Similarly, pedigree studies have been very successful at identifying rare miRNA variants and miRNA dysregulation for a number of Mendelian disorders (miR-96 and autosomal dominant deafness (45, 46); miR-184 and eye diseases (47, 48); miR-204 in Inherited Retinal Dystrophy (49, 50); Retinitis Pigmentosa (51)) and brain disorders through altered regulation of neuronal morphology, neural induction, synaptic function, glutamatergic neurotransmission, adult neurogenesis, and neural progenitor cell proliferation (52, 53).

In this study, we set out to investigate the potential contribution of rare miRNA variants to psychiatric disorders, using 25 ancestrally diverse psychiatric pedigrees. Diagnoses in these pedigrees include mood disorders, psychotic disorders, developmental and behavioural conditions, with many pedigrees having mixed diagnoses. 20 of the 25 pedigrees had previously been examined for rare coding variants, where pedigree specific deleterious missense variants were identified in genes such as SHANK2, SMARCA1, NPY5R and ATP2B2, but non-coding variation, including miRNA mediated regulatory variation, was not investigated (40, 41, 44, 54, 55). 1). Six samples failed sample QC filtering (three were found to be unrelated; and three due to sample-level missingness) and were excluded from analysis (final N=196). Diagnoses in these pedigrees include SCZ, psychosis, BD, MDD, OCD, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and SUD. Pedigrees ranged in size from 4-16 sequenced samples.

miRNA Variant Annotation and Filtering See Suppl. Figure 1 for the full variant filtering pipeline. Genomic locations of human miRNAs were taken from miRBase v22 (all miRNAs excluding "Low Confidence" miRNAs; n=1,900) (56). SNVs and indels overlapping these miRNA regions were extracted and annotated by their position within the miRNA region as either precursor, mature, or seed-region miRNA variants. miRBase classifies miRNA annotations by confidence level (high, low, or not enough data). In this study, we focus on miRNAs with high-confidence annotations, while also exploring those labelled as "not enough data" where additional supporting evidence was identified through manual review.

Population allele frequencies were incorporated using gnomAD v4.1 (57). A gnomADannotated VCF was generated using bcftools annotate, applying population-level allele-frequency fields from the "group max joint" metric. Rare variants were defined as having global max allele frequency < 0.01 in gnomAD v4.1. miRNA Segregation Variants were prioritised based on segregation patterns within each pedigree. Variants present in all affected individuals or obligate carriers and absent from individuals unaffected by any psychiatric diagnosis were classified as highest interest (full cosegregation), followed by those present in all but one affected individual (reduced cosegregation). Where pedigrees had mixed diagnoses, this analysis focused on variants shared by the individuals with genetically-related psychiatric diagnoses, based on a recent factor analysis investigating the genetic relationship of 14 psychiatric disorders (9). Although there is known to be substantial overlap across most psychiatric disorders (see Brainstorm paper), this analysis grouped the disorders into the following five factors: Compulsive disorders (anorexia nervosa, OCD, Tourette Syndrome), Schizophrenia/Bipolar (SB) (SCZ, psychosis, BD), Neurodevelopmental disorders (autism spectrum disorder, ADHD), Internalizing disorders (PTSD, MDD, anxiety) and SUD (cannabis-use disorder, alcohol-use disorder, nicotine dependence, and opioid-use disorder). For example, in pedigree pSB285 WGS data were available for nine individuals (3 with SB, 2 with internalizing disorders (MDD) and 4 with SUD). Segregation of miRNA variants in this pedigree was assessed with respect to the SB disorders (SCZ, psychosis, BD). These diagnoses were classified as main diagnoses, whereas all other psychiatric diagnoses were classified as "other" and not considered as unaffected controls. Consequently, carriage of the candidate variant by these individuals was not required for a variant to be considered segregating within the pedigree.

To provide additional context for variant interpretation, all variants of interest identified within a pedigree were also assessed across the remaining pedigrees. Given that variant prioritisation relied heavily on segregation patterns within families and focused on rare variants, carrier status was evaluated in both affected and unaffected individuals from other pedigrees. This cross-pedigree assessment was performed to identify variants that, despite demonstrating apparent segregation within a single pedigree, may also be present in unaffected individuals from unrelated pedigrees.

Such observations may reduce the likelihood that a variant represents a highly penetrant causal or risk allele. No formal exclusion thresholds were applied based on cross-pedigree carrier status; rather, these data were used to provide additional transparency and context when interpreting the strength of evidence supporting each miRNA variant.

miRNA Target Prediction

Predicted miRNA targets were assessed for both the native sequences and, where a variant affected the seed region and thereby altered the miRNA binding sequence, the corresponding variant sequences. Firstly, mature miRNA sequences were derived from miRBase v22, and variant sequences were generated by incorporating the corresponding nucleotide substitution (58). Predicted targets for each miRNA were identified using miRDB v6.0 (59). Only targets with a stringent miRDB score greater than 80 were included (Suppl. Table 2). Gene set enrichment analysis was performed using the synapse-specific gene ontology resource SynGo v1.3 (60). SynGo performs both biological process and cellular component enrichment analysis for native and variant miRNA target sets, and pathways with a false discovery rate (FDR) adjusted p-value < 0.05 were considered significant (Suppl Tables 3 4 5 6 7 8 9 10).

Predicted Structural Changes

Predicted structural consequences of each variant were evaluated using miRNASNP-v4 (61) and RNAfold from the ViennaRNA Package 2.0 (62), through visual assessment of the change to miRNA structure and calculating change to the Minimal Free Energy (ΔMFE), representing the amount of energy required to form its most stable secondary structure, with more negative values indicating greater structural stability.

Overlap with known schizophrenia risk genes

A set of genes known to be associated with SCZ was generated using the SCZ whole exome sequencing (WES) study SCHEMA II (FDR < 0.05) (63) (Suppl. Table 11). An overrepresentation analysis was performed of miRNA target genes in SCHEMA II using a one-tailed Fisher's exact test (Suppl. Methods).

Results

Identifying miRNA variants of interest

Across the 25 pedigrees (196 individuals), 888 variants (SNVs and indels) were identified in annotated human miRNA regions (see Supplementary Results for further details). Pedigree-level filtering identified five rare miRNA SNVs that showed inheritance patterns compatible with segregation of SB: two SNV with full cosegregation, and three with reduced co-segregation (see Table 1). Apart from miR-4794, which segregated with both SB and SUD in the same pedigree, no miRNAs were identified segregating with the other factors. Four of the variants correspond to high-confidence miRNA annotations, and one (miR-4794) occurred in a less-studied miRNA annotation ("not enough data") (64). The variants mapped to different miRNA structural regions: two were located within the seed region, one in the mature miRNA sequence outside of the seed, and two in the precursor hairpin (Suppl. Figure 2). These positional differences allowed downstream comparison of how structural context relates to changes in target prediction and pathway-level consequences. Each variant was annotated (for both the native and variant miRNA) with data on potential structural changes, SynGO pathways, and whether their gene targets overlap with genes identified in rare variant association studies for SCZ.

Seed region variants (miR-4423-3p and miR-4749-3p)

In pedigree pSB285 from the USA, rs144289108 was identified in the seed region of miR-4423-3p (Figure 2. A; Suppl. Figure 2. A.). This variant is carried by all three SB cases in this pedigree. Investigation of the full pedigree showed that it was also carried by the two individuals with MDD (one an affected obligate carrier), one of the four SUD individuals and an unaffected obligate carrier. rs144289108 is predicted to alter the structure of the miRNA, causing a ΔMFE of +1.7 kcal/mol, a change of +5.8%, reducing RNA stability. The native miRNA has 135 predicted gene targets, 130 of which were lost for the variant miRNA, which gained 225 new targets (Suppl. Table 2).

Discussion

Genetic variation within miRNA genes can alter miRNA biogenesis, stability, and target recognition (14). Even single-nucleotide substitutions in these regions can redirect the miRNA to entirely different targets, potentially causing substantial disruption to sensitive neural regulatory systems. Variants in miRNAs can lead to the loss of the entire miRNA, creation of new seed regions, changes to MFE, miRNA abundance or half-life, and disruption of the balance of 3p and 5p arms (14). The 3p and 5p arms usually contain sequences which target different genes, often depending on tissue type and developmental stage, which is a key evolutionary function (67).

Understanding the role of miRNAs in human disease can lead to clinical benefits, such as novel biomarkers or therapeutic targets (31, 68).

The aim of this analysis was to investigate rare miRNA variants segregating in psychiatric pedigrees and how they impact miRNA secondary structure and target binding. We used a pipeline modelled on the approach presented by Brusati et al.

(2022) to: (i) identify rare miRNA variants from WGS data that segregate with illness in psychiatric pedigrees (Table 1, Figure 2); and (ii) predict the biological effects of these variants on miRNA folding, maturation, and target gene recognition (Suppl.

Figure 2). By integrating inheritance-based filtering with functional prediction (gene set enrichment and pathway analysis), we aimed to overcome some of the limitations inherent in interpreting non-coding variation.

Given the central role of miRNAs in posttranscriptional gene regulation, and the strong prior evidence implicating dysregulated miRNA networks in psychiatric disorders, we predicted that the miRNAs carrying segregating variants would be involved in biological pathways relevant to the synapse and that genes associated with psychiatric disorders are likely to be miRNA target genes. WGS followed by bioinformatic filtering and variant prioritization identified five rare miRNA variants. These all segregated with the SB diagnosis factor (SCZ, psychosis or BD) and were also found to be carried by additional family members with other psychiatric diagnoses such as MDD and SUD. No miRNA variants were identified segregating within the pedigree predominantly affected by Compulsive disorders (OCD).

Of particular note, the variant rs191156964 in miR-4794 segregated in two independent pedigrees in our cohort, pSB285 and K1561 (Figure 2. C), carried by all eight SB cases (13 of the 14 cases with any psychiatric diagnosis) and two unaffected parents (obligate carriers) across these two pedigrees. This variant was also found to be carried by a parent-offspring pair with SCZ in a third independent pedigree (Suppl.

Figure 4). rs191156964 is located within the mature miRNA region of miR-4794 and is predicted to lead to a 10% increase in stability, potentially limiting miRNA abundance and raising the possibility of altered strand selection or changes to positioning in precursor miRNA machinery. miR-4794 is located in intron 2 of CACHD1, which has been associated with neurodevelopmental disorders (69) and is a regulator of presynaptic functions (70). Intragenic miRNAs such as miR-4794 are often cotranscribed with their host genes and thought to regulate them by both direct and indirect effects (71) CACHD1 is a predicted target of miR-4794 (Suppl Table 2) (72,73), suggesting a potential biologically relevant function for this miRNA, warranting further investigation.

In pedigree K1527 the miR4749-3p variant rs147943327 was found to segregate in four of the five SCZ cases (absent in controls). This variant was also identified in subbranches of three additional pedigrees, in total comprising five affected individuals (three SCZ cases, one BD and one MDD) and two unaffected parents (obligate carriers). rs147943327 is predicted to increase miRNA stability, which can also increase the miRNA half-life and affect temporal expression (74). While miR-4749 is has been shown to be expressed in the brain, especially during prenatal brain development (75) and in the central nervous system (76), SynGO analysis showed no significantly over-represented terms in either the native or variant miR-4749-3p predicted targets.

Predicting the potential biological consequence of miRNAs is limited by a number of factors, including the relative lack of knowledge around the functional consequences of variants outside of the seed region. Much like how the change to protein structure and function can be more easily predicted for missense and protein truncating variants in protein-coding genes, variants within the seed region of miRNAs can be more easily assessed than variants outside the seed region by predicting how their gene targets are altered. For example, the variant rs144289108 in miR4423-3p, segregating in pSB285, was predicted to lead to a dramatic shift in targets and regulated pathways, changing 96% of its gene targets (130 targets lost, 225 gained). These gene-level changes also led to changes in synaptic GO enrichment of the predicted target genes, with only four of the fourteen SynGO terms remaining significant for the variant form of this miRNA, along with an additional term, regulation of post-synaptic membrane potential. Further examination of the 225 novel targets for the variant form of miR-4423-3p by Panther GO analysis revealed a gain of statistically significant pathways (FDR < 0.05), including "regulation of the canonical Wnt signalling pathway" and "regulation of neurogenesis". These pathways are key regulators of neurodevelopment and have been consistently associated with SCZ and BD (77, 78). This suggests that re-wiring of target networks may do more than reduce native miRNA function (i.e. lossof-function), but could also redirect regulatory activity toward novel gene sets (i.e. gainof-function) (79) leading to altered function in the brain. Taken in conjunction with its 5.8% decreased stability, targeting of novel pathways supports both rs144289108 and miR-4423-3p as candidates for impacting risk for psychiatric disorders in this pedigree.

While studies have identified an enrichment of miRNA targets in GWAS (33) the opportunity to investigate the role of rare miRNA variants has been hindered by the fact that rare variant association studies typically rely on WES, not WGS, meaning miRNAs are excluded from the study design. However, the function of miRNAs can also be assessed through their regulation of known psychiatric risk genes. Of particular relevance is SCHEMA, which meta-analysed WES data from 24,248 schizophrenia cases and 97,322 controls to identify genes enriched for ultra-rare protein-coding variants conferring risk for schizophrenia. A recent update of the SCHEMA browser (SCHEMA II: 87,959 cases and 150,587 controls) identified 40 genes (FDR<0.05), 30 of which are novel associations not identified in SCHEMA I (6). This suggests that as the sample size increases, more genes conferring risk to schizophrenia will be identified. It is intriguing that ten of the FDR-significant SCHEMA II genes were found to be targets of three of the miRNAs investigated in this study. Over-representation analysis showed this to be a statistically significant enrichment of SCHEMA genes in the predicted targets of the miRNAs in this study (p-value: 0.00027) (See Suppl.

Fig. 12). Of particular note, both miR-96-5p and the variant form of miR-4423-3p are predicted to target multiple SCHEMA II genes (six and four respectively) (Table 2; Suppl. Fig. 11), suggesting biological mechanisms by which these rare miRNA variants may be contributing to psychiatric aetiology in these pedigrees.

miR-96 is associated with autosomal dominant deafness (45, 46), and the miR-96 precursor variant rs41274239 was found to segregate in three of the four sequenced cases in pedigree p1250 (2 SCZ; 1 SUD) (Figure 2. E). This variant has a CADD score of 20.1, placing it in the top 1% of deleterious variants in the human genome, including protein altering variants. Precursor variants are predicted to alter hairpin structure and/or stability, a region increasingly recognised as functionally sensitive (80), with potential effects on miRNA processing and abundance. This suggests the functional consequences of the miR-96 variant in this pedigree may be related to changes to the regulation of its predicted gene targets, including SCHEMA genes.

In pedigree K2159 the precursor variant rs146892675 in miR-199b, was identified in three of the four SB cases (two SCZ and one BD). This variant was also identified in three affected individuals (one BD and two MDD) in a Scottish pedigree (Suppl. Figure 5). rs146892675 has a 16.6 CADD score, placing it in the top 2.5% of deleterious variants. miR-199b has distinct 3p-and 5p-arm targeting patterns, suggesting that the variant may cause structural changes that could influence strand selection or arm usage rather than target identity. However, both arms are predicted to target the SCHEMA gene RFX3, also targeted by the variant form of miR-4423-3p. RFX3 is a high confidence risk gene for ASD (https://gene.sfari.org/database/human-gene/RFX3). De novo and rare inherited deleterious variants in this gene have also been associated with intellectual disability and ADHD (81).

Several SCHEMA genes predicted to be targeted by these miRNAs are associated with multiple psychiatric and neurodevelopmental disorders: TRIO is also a neurodevelopmental disorder gene (82, 83); SLF2 (also known as FAM178A), a DNA damage repair gene predicted to be a target of two of the miRNAs (miR-199b-5p and miR-96-5p), was identified in a GWAS meta-analysis of alcohol consumption in UK Biobank and AlcGen and CHARGE+ (84, 85); STAG1 is a neurodevelopmental disorder gene (OMIM 617635)(86) with additional support for SCZ from WES study of >4.5K new SCZ cases (87).

Comparing the predicted gene targets of the five miRNAs, including the variant forms, found that most genes were targeted by individual miRNAs (Suppl. Figure 9).

However, as two different miRNA variants in this study segregated in pedigree pSB285 (miR4432 and miR-4794) we chose to compare their predicted gene targets and identified five genes targeted by both miRNAs (APPL2, SORT1, FAT1, RSBN1L, and SPATA5L1). Intriguingly, one of these genes, SORT1, is also a predicted target of miR96-5p, one of only sixteen genes to be targeted by three of the miRNAs from this study. While none of these miRNA's targets are SCHEMA genes, SORT1 is a genomewide significant hit for general cognitive ability (meta-analysis p-value 1.32E-09) (88) and Alzheimer's disease (meta-analysis p-value 1.13E-09 (89); meta-analysis p-value 7.5E-09 (90)).

While the convergence across variants segregating with SB was limited at the level of individual gene targets, there was greater convergence at the level of biological processes, particularly synaptic pathways. This supports a model in which genetic heterogeneity converges on shared functional systems rather than specific genes, consistent with broader findings in psychiatric genetics (91). However, as predictive methods often include false positive miRNA targets, we acknowledge that it is better to focus on shared pathways of target genes rather than specific target genes, and that functional validation would be required to confirm that SORT1 is a target of miR-4794, miR96-5p and the variant form of miR-4423-3p.

These results, while supporting a potential role for rare miRNA variants in the major psychotic disorders (SZ, BP and psychosis), have a number of caveats that need to be acknowledged. These pedigrees are small, meaning that they are under-powered for linkage analysis. Due to the relatively under-studied nature of miRNAs, appropriate training data to generate models for predicting deleteriousness of miRNA variants is not available for statistical approaches. Rare variant association studies are also not currently designed to investigate miRNA variants, which require WGS instead of WES.

Therefore, the segregation and functional prediction of these variants, while interesting, do not by themselves suggest causation. In addition, miRNA target prediction is probabilistic and requires functional validation to confirm. Individual gene targets may be erroneous, which is why gene ontology and gene set enrichment analysis are better suited to supporting functional effects of miRNAs. Despite these limitations, this study provides a unique investigation into rare miRNA variants in psychiatric disorders, taking advantage of pedigrees to identify candidate miRNAs that are suitable for further investigation. This study also highlights the value of WGS and allowing the full spectrum of genomic variants, including non-coding variation, to be investigated.

Conclusion

We have identified five miRNA variants segregating across five unrelated psychiatric pedigrees with schizophrenia/bipolar disorder. Notably, one of these miRNA variants, in miR-4794, was found to segregate in two unrelated pedigrees (pSB285 and K1561), while pedigree pSB285 was found to segregate a second miRNA variant in miR-4423-3p. Four of the five miRNA variants were found to be carried in sub-branches of eight of the twenty-five pedigrees investigated, in total representing ten additional affected individuals, three unaffected parents (obligate carriers) and one individual with unknown diagnosis. The presence of pedigree-specific variants suggests rare, potentially high-impact events might be contributing to illness within individual families.

In parallel, recurrent variants may represent more generalisable risk factors and suggest potential involvement of shared molecular mechanisms particularly underlying overlapping SZ and BD susceptibility, despite incomplete segregation in some of these pedigrees.

In silico prediction tools showed that these five variants exert multifaceted functional consequences, including extensive changes to gene targets, potential disruptions to secondary miRNA structure, and enrichment in synaptic gene ontology categories, many of which have previously been associated with SCZ and other psychiatric disorders (78). Significantly, FDR-significant SCHEMA II genes were found to be overrepresented in the gene targets of these miRNAs.

These findings collectively implicate miRNA variation as a contributor to psychiatric disorder risk in familial contexts and highlight the importance of non-coding regulatory elements in disease genetics. This further highlights the importance of whole-genome analyses, rather than targeting protein-coding and protein altering variants as is the case for most psychiatric rare variant association studies. Although replication of these findings is necessary, there are currently insufficient publicly available datasets to enable robust independent validation. Further functional validation and expanded cohort analyses will be required to determine the extent of pathogenicity and to clarify the mechanistic impact of these variants on neuronal regulatory networks.
