Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Material hardship represents one of the most salient forms of early-life adversity (Mersky et al., 2021) and often shapes the organizing structure of children's early environmental experiences (Conger, 1981; Gagnon et al., 2026; Rodems & Shaefer, 2020). Unlike incomebased indicators of socioeconomic status, material hardship directly measures, rather than infers, a household's lived experience of poverty by assessing a household's ability to afford necessities such as food, stable housing, utilities, and medical care (Liu et al., 2024). Exposure to significant material hardship may be especially consequential in early childhood, as the body undergoes rapid developmental changes that are uniquely sensitive to physiological stress exposure (Gabard-Durnam & McLaughlin, 2019; Sisk & Gee, 2022). Material hardship may influence long-term health partly through dysregulation of stress-response systems such as the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, systemic inflammation, and cardiometabolic functioning (Oh et al., 2018; Wesarg et al., 2020), and has been consistently associated with long-term physical and mental health problems (Gagnon et al., 2026; Rodems & Shaefer, 2020). Yet, much of this work has focused on direct associations between material hardship and developmental outcomes, often under examining the proximal processes through which material hardship shapes children's daily experiences.

Parenting represents one of the most proximal and developmentally salient contexts through which children experience, interpret, and regulate stress (Darling & Steinberg, 1993; Taraban & Shaw, 2018). Thus, material hardship may become biologically embedded not only through children's direct exposure to deprivation, but also through its effects on the caregiving environment. The Family Stress Model provides a useful framework for understanding this process, proposing that economic pressure strains caregivers' psychological and emotional resources which increases parenting-related stress and subsequently shapes caregiving behavior and children's developmental outcomes (Conger, 1981; Masarik & Conger, 2017). Although this model has been widely used to explain children's behavioral and emotional adjustment (Masarik & Conger, 2017), it has yet to be extended into biological aging outcomes. This is an important omission because parenting behaviors may function as repeated stress-regulatory experiences through which children learn whether their environments are predictable, threatening, or supportive. Thus, parenting may represent a modifiable leverage point in the biological embedding of stress in early childhood.

Epigenetic Aging

One approach to examining the biological embedding of environmental stress is through epigenetics, which refers to biochemical modifications that regulate gene expression without altering the underlying DNA sequence (Aristizabal et al., 2020; Horvath, 2013; Kusters & Horvath, 2025). Unlike static genetic variation, epigenetic patterns are dynamic and reflect both intrinsic developmental processes and interactions between biological systems and lived experience (Aristizabal et al., 2020). One of the most studied epigenetic mechanisms is DNA methylation, a process through which methyl groups attach to specific regions of DNA (CpG sites) and influence which genes are more likely to be expressed (e.g., "turned on or off") (Horvath, 2013; Kusters & Horvath, 2025). Because DNA methylation profiles shift in systematic and highly predictable ways across the lifespan, they can be used to estimate biological age through epigenetic "clocks" (Horvath, 2013; B. S. McEwen, 2013; Raffington, 2024) which are increasingly recognized as informative biomarkers of aging and age-related biological processes (Kusters & Horvath, 2025).

Epigenetic clocks differ in how they operationalize biological aging and therefore should not be treated as interchangeable measures of a single aging process. Broadly, these clocks compare an individual's methylation profile to normative age-related patterns (Horvath, 2013), but they operationalize biological aging in conceptually distinct ways. These differences reflect the biological processes on which they were trained, the developmental periods for which they are most appropriate, the population contexts in which they were derived and validated, and their clinical utility as biomarkers of aging-related risk (Horvath, 2026; Sehgal et al., 2026). Recent evidence demonstrates that these distinctions have meaningful consequences for biomarker performance. Across 51 longitudinal intervention studies, second-generation epigenetic biomarkers trained to capture mortality risk or pace of aging were more consistently responsive to interventions than first-generation clocks designed primarily to predict chronological age, with technically reliable measures showing particularly strong responsiveness (Sehgal et al., 2026).

These findings suggest that epigenetic aging measures may capture distinct dimensions of biological change rather than interchangeable estimates of a unitary aging process.

Age-based and pace-based measures illustrate this distinction. For instance, epigenetic age acceleration (EAA) reflects the extent to which an individual's estimates biological age differs from their chronological age, such that positive age acceleration indicates an older-than-expected methylation profile (Horvath, 2013). In contrast, pace-of-aging indices estimate the rate at which physiological systems are changing over time rather than a deviation from chronological age (Belsky et al., 2022). Thus, higher pace-of-aging estimates reflect a faster rate of biological change relative to the reference population on which the measure was calibrated (Belsky et al., 2022). Importantly, although these biomarkers are associated with morbidity, mortality, and other aging-related outcomes, no epigenetic clock has yet been established as a validated surrogate endpoint for clinical aging outcomes (Horvath, 2026). Accordingly, epigenetic aging indices are best conceptualized as complementary biomarkers of underlying biological processes rather than deterministic measures of health or aging.

Epigenetic Aging and Material Hardship

A growing body of research suggests that adverse and socioeconomically disadvantaged early environments are associated with variation in epigenetic aging across the lifespan. Metaanalytic and longitudinal evidence indicates that greater exposure to childhood adversity is associated with EAA and faster pace-of-aging estimates, although associations vary across the type of adversity and epigenetic measure examined (Marini et al., 2020; McCrory et al., 2022; Russell et al., 2026). Early-life socioeconomic disadvantage has likewise been associated with altered epigenetic aging in children and adults (Raffington et al., 2021; Reed et al., 2026).

However, much of this literature relies on broad indicators of socioeconomic adversity rather than material hardship specifically, leaving less understood about how families' lived experiences of unmet basic needs become biologically embedded.

Emerging developmental research further suggests that variation in epigenetic aging is detectable early in the lifespan. Studies applying pediatric epigenetic clocks, such as the Pediatric-Buccal-Epigenetic (PedBE) clock, indicate that biological aging trajectories may begin to diverge in childhood and may be sensitive to variation in children's environmental experiences (McEwen et al., 2019; Raffington et al., 2021). Yet, despite growing evidence that adversity is associated with EAA, less is known about the proximal developmental processes through which these associations emerge. This limitation is especially important because broad indicators of adversity may obscure the family-level mechanisms through which stress is repeatedly experienced, regulated, and biologically embedded.

Parenting Under Material Hardship

Parenting under conditions of material hardship is unlikely to change uniformly across caregiving domains. Consistent with the Family Stress Model, economic pressure may increase parenting stress by straining caregivers' emotional and cognitive resources, thereby altering the quality of everyday parent-child interactions (Masarik & Conger, 2017). Under conditions of financial instability, competing demands, and limited support, elevated parenting stress may make warm, responsive caregiving more difficult to sustain and may increase the likelihood of hostile or punitive responses during parent-child conflict (Deater-Deckard, 1998; Niu et al., 2018). At the same time, stress may not uniformly reduce behavioral control. Behavioral control refers to non-harsh efforts to structure children's behavior through rules, monitoring, explanations, consequences, and limit-setting and is conceptually distinct from psychologically controlling or coercive parenting (Grolnick & Pomerantz, 2009; Grusec & Davidov, 2010). In disadvantaged or unpredictable environments, maintaining behavioral structure may serve a protective or adaptive function by helping caregivers establish clear expectations and promote children's safety within otherwise constrained contexts (Brody & Flor, 1998; White et al., 2013).

More broadly, parenting reflects not only parents' responses to environmental stress but also a central socialization process through which children develop expectations about their environments and learn how to regulate their emotions and behavior within them (Grusec & Davidov, 2010).

Distinguishing warmth, behavioral control, and hostility is important because these dimensions may provide qualitatively different stress-regulatory experiences for children. Warm, responsive caregiving can promote emotional security and support children's developing capacity for emotional and physiological co-regulation (Eisenberg et al., 2005; Grusec & Davidov, 2021). Behavioral control, when implemented without harshness or psychological coercion, may support regulation by providing clear expectations, predictable contingencies, and external scaffolding for children's developing self-control (Grolnick & Pomerantz, 2009; Grusec & Davidov, 2021). Parental hostility, in contrast, may increase children's exposure to interpersonal threat and physiological arousal through harsh, coercive, or unpredictable parentchild interactions (Chen et al., 2015; McKee et al., 2007; Whittle et al., 2022). Importantly, these behaviors do not fall along a single positive-to-negative continuum; supportive and harsh parenting practices can coexist within the same caregiving environment (Parent et al., 2016).

Emerging epigenetic evidence further suggests that caregiving experiences may contribute to biological aging. Supportive parenting has been associated with slower epigenetic aging and may buffer the biological consequences of adversity under some environmental conditions (Brody et al., 2016; Merrill et al., 2024; Metrailer et al., 2026; Sullivan et al., 2023 Sullivan et al., , 2024)). For example, longitudinal findings from the Future of Families and Child Wellbeing Study (FFCWB) indicated that higher observed positive parenting (e.g., warmth, responsiveness) was associated with a slower adolescent pace of aging, with the slowest aging observed among youth exposed to both higher positive parenting and lower community threat; this protective association was attenuated under conditions of high community threat (Metrailer et al., 2026).

These findings suggest that caregiving may shape biological aging in context-dependent ways, but broad measures of positive parenting may obscure which specific dimensions of the caregiving environment are most consequential for biological embedding.

The Current Study

The present study extends the Family Stress Model into the domain of biological embedding by examining whether parenting processes serve as proximal pathways through which material hardship is associated with children's epigenetic aging. Specifically, the study tests a developmental pathway in which material hardship is associated with greater parenting stress, which in turn predicts three distinct dimensions of parenting: warmth, behavioral control, and hostility. These parenting dimensions are then examined as unique predictors of EAA and pace of aging. By distinguishing among emotional support, behavioral structure, and punitive or harsh caregiving, the present study moves beyond broad positive-versus-negative parenting composites to test whether qualitatively different caregiving behaviors are differentially associated with biological change.

Consistent with the Family Stress Model, greater material hardship was hypothesized to be associated with greater parenting stress. Greater parenting stress was expected to be associated with lower warmth, greater behavioral control and greater hostility. In turn, greater warmth and behavioral control were hypothesized to be associated with lower EAA and a slower pace of aging, whereas greater hostility was hypothesized to be associated with EAA and a faster pace of aging. Because PedBE and DunedinPACE index conceptually distinct dimensions of epigenetic aging, associations were examined separately across both outcomes rather than assuming equivalent effects across epigenetic measures. Finally, indirect effects were tested to evaluate whether material hardship was associated with epigenetic aging through parenting stress and subsequent caregiving behaviors, consistent with a developmental pathway of biological embedding.

Methods

Participants

Data were drawn from the FFCWB study, an ongoing longitudinal study of 4,898 families recruited from 20 large U.S. cities (Reichman et al., 2001). Families were recruited following the focal child's birth between 1998 and 2000, with nonmarital births oversampled at a ratio of approximately 3:1. This sampling design resulted in a cohort enriched for single-parent and socioeconomically disadvantaged families. The present study included 1,971 children with epigenetic data available at age 9. Data collected when children were ages 3 and 5 were used to assess material hardship, parenting stress, and parenting behaviors, and age-9 saliva samples were used to assess epigenetic aging. Full demographic information can be found in Table 1.

Procedure

Data for the present study were collected at birth and when the focal children were approximately 3 (2001-2003), 5 (2003-2006), and 9 (2007-2010) years of age. At each wave, primary caregivers completed in-home assessments addressing family and household characteristics, health, caregiving experiences, and parenting practices. Trained interviewers also completed observational assessments of the parent-child environment. At age 9, saliva samples were collected from focal children for DNA methylation analysis. All original FFCWB datacollection procedures were overseen by the Princeton University Institutional Review Board. Questionnaire, the 1997 and 1999 New York City Social Indicators Surveys (Social Indicators Survey Center, 1997 , 1999), and measures developed for the Family Well-Being and Welfare Reform in Iowa study. Primary caregivers responded to 10 dichotomous items assessing financial and housing-related difficulties experienced during the previous year, including difficulties paying bills, food insecurity, housing instability, and unmet basic needs. Items were coded such that higher scores indicated greater hardship and were modeled as indicators of a latent material hardship construct.

Measures

Preliminary confirmatory factor analyses (CFAs) were conducted using a diagonally weighted least squares estimator. Two rare and conceptually distinct items were excluded because of weak factor loadings and poor contribution to the measurement model (see the Supplementary Appendix for more details). The final latent construct included eight indicators.

In the final measurement model, the final latent material hardship factor demonstrated adequate fit (CFI = .97, TLI = .96, RMSEA = 0.04, SRMR = 0.08) with factor loading ranging from .39 to .76.

Parenting Stress.

Parenting stress was assessed when children were 5 years old using four items derived from the Child Development Supplement of the Panel Study of Income Dynamics. Items assessed caregivers' feelings of being overwhelmed, frustrated, or burdened by parenting responsibilities and were rated on a 4-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (strongly agree) to 4 (strongly disagree). Items were reverse coded as needed so that higher scores reflected greater parenting stress. The one factor model demonstrated adequate fit (CFI = .99, TLI = .98, RMSEA = 0.07, SRMR = 0.02) with factor loadings ranging from .61 to .74.

Parenting Dimensions.

Three conceptually distinct dimensions of parenting-warmth, behavioral control, and hostility-were examined at ages 3 and 5. Age-3 parenting constructs were included as baseline controls for their corresponding age-5 dimensions, allowing age-5 parenting to reflect variation above and beyond earlier levels of the same caregiving behavior.

The final SEM therefore included separate latent factors for warmth, behavioral control, hostility at both developmental waves.

Warmth.

Warmth was assessed through interviewer observations derived from the Home Observation for Measurement of the Environment (HOME) Scale (Bradley & Caldwell, 1984).

The HOME assesses characteristics of the caregiving environment through standardized observations made during the in-home assessment. Items were coded dichotomously to indicate whether specific supportive parent-child behaviors were observed. Three indicators were available at age 3 and five indicators were available at age 5. Higher scores reflected greater observed parental warmth and responsiveness. Standardized factor loadings ranged from .83 to .93 at age 3 and from .76 to .90 at age 5, supporting the coherence of a latent warmth construct.

The final SEM specifically modeled the three age-3 indicators and five age-5 indicators as separate latent warmth factors.

Behavioral Control and Hostility.

Behavioral control and hostility were assessed using items from the Parent-Child Conflict Tactics Scale (CTS-PC; Straus et al., 1998). Primary caregivers reported how frequently they had used each parenting behavior during the previous year on a 7-point scale ranging from 0 (never) to 6 (more than 20 times). Behavioral control items reflected non-harsh strategies used to structure and guide children's behavior, whereas hostility items reflected harsh, coercive, or psychologically aggressive responses. Four indicators were used to define behavioral control, and six indicators were used to define hostility at both ages 3 and 5. Standardized factor loadings ranged from .60 to .77 for behavioral control and from .57 to .80 for hostility at age 3. At age 5, loadings ranged from .66 to .78 for behavioral control, with the hostility items similarly demonstrating substantive loadings on their intended factor.

The distinction between behavioral control and hostility was supported by a preliminary CFA. A two-factor model separating the parenting dimensions fit significantly better than a one-factor model in which all items were constrained to load on a common parenting factor, Δχ²(1) = 356.34, p < .001, supporting their treatment as related but distinct caregiving dimensions.

Epigenetic Aging.

Saliva samples were collected during age-9 in-home visits by Westat Inc., the FFCWS data-collection subcontractor, using Oragene® DNA Self-Collection kits (OGR-500; DNA Genotek Inc.). DNA methylation was assayed using the Infinium HumanMethylation450 BeadChip and Infinium MethylationEPIC BeadChip arrays (Illumina) according to manufacturer protocols. Quality-control procedures excluded samples with outlying methylation or bisulfite-conversion values. Cell-type proportions were estimated from saliva DNA methylation data using established reference-based procedures for pediatric samples (Middleton et al., 2022). Two complementary measures of epigenetic aging were examined: PedBE epigenetic age acceleration and DunedinPACE.

PedBE Epigenetic Age Acceleration (EAA).

EAA was assessed using the Pediatric Buccal Epigenetic Clock (PedBE; McEwen et al., 2020), an age-based methylation estimator developed specifically for pediatric oral tissue. PedBE EAA was operationalized as the residual variation in estimated methylation age after accounting for chronological age, such that positive values indicated an older-than-expected methylation profile and negative values indicated a younger-than-expected profile. Buccal epithelial cell composition was incorporated into the estimation procedure as recommended.

DunedinPACE. Pace of biological aging was assessed using DunedinPACE (Belsky et al., 2022), a DNA methylation biomarker developed from longitudinal change across multiple physiological systems. Unlike age-based clocks, DunedinPACE estimates the rate of biological change rather than deviation between estimated and chronological age. Values are scaled relative to a pace of approximately 1 biological year per chronological year, with higher values reflecting a faster estimated pace of aging. The present study used the most recent DunedinPACE specification available in the FFCWB dataset, PoAm45.

Statistical Analysis

Structural equation modeling (SEM) was conducted in R using the lavaan package to evaluate the hypothesized developmental pathway linking material hardship, parenting stress, parenting dimensions, and epigenetic aging. Models were estimated using the weighted least squares mean-and variance-adjusted (WLSMV) estimator because the latent measurement model included categorical and ordinal indicators. Pairwise present data were used to accommodate missingness. The final analytic model included 1,971 participants.

Latent variables were specified for material hardship, parenting stress, warmth, behavioral control, and hostility. Parenting behaviors were modeled at ages 3 and 5, with each age-5 parenting dimension regressed on its corresponding age-3 factor to account for earlier levels of the same behavior. PedBE EAA and DunedinPACE at age 9 were then regressed simultaneously on all three age-5 parenting dimensions, parenting stress, and material hardship. Results

Preliminary Analyses and Model Fit

The hypothesized structural equation model demonstrated acceptable-to-good fit to the data, χ²(867) = 2198.54, p < .001, CFI = .94, TLI = .94, RMSEA = .03, 90% CI [.026, .029], and SRMR = .06. Although the chi-square test was significant, this is common in large samples; the remaining fit indices indicated that the proposed model adequately represented the observed data.

Structural Associations

As hypothesized, greater material hardship was associated with greater parenting stress, β = .35, p < .001. In turn, greater parenting stress was associated with greater behavioral control, β = .10, p = .008, greater hostility, β = .20, p < .001, and lower warmth, β = -.21, p < .001, after accounting for earlier levels of each parenting dimension. Material hardship was not directly associated with any age-5 parenting dimension once parenting stress and baseline parenting were included in the model.

Parenting dimensions showed distinct associations with children's epigenetic aging.

Greater behavioral control was associated with a slower DunedinPACE, β = -.10, p < .001, and greater warmth was similarly associated with a slower pace of aging, β = -.06, p = .007. In contrast, greater hostility was associated with a faster DunedinPACE, β = .07, p = .011. Hostility was also associated with greater PedBE EAA, β = .10, p = .003, whereas behavioral control and warmth were not significantly associated with PedBE. Neither material hardship nor parenting stress showed significant direct associations with either epigenetic aging outcome after accounting for parenting behaviors or covariates. Indirect Effects

Material hardship was indirectly associated with all three age-5 parenting dimensions through parenting stress. Greater hardship was associated with greater behavioral control, b = .043, 95% CI [.009, .076], p = .013; greater hostility, b = .062, 95% CI [.034, .091], p < .001; and lower warmth, b = -.095, 95% CI [-.144, -.047], p < .001, through greater parenting stress.

Several serial indirect pathways from material hardship to epigenetic aging were also significant. For DunedinPACE, significant indirect effects emerged through parenting stress and subsequent behavioral control, b = -.001, p = .036; hostility, b = .001, p = .030; and warmth, b = .001, p = .033. The directions of these pathways reflected the distinct parenting processes: hardship was associated with greater parenting stress, which predicted greater behavioral control and thereby a slower pace of aging; greater hostility and thereby a faster pace of aging; and lower warmth and thereby a faster pace of aging. For PedBE EAA, only the serial indirect pathway through parenting stress and hostility was significant, b = .007, 95% CI [.001, .013], p = .016. Serial indirect effects through behavioral control and warmth were not significant for PedBE EAA. Discussion

This study examined whether family processes help explain how material hardship becomes biologically embedded across early childhood development. Guided by the Family Stress Model (Conger, 1981; Masarik & Conger, 2017), a longitudinal pathway model tested whether material hardship early in life was associated with subsequent parenting stress, whether parenting stress predicted three distinct dimensions of parenting, and whether these parenting behaviors were, in turn, associated with children's epigenetic aging. Overall, the findings were consistent with a biologically embedded family stress model in which socioeconomic adversity was associated with children's biological aging through proximal family processes rather than through direct associations alone. More specifically, material hardship increased parental stress and disrupted parenting practices that were associated with accelerated epigenetic aging.

Together, these findings extend the Family Stress Model by positioning parental stress and parenting as proximal, modifiable pathways through which broader socioeconomic adversity may become biologically embedded during childhood.

Material Hardship & Parenting Stress

Consistent with the Family Stress Model, greater material hardship during early childhood was associated with greater parenting stress two years later. Experiences of material hardship have been shown to place repeated demands on parents by increasing uncertainty, constraining available resources, and requiring parents to manage competing financial and caregiving demands. Accordingly, the association between material hardship and parenting stress is consistent with longstanding evidence that economic pressure affects children's development partly through altering parental emotional, temporal, and regulatory resources (K. A. Crnic, 2024; K. Crnic & Ross, 2017; Gagnon et al., 2026; Masarik & Conger, 2017). However, this pathway should not be interpreted as situating the developmental consequences of socioeconomic adversity primarily within parents. Instead, parenting stress should be understood as a process that unfolds within a broader ecology characterized by structural and material constraints, as well as the broader contexts surrounding the family. In this sense, parenting stress represents one potential point of transmission between distal socioeconomic conditions and children's proximal developmental environments.

Parenting Stress, Multidimensional Parenting, & Epigenetic Aging

Parenting stress was subsequently associated with multiple dimensions of parenting behavior, including greater hostility, lower warmth, and greater behavioral control. Heightened parenting stress may reduce parents' capacity to regulate affect and behavior during challenging parent-child interactions (Barroso et al., 2018; Deater-Deckard, 1998; Jackson & Choi, 2018).

Parents under significant stress have been shown to rely more on reactive, coercive, and harsh disciplinary responses when attempting to regulate their child's behavior, and research consistently finds these parenting behaviors to be consequential to children's social, psychological, and biological development (Brody et al., 2014; Burt et al., 2021; Jackson & Choi, 2018).

Importantly, in the present study, hostility was subsequently associated with both a faster pace of biological aging and a greater EAA, making it the parenting dimension most consistently associated with biological aging across the three parenting dimensions measured. Repeated exposure to parental hostility may, therefore, contribute to a more chronically demanding stressregulatory environment as children attempt to navigate moments of conflict under heightened physical arousal. Consistent with this interpretation, prior work has linked punitive and harsh discipline with alterations in children's stress-response and neurodevelopmental systems (Chen et al., 2015; Whittle et al., 2022).

In contrast to the stress-related risk associated with hostility, warmth represented a distinct dimension of the caregiving environment that was associated with slower biological aging. Greater parenting stress was associated with lower observed warmth, suggesting that the consequences of stress for parenting behavior and subsequent biological aging may extend beyond increases in hostile or punitive behavior to reductions in supportive and emotionally responsive interactions. Warmth may be particularly relevant for children's psychosocial and biological development because responsive parenting can provide a predictable source of safety and co-regulation during early childhood (Bornstein et al., 2026; Carroll et al., 2013; Padilla-Walker et al., 2016). Repeated experiences of warmth may therefore reduce the regulatory demands placed on children as they navigate broader environmental stress, providing an interpersonal context in which children can rely on caregivers for support and regulation (Metrailer et al., 2026). At the same time, maintaining warm and responsive interactions may become increasingly difficult as parents contend with the persistent demands associated with socioeconomic adversity (Gagnon et al., 2026; Lee et al., 2024; Masarik & Conger, 2017). Thus, the association between greater parenting stress and lower warmth highlights one way in which broader economic conditions may constrain a potentially supportive feature of children's immediate caregiving environments.

Behavioral control (e.g., redirection, taking away privileges, utilizing time-outs) may be another parenting dimension that protects against the biological embedding of adversity, particularly for families facing socioeconomic hardship (Highlander et al., 2025). Greater parenting stress was associated with greater behavioral control which, in turn, was associated with a slower pace of aging. This finding further challenges a characterization of parenting under stress as uniformly compromising and highlights the importance of distinguishing behavioral structure from coercive or psychologically controlling forms of parenting (Bean et al., 2006; Bornstein et al., 2026; Bornstein & Bradley, 2012). For instance, behavioral control may function differently from harsh and punitive discipline because it involves parents' efforts to establish expectations, monitor behavior, and maintain consistent boundaries rather than relying on coercion or hostility (Barber et al., 1994). Under conditions of heightened family stress, greater structure may provide children with predictability and behavioral guidance within an otherwise uncertain environment.

From this perspective, the positive association between parenting stress and behavioral control may reflect parents' efforts to increase structure in response to contextual demands, rather than simply another manifestation of disrupted caregiving (Bornstein & Bradley, 2012; Putnick et al., 2008). Consistent with this possibility, research examining parenting in disadvantaged environments suggests that behavioral control, when characterized by clear expectations, guidance, and supportive limit setting, may represent an adaptive caregiving strategy that promotes children's regulation and adjustment (Bocknek et al., 2020; Brody & Flor, 1998; Goldner et al., 2016). Evidence from Behavioral Parent Training programs further underscores the potential benefits of effective behavioral management practices. For example, Merrill et al. (2024) found that children receiving internet-delivered Parent-Child Interaction Therapy (PCIT), which emphasizes positive reinforcement and consistent behavioral management, exhibited a slower epigenetic pace of aging. Within this context, the association between behavioral control and a slower pace of aging in the present study raises the possibility that predictable behavioral structure may represent one component of the caregiving environment that supports children's regulation under adversity beyond parental warmth and support.

Developmental Coupling of Bioecological Processes

The pattern of indirect rather than direct associations between material hardship and epigenetic aging may also inform dimensional models of childhood adversity. The threatdeprivation framework distinguishes experiences involving the absence of expected environmental inputs from those involving threat of harm and proposes that these dimensions influence development through partially distinct mechanisms (McLaughlin et al., 2014 (McLaughlin et al., , 2021)).

Prior research on biological aging has similarly suggested that threat-related experiences may be more consistently associated with accelerated aging than deprivation-related experiences (Hogan et al., 2024; Sumner et al., 2019). However, the present findings raise the possibility that distal deprivation-related conditions may alter family processes in ways that subsequently shape parenting practices that reflect both threat (e.g., hostility, punitive discipline) and deprivationrelated (e.g., lower warmth) domains. Specifically, material hardship was associated with greater subsequent parenting stress, which was in turn associated with greater hostility, lower warmth, and greater behavioral control. Thus, material hardship may represent one deprivation-related starting point in a developmental sequence rather than being directly or immediately associated with child biological embedding.

Building on bioecological, transactional, and developmental systems prospectives (Bronfenbrenner, 1977; Sameroff, 2000; Smith & Thelen, 2003), we conceptualize this process as developmental coupling of bioecological processes, whereby theoretically distinct dimensions of adversity may become interconnected through ecological and relational processes that shape children's exposure to both adversity and protection across development. Importantly, this perspective does not suggest that deprivation "becomes" threat or that these dimensions operate through identical developmental mechanisms. Rather, distal conditions associated with one dimension of adversity (e.g., SES-related deprivation) may alter family processes, which in turn shape the frequency and intensity of children's exposure to proximal forms of adversity (e.g., threat-related parenting practices) or protection (e.g., behavioral control), with potentially distinct implications for biological and psychosocial development. Although parenting dimensions such as hostility, warmth, and behavioral control may arise from a shared ecological condition (e.g., material hardship), they contribute to divergent developmental pathways, particularly with respect to biological aging. In the present study, although material hardship indirectly increased hostility and behavioral control, hostility was associated with increased EAA and a faster pace of aging, whereas behavioral control was associated with a slower pace of aging, suggesting a potentially protective function. These findings raise the possibility that adversity may initiate interconnected developmental processes that simultaneously increase vulnerability while maintaining or strengthening sources of protection.

The developmental coupling of bioecological processes perspective may help bridge dimensional and person-centered approaches to understanding the long-term consequences of childhood adversity (Chow et al., 2025; McLaughlin et al., 2023; Sisitsky et al., 2023). Whereas dimensional approaches emphasize the distinct mechanisms through which different forms of adversity influence development, person-centered approaches characterize patterns of cooccurring adversity (Jobe-Sheilds et al., 2015; McLaughlin et al., 2014). Developmental coupling offers a complementary perspective by shifting attention toward the ecological and relational processes through which these patterns emerge, influence each other, and change across devel-opmental time. By considering how distal adversity may simultaneously influence proximal riskenhancing and resilience-enhancing processes, this perspective suggests that the developmental consequences of adversity cannot necessarily be understood by examining individual exposures or pathways in isolation. Rather, understanding biological and psychosocial development may require consideration of how adverse and protective processes become organized within the same developmental context

Limitations, Strengths, and Future Directions

Several limitations warrant consideration when interpreting the present findings. First, although material hardship was assessed before parenting stress and subsequent parenting behaviors, parenting stress and age-5 parenting dimensions were measured concurrently, limiting conclusions about temporal ordering. Moreover, despite the longitudinal design, a prospective longitudinal approach cannot establish causality, and unmeasured family characteristics or reciprocal influences between child behavior and parenting may contribute to the observed associations. Nevertheless, emerging experimental evidence supports the possibility that modifying children's socioeconomic environments may influence epigenetic aging. For example, Raffington et al. (2026) found that children whose mothers were randomized to receive larger unconditional cash transfers exhibited a slower epigenetic pace of aging compared with those receiving smaller transfers, although the developmental processes underlying these effects remain unclear.

Similarly, intervention research suggests that modifying parent-child relationships may influence children's epigenetic aging. Merrill et al. (2024) found that children randomized to internet-delivered behavioral parent training program exhibited a slower epigenetic pace of aging, whereas Sullivan et al. (2024) found lower EAA among children receiving Child-Parent Psychotherapy, a family intervention focused on strengthening protective and responsive caregiving relationships, relative to a matched community sample. Together with the present findings, these three studies highlight the potential value of experimental and intervention research examining whether changes in socioeconomic conditions and parenting behaviors independently or jointly influence children's epigenetic aging. Future studies that integrate economic and parental stress management support and parenting practices interventions could clarify the causal processes through which socioeconomic adversity becomes biologically embedded.

An additional limitation concerns the measurement and interpretation of biological aging.

DNA methylation was assessed only at age 9, precluding examination of earlier differences or within-person changes in epigenetic aging across early childhood development. Furthermore, extending this work beyond epigenetic aging algorithms to examine broader patterns of epigenetic regulation, including epigenome-wide associations and methylation signatures related to inflammation and hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis functioning, may help clarify the molecular processes underlying the observed associations (Danese et al., 2011; Wesarg et al., 2020). Such an approach could provide a more comprehensive understanding of how repeated parenting experiences become biologically embedded and whether different parenting dimensions are associated with distinct patterns of epigenetic regulation.

Finally, although the FFCWB study sample includes substantial socioeconomic and demographic diversity, its sampling design and the availability of epigenetic data within a subset of participants may limit generalizability. The available measures also did not fully capture social and structural experiences that may shape parenting and children's biological development, including but not limited to racial discrimination and culturally relevant protective processes such as racial and ethnic socialization (Del Toro et al., 2024; Timperio et al., 2015). Future research incorporating these experiences, alongside broader environmental conditions such as neighborhood disadvantage or community-level threat, could clarify how parenting processes operate within different ecological contexts. This is particularly relevant given evidence that community-level threat may attenuate the protective association between positive parenting and epigenetic aging (Metrailer et al., 2026), suggesting that the developmental significance of parenting may depend partly on the contexts in which families inhabit.

Despite these limitations, the present study has several methodological and conceptual strengths. The use of a large socioeconomically and demographically diverse longitudinal cohort enabled the examination of associations between material hardship in early childhood, subsequent family processes, and epigenetic aging in middle childhood-processes that might not be easily captured in smaller research samples and designs. The integration of parent-report, observational methods, and DNA methylation biomarkers reduced reliance on a single measurement method and the potential for shared method bias. Moreover, distinguishing among warmth, behavioral control, and hostility, rather than relying on broad positive and negative parenting composites, allowed for a more differentiated examination of caregiving processes that may have distinct associations with children's biological development. Accounting for earlier levels of each parenting dimension further strengthened the longitudinal analysis by examining subsequent parenting behaviors beyond their corresponding age-3 levels. Finally, incorporating both PedBE and DunedinPACE allowed the study to examine whether hypothesized family processes were associated with epigenetic indicators developed to capture different aspects of biological aging.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the present study extends the Family Stress Model into the domain of biological embedding by identifying distinct family processes through which material hardship is associated with children's biological development. Greater material hardship was associated with elevated parenting stress, which in turn was associated with lower warmth, greater behavioral control, and greater hostility. These parenting dimensions demonstrated contrasting associations with children's epigenetic aging, with warmth and behavioral control associated with a slower pace of aging and hostility associated with both a faster pace of aging and greater EAA.

Importantly, the significant serial indirect pathways suggest that socioeconomic adversity may become biologically embedded through intervening family processes rather than operating solely through direct associations with children's epigenetic outcomes. Understanding the biological embedding of adversity may, therefore, require examining not only the consequences of individual environmental and interpersonal exposures, but also the developmental processes through which these experiences become interconnected across time. Through identifying parenting, a potentially modifiable component of these pathways, the present findings provide a foundation for future research examining how economic and parenting interventions may jointly support children's biological development. Material hardship was assessed when children were 3 years old using items derived from the Basic Needs-Ability to Meet Expenses section of the Survey of Income and Program Participation, the 1996 Panel Wave 8 Adult Well-Being Topical Module outcomes.

Model fit was evaluated using the comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR). CFI and TLI values of .90 or greater and RMSEA and SRMR values of .08 or lower were interpreted as indicating acceptable model fit, with CFI and TLI values approaching .95 and RMSEA values approaching .05 indicating stronger fit. Indirect effects were estimated to evaluate several components of the hypothesized developmental pathway. First, indirect associations between material hardship and each age-5 parenting dimension through parenting stress were estimated. Second, serial indirect effects tested whether material hardship was associated with each epigenetic aging outcome through parenting stress and subsequent warmth, behavioral control, or hostility. Additional indirect effects tested pathways from material hardship through parenting behavior without parenting stress and pathways through parenting stress without parenting behavior. Statistical significance of indirect effects was evaluated using model-based confidence intervals.
