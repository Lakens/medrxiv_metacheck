Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Introduction

Estimating individualized treatment effects (ITEs) provides the evidence base for precision medicine by helping clinicians identify patients most likely to benefit from a given treatment [1] [2] [3].

Longitudinal electronic health records (EHRs) offer a rich source of real-world data for this purpose, capturing patients' diagnoses, treatments, and disease progression over time [4] [5] [6]. Here, we present ITE-Transformer, a pre-trained clinical foundation model that combines longitudinal patient representations and causal machine learning [2] to predict ITEs from EHR data.

Recent advances in transformer-based foundation models have demonstrated strong performance in modeling longitudinal patient trajectories from EHR data by capturing complex dependencies across diagnoses, treatments, and disease progression [7] [8] [9] [10] [11] [12] [13] [14]. However, existing clinical foundation models have largely focused on disease risk prediction (e.g., Delphi-2M predicts the risk of over 1,000 diseases [10]). While extremely valuable for risk stratification and preventive care, such foundation models do not directly provide comparative clinical evidence on how patients would respond to different treatments. Estimating such treatment effects involves counterfactual questions and, when using real-world data, requires accounting for non-random treatment assignment and confounding [15] [16] [17]. Importantly, failing to account for such confounders can lead to biased estimates, as patients receiving more intensive treatments may also have more severe disease and worse underlying prognoses. In principle, causal machine learning [2, 18] offers methods for estimating treatment effects from high-dimensional data [2], yet approaches specifically tailored to clinical foundation models are missing.

We introduce ITE-Transformer, a pre-trained transformer-based foundation model that combines longitudinal EHR representations with doubly robust causal machine learning to predict ITEs. The ITE-Transformer is pre-trained on temporally ordered sequences of clinical events to learn contextualized representations of patient histories; these representations are subsequently used to predict treatment assignment and treatment-specific outcome risks, which are combined 3 within a doubly robust framework to compute ITEs. The ITE-Transformer is pre-trained on structured EHR data from ∼350,000 patients in the All of Us Research Program [19] . We evaluate the ITE-Transformer using two clinically different treatment comparisons: (i) GLP-1 receptor agonists versus DPP-4 inhibitors in patients with type 2 diabetes, and (ii) immunotherapy versus chemotherapy in a pan-cancer cohort spanning multiple malignant tumor types. More broadly, this work extends clinical foundation models beyond risk prediction toward analyzing ITEs for evidence generation.

Patient histories were represented as temporally ordered sequences of clinical events (Figure 1a), analogous to token sequences in language modeling. Tokens were ordered by event time and paired with the patient's age at the corresponding event. A token indicating biological sex was added at the beginning of each sequence. We used a standard decoder-only transformer architecture with causal self-attention to ensure that each token attended only to preceding clinical events and thus to prevent tokens from attending to subsequent events in the patient's trajectory. The 4 architecture was customized for the clinical setting by replacing conventional positional encoding with a continuous age-based encoding [10] to capture the irregular timing of patient trajectories.

The ITE-Transformer was then pre-trained using a self-supervised next-token prediction objective (Figure 1b).

For downstream ITE prediction (Figure 1c), we integrated the pre-trained transformer into a causal machine learning framework that builds on principles of doubly robust estimation [18, 20, 21]. The idea of doubly robust learners is to, instead of estimating the treatment effects directly, to first model both the treatment assignment and the treatment-specific outcomes separately, before combining these predictions to estimate treatment effects in a way that reduces sensitivity to misspecification of either prediction component. Importantly, both stages leverage the longitudinal patient representations learned by the pre-trained transformer. For this, we use the hidden representation of the last non-padded token before the treatment index date to summarize each patient's pre-treatment clinical history. Formally, the ITE-Transformer proceeds in two stages (Figure 1c). In the first stage, dedicated prediction heads are attached to the transformer to jointly estimate two nuisance components (i.e., auxiliary predictions that are used in the second stage), namely, the propensity for treatment assignment and the expected outcomes under each treatment alternative. The estimates are then used to construct doubly robust pseudo-outcomes based on augmented inverse probability weighting (AIPW) [18]. AIPW combines outcome predictions with inverse-propensity-weighted corrections based on observed treatments and outcomes, thus adjusting for measured differences in treatment assignment, while reducing sensitivity to errors in the nuisance components. In the second stage, a dedicated prediction head is used to learn the conditional average treatment effect (CATE) from the pseudo-outcomes using the transformer-derived patient representations, while keeping the transformer backbone frozen. As a result, the output are patient-specific CATE predictions, which we refer to as ITEs and which predict the expected difference in outcome risk between the study treatment and the comparator varies across patients with different pre-treatment histories. For the GLP-1 versus DPP-4 comparison, the most influential features included endocrine, nutritional, and metabolic diseases (ICD chapter E), consistent with the central role of diabetes severity, obesity, dyslipidaemia, and related metabolic disorders in determining cardiovascular risk and treatment response [27] [28] [29]. Diseases of the musculoskeletal system and connective tissue (ICD M) and the use of analgesics (ATC N02) may capture obesityrelated complications, chronic pain, or reduced mobility, which are associated with cardiovascular health [30, 31]. Other influential groups included diseases of the circulatory system (ICD I), diseases of the nervous system (ICD G), and diseases of the eye and adnexa (ICD H). These features may reflect underlying cardiovascular risk, neurological comorbidities, and diabetes-related complications, thus providing additional information about patients' overall health status.

For the immunotherapy versus chemotherapy comparison, the SHAP analysis identified clinically meaningful groups that are consistent with established determinants of cancer prognosis and treatment selection. The most influential feature group was malignant neoplasms (ICD chapter C), which captures differences in cancer type, disease burden, and disease progression [32] and are known to influence the relative effectiveness of immunotherapy and chemotherapy across different solid tumors [33, 34]. Other influential groups included musculoskeletal diseases (ICD 18 M) and endocrine, nutritional, and metabolic diseases (ICD E). These conditions may reflect differences in comorbidity burden, body composition, functional status, and systemic inflammation, which have been linked to differences in both cancer outcomes and immune checkpoint inhibitor response [35] [36] [37].

Overall, the SHAP analysis suggests that the ITE-Transformer relies on clinically meaningful features when predicting ITEs across both clinical settings. Beyond improving model interpretability, ITE-Transformer may thus help identify patient subgroups with differential treatment benefit and help discover potential predictive biomarkers. Examining feature contributions at both the broader population level and the individual patient level may further support hypothesis generation and guide future confirmatory studies of treatment response to inform personalized treatment recommendations.

Discussion

In this work, we introduced the ITE-Transformer, a transformer-based framework that combines self-supervised pre-training on longitudinal EHR trajectories with doubly robust causal machine learning to predict ITEs. We evaluated the ITE-Transformer across two clinically different settings (i.e., GLP-1 receptor agonists versus DPP-4 inhibitors in patients with type 2 diabetes, and immunotherapy versus chemotherapy in a pan-cancer cohort). Across both settings, the ITE-Transformer improved the discrimination and calibration of treatment assignment and outcome models, compared with conventional, tabular machine learning baselines and an otherwise identical transformer without pre-training. The ITE-Transformer identified substantial heterogeneity in predicted ITEs across patients. Together, these findings demonstrate the potential of integrating causal machine learning [2, 38, 39] with longitudinal EHR foundation models to support patientlevel comparative effectiveness assessments and thus support real-world evidence generation. By identifying patients with different predicted treatment responses, ITE-Transformer can further generate hypotheses to guide prospective studies of personalized treatment strategies.

Existing clinical foundation models for EHR data have primarily focused on predicting future diagnoses [7] [8] [9] [10] [11] [12] [13] 40] or claims information [11], or predicting how likely patients respond to a given treatment [9, 14]. Although clinically informative, these approaches do not directly answer the counterfactual question of how patient outcomes would differ under different alternative strategies. Our ITE-Transformer addresses this gap by combining a pre-trained transformer with a doubly robust causal learning framework to predict ITEs from longitudinal patient histories.

Our results highlight the importance of self-supervised pre-training for ITE prediction.

Compared with an otherwise identical transformer trained from scratch, the pre-trained ITE-Transformer led to more stable optimization and achieved a better performance in terms of discrimination and calibration metrics. This suggests that pre-training on large longitudinal EHR trajectories yields transferable representations of diagnoses, medication histories, comorbidities, 22 and temporal disease patterns that remain informative when the downstream cohorts for treatment effect estimation are substantially smaller than the pre-training population. Our analyses of the learned embeddings further support this interpretation, where clinically related diagnoses and medication are mapped into similar, meaningful clusters, despite being learned only through self-supervised next-event prediction.

The improved discrimination and calibration of propensity and outcome models are important for the reliability of the doubly robust ITE estimation. In observational EHR data, treatment assignment is non-random and depends on clinical indications, disease severity, comorbidities, access patterns, and physician or patient preferences; accurately modeling treatment assignment and outcomes helps account for these systematic differences between treatment groups and reduce bias from measured confounding [16, 41]. Importantly, errors in propensity and outcome predictions can propagate through doubly robust pseudo-outcomes to the estimated treatment effects [20, 42], which explains the relevance of accurate and well-calibrated nuisance components. Our results suggest that ITE-Transformer captures clinically relevant information underlying treatment effect heterogeneity from longitudinal patient histories beyond conventional tabular approaches.

The ITE-Transformer predicted substantial heterogeneity in ITEs across both clinical settings. Some patients showed lower predicted MACE risk under GLP-1 receptor agonists than under DPP-4 inhibitors, while others showed smaller benefits or were predicted to benefit more from DPP-4 inhibitors. Similarly, in the pan-cancer cohort, the predicted mortality benefit of immunotherapy relative to chemotherapy varied across patients. These findings are consistent with the clinical expectation in both settings that the treatment response varies with a patient's pre-treatment health status, comorbidities, prior medications, and disease histories [29, 34].

Our study has limitations, many of which are inherent to generating clinical evidence using real-world data [43]. First, although the pre-training cohort is large and diverse, it is drawn from a single data resource, which may limit the generalizability to other healthcare settings. Second, interpreting the predicted treatment effects causally requires assumptions that cannot be fully 23 verified in observational data [2, 16, 17]. Although ITE-Transformer accounts for a broad set of demographic characteristics and longitudinal diagnosis and medication histories, unmeasured or incompletely recorded confounders may still bias treatment effect estimates. The results should therefore be interpreted as confounder-adjusted, model-based estimates for hypothesis generation rather than validated causal effects [44]. Clinical translation therefore requires rigorous validation of the predicted treatment effects using established approaches for real-world evidence generation, including sensitivity analyses (e.g., E-values [45]), target trial emulation [46, 47], and negative control outcomes [48]. Third, our model uses structured diagnoses and medication records; future research may include additional modalities such as laboratory measurements, procedures, clinical notes, imaging, or genomics. Fourth, validating ITE predictions is inherently challenging because the counterfactual outcome for each patient is unobserved. We therefore followed best practice [2, 23] for evaluating causal ML models by assessing propensity score overlap and effect score calibration [24], but external and prospective evaluation are left for future work. Our predictions should therefore primarily serve to generate hypotheses for future studies of personalized treatment strategies.

Overall, the ITE-Transformer is a clinical foundation model to predict ITEs from longitudinal EHR data, thereby advancing real-world evidence generation beyond conventional risk prediction.

The ITE-Transformer provides a scalable framework for identifying who may benefit from alternative therapies to support hypothesis generation and personalized care.

Methods

Data

Patient cohort. We used data from the All of Us Research Program [19], with all analyses conducted within the All of Us Researcher Workbench [49]. At the time of study, the program included 626,383 participants aged 18 years or older recruited from a more than 340 sites. The available data 24 are de-identified and include participant surveys, physical measurements, and electronic health records (EHRs). For this study, we included the 350,000 participants with available structured EHR data. Cohort characteristics are reported in the Supplementary Table S1 and Supplementary Figure S1. Data preprocessing complied with all relevant regulation of the All of Us Research Program. No ethical approval was necessary at the host institution due to the de-identified, noninterventional design of the study.

Data preprocessing.

We represented longitudinal patient histories using diagnosis and medication codes aggregated at clinically interpretable levels. Diagnoses were encoded using 1,257 level-3 ICD codes (e.g., E11 -"Type 2 diabetes mellitus") from chapters I-XVII. Medication exposures were represented using 215 level-3 ATC codes (e.g., A10A -"Insulins and analogues") across all ATC chapters. Together, these codes formed a vocabulary of 1,472 clinical concepts, which was used to balance clinical specificity with reduced sparsity. Clinical concepts were mapped to the respective coding hierarchies using standardized OMOP vocabulary tables available in the All of Us Researcher Workbench. The included ICD and ATC chapters are listed in Supplementary Tables S2 and S3.

To reduce redundant clinical events, we applied temporal deduplication within each patient trajectory (similar to but less strict as in Delphi-2M [10]). For diagnoses, we retained at most one occurrence of each code within a six-month window. For medications, we retained only the first recorded exposure to each medication concept.

Study cohorts.

We evaluated the ITE-Transformer in two comparative-effectiveness tasks designed as target trial emulations [46]. Each analysis was defined by a target patient population, a study treatment, an active comparator, and a clinical outcome. Following an active-comparator, new-user design [17], we defined the treatment index date as initiation of either treatment and used only pre-treatment clinical histories as model input [46]. Eligible patients were required to have the relevant diagnosis before treatment initiation and at least one recorded exposure to either the study treatment or comparator. Patients who initiated both treatment classes on the same date or 25 had otherwise ambiguous treatment assignment were excluded.

We considered the following two treatment comparisons: • Type 2 diabetes cohort.

GLP-1 receptor agonists and DPP-4 inhibitors are used at comparable stages in the treatment pathway for type 2 diabetes [50], yet differ in their cardiovascular benefits. GLP-1 receptor agonists have been shown to reduce major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), while DPP-4 inhibitors showed no corresponding benefit [28, 51], but studies reported heterogeneity in treatment response [29]. Patients diagnosed with type 2 diabetes mellitus (ICD-10 code E11) initiating either a GLP-1 receptor agonist or a DPP-4 inhibitor were included, with the treatment index date defined as the first recorded prescription of either drug class following the diabetes diagnosis. The outcome was the occurrence of a major adverse cardiovascular event (MACE) within three years after treatment initiation.

• Pan-cancer cohort. Patients diagnosed with a solid malignant neoplasm were identified using the corresponding ICD-10 codes. Eligible patients initiated either immunotherapy or chemotherapy, with the treatment index date defined as the first recorded administration of either treatment following cancer diagnosis. The outcome was all-cause mortality within three years after treatment initiation.

Details are provided in the supplements regarding eligibility criteria (Supplementary Tables S4 and S5), treatment definitions (Supplementary Tables S6 and S7), and outcome definitions (Supplementary Table S8).

ITE-Transformer

Overview

The ITE-Transformer consists of four main components . First, longitudinal EHRs are represented as temporally ordered sequences of diagnoses and medication exposures, together with information on patient age and sex (patient trajectory representation). Second, these trajectories 26 are processed by a decoder-only transformer that produces contextualized representations of clinical events and patient histories (transformer architecture). Third, the transformer is pre-trained on a large EHR cohort using self-supervised next-event prediction to learn general representations of clinical concepts in longitudinal patient trajectories (self-supervised pre-training). Fourth, the pretrained transformer is leveraged for comparative effectiveness assessments using a doubly robust causal learning framework (treatment effect prediction). In the fourth component, the transformerderived patient representations are first used to predict treatment propensity and treatment-specific outcome risks, which are subsequently combined into doubly robust pseudo-outcomes to train a model for predicting ITEs (defined by the conditional average treatment effect; CATE). These first three components are adapted from recent clinical foundation models [9] [10] [11]. The key methodological novelty of the ITE-Transformer is the fourth component, which allows ITE-Transformer not only to use patient trajectories for risk or outcome modeling but for treatment effect modeling. As a result, we integrate principles from causal machine learning [2, 18] to predict ITEs under different treatment strategies. This enables ITE-Transformer to make individualized assessments of the comparative effectiveness of treatments from longitudinal patient trajectories.

Patient trajectory representation

Following recent longitudinal EHR foundation models [9] [10] [11], we represent each patient as a chronologically ordered sequence of clinical events. Each event corresponds to a diagnosis or medication token from a predefined clinical vocabulary and is paired with the patient's age at the time of the event. A token encoding the biological sex is added at the beginning of the sequence. Clinical tokens are mapped to learnable vector embeddings and combined with a continuous age-based encoding [10]. Unlike conventional positional encodings, which capture the order of events, the age-based encoding also captures the relative timing and therefore accommodates for the irregular timing between clinical events.

27

Transformer architecture

Patient trajectories are encoded using a decoder-only transformer with causal self-attention [52].

The architecture consists of stacked transformer blocks with multi-head self-attention, positionwise feed-forward layers, residual connections, and layer normalization. Causal masking restricts each position to information observed at or before that time point in the trajectory, thus preventing that information from future events from entering the representation.

The transformer produces a contextualized hidden representation for each token in the patient trajectory. During self-supervised pre-training, these representations are used to predict subsequent clinical events. For the downstream task of predicting treatment effects, we use the hidden state of the last non-padded token before treatment initiation as a fixed-dimensional representation of the patient's pre-treatment clinical history.

A schematic overview of the architecture is provided in Supplementary Figure S2. Hyperparameters, including the number of transformer layers, attention heads, embedding dimension, feed-forward dimension, dropout, and total number of trainable parameters, are reported in Supplementary Table S9.

To reduce overfitting bias and enable valid inference, we use K-fold cross-fitting [53] when estimating the propensity and outcome models and constructing the pseudo-outcomes. For this, the development cohort is partitioned into K folds; for each fold, the propensity and outcome models are trained on the remaining K -1 folds and used to generate predictions for the heldout fold. Each patient's pseudo-outcome is therefore constructed from propensity and outcome predictions generated by a model that was not trained on that patient.

The transformer backbone is frozen during this stage, and the CATE prediction head is optimized using the Huber loss. Baselines

We compared the ITE-Transformer with four baselines using identical data splits and cross-fitting procedure. The comparisons focused on the propensity and outcome predictions, which can be evaluated directly against observed data by comparing model discrimination and calibration. This allows us to assess whether ITE-Transformer provides more reliable estimates of the nuisance components used for subsequent treatment effect estimation tasks, without the need to have information 31 about unobserved counterfactual outcomes.

(1) Age and sex classifier. Following prior work on clinical foundation models [9, 10], we trained classifiers using only patient age at treatment initiation and sex to predict treatment assignment and observed outcomes as a minimal baseline.

(2) Random forest. We trained a random forest classifier on fixed-width tabular representations of the pre-treatment history, consisting of binary indicators for the most prevalent clinical codes together with summary features of the patient trajectory. Separate models were trained for propensity and outcome prediction. The models were implemented using scikit-learn [54]. Hyperparameters are reported in Supplementary Table S13.

(3) XGBoost. We trained an XGBoost classifier on the same tabular representation as the random forest baseline to provide a strong nonlinear benchmark [55]. Separate models were again trained using the XGBoost implementation from [56] for propensity and outcome prediction. Hyperparameters are reported in Supplementary Table S14.

(4) Non-pretrained transformer (NP-Transformer). To isolate the contribution of selfsupervised pre-training, we trained an otherwise identical transformer with the same architecture, vocabulary, age encoding, and prediction heads as the ITE-Transformer, but with randomly initialized weights and no self-supervised pre-training. This baseline was trained directly on each downstream cohort using the same supervised first-stage objective. Hyperparameters are reported in Supplementary Table S12.

Evaluation

Following established practice for causal machine learning [2], we evaluated the ITE-Transformer as follows. First, we assessed the predictive performance of the propensity model and treatmentspecific outcome models on the held-out folds. Discrimination was quantified using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) and the area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC) [57]. Calibration was assessed by grouping patients according to their predicted prob-32 abilities and comparing the mean predicted probability within each group with the corresponding observed treatment or outcome frequency [58]. We additionally examined the distributions of estimated propensity scores across treatment groups to assess empirical overlap [59].

Second, we assesed the effect score calibration [24] to examine whether predicted ITEs aligned with stratum-specific average treatment effects across patient strata with varying predicted treatment benefit. Intuitively, well-calibrated effect scores should align with the predicting treatment effect within each patient stratum. To assess this, patients were ranked by their predicted ITEs and divided into strata. Within each stratum, the mean over the predicted ITEs was compared with the corresponding average treatment effect (ATE) estimated using augmented inverse probability weighting (AIPW) [42]. This analysis evaluates whether the CATE learner captures systematic variation in treatment effects across patient strata.

Experiments were repeated across five random seeds, using the same seeds across models to allow consistent comparisons.

Interpretability of the predicted ITEs

To examine which clinical factors of the pre-treatment clinical history contributed to the predicted ITEs, we used SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) [26]. For this, we aggregated diagnosis and medication concepts to higher-level ICD and ATC groups to reduce sparsity and thus improve interpretability. SHAP values were then used to quantify the contribution of each feature group to the predicted ITE for individual patients. The magnitude of a SHAP value reflects the strength of a feature's contribution, while the corresponding sign indicates whether the feature shifts the prediction toward a larger relative benefit from the study treatment or from the comparator.

Implementation details

All analyses were performed in Python. We implemented the ITE-Transformer using PyTorch [60].

The model is optimized using AdamW [61], gradient clipping with a maximum norm of 1.0, and 33 a cosine learning-rate schedule with linear warm-up. For computational efficiency, trajectories are represented using a fixed maximum context length. For sequences exceeding this limit, we retain the most recent clinical history; shorter trajectories are padded to the maximum sequence length.

Padding tokens are excluded from both attention and loss computation. Hyperparameters were selected through empirical tuning to balance validation performance and computational efficiency; see Supplementary Table S9.

For the task-specific adaptations, separate learning rates were used for the pre-trained transformer backbone and the newly initialized prediction heads to preserve learned representations while adapting to each treatment comparison. Estimated propensity scores were clipped [62] before construction of the AIPW pseudo-outcomes to reduce instability from extreme inverse probability weights. Further training details, including learning rates, batch sizes, number of epochs, dropout, and weight decay, are reported in Supplementary Tables S10 and S11.

In the second stage, a conditional average treatment effect (CATE) learner uses these pseudo-outcomes to compute the ITEs.
