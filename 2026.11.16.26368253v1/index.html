To fix Exact P-Values

What we found

We found 3 imprecise p values out of 23 detected p values.

Show details

Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

P > 0.125 We first tested if individual MRI-derived biomechanical parameters differed between normal and diseased states, but none of the five measured parameters differed significantly between ATAA patients and healthy imaging controls after Holm correction (Holm P > 0.125; Figure S4).

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 3 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Background: Current diameter-based risk stratification for ascending aortic aneurysms incompletely captures patient-specific dissection risk. Here, we apply machine learning to ex vivo aortic biomechanics to define a tissue-derived risk continuum, termed aortic biomechanical vulnerability index (AortoBVI), and use magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to predict this index in vivo.

Methods: Ascending aortic tissue from 353 aneurysms, 45 normal aortas, and 46 acute aortic dissections underwent biomechanical testing. Biomechanical features were used to train a classifier distinguishing normal-like, intermediate, and dissection-like biomechanical phenotypes. Magnetic resonance imaging was performed on 38 aneurysm patients pre-operatively and 8 normal controls.

Results: The classifier achieved an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.901 (95% CI 0.836-0.954). Among 38 aneurysms with pre-operative MRI, a model combining age, aortic stiffness index (ASI), and aortic pulse wave velocity (aPWV) estimated AortoBVI (R = 0.580), identifying dissection-like aneurysms with an AUC of 0.928 (95% CI 0.826-0.994. These results significantly outperform aortic diameter (R = 0.116, AUC 0.620).

Conclusion: These findings demonstrate substantial biomechanical heterogeneity at similar aortic diameters and support biomechanics-based risk stratification of ascending aortic aneurysms.

What is New?  AortoBVI integrates five ex vivo biomechanical measurements into a continuous index anchored to normal and acute-dissection tissue phenotypes.  Among 353 ascending aortic aneurysms, tissue-derived AortoBVI revealed substantial biomechanical heterogeneity despite similar aortic diameters.  A model combining age with MRI-derived aortic stiffness index and aortic pulse wave velocity estimated tissue-derived AortoBVI more accurately than maximum aortic diameter.

 Aortic diameter alone does not fully capture the biomechanical variation among ascending aortic aneurysms.  MRI-based AortoBVI could provide a non-invasive approach to biomechanical phenotyping that complements diameter-based assessment.  Biomechanically phenotyping patients with ascending aortic aneurysms using AortoBVI may help individualize surveillance intensity and the timing of surgery.

Introduc on

Aneurysms of the ascending aorta affect up to 4% of the general population 1, 2 and place patients at risk of acute type A aortic dissection (ATAAD), with catastrophic consequences including death and organ malperfusion. Replacement of the ascending aorta eliminates this lifetime risk. However, population-based estimates of ATAAD in patients with aortic aneurysm are only 2% per patient-year 3 . Therefore, patient selection for surgery, which carries a similar risk of death and stroke, remains the central conundrum in treating aortic disease.

Current clinical guidelines for elective surgery are based on maximum aneurysm diameter 4, 5 , despite its limited ability to predict dissection risk. In the International Registry of Acute Aortic Dissection, 59% of 591 patients with ATAAD had a maximum aortic diameter less than the current intervention threshold of 55 mm and 40% had a diameter <50 mm 6 , with similar findings reported in single center contemporary studies 7, 8 . In studies accounting for the acute changes in aortic dimensions with the dissection event, maximum pre-dissection aortic diameter was estimated to be <55 mm in 72-92% of patients [9] [10] [11] . Our group and others have shown that diameter provides limited insight into tissue microstructure and biomechanical behavior [12] [13] [14] [15] . Other metrics that more directly describe patient-specific aortic wall fragility and individualized risk of dissection and rupture are therefore needed.

Bench-top ex vivo mechanical testing uses several biomechanical parameters to describe the spectrum of material properties of the diseased aortic wall. These include delamination strength, derived by peel testing the aortic wall to simulate dissection 16 , and elastic modulus, which is increased in diseased tissue 17 . Aneurysmal tissue also exhibits reduced performance in returning stored mechanical energy to blood flow (the Windkessel function) each cardiac cycle. Energy loss, describing the hysteresis between loading and unloading of a stress-strain curve, has been found to correlate with aortic wall histopathology and delamination strength 13, 18.

For clinical translation, aortic biomechanical parameters require predictive value for aortic dissection and accurate non-invasive estimation, such as via medical imaging 19 . Several parameters have been suggested to measure vessel stiffness in vivo, including strain 20, 21 , strain-based parameters (e.g., distensibility, compliance, and aortic stiffness index (ASI)) [22] [23] [24] [25] , and aortic pulse wave velocity (aPWV) [26] [27] [28] . Unfortunately, none are in clinical use as their correlations with ex vivo biomechanics and clinically relevant endpoints are underexplored, hindering their interpretation and application 19.

To address these limitations, we aimed to risk-stratify patients into clinically meaningful categories based on their ex vivo aortic biomechanics using machine learning. We also explored whether magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-derived parameters could reproduce this risk stratification, providing a non-invasive method of personalized risk assessment for patients with aortic aneurysms.

Methods

Study Design and Patient population

This prospective cohort study was approved by the research ethics board of the University Health Network, Toronto (16-6285, July 27, 2017). Included for ex vivo mechanical testing of aortic tissue were patients with ascending thoracic aortic aneurysms who were scheduled for elective surgery, transplant donors with normal aortas, and patients with acute aortic dissection. A subset of patients with ascending thoracic aortic aneurysms also had corresponding histological data. A separate subset of the ascending thoracic aortic aneurysm group underwent pre-operative MRI, and healthy volunteers without aortic disease underwent the identical MRI protocol as imaging controls. Written consent was obtained from all participants at the time of pre-operative clinic visits for elective aneurysm patients, post-operatively or through the substitute decision-maker in a deferred consent process for emergent dissection patients, or by research consent through Trillium Gift of Life for organ donors. A flow chart of encapsulating patients participating in the different phases of the study is shown in Figure S1.

Tissue collection and preparation

Aortic tissue was collected from the operating room (or the multi-organ transplant coordinator in the case of tissue donors) and immediately placed on ice. Tissues were stored in Ringer's lactate solution on ice during transportation (10-minute indoor walk from operating room to the laboratory) and kept at 4°C until testing. Mechanical testing was performed within 24 hours of resection as previously described 10.

Ascending aortic tissue samples were collected as complete rings (Figure 1a) with the distal outer curvature marked with a surgical clip by the surgeon in the operating room. In the case of acute aortic dissections, the resected aortic tissue was also oriented by a clip with case-by-case explanation to the research team by the surgical team. The undissected portion, wherever that occurred for the aortic specimen, was used for biomechanical testing. Previous work by our group and others found that while some regional variability exists within a single ascending aorta, it is much less than variability between aortas, and that the biomechanics in one region of the aorta strongly predicted the biomechanics on the other side 42, 43.

From the outer curvature (OC), up to three 14 mm x 14 mm squares were sectioned for biaxial tensile testing. Additionally, ascending aortic tissue samples collected during surgery were cut into 6 mm x 30 mm strips from the OC adjacent to the samples taken for biaxial testing (Figure 1b).

Biaxial tensile testing

The low strain modulus (LSM), defined as secant modulus from the origin to first deviation from linearity, and energy loss (EL) of the tissue specimens were measured through biaxial tensile testing using a BioTester (CellScale) as before 16, 35, 42 . First, tissue thickness was captured using a high magnification zoom lens (Navitar) and measured with ImageJ for subsequent stress calculations. Samples were then submerged in 37°C Ringer's lactate solution and subject to 10 preconditioning cycles and 3 test cycles of 25% equibiaxial strain. Stress-strain curves generated from each of the three test cycles were then analyzed using an in-house MATLAB script to quantify the LSM and EL in the circumferential and longitudinal directions. Results from each test cycle were then averaged prior to use in subsequent analysis.

Peel testing (delamination strength)

During aortic dissection, the aortic media splits along its length. The ease with which this failure mechanism may occur is represented by delamination strength (Sd), measured as the average force required to peel the layers of the aortic wall normalized to the width of the delaminated sample. Tissues at higher risk of aortic dissection would exhibit lower delamination strengths 16 . The 6 mm x 30 mm strips obtained from the OC adjacent to the biaxial testing specimen were prepared by introducing a small incision into the medial layer using a scalpel. The tissue was peeled manually from proximal to distal end to leave 14 mm of intact tissue for testing. The strip was subsequently submerged in 37 °C Ringer's lactate solution and subject to uniaxial peel testing using the BioTester 16, 35, 42, 44 . Average force was calculated following initial peak (x1 in Figure 1b) and prior to complete failure (x2 in Figure 1b) to capture steady-state dissection propagation.

Histology Analysis

Following biomechanical testing, the 14 × 14 mm outer-curvature square samples were fixed in 10% formalin and paraffin-embedded. Samples were cut circumferentially before edge-embedding to allow multiple regions of the tissue to be represented on each slide. Sections (4 μm) were stained with Movat's pentachrome and digitally scanned at up to 20× magnification. Three 20× images of the aortic media were acquired at random locations from each slide. The area fractions of elastin, collagen, proteoglycans and cytoplasm were quantified by colorimetric analysis using previously published custom MATLAB scripts 45 , and the three measurements were averaged to obtain one value for each constituent per specimen. Histological analysis was limited to outer-curvature tissue and was not available for all specimens with complete mechanical testing.

Pre-operative Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)

Patients were imaged on a Siemens Magnetom Biograph mMR (3T) scanner using a dedicated 32-channel receiver coil. Standard 2D phase contrast (PC) images were acquired with breath holding and retrospective electrocardiographic (ECG)-gating at multiple defined locations, acquired perpendicular to the flow of blood at each level, sinotubular junction (STJ), mid-ascending aorta, and distal descending aorta at the level of the diaphragm (typical scan parameters 75-100 phases, 1.5 x 1.5 mm pixel spacing, 6 mm slice thickness, velocity encoding (VENC) of 150 cm/s). Balanced cine steady-state free precession (SSFP) imaging was obtained in sagittal oblique candy cane orientation for centerline length measurements.

Derivation of in vivo biomechanical parameters

We investigated MRI derived markers for aortic stiffness including aortic strain, distensibility, compliance, and aortic stiffness index (ASI) as defined previously 19 . Aortic strain was defined as the change in cross-sectional area of the aorta divided by the minimal cross-sectional area. The related parameters of distensibility, compliance, and ASI were then derived by using maximal aortic strain and including the effect of pulsatile hemodynamic loading on the vessel wall as represented by the pulse pressure (difference between systolic and diastolic blood pressure) 46 . To measure the change in cross sectional area across the cardiac cycle, the aortic contours were segmented at midascending aorta on 2D-PC MRI using CVI42 software, version 5.12.7 (Circle Cardiovascular Imaging, Canada). Each phase was reviewed by an operator and manual correction performed when the automatically detected boundaries deviated from the vessel wall. Standard systolic and diastolic brachial pressures by sphygmomanometer were obtained immediately prior to start of the MRI.

An alternative approach to estimate arterial stiffness independently of strain involves the measurement of pulse-wave velocity (PWV). PWV measures the speed of fluid wave propagation through a vessel and is related to the vessel stiffness by the Moens-Korteweg equation 47 . We measured aPWV using 2D-PC MRI acquired at the level of the STJ and the distal descending thoracic aorta (DDTA) at the aortic hiatus. The 2D-PC MRI contained encoded in-plane blood flow velocity that was used to a reconstruct blood flow over time curve at both STJ and DDTA levels using CVI42 software, version 5.12.7 (Circle Cardiovascular Imaging, Calgary, Canada). We then defined the zero of these flow curves as the arrival time of the fluid pulse wave and the lag between arrival times at STJ and DDTA as the transit time (TT) of the fluid pulse wave between both sampling sites. We measured the distance between both sampling sites as the centreline distance between STJ and DDTA on the three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction of the thoracic aorta. Machine learning model for risk group classification based on ex vivo aortic biomechanics

The ground truth of how aortic biomechanics reflects risk of aortic dissection was defined by considering the ex vivo biomechanical properties of patients with aortic dissection and the normal aortas of transplant donors without aortic disease. To assess how the ex vivo properties jointly discriminate dissected from normal aortas, seven classifiers were evaluated including L2-logistic regression, elastic-net logistic regression, shrinkage linear discriminant analysis (shrinkage LDA), partial least square with logistic regression (PLS-LR), random forest, Gaussian process with a radial basis function kernel, and gradient boosted trees (XGBoost). Five ex vivo predictors were used including S , longitudinal and circumferential EL, and longitudinal and circumferential LSM. To prevent information leakage, predictors were z-scored within each training fold using only that fold's mean and standard deviation, applied as the first step of a pipeline fitted inside the crossvalidation loop. Models were fitted by nested repeated stratified cross-validation with shuffling; the outer loop (five folds, five repeats, giving 455 out-of-fold predictions per model) provided out-of-fold predicted probabilities, while the inner loop (five folds, five repeats) selected the best hyperparameters by maximizing area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Shrinkage LDA and the Gaussian process classifier required no inner search, since their regularization was calculated from the training fold directly by the Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage estimate and marginal likelihood respectively. Class weights were balanced wherever applicable. The pooled out-of-fold predictions were used to estimate discrimination performance, and the 95% confidence interval for the AUC was estimated using a 2,000-sample nonparametric bootstrap, with resampling performed at the patient level to preserve all repeated cross-validation predictions within each patient. Pairwise DeLong comparisons between classifiers used one repeat-averaged out-of-fold probability per patient (n = 91), rather than the pooled 455 predictions, to avoid treating correlated repeated cross-validation scores as independent observations. Discrimination was evaluated against a permutation-based null distribution. Class labels were permuted 1,000 times, and the full nested cross-validation procedure was refitted for each permutation using three outer repeats for computational tractability. The permutation P value was calculated using add-one smoothing, yielding a minimum attainable P value of 1/1,001. No post hoc recalibration was applied. Calibration of the raw predicted probabilities was assessed using the Brier score and the expected and maximum calibration errors across five equal-width probability bins, with confidence intervals obtained from the same patient-level bootstrap. From the out-of-fold predicted probabilities, two decision thresholds were defined to form a three-zone ("rule-out / intermediate / rule-in") policy: (i) a low "rule-out" threshold set at the 5th percentile of the out-of-fold predicted probabilities among dissection patients, giving 95% sensitivity, and (ii) a high "rule-in" threshold set at the 95th percentile of the out-of-fold predicted probabilities among donor patients, giving 95% specificity. Because both thresholds were selected from the out-of-fold predictions, their operating characteristics were not considered independent estimates. Threshold selection was therefore repeated within cross-validation, with thresholds derived exclusively from each training fold's out-of-fold predictions and then applied only to held-out patients who had no influence on their selection. After freezing the classifier, aneurysm cases were projected onto the risk classifier to visualize their position and classifier-estimated probability relative to the normal and dissection reference classes.

Aortic Biomechanical vulnerability index (AortoBVI)

We refer to the continuous probability output of the classifier as the aortic biomechanical vulnerability index (AortoBVI). Values near 0 indicate a biomechanical profile similar to normal donor tissue, whereas values near 1 indicate a profile similar to tissue from patients with aortic dissection. The classifier was trained once using the donor and dissection specimens and was then fixed. AortoBVI values for elective aneurysm cases were therefore obtained by projecting them onto this fixed reference axis rather than by refitting the model. The aneurysm cases were subsequently positioned along this axis to show their relative similarity to the donor and dissection reference groups.

Estimation of AortoBVI from MRI

Each aneurysm patient's AortoBVI was obtained from the tissue-derived risk classifier.

The regression target was then the logit-transformed AortoBVI, y = ln , which maps AortoBVI's bounded probability scale onto an unbounded continuous scale suitable for linear regression while preserving signed distance from the decision boundary. This target was modeled using ordinary least squares regression. To limit overparameterization in this small cohort, the multivariable model family was prespecified to include age, aortic stiffness index (ASI), and pulse-wave velocity (aPWV). The strainbased family of in vivo biomechanical parameters (i.e., strain, distensibility, compliance, and ASI) are highly inter-related and derived from the same set of constituent variables 19 . Therefore, only ASI was selected as the representative pressure-deformation stiffness metric based on superior performance when comparing single-variable models. aPWV was included as a complementary velocity-based measure of aortic stiffening. Of candidate clinical parameters to include, our previous studies 16, 48 along with others [49] [50] [51] have consistently reported age to be most predictive of ex vivo aortic biomechanics.

Alongside the multivariable model and its single-predictor counterparts, maximum aortic diameter was also evaluated as a standalone benchmark, since it remains the primary parameter governing current clinical guidelines for risk stratification and intervention planning.

Candidate functional forms were intentionally restricted to linear terms to preserve interpretability and numerical stability at n=38. Because all models were fixed and prespecified in advance, no selection among equations was performed. Coefficient of determination (R ), root-mean-square error (RMSE), and mean absolute error (MAE) are reported both in the leave-one-out cross-validated (LOOCV) form and in-sample.

Each candidate equation generated a predicted AortoBVI for every aneurysm patient, representing the patient's estimated location on the tissue-derived risk classifier. These predicted locations were then compared directly, on a patient-by-patient basis, against the corresponding tissue-derived AortoBVI from the frozen classifier. Uncertainty was characterized in terms of both precision and accuracy. Precision was quantified using a 95% prediction interval (PI) for each patient, incorporating residual variance and coefficient uncertainty. Prediction intervals were used rather than confidence intervals because the clinically relevant quantity is uncertainty in an individual patient prediction rather than in the mean response for patients with similar measurements. Because prediction intervals are model-specific, their widths were compared directly between models. Accuracy was assessed separately by determining whether each model's prediction fell within a common tolerance of the measured value. A shared tolerance was required because evaluating each model against its own prediction interval would favour less precise models with wider intervals. The tolerance was therefore fixed at the smallest cross-validated RMSE of all models since fixing it at the larger value would have let both models clear a lenient band and obscured any real difference between them. Model performance was assessed by examining how well these predicted locations captured the true patient locations, including with respect to the predefined rule-out, intermediate zone, and rule-in boundaries.

Statistical analysis

All statistical tests were two-sided, and the patient was the unit of analysis throughout. Repeated biaxial test cycles, adjacent outer-curvature tissue regions and the three histological fields acquired per slide were averaged to obtain a single patient-level value before statistical analysis. Continuous variables are reported as mean ± s.d., except aortic diameter, which is reported as median [interquartile range], and categorical variables as n/N (%) or n (%) as appropriate. Normality was not assumed, and non-parametric tests were used for group comparisons because several ex vivo mechanical and MRI-derived variables showed non-Gaussian distributions. Differences among transplant donor, aneurysm and dissection groups were assessed using Kruskal-Wallis tests. Maximum diameter was compared between aneurysm and dissection groups using a two-sided Mann-Whitney test. Omnibus P values were Holm-corrected across the five prespecified ex vivo mechanical comparisons, and pairwise group differences were assessed using Dunn's post-hoc tests with Holm correction. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or, when any expected cell count was below five, the Fisher-Freeman-Halton test with Monte Carlo estimation of the P value (20,000 replicates). For comparisons between aneurysm patients and healthy MRI volunteers, continuous variables were assessed using two-sided Mann-Whitney tests and categorical variables using two-sided Fisher's exact tests. P values for the five MRI-derived variables were Holm-corrected across comparisons. Associations between the five MRI-derived indices and five ex vivo mechanical properties were first assessed using ordinary linear regression. Inference across this prespecified family of 25 MRI-mechanics associations used HC3 heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors, with P values adjusted across all 25 tests using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure and reported as q values. Spearman rank correlations, with Benjamini-Hochberg correction across the same 25 pairs, were additionally calculated as a rank-based sensitivity analysis. We further modelled the relationship for some in vivo parameters (strain, distensibility, compliance, and ASI) using piecewise linear regression due to noted nonlinearity. The breakpoint was estimated as the value jointly maximizing the summed R² across all five ex vivo outcomes, with breakpoints constrained to values supported by at least 20% of patients on each side. This criterion was met on the raw scale for strain, distensibility and compliance. For ASI, because of its more heavily right-skewed distribution, adequate support on both sides was achieved only on the natural log scale. The resulting breakpoint was therefore backtransformed to raw units and modelled as a linear breakpoint on the raw scale, consistent with the other three parameters. The breakpoint was additionally tested with Davies test in Python using the 'piece-wise regression' toolbox 52 . For estimation of tissue-derived AortoBVI from MRI, predictive performance was evaluated using leave-one-out crossvalidation (LOOCV) and summarized by cross-validated 𝑅 , root-mean-square error (RMSE) and mean absolute error (MAE), together with Pearson r and Lin's concordance correlation coefficient. Apparent in-sample 𝑅 values were reported separately for comparison. Histological constituents were compared across the three AortoBVI zones using Kruskal-Wallis tests with Dunn's post-hoc tests, and correlated with continuous tissue-derived logit AortoBVI using Spearman rank correlation, with Benjamini-Hochberg correction applied across the five constituents. Associations between each constituent and AortoBVI were further assessed using sequential linear regression models with HC3 heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors, adjusting stepwise for age, diameter, sex and bicuspid aortic valve status, with standardized coefficients reported and P values Benjamini-Hochberg-corrected across the five constituents within each adjustment level. A selection audit compared patients with and without available histology using standardized mean differences for tissue-derived AortoBVI and the five ex vivo mechanical parameters. Statistical significance was defined as two-sided P < 0.05 or multiplicity-adjusted P or q < 0.05, as applicable. Analyses were performed in Python 3.13 using NumPy 2.5, pandas 3.0, SciPy 1.18, scikit-learn 1.9, statsmodels 0.14.6, scikitposthocs 0.14, xgboost 3.4 and piecewise-regression 1.5. Random seeds were fixed for all stochastic procedures. Statistical significance was defined as two-sided P < 0.05 or multiplicity-adjusted P or q < 0.05, as applicable.

Results

Ex vivo biomechanics differed across normal, aneurysm, and dissected aortas For the ex vivo biomechanical analysis, 353 elective ascending thoracic aortic aneurysm (ATAA) patients, 45 transplant donors with normal aortas, and 46 patients with acute aortic dissection underwent biaxial tensile and delamination testing (Figure 1 and Figure S1). Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. In the ATAA group, the mean age was 61.3 ± 13.5 years, 252 patients (71.4%) were male, 143 patients (40.5%) had a bicuspid aortic valve, and the median maximum aortic diameter was 52.0 [48.0-55.0] mm. In the donor group, the mean age was 46.4 ± 13.8 years, and 28 patients (65.1%) were male; aortic diameter was not recorded because preoperative imaging was unavailable. In the dissection group, the mean age was 65.8 ± 14.1 years, 31 patients (67.4%) were male, and the median maximum aortic diameter was 48.5 [45.0-53.8] mm.

All ex vivo biomechanical properties differed among transplant donors, ATAA, and dissection groups after Holm correction across the five Kruskal-Wallis tests (all H(2) ≥ 12.41, adjusted P ≤ 0.002; Figure 2). Circumferential EL (EL ) was significantly higher in aneurysm and dissection patients than in donors (Figure 2a). In contrast, circumferential LSM (LSM ) was lower in dissection patients than in donors (P = 0.011, Figure 2b) and aneurysm patients (P < 0.001, Figure 2b). Longitudinal EL (EL ) showed a similar pattern to EL , being higher in both aneurysm and dissection patients than in donors (P < 0.001; Figure 2c). Longitudinal LSM (LSM ) was lower in dissection than in aneurysm patients (P = 0.001; Figure 2d), but did not differ significantly between dissection patients and donors (P = 0.095). Delamination strength (S ) was lower in aneurysm and dissection patients than in donors (both P < 0.001, Figure 2e) and in dissection than aneurysm patients (P = 0.013, Figure 2e).

A classifier separated normal from dissected aortas

We trained seven supervised binary classifiers to separate donor (N = 45) from dissected aortas (N = 46) using five ex vivo biomechanical parameters. Across all classifiers, discrimination was comparable (out-of-fold AUCs = 0.874-0.915; Figure 3a). Bootstrap confidence intervals showed substantial overlap across models (Figure 3b), with no significant pairwise differences after multiplicity correction of the DeLong tests (minimum adjusted q = 0.175). The full seven-model comparison is provided in Tables S1a and S1b.

Among classifiers without statistically significant pairwise differences, we selected L2regularized logistic classifier as the primary model for its clinical familiarity and closedform deployment equation. This classifier distinguished donors from dissected aortas with strong discriminative performance (nested cross-validation out-of-fold AUC = 0.901, 95% bootstrap CI 0.836-0.954). Permutation testing with 1,000 label permutations confirmed that this discrimination exceeded that expected by chance (P < 0.001; mean null AUC = 0.495; Figure 3c). The model also showed good calibration, with a Brier score of 0.129 and an expected calibration error of 0.042 (Figure 3d and Tables S1a).

Aortic biomechanical vulnerability index (AortoBVI) stratified patients into clinically meaningful categories

The fitted L2 classifier's continuous probability output was defined as the Aortic Biomechanical Vulnerability Index (AortoBVI) and calculated for all 353 elective aneurysms without model refitting. To translate AortoBVI into clinically interpretable risk categories, we defined two clinically motivated thresholds forming a three-zone risk classifier: a rule-out threshold (threshold = 0.313) corresponding to 95% sensitivity and a negative predictive value of 0.909, and a rule-in threshold (threshold = 0.671) corresponding to 95% specificity, yielding a positive predictive value of 0.912. Between these thresholds, cases were classified as an "intermediate zone" phenotype. Aneurysm patients spanned across these decision boundaries, with 26.3% (N = 93) falling below the rule-out threshold (normal-like biomechanical phenotype), 46.8% (N = 165) exceeding the rule-in threshold (dissection-like phenotype), and 26.9% (N = 95) lying within the intermediate risk zone (Figure 3e). A parallel sensitivity/specificity analysis and threshold re-selection altered classification proportions for three alternative boundary definitions (Figure S2).

AortoBVI correlated with extracellular matrix content

As an independent biological validation of the biomechanics-derived phenotypes, we compared histology across the three AortoBVI zones in 184 aneurysm patients with available histology (Figure 1c). The histology subset was representative of the full cohort with respect to AortoBVI (standardized mean difference = 0.011; Figure S3a). Elastin area fraction differed across zones (Kruskal-Wallis P < 0.001, Figure 4a), with lower elastin in dissection-like than normal-like and intermediate patients (both P < 0.001). Collagen area fraction also differed across zones (Kruskal-Wallis P = 0.007, Figure 4b), with dissection-like patients showing higher collagen content than normal-like patients (P = 0.006). As a continuous measure, tissue-derived AortoBVI correlated inversely with elastin (Spearman ρ = -0.458, q < 0.001) and positively with collagen (ρ = 0.288, q < 0.001) (Figure S3b,c). The elastin association persisted after sequential adjustment for age, diameter, sex and bicuspid aortic valve status (fully adjusted standardized β = -0.303; q = 0.013), whereas the collagen association did not survive after adjustment for age and was not significant in the fully adjusted model (q = 0.179; Figure S3d).

MRI-derived parameters did not differ between ATAA patients and healthy controls

For AortoBVI to be useful for clinical risk assessment, it must be predictable noninvasively using, e.g, medical imaging from which in vivo biomechanical parameters can be estimated. Among ATAA patients whose aortas underwent mechanical testing, 44 had preoperative MRI with at least one MRI-derived biomechanical parameter (strain, distensibility, compliance, ASI, or aPWV) available. Of these, 38 had complete MRI data and corresponding tissue measurements and were included in the paired in vivo-ex vivo analyses. An additional 8 healthy volunteers also underwent the same MRI protocol (Figure S4). Baseline characteristics of the MRI-available cohorts are summarized in Table 2.

We first tested if individual MRI-derived biomechanical parameters differed between normal and diseased states, but none of the five measured parameters differed significantly between ATAA patients and healthy imaging controls after Holm correction (Holm P > 0.125; Figure S4).

ASI associated most consistently with ex vivo biomechanics

We next considered relationships between in vivo MRI-derived vs. ex vivo biomechanical parameters (Figure 5). Linear regression yielded some significant associations (q < 0.05), most notably ASI with all ex vivo parameters, but with weak to moderate R² of 0.20 to 0.31 (Figure S5). Because several in vivo-ex vivo relationships exhibited inflection points not captured by a linear model, we used piecewise linear regression to assess whether model fit improved (Figure 5 and Figure S6). Piecewise regression comparing aortic strain with the circumferential metrics yielded R² = 0.29 for EL , R² = 0.076 for LSM , and R² = 0.23 for S , all improved over the corresponding linear models (Figure 5a-c and Figure S5). The piecewise model identified a breakpoint at 7.20% strain. Overall, higher strain was associated with lower EL and higher S , while the relationship with LSM was not significant. The corresponding piecewise relationships for the longitudinal metrics also follow similar patterns with R² = 0.21 for EL and R² = 0.14 for LSM (Figure S6a-b). Similarly, piecewise regression for distensibility improved over the linear models with R = 0.30 for EL , R = 0.22 for LSM , and R = 0.24 for S , with a breakpoint at 1.19 × 10⁻³ mmHg -1 (Figure 5d-f). The corresponding longitudinal relationships yielded R² = 0.34 for EL and R² = 0.28 for LSM (Figure S6c-d). For compliance, the piecewise models yielded R = 0.25 for EL , R = 0.17 for LSM , and R = 0.087 for S with the breakpoint at 1.77 mm² mmHg⁻¹ (Figure 5g-i). Longitudinal fits yielded R = 0.23 for EL and R = 0.24 LSM (Figure S6e-f). For ASI, the piecewise models improved over the linear models and were significantly associated with all five ex vivo parameters, with R = 0.23 for EL , R = 0.36 for LSM , and R = 0.20 for S , with a breakpoint at 9.27 (Figure 5j-l). Longitudinal relationships showed R = 0.31 for EL and R = 0.34 for LSM (Figure S6g-h). In contrast to the strain-derived parameters, aPWV showed no significant association with ex vivo biomechanics in either direction (R² = 0.005-0.060, all P ≥ 0.139), and no breakpoint was detected (Figure 5m-o and Figure S6i-j).

MRI-derived measures estimated AortoBVI more closely than diameter

Given the strong performance of AortoBVI in stratifying patients by biomechanical phenotypes, we used AortoBVI to evaluate candidate imaging models. For the 38 aneurysm patients with complete imaging and tissue measurements, AortoBVI classified 8 patients (21.1%) as normal-like, 15 patients (39.5%) as dissection-like, and 15 (39.5%) patients as intermediate.

We evaluated five prespecified imaging models against tissue-derived AortoBVI using leave-one-out cross-validation (Figure 6a): ASI alone, age alone, aPWV alone, maximum aortic diameter alone, and a multivariable model combining age, ASI and aPWV. Age was included for its established association with ex vivo biomechanics 16 , ASI for its strong and consistent associations with ex vivo biomechanics among strain-derived indices (Figure 5), and aPWV as a complementary velocity-based measure of aortic stiffening. Diameter was deliberately excluded from the multivariable model and evaluated separately as the clinical benchmark, allowing direct comparison with current intervention criteria.

The full comparison of all five models is shown in Figure 6 and Table S2. ASI was a stronger single predictor of tissue-derived AortoBVI (R² = 0.406, P < 0.001; Figure 6b) than age (R² = 0.357, P < 0.001; Figure 6c) or aPWV (not significant; Figure 6d), whereas the multivariable model achieved the best overall performance (R² = 0.580, P < 0.001; Figure 6e), exceeding diameter alone which was weakly (R² = 0.116, P=0.034) correlated with AortoBVI (Figure 6f). This represented nearly a 5-fold increase in explained variation (58.0% versus 11.6%). The multivariable model also had lower prediction error than diameter (RMSE, 1.324 versus 1.921; MAE, 1.075 versus 1.497) and stronger agreement with tissue-derived AortoBVI (Lin's concordance correlation coefficient, 0.742 versus 0.236). Additionally, for distinguishing the dissection-like zone (n = 15) from the remainder of the cohort (normal-like and intermediate, n = 23) at the rule-in threshold (AortoBVI = 0.671), the multivariable model achieved an AUC of 0.928 (95% CI, 0.826-0.994; Figure 6g), with 80% sensitivity, 87% specificity and 84% accuracy (Figure 6h), whereas diameter alone showed limited discrimination (AUC = 0.620; Figure 6g), with 47% sensitivity, 83% specificity and 68% accuracy (Figure 6h). For distinguishing the normallike zone (n = 8) from the remainder of the cohort (intermediate and dissection-like, n = 30) at the rule-out threshold (AortoBVI = 0.313), the multivariable model again showed greater discrimination than diameter (AUC = 0.771 versus 0.304; Figure 6i), identifying 50% of normal-like patients with 87% specificity and 79% accuracy, compared with 0% sensitivity for diameter despite its higher specificity (97%) and similar overall accuracy (76%) (Figure 6j). The higher specificity of diameter in this analysis resulted from its failure to classify any patients as normal-like, rather than from accurate identification of the normal-like phenotype (0% sensitivity). At the individual-patient level, the 95% prediction intervals for the multivariable model were narrower in all patients than those of diameter, with a 32.1% mean reduction in width (Figure 6k). Using a common tolerance of ±1.324 logit-AortoBVI units, the lowest cross-validated RMSE across all models, 27 of 38 multivariable predictions fell within tolerance of the tissue-derived AortoBVI, compared with 22 of 38 diameter predictions (Figure 6l). Exact three-zone agreement was also better for the multivariable model (25 of 38 patients, 65.8%, Figure S7a) than for diameter alone (21 of 38, 55.3%, Figure S7b). Although neither model classified a dissection-like patient as normal-like, diameter classified 4 of 8 normal-like patients as dissection-like, compared with 1 of 8 for the multivariable model (Figure S7a, b). Because ASI was the strongest MRI-derived predictor, we further examined whether a simple ASI threshold could enrich for the dissection-like phenotype. An ASI threshold >9.49 was exceeded exclusively by dissection-like patients, identifying 9 of 15 cases (60%) without any normallike or intermediate cases exceeding this value (Figure S7c). This threshold was close to the breakpoint independently identified in the ex vivo piecewise analysis (ASI = 9.27; Figure 5j-l), despite being derived from a different cohort and a different criterion.

Additionally, as a face-validity check, the multivariable model classified eight independent healthy MRI volunteers as five normal-like (AortoBVI 0.189 ± 0.072) and three intermediate (AortoBVI 0.471 ± 0.076), with no dissection-like. The three intermediate volunteers were aged 45, 67 and 78 years (AortoBVI 0.384, 0.515 and 0.515) (Figure S7d).

Discussion

Biomechanics of the ascending aorta are nonlinear and anisotropic, driven by the interplay among extracellular matrix components 18, [29] [30] [31] . In the aneurysmal state, aortic stress-strain behaviour is influenced by the heterogeneous nature of medial degeneration. Elastin fragmentation and loss, lamellar unit collapse, accumulation of immature disorganized collagen and pooling of glycosaminoglycans vary across diseased aortas and jointly influence aortic mechanical behaviour at low and high stresses, the transitions between stress domains, and aortic viscoelastic behaviour 13, 18, 30 . These complexities have prevented any single mechanical metric from capturing aortic wall fragility, which clinically manifests as aortic dissection, rupture and death. Aortic diameter, the dominant clinical metric, is even further removed, acting as an indirect surrogate of wall stress and disease severity, and likewise fails to capture aortic wall fragility.

To address this need, we avoided reductionism and integrated multiple biomechanical parameters via a machine learning approach capable of handling correlated variables, reflecting the nonlinear, heterogeneous nature of aortic wall mechanical behavior. The key innovation was to connect aortic biomechanics to clinically relevant endpoints and then present a pathway to clinical translation via imaging. Previous attempts to evaluate determinants of aortic risk have been hampered by the rarity of aortic dissection 21, 32, 33 , limiting traditional epidemiological approaches such as prospective trials. Alternative unproven endpoints such as growth are sometimes used 21, 32, 33 , but these studies are muddied by surgical interventions curtailing natural history 34 . The advent of machine learning in medicine has allowed this problem to be reimagined. By uniquely curating dissected and normal aortas (transplant donors) over 10 years, we trained our model on patients with unambiguous risk categorization. We observed a spectrum of risk which we termed the aortic biomechanical vulnerability index (AortoBVI), onto which aneurysm patients could be mapped. This first iteration demonstrated strong discriminative performance (AUC = 0.901, 95% bootstrap CI 0.836-0.954; Figure 3a,b) and good calibration (Brier score = 0.129; Figure 3d), indicating that aortic biomechanics can reliably stratify biomechanical risk phenotypes beyond a proof of concept. AortoBVI itself may serve as the elusive aortic endpoint for future studies exploring novel biomarkers and therapeutics.

The histological associations we observed provide orthogonal biological support for this interpretation. Elastin area fraction was inversely associated with tissue-derived AortoBVI, whereas collagen area fraction was positively associated with AortoBVI (Figure S3b,c). Elastin loss and collagen accumulation are consistent with the extracellular-matrix remodeling in medial degeneration, supporting AortoBVI's biological relevance beyond the mechanical measurements from which it was derived.

Of the 353 aneurysms undergoing elective surgery, 165 were categorized as dissectionlike, 93 normal-like, and 95 as intermediate (Figure 3e). Thus, while many aneurysms were appropriately operated on, we argue that many were not. There are, therefore, patients who have suffered surgical complications for whom durations of complicationfree survival were unnecessarily shortened. Aortic diameter explained only a small proportion of the variance in tissue-derived AortoBVI across the full cohort (R² = 0.075, n = 350; Figure S8a) and diameter strata showed 10 of 29 patients (34%) with diameter <45 mm were dissection-like, while 20 of 113 patients (17.7%) at or above the 55 mm surgical threshold remained normal-like (Figure S8b). These findings align with the prior clinical and translational studies describing the poor performance of maximal aortic diameter in predicting aortic risk 6, 12, 13 . Yet, diameter thresholds for surgical decisionmaking driven by isolated population-based studies currently inform all cardiothoracic surgical society guidelines 4, 5 . Further shifting of size thresholds, as occurred in recent guideline iterations, is unlikely to resolve these issues 4, 5 . In our cohort, at the current 55 mm diameter threshold, 93 of 165 (56.4%) aneurysms with a dissection-like AortoBVI had diameters below this cutoff. Lowering the threshold to 50 mm would still leave 37 of 165 (22.4%) dissection-like aneurysms below the threshold while increasing the proportion of normal-like aneurysms exceeding the threshold from 22.0% to 58.2% (Figure S8c). Here, we proposed a personalized, biomechanics-based approach to aneurysm risk assessment.

To make this risk assessment clinically tractable, we introduced a novel multi-parameter MRI-based solution that outperforms diameter in estimating AortoBVI. This MRI-AortoBVI could function as a virtual biopsy after an aneurysm diagnosis is made, providing insight into the biomechanical phenotype of the patient, thereby aiding clinical decision-making such as reducing surveillance imaging burden in low-risk patients and enabling earlier life-saving intervention in high-risk patients.

As a first step in its development, we interrogated individual MRI-based metrics for measuring aortic biomechanics in vivo. We found piecewise linear relationships with inflection points between strain, compliance and distensibility, and energy loss (EL) and delamination strength (S ). The inflection points may potentially be useful for dichotomizing these parameters into high-and low-risk categories. The relationships between the in vivo and ex vivo parameters were surprising and multi-pronged. For instance, strain and strain-based variables only moderately correlated with low strain modulus, despite an expected strong relationship based on their definition. This reflects the complex nonlinear stress-strain relationship of aortic tissue and the unpredictability of where each individual patient is positioned on the stress-strain curve in vivo. Notably, strain, compliance, distensibility, and ASI also correlated with EL and S , two clinically relevant parameters that have been correlated with the underlying tissue microstructure and with different at-risk patient groups 13, 15, 16, 18, 35 , but on the surface are unrelated to strain. Energy loss reflects the viscoelastic properties of the ascending aorta, partly attributed to proteoglycan content 18, 30, 36, 37 . Increased proteoglycan content is a prototypical feature of medial degeneration 36 , in which pools of excess proteoglycans generate localized Donnan swelling pressures that lead to aortic tissue delamination or dissection 38, 39 . This may explain why energy loss tracks with delamination strength. Additionally, our group and others have demonstrated the interdependence of extracellular matrix components within the aortic wall, whereby proteoglycan content was found to directly influence the elastin and collagen networks by pre-straining fibers 30, 37 . This interaction was hypothesized to modulate fiber recruitment and has been experimentally shown to modulate the nonlinear tensile behaviour of the tissue 30, 37 . Because aortic viscoelastic and failure behaviour are impractical to directly measure in vivo, these results encouragingly suggest that the interdependency of extracellular matrix components may be leveraged and simply-obtained in vivo biomechanical properties may still capture the desired ex vivo parameters.

In contrast to the strain-based parameters, aPWV did not show a significant univariate association with ex vivo tissue mechanics in this cohort (Figure 5m-o, Figure S6i-j). This was initially surprising given that aPWV has well-recognized associations with atherosclerosis, hypertension, and aging 40, 41 and that previous work using carotid-femoral pulse wave velocity (cfPWV) in patients with ATAAs has demonstrated correlations with aortic growth rate 26, 27, 32 . One possible explanation is that aPWV was measured from the sinotubular junction to the distal descending aorta and does not reflect the ascending aorta alone. However, its ongoing utility in imaging-based biomechanical phenotyping will require iterative re-examinations.

While these associations ground the in vivo measurements in established ex vivo aortic mechanics, the objective was not to estimate any single tissue property in isolation, but to determine whether clinically accessible variables could reproduce each patient's position along the tissue-derived vulnerability continuum (AortoBVI). The combination of age, ASI and aPWV provided the strongest approximation of tissue-derived AortoBVI (R² = 0.580; P < 0.001), outperforming all single-variable models, including maximum aortic diameter (R² = 0.116). The ability to non-invasively identify a dissection-like biomechanical phenotype with an AUC of 0.928 at this early stage is a convincing signal that this workflow can generate a clinically useful tool for aortic aneurysm risk stratification.

Importantly, the components of the multivariable model are captured in a single scan and require no extensive computational modeling, giving it tremendous scalability potential. MRI remains the predominant method of following young patients with thoracic aortic aneurysms who require lifelong surveillance imaging. Therefore, adoption of MRI-based aortic biomechanics lies within current clinical workflows.

Some limitations of our approach should be acknowledged. Although the ex vivo risk classifier was internally validated using nested cross-validation and bootstrap confidence intervals, its structure, thresholds, and performance estimates were all derived within a single-center dataset and therefore still require external validation in independent cohorts. Moreover, AortoBVI should be interpreted as a continuous measure of similarity along a donor-to-dissection biomechanical phenotype rather than as an absolute probability of future dissection. The extent to which this biomechanical axis determines prospective clinical events remains to be established. AortoBVI also describes the material properties of the aorta alone and should be interpreted in the context of the forces applied to that aorta. For example, one dissected aorta in our study demonstrated low AortoBVI but belonged to a patient with a resting blood pressure above 200 mmHg. Additionally, the categorical AortoBVI zones depend on the selected operating characteristics. The primary rule-out and rule-in boundaries were chosen to prioritize high sensitivity and specificity, respectively, and alternative clinically plausible boundary definitions altered the proportions of aneurysms assigned to the three zones (Figure S2). To address the patients who lie close to these boundaries, the predicted AortoBVI is provided as an interval rather than a singular number, therefore avoiding absolute risk zone assignment for patients for whom there is uncertainty. In relating ex vivo and in vivo parameters, aPWV was pre-selected in the multivariable model given its alternative mechanism to the stiffness-based group of features. Its incremental contribution to the multivariable imaging model (Figure 6), may reflect complementary, subtle information about ascending aortic biomechanics beyond stiffness-based parameters alone. However, as aPWV was not associated with tissue-derived AortoBVI in this cohort in univariate analysis, this interpretation requires caution; a larger paired cohort is needed to confirm whether aPWV meaningfully improves risk estimation beyond age and ASI. Similarly, because the paired in vivo-ex vivo cohort is small (n = 38), overfitting may compromise the regression equations. We mitigated this in two ways: first, by restricting the candidate model space to simple, prespecified, and interpretable functional forms rather than a data-driven search; and second, by reporting performance via leave-one-out cross-validation rather than in-sample fit (Table S2, Figure 6). Even so, the current regression coefficients and prediction intervals should be viewed as a demonstration of signal and biological plausibility.

In conclusion, we present AortoBVI, a tissue-derived, machine-learning-defined continuum of aortic biomechanical vulnerability that can be estimated non-invasively from MRI. By moving beyond diameter as an imperfect surrogate of aortic wall behavior, this framework provides a path toward personalized aneurysm assessment by identifying biomechanical phenotypes that are not captured by size alone. Because AortoBVI is built from routinely available clinical variables and a quantitative imaging workflow that could be adapted to existing surveillance imaging platforms, it offers a practical foundation for prospective validation and the future transition from population-based size thresholds toward individualized, biomechanics-informed decision-making.
