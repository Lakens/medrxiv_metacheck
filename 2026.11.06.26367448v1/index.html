Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Introduction

Previous work has shown that experimentally induced colonisation with Neisseria lactamica (Nlac) is safe and associated with reduced meningococcal colonisation. This pilot study aims to establish the safety, induction of colonisation and immunogenicity following intranasal inoculation with a mixture of four strains of genetically modified Nlac (GM-Nlac). Each of these strains express meningococcal vaccine antigens present in current vaccines: Neisseria adhesin A (NadA) and one of four different variants of Factor H-binding protein (FHbp), the latter of which is specifically mutated to abrogate its ability to bind the human complement protein, Factor H.

Introduction

Meningococcal disease and vaccination strategies

The development of invasive meningococcal disease (IMD) requires prior nasopharyngeal colonisation with Neisseria meningitidis (Nmen), which is governed by complex interactions between the organism and its host [1]. The majority of meningococcal colonisation events remain asymptomatic, with only a minority progressing to invasive disease. Nmen colonisation prevalence varies by location and age. In high-income settings, between approximately 2.5% to 12% of the population are colonised, increasing from very low prevalence in infants, to a peak of approximately 25% in some demographics of young adults [2] [3] [4]. This surge in colonisation prevalence is associated with increased IMD incidence, particularly amongst university and college students [5]. Sadly, such outbreaks continue to occur; in the UK an outbreak of serogroup B meningococcal disease in 2026 was associated with 21 confirmed cases including 2 fatalities [6].

Glycoconjugate meningococcal vaccines such as the quadrivalent 'ACWY' have dramatically reduced the incidence of IMD in a serogroup-specific manner. Such vaccines provide individual protection against IMD through induction of serum bactericidal antibody (SBA) activity, but also contribute to herd protection by reducing the acquisition of asymptomatic nasopharyngeal colonisation, thereby interrupting transmission [7]. However, for serogroup B meningococcal strains, conjugate vaccination strategies are precluded by poor immunogenicity of the polysaccharide capsule, which results from structural homology between the capsular polysaccharide (α-2,8-polysialic acid) and neural carbohydrates [8].

Vaccines for serogroup B meningococcal strains have therefore focused on utilisation of surface exposed, sub-capsular proteins, with two such products currently licenced (4CMenB and MenB-FHbp). While both provide individual protection from IMD via induction of SBA, they do not impact upon acquisition of asymptomatic colonisation and are therefore unlikely to induce herd protection [2, 9, 10]. In keeping with the World Health Organization's mission to defeat meningitis by 2030, alternative strategies are required to interrupt the asymptomatic colonisation state for serogroup B meningococcal strains in order to induce herd protection, noting that the meningococcus resides in a strictly human niche [11, 12].

Exploiting Neisseria lactamica to reduce meningococcal colonisation and disease

The human pharynx is colonised by a wide range of bacterial species which, over time and repeated exposure, provide natural immunity against diseases caused by opportunistic pathobionts such as Nmen [13] [14] [15]. Neisseria lactamica (Nlac) is a non-capsulate, nonpathogenic, obligate human commensal that is closely related to Nmen. Like its pathobiont cousin, the exclusive reservoir of Nlac is the human upper respiratory tract. Nlac colonises a high proportion of young children (up to ~ 60-70% in some cross-sectional surveys) [16] [17] [18], but colonisation frequency wanes through adolescence into adulthood, where it is encountered only rarely. Age-specific rates of meningococcal colonisation and disease inversely correlate with Nlac colonisation prevalence, and a causative relationship has been demonstrated between Nlac colonisation and protection against meningococcal colonisation in the controlled human infection (CHI) setting [16, 19, 20]. While the mechanism(s) underpinning this effect remain incompletely understood, mathematical modelling predicts that the protective effect of Nlac against meningococcal colonisation persists for several years, suggesting immune-mediated component [21].

In support of this hypothesis, we have previously demonstrated that experimental colonisation with Nlac induces systemic adaptive immune responses, including Nmen-crossreactive functional (opsonophagocytic) IgG, IgA/IgG-secreting plasma cells, and IgG memory B cells [22, 23]. Indeed, Nlac and Nmen are closely related at the genomic level, and both species express many similar, surface-expressed proteins that likely form the basis of observed immunological cross-reactivity [24]. Notably, colonisation with wild-type Nlac does not reliably induce cross-reactive anti-meningococcal SBA, the defined immune correlate of protection for IMD upon which new meningococcal vaccines are licenced. However, when considering the development of novel strategies to protect against meningococcal colonisation, generation of SBA may be redundant, noting that currently licenced serogroup B meningococcal vaccines induce potent SBA but offer no protection against nasopharyngeal colonisation with serogroup B strains [10]. On the contrary, experimental data pertaining to immunological studies of other upper respiratory tract pathobiont/pathogenic bacterial species, for example Streptococcus pneumoniae and Bordetella pertussis, suggest that protection against nasopharyngeal colonisation is mediated via resident mucosal adaptive immune responses, specifically via induction of IL-17 + CD4 + T cells and/or mucosal secretory IgA responses [25] [26] [27] [28] [29].

Neisseria lactamica Controlled Infection Model: Safety Considerations

Nlac is a strict commensal species of the upper respiratory tract and is non-pathogenic in the immunocompetent host. Rare case reports of invasive disease caused by Nlac are described but such cases are restricted either to patients with bony injury of the skull, which facilitates contiguous spread of this organism from the upper respiratory tract to the cerebrospinal fluid, or to those with severe immunocompromise [30].

To date, seven studies have been performed utilising wild-type Nlac (strain Y92-1009), including one study performed in late pregnancy [20, 22, 23, [31] [32] [33]. In addition, one further study involved inoculation of participants with genetically modified (GM) derivatives of Nlac strain Y92-1009, each either expressing the meningococcal vaccine antigen, NadA (strain 4NB1), or a silent construct that rendered the GM strain effectively wild type with respect to gene content (strain 4YB2) [34]. ln all previously performed studies, inoculation was via the intra-nasal route using inocula prepared from frozen or lyophilised inoculum stocks. Doses of Nlac utilised across the studies to-date range from ~10 4 -10 7 colony forming units (CFU). Dose-escalation studies performed in different settings have demonstrated variable kinetics relating to dose saturation. In the UK, an intra-nasal inoculum dose of ~ 1x10 5 CFU has resulted in a colonisation fraction of ~90-100% [23, [32] [33] [34], while in Mali the maximal attainable colonisation fraction was ~65% despite dose-escalation to 10 7 CFU [33].

Across all eight previous studies (~500 Nlac-inoculated participants in total), no serious adverse events associated with Nlac-attributable symptoms or signs have been reported following Nlac inoculation. Furthermore, in studies that were placebo-controlled, there has been no evidence that inoculation or colonisation with Nlac has induced adverse clinical symptoms in the respiratory tract or elsewhere above the frequency observed amongst placebo recipients [20, 23].

In nature, Nlac is transmitted amongst close household contacts, with evidence suggesting that transmission may be more common from children to adults [32]. Although data are limited, factors associated with increased likelihood of Nlac colonisation include increasing number of people per bedroom, presence of runny nose, and young age [18]. However, in our previous GM-Nlac study, bedroom sharers of study contacts were enrolled but no detectable transmission events occurred (n = 8 contact participants across a 90-day study period) and no environmental shedding of viable GM-Nlac bacteria was detected by culture of mask and exhaled air samples, routinely collected from all colonised participants [34].

Importantly, Nlac strains utilised across our CHI studies remain exquisitely sensitive to first line antibacterials that may be utilised to treat disease caused by Neisseria spp., including susceptibility to third-generation cephalosporins and fluoroquinolones. This significantly mitigates the risk to participants should rescue antimicrobials be considered necessary by clinical investigators. Consistent with the broader meningococcal literature, single dose oral ciprofloxacin (500mg) has been demonstrated to eradicate wild type or GM Nlac colonisation in 100% (n = 31) of experimentally-colonised participants in studies where antimicrobial eradication therapy was attempted [23, 34].

A multi-strain, genetically modified Neisseria lactamica challenge agent: 4xrNlac

The established safety, colonisation characteristics and antimicrobial susceptibility of Nlac provide a platform through which defined determinants of immunity elicited in response to nasopharyngeal colonisation can be investigated experimentally. In the current study, GM-Nlac strains have been developed to express two meningococcal surface antigens: Neisseria adhesin A (NadA) and factor H-binding protein (FHbp).

NadA is a type V trimeric autotransporter expressed by a relatively restricted subset of meningococcal strains. Expression of the nadA gene is negatively regulated by the NadR repressor protein and is subject to phase variation. It is a component of the licensed fourcomponent serogroup B meningococcal vaccine, 4CMenB, and induces functional antigenspecific antibody responses [35, 36]. Although the receptor for NadA binding has yet to be formally identified, eliminating NadA expression in Nmen strains reduces the capacity of knock-out mutants to bind epithelial cell lines [37]. Previous work has demonstrated that NadA can be functionally expressed by GM-Nlac and be safely delivered to the human nasopharyngeal mucosa. Sustained, prolonged nasopharyngeal colonisation with NadAexpressing GM-Nlac leads to induction of NadA-specific IgG-secreting plasma cells, increases in the proportion of circulating NadA-specific IgG memory B cells and, in a subset of colonised participants, increases reciprocal SBA titres against a NadA-overexpressing strain of Nmen (strain 5/99) from <4 to ≥4, consistent with protection against IMD caused by that strain [34]. Note that in the current study, each strain expresses the NadA variant, NadA-1.

FHbp is a surface-expressed lipoprotein produced by the large majority of circulating meningococcal strains, although its sequence and antigenic properties vary considerably between isolates [38]. The only definitive function of FHbp is its ability to bind human complement regulator Factor H (hFH), thereby reducing complement activation on the meningococcal surface and enhancing bacterial survival in human serum [39]. FHbp is included in both currently licensed serogroup B meningococcal vaccines and is an established target of serum bactericidal antibodies [40]. The family of FHbp proteins resolves phylogenetically into two subfamilies (A and B), which can be further subdivided into three variant groups (1, 2 and 3). Whilst Subfamily B contains only variant group 1 fhbp alleles, subfamily A is comprised of fhbp alleles from variant groups 2 and 3. Variants within subfamilies are more antigenically similar to one another than they are to members of the other subfamily, such that antibodies raised against variant 1 FHbp are only mildly crossreactive with FHbp from variant groups 2/3, and vice versa. Therefore, to represent the antigenic diversity of FHbp in this experiment, the bacterial inoculum consists of four otherwise-closely-related GM-Nlac strains, each one expressing a genetically attenuated version of one of the following four FHbp variants: 1.4, 1.13, 2.19 or 3.45. These variants are among the most prevalent FHbp alleles in invasive meningococcal strains circulating in the UK [38]. As a precaution against potentially introducing gain-of-function (i.e. hFH-binding) into Nlac, each heterologous antigen has undergone site-specific mutagenesis to reduce its ability to bind hFH. Multiple reports have identified amino acid residues specific for the interaction of FHbp variants and hFH, which have been demonstrated not only to attenuate hFH binding [41, 42], but to enhance the immunogenicity of FHbp in animal models [43, 44]. Additionally, in the case of variant group 2 FHbp, a pair of specific mutations have been identified that simultaneously disrupt FHbp-hFH interactions and thermally stabilise the relatively heat-labile N-terminal domain of the protein [45]. These thermostabilising mutations were utilised in this project. The FHbp variants expressed in this study are therefore: FHbp1.4(H248L), FHbp1.13(H248L), FHbp2.19(L130R G133D) and FHbp3.45(E254A).

The alleles coding for these proteins were rationally designed, synthetic genes which are designed to be as different from one another as possible at the nucleotide sequence level. This diversity has enabled the design of bespoke primer/probe combinations for discriminatory quantitative PCR (qPCR) assays. Transcriptional control of fhbp expression is driven by the same UAS-enhanced, hybrid porA/porB promoter as used in the previous GM-Nlac study [34]. All four strains challenge strains contain identical, Nlac-codon-optimised nadA-1 alleles, expression of which is driven by a second copy of the hybrid porA/porB promoter. Due to the endogenous expression of the lacZ gene (coding for β-Dgalactosidase) by wild type Nlac, our cloning strategy exploits the activity of this gene product to screen for new transformants. Because β-D-galactosidase activity is a key diagnostic criterion for identification of Nlac, allowing Nlac to grow as blue coloured colonies on bacteriological agar containing 5-bromo-4-chloro-3-indolyl-β-D-galactopyranoside (X-Gal), we had to ensure all resulting GM-Nlac strains retained β-D-galactosidase activity. As a result, each strain was transformed with a synthetic, rationally-designed copy of the lacZ gene. All nucleic acid constructs are stably incorporated into an intergenic locus in the Nlac chromosome (NHCIS1).

Extensive pre-clinical characterisation of each strain has been conducted, the results of which were submitted to the Advisory Committee on Releases to the Environment (ACRE) as part of our successful application for permission to conduct a Deliberate Release of these genetically modified organisms (GMOs) from the Department for Environment, Food and Rural Affairs (DEFRA) (https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/genetically-modified-organisms-university-of-southampton-25r5001). Briefly, we demonstrated: (i) surface coexpression of NadA and FHbp variants, (ii) attenuation of hFH binding to GM-Nlac strains expressing specifically mutagenised, as opposed to wild type FHbp variants, such that each of the 4 challenge strains bound only a similar amount of hFH as did control GM-Nlac strain, 4YB2, (iii) that each of the 4 challenge strains remained acutely susceptible to killing by 50% (v/v) native human plasma, (iv) that each of the 4 challenge strains remained acutely susceptible to frontline, clinically relevant antibiotics: ciprofloxacin, rifampicin and ceftriaxone, (v) that there were no changes to the internalisation of the 4 challenge strains by a human epithelial cell line, suggesting that expression of the heterologous antigens was unlikely to provoke a radical shift in the dynamics of infection nor the typical infective life course of the bacteria following challenge, (vi) that the genetic modifications to the Nlac chromosome were targeted to the correct locus, and that each strain contained only very minor nucleotide sequence differences (consistent with, for example, mechanisms of phase variation) from the wild type precursor Nlac strain, and (vii) that each strain remains refractory to transformation with exogenous nucleic acids derived from serogroup B Nmen strains.

The four strains have been combined into a single multistrain inoculum, enabling concurrent evaluation of immune responses to epidemiologically relevant FHbp variants spanning all three variant groups within a controlled colonisation and homologous rechallenge model. The ratio of GM-Nlac strains: 4NFA313_H (expressing NadA and FHbp1.4(H248L)), 4NFB313_H (expressing NadA and FHbp1.13(H248L)), 4NFC313 (expressing NadA and FHbp2.19(L130R G133D)) and 4NFD313_E (expressing NadA and FHbp3.45(E254A)) within this multistrain inoculum was empirically determined to be 1.05 : 1.00 : 2.33 : 1.69, respectively by colony genotyping of fhbp alleles.

Study Rationale

This study aims to establish the safety, colonisation potential and immunogenicity of nasal inoculation with a single multistrain inoculum containing four GM-Nlac strains, each expressing NadA and one of four FHbp variants. Data generated from this pilot study will inform the development of a subsequent randomised, double-blind, placebo controlled clinical study to determine whether colonisation with GM-Nlac induces sterilising protection against homologous rechallenge. If GM-Nlac colonisation induces homologous protection, subsequent studies will assess its impact on Nmen colonisation and determine whether GM-Nlac could be utilised to induce herd protection against Nmen by depleting the strictly human colonisation reservoir.

Methods and analysis

Study overview

In this prospective, single-arm, open label, controlled human infection model (CHIM) experiment, 10 healthy adults, enrolled as "challenge participants", will be challenged with 4xrNlac, administered as a nasal inoculum. Challenge participants will be followed up over 28 days to assess safety and nasopharyngeal colonisation with the four GM-Nlac strains, and to assess immunogenicity. Any individual who regularly shares a bedroom with a challenge participant will be enrolled as a "contact participant" and will be reviewed at day 28 post challenge of the challenge participant to assess transmission of the GM-Nlac strains. All challenge participants, and any colonised contact participants, will then receive a single dose of oral ciprofloxacin to clear GM-Nlac colonisation, with a final visit to confirm clearance. All visits will be conducted in the NIHR Clinical Research Facility located within University Hospital Southampton (see Figure 1 for study schedule).

This study aims to establish the safety, colonisation potential and immunogenicity of nasal inoculation with 4xrNlac, to assess for transmission to close contacts, and to demonstrate the efficacy of a single dose of oral ciprofloxacin in clearing nasopharyngeal colonisation. Safety and infection control considerations

Although pre-clinical testing has demonstrated no potential for increased virulence or transmissibility of the GM-Nlac strains, strict safety and infection control measures will be adhered to throughout the study to ensure the safety of participants and to minimise the potential for onward transmission to study team members, other contacts of participants, and the wider community. Eligibility criteria for both challenge and contact participants will exclude individuals who are at increased risk of disease, and those who have close contact with any individuals who are vulnerable or have higher transmission potential (see Supplementary Tables 1 and 2 for full eligibility criteria). Bedroom-sharers of challenge participants, who are at the highest risk of onward transmission, will be invited to participate as contact participants. A maximum of one contact participant will be enrolled per challenge participant, and if a bedroom-sharer is unwilling or ineligible, the challenge participant will not be eligible to participate. As 4xrNlac has not previously been administered to human participants, enrolment will follow a first-in-human process with sentinel participants and a safety review prior to further challenges. Challenge participants will be monitored closely for safety concerns during the first seven days following challenge with a day 1 telephone contact, daily diary card for seven days, and face-to-face visits on days 4 and 7 post challenge. This period corresponds to the period of highest risk of meningococcal disease following Nmen colonisation. Throughout the study, challenge and contact participants will be encouraged to contact the study team via a 24-hour telephone number if they become unwell. In the event of any participant developing symptoms suggestive of respiratory or systemic infection, additional visits and investigations will be conducted at the discretion of the investigators, and early antibiotic clearance (single-dose) or treatment course administered if the study team suspect it to be related to GM-Nlac colonisation. In the event of systemic illness suggestive of GM-Nlac bacteraemia or sepsis, the participant will be referred to the acute medical services in University Hospital Southampton.

The study will be paused for a safety review in the event of any participant developing a respiratory or systemic infection assessed as related to GM-Nlac and requiring a treatment course of antibiotics. Additionally, if early antibiotic clearance therapy (single dose) is given due to safety concerns to two of the first five participants, or more than three participants overall, a study pause will be triggered. An independent Data and Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB) will oversee study conduct and review all safety data. Hampshire and Isle of Wight Health Protection Team South-East will be informed when the study commences. Study participants' GPs will be notified about their inclusion in the study, and any occurrence of disease in participants will be notified to the DSMB.

The GM-Nlac inocula and all clinical samples potentially containing any GM-Nlac bacteria will be handled within Class II microbiological safety cabinets, and clinical procedures including inoculation will be performed within an environmental chamber. Study personnel will be trained in handling GM-contaminated materials and will wear appropriate personal protective equipment. Any potentially GM-contaminated materials will be disposed of according to study-specific operating procedures.

Challenge with 4xrNlac

Challenge participants will attend for a pre-challenge visit five to 10 days prior to GM-Nlac challenge to ensure that they remain eligible. A throat swab will be taken to assess for acquisition of Nlac or Nmen colonisation since the screening visit, for which they would be deemed ineligible. Additional respiratory samples will also be taken to assess baseline mucosal immunological parameters. On the day of challenge, understanding, consent and eligibility will be re-confirmed and blood samples will be taken for baseline systemic immunological parameters. Once eligibility for challenge has been confirmed, a dose of inoculum will be prepared from a single inoculum stock vial. The vial will be thawed and diluted in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) with dilution steps calculated using the known batch viability of the stock vials, aiming for an inoculum viability of 4 x 10 5 CFU suspended in 1ml PBS. The inoculum will be transferred immediately to the participant and administered by a study doctor within 30 minutes of preparation, as previously described [34, 46]. Briefly, the participant will lie supine with their neck extended and 0.5 mL inoculum will be administered per nostril via automated pipette. Following challenge, the participant will be asked not to swallow for one minute and then remain supine for five minutes. Inoculum purity will be confirmed by culture of the inoculum prior to inoculation, and the administered inoculum dose calculated by viable count of the inoculum immediately prior to, and following inoculation. Some variation in administered inoculum dose is expected, but if an administered dose is found to be less than 4 x 10 4 CFU/mL then this will be considered an inadequate inoculum and the participant may be replaced. If an inoculum contains >5 times the intended dose (i.e. 2 x 10 6 CFU), it will be reported as an adverse event. Any inoculum >10 times the intended dose (i.e. 4 x 10 6 CFU) will be classed as an adverse event of special interest and will trigger reassessment of inoculum preparation procedures and batch viability.

Assessment of study endpoints

Study endpoints are detailed in Table 2 and include safety, microbiological and immunological parameters. Challenge participant safety endpoints will be assessed by collection of adverse event data throughout the study. Solicited symptoms suggestive of possible respiratory or systemic infection (Table 4), any unsolicited adverse events and concomitant medication use will be recorded at the Day 1 telephone contact, via a paper diary card daily for seven days following challenge, and at each participant visit. Vital signs will be monitored at each participant visit and blood samples will be taken for assessment of haematological and biochemical parameters at each challenge participant visit to Day 28 post challenge. Adverse event severity, seriousness and relatedness to study interventions and/or colonisation with the GM-Nlac strains will be reported. Contact participant adverse event data will be collected at the Day 28 visit and any additional visits if required.

Nasopharyngeal colonisation of challenge participants will be assessed by culture of throat swabs and nasal wash samples collected at all post-challenge visits, while transmission to contact participants will be assessed with throat swabs at day 28 and any additional visits if required. Positive colonisation or transmission will be defined as the detection of one or more colonies of any challenge strain in samples taken up to and including the day 28 visit. The composition of colonising GM-Nlac strain(s) will be determined by bespoke qPCR assay performed directly on participant throat swabs.

Baseline systemic immunological parameters will be assessed in serum and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) extracted from baseline blood samples taken immediately prior to challenge, with comparison to samples taken at Days 14 (PBMCs only) and 28 post challenge (PBMCs and serum). Comparison will be made between those participants who are colonised, and those who are not colonised with the GM-Nlac strains. For challenge participants only, mucosal immunological parameters will be assessed using: (i) nasosorption strips (for collection of nasal mucosal fluid), (ii) saliva samples, and (iii) inferior turbinate swabs, comparing baseline with post-challenge timepoints. Existing immunological assays will be optimised to detect responses against heterologous antigens throughout this study, for later use in the planned GM-Nlac randomised placebo-controlled study.

Antibiotic clearance of GM-Nlac colonisation

A single 500mg oral dose of ciprofloxacin will be used to clear nasopharyngeal colonisation with GM-Nlac strains. This will be administered to all challenge participants under direct supervision at the Day 28 visit. Contact participants will be provided with a dose of ciprofloxacin to take home from the day 28 visit, and once their colonisation status is known, they will be instructed to either take or dispose of the antibiotic. Participants with any contraindications to ciprofloxacin will be excluded at screening, and all female participants of childbearing age will perform a pregnancy test prior to receiving the antibiotic.

Any participants who withdraw prior to the day 28 visit, or in the event of safety concerns, may receive early antibiotic clearance.

All study participants who have taken antibiotic therapy to clear colonisation with GM-Nlac will have a further visit 1-4 days after antibiotic administration to confirm clearance of colonisation. If a participant shows microbiological evidence of persistent colonisation despite receiving the antibiotic, they will be invited for a further visit for consideration of additional treatment by the study steering committee, with consultation with DSMB members where necessary.

Withdrawal of participants

In accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, a participant can withdraw from the study at any time, and is not obliged to give a reason for this. Similarly, a participant may be withdrawn by the study team if there are concerns surrounding their health, or if they do not adhere to study requirements. In the event of a challenge participant withdrawing from the study, their corresponding contact participant will also be withdrawn.

Statistical considerations

Ten challenge participants is considered to be sufficient to estimate colonisation fraction and provide initial safety data. Therefore, no formal sample size calculation has been performed. The pilot study will also enable the optimisation and validation of immunological and microbiological assays for use in subsequent studies. Safety endpoints will be assessed using data from all inoculated challenge participants and enrolled contacts, including both solicited and unsolicited adverse events, with comparisons between colonised and non-colonised participants. Microbiological and immunological endpoints will be evaluated in challenge participants deemed evaluable. Transmission analyses will focus on contact participants linked to colonised challenge participants, using their microbiological data.

Discussion

IMD remains a significant global health concern despite current vaccination strategies, with transmission underpinned by asymptomatic pharyngeal colonisation. While conjugate meningococcal vaccines reduce asymptomatic colonisation with subsequent induction of potent herd protection through interruption of transmission, currently licenced serogroup B vaccines do not impact upon this crucial endpoint [10]. Novel strategies are required to reduce asymptomatic Nmen colonisation to prevent asymptomatic transmission and associated IMD burden, particularly for serogroup B strains. Mucosal vaccination strategies, including those employing live-attenuated organisms, have shown promise in eliciting sterilising mucosal immunity for other upper respiratory tract bacterial pathogens, providing proof of principal that similar approaches may help overcome this limitation of non-conjugate meningococcal vaccines [26, 47, 48].

This study builds on over 15 years' work where we have demonstrated that colonisation with wild type Nlac is safe and immunogenic [20, 22, 23, [31] [32] [33] [34]. Although colonisation with wild type Nlac protects against Nmen acquisition events throughout the active colonisation period, any immune-mediated protection conferred by natural Nlac colonisation is anticipated to be limited, as induction of Nmen-cross-reactive adaptive immune responses is not universally observed among Nlac-colonised participants [20, 22, 23]. Genetic modification offers a potential solution to this problem and, in the case of GM-Nlac, will enable sustained heterologous antigen delivery to the nasopharyngeal mucosa, with subsequent induction of resident adaptive immune responses. In support of this claim, in our first GM-Nlac CHIM, we demonstrated that colonisation with a NadA-expressing strain of Nlac (4NB1) was safe and immunogenic, eliciting anti-meningococcal SBA activity in ~50% of participants who were serologically susceptible to IMD caused by Nmen strain 5/99 at baseline, but who became serologically protected by IMD caused by this strain after becoming colonised by 4NB1 [34]. Here, we expand upon this earlier work and plan to determine whether inoculation with 4xrNlac, a multi-strain GM-Nlac inoculum comprising four stains expressing NadA and one of 4 FHbp variants representative of UK-circulating meningococcal strains, results in safe colonisation and enhances anti-meningococcal immune responses. If safe colonisation is confirmed, a subsequent randomised, placebo-controlled study will be performed within the same funding envelope where we will establish whether colonisation with 4xrNlac induces sterilising protection against homologous rechallenge, and outline associated immune correlates of protection, including generation of resident memory adaptive immune responses. If positive, such a study would pave the way for future investigation to determine whether 4xrNlac might induce heterologous protection against Nmen.
