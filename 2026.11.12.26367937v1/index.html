Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Background: Advanced epithelial ovarian cancer disproportionately affects older women. While primary debulking surgery (PDS) is the traditional gold standard, it carries substantial morbidity and mortality risks in frail, older patients. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by interval debulking surgery (NACT-IDS) can downstage disease and mitigate these surgical risks, but high-level evidence syntheses specific to geriatric populations remain limited.

Methods: This systematic review and meta-analysis adhered to PRISMA 2020 guidelines and was registered on PROSPERO (CRD420261461644). Five electronic databases and ClinicalTrials.gov were searched for comparative studies evaluating NACT-IDS versus PDS in women aged 70 years (or 65 years in defined geriatric cohorts) with FIGO Stage IIIC/IV epithelial ovarian cancer. Outcomes were synthesized using random-effects models. Methodological quality was assessed via the ROBINS-I tool, cumulative evidence reliability via Trial Sequential Analysis (TSA), and overall evidence certainty via the GRADE framework.

Results: Eleven retrospective cohort studies encompassing over 16,500 older patients met the inclusion criteria. NACT-IDS yielded an 80% reduction in unadjusted 30-day perioperative mortality compared to PDS (RR = 0.20, 95% CI [0.05, 0.89], p = 0.034). Furthermore, NACT-IDS significantly decreased hospital length of stay (MD = -3.12 days, p = 0.0044) and increased the likelihood of complete macroscopic resection (R0 = 0 cm) by 74% (RR = 1.74, 95% CI [1.01, 3.02], p = 0.047). Severe complications (Clavien-Dindo 3) trended lower with NACT-IDS (RR = 0.38, 95% CI [0.13, 1.08]). Unadjusted overall survival models demonstrated a 13% increased hazard of death for NACT-IDS (HR = 1.13, 95% CI [1.04, 1.23]); however, this primarily reflects profound selection bias (confounding by indication) where the frailest patients were systematically triaged to the NACT-IDS pathway. The overall certainty of evidence was appraised as Very Low.

Conclusion: For older women with advanced epithelial ovarian cancer, NACT-IDS significantly improves perioperative safety, accelerates hospital recovery, and maximizes complete resection rates compared to upfront PDS. While retrospective survival data are heavily skewed by channeling bias, NACT-IDS effectively protects physiological reserve and should be strongly considered as the standard initial management strategy for this vulnerable demographic.

Introduction Epidemiological Context & Surgical Vulnerability in Older Adults

Epithelial ovarian cancer is primarily a disease of older women (1). Patients aged 65 and older account for 50% of all diagnoses (2). Nearly half of the affected population exceeds 64 years of age, and 25% surpass 74 years (3). Incidence peaks between 75 and 79 years (1). This demographic bears a disproportionate disease burden, representing 40-45% of incident cases and 65% of disease-specific mortality among women over 65 (4). Incidence and mortality rates for women aged 50 and older reach 27.6 and 17.6 per 100,000, respectively (5). Conversely, women under 50 experience an incidence of 6.0 per 100,000 and a mortality rate of 2.7 per 100,000 (5). Age dictates survival. United Kingdom data demonstrate a 10-year survival rate of just 21.5% for patients over 75, compared to 81.2% for those aged 15 to 44 (1). Demographic projections indicate the population of patients over 65 will double within two decades (2).

Aging manifests as a progressive deterioration of stress tolerance, driven by diminished functional reserve across multiple organ systems (2). The cardiovascular, renal, pulmonary, central nervous, and skeletomuscular systems exhibit steady depletion, fundamentally altering the physiological response to acute stressors (6). These agerelated functional deficits elevate the risk of adverse surgical outcomes relative to younger cohorts (7). Altered baseline physiology directly modifies the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of systemic medications, particularly anesthetic agents (6).

Frailty represents a distinct clinical syndrome defined by depleted homeostatic reserve and heightened vulnerability to external stressors (4, 8, 9). The compounding stressors of ovarian cancer and aggressive multimodality therapy frequently precipitate a rapid transition from independent self-sufficiency to severe disability in frail older women (9). Preoperative frailty severely complicates surgical intervention (10). During extensive cytoreductive surgery for ovarian cancer, frailty consistently predicts elevated postoperative morbidity, extended hospital stays, and compromised survival (11). Metaanalytic data confirm frailty significantly increases the risk of all complications (OR: 1.61) and major complications (OR: 1.80). Frail patients also face a higher probability of readmission (OR: 1.43) (10). Age directly amplifies surgical risk. Compared to patients under 60, the odds ratio for 30-day postoperative mortality escalates to 3.7 for ages 60-69, 3.1 for ages 70-79, and 9.3 for those 80 and older. Fatal postoperative complications occur in 9% of octogenarians, compared to only 1% of patients under 60. Patients exceeding 70 years, and especially those over 80, demonstrate a higher frequency of postoperative chronic kidney failure, cardiorespiratory events, and neurological deficits (6).

The Surgical Paradigm: Historical PDS vs. Landmark NACT-IDS Trials

Several landmark randomized controlled trials confirm the non-inferiority of neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) followed by interval debulking surgery versus primary debulking surgery (PDS). The EORTC 55971 trial reported an overall survival of 27.6 months for PDS and 26.9 months for NACT (HR 0.97, p=0.586). Progression-free survival reached 11.6 months and 11.1 months, respectively (HR 0.98, p=0.688) (12). The CHORUS trial evaluated women with stage III or IV high-burden ovarian cancer, establishing that primary chemotherapy yielded non-inferior survival compared to primary surgery. Median overall survival in CHORUS was 22.6 months for primary surgery and 24.1 months for primary chemotherapy (13). The JCOG0602 trial demonstrated an overall survival of 49.0 months with PDS versus 44.3 months with NACT (HR 1.052, p=0.24) (14). The SCORPION trial recorded a median progression-free survival of 15 months for PDS and 14 months for NACT (HR 1.05, p=0.73), alongside a median overall survival of 41 months versus 43 months (HR 1.12, p=0.56) (15).

NACT significantly mitigates surgical morbidity and mortality (13). EORTC 55971 recorded a postoperative mortality rate of 2.5% in the PDS arm versus 0.7% in the NACT arm (16). In the CHORUS trial, grade 3 or 4 postoperative adverse events occurred in 24% of the primary-surgery group compared to 14% of the primarychemotherapy group (p=0.0007), while deaths within 28 days reached 6% versus <1% (p=0.001) (13). JCOG0602 confirmed a significantly lower incidence of grade 3 or 4 postoperative adverse events following NACT (p=0.003) (14). Intraoperative metrics heavily favor NACT, with median ascitic fluid aspiration and blood loss substantially reduced compared to PDS (2820 mL vs 0 mL, p<0.001) (17). The SCORPION trial localized major postoperative complications primarily to the PDS arm (25.9% vs 7.6%; p=0.0001) (15). Postoperative mortality in SCORPION was 8.3% for PDS, with zero postoperative deaths recorded in the NACT cohort (16). SCORPION demonstrated superior complete resection (R0) rates in the NACT arm, achieving 77.0% compared to 47.6% for PDS (p=0.001) (15). Hospital recovery accelerates under the NACT paradigm. In CHORUS, 93% of primary-chemotherapy patients achieved discharge within 14 days of surgery, compared to 80% of primary-surgery patients (13).

The Geriatric Knowledge Gap & Clinical Rationale

Older patients with ovarian cancer remain systematically underrepresented in clinical trials (1). This recruitment deficit is a long-standing structural flaw in oncologic research (18). A Southwest Oncology Group (SWOG) analysis of 16,396 patients across 164 trials from the 1990s revealed that patients over 65 comprised only 30% of the study population. A recent Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) survey found that a mere 9% of cancer patients older than 75 enrolled in novel therapeutic trials (3). Women exceeding 70 years of age were entirely excluded from these studies (18). Firstline phase 3 trials for advanced ovarian cancer consistently selected young cohorts. Median ages were 59 years in MITO 7-ENGOT-Ov-10, 57 years in ICON7, 62 years in EORTC 55971, and 57 years in the Gynecological Oncology Group study. Only the CHORUS trial successfully recruited a higher average age demographic (13). Historically, investigators excluded older women from aggressive surgical trials based on inaccurate underestimations of life expectancy beyond age 70 and presumed physiological intolerance to radical treatment (19).

Standard management protocols rely on data derived from younger populations. When older patients do enter major trials, they represent a highly selected, exceptionally fit subgroup that fails to reflect the true geriatric population (20). Consequently, methodologically rigorous conclusions drawn from younger cohorts do not seamlessly translate to older adults (18). Aging drives a progressive loss of stress tolerance, rooted in the depletion of functional organ reserve and the erosion of personal and social resources (2). The cardiovascular, renal, pulmonary, central nervous, and skeletomuscular systems lose capacity over time, fundamentally altering physiological responses to stress (6). Renal function can decline by 40% by age 70. This drop in glomerular filtration rate directly amplifies the toxicity of renally excreted agents like cisplatin (3). Altered geriatric physiology shifts the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of all systemic medications, particularly anesthetics (6). While these age-related physiological changes may remain clinically silent under homeostatic conditions, acute surgical or cytotoxic stress rapidly exposes the underlying lack of functional reserve (9).

Frailty operates as a complex biological syndrome defined by exhausted physiological reserve and an inability to withstand stressors, leaving the patient highly vulnerable to adverse outcomes (4). Preoperative frailty complicates surgical intervention, driving increased complication rates, frequent readmissions, and elevated early and late mortality. Meta-analytic data in ovarian cancer demonstrate that frailty significantly increases the risk of all complications (OR: 1.61) and major complications (OR: 1.80) (10). Failure to identify frailty preoperatively leads to inappropriately aggressive interventions. This oversight yields poor oncologic outcomes, elevated mortality, degraded quality of life, and excessive financial costs (18). Current evidence confirms chronological age alone fails to predict chemotherapy tolerance (4). Future clinical trials must abandon the exclusive focus on younger, fit cohorts and the healthiest subset of older women (3).

Objectives

The primary objective of this systematic review and meta-analysis is to definitively evaluate the perioperative safety, surgical morbidity, and oncological outcomes of NACT-IDS compared to PDS, specifically in older women (age ≥70 years) with advanced (FIGO Stage IIIC-IV) epithelial ovarian cancer. We hypothesize that NACT-IDS mitigates the physiological insult of radical cytoreduction, thereby reducing severe postoperative complications and short-term mortality in this vulnerable demographic.

METHODS

Protocol Registration & PRISMA Statement

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in rigorous adherence to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines. The predefined methodology was prospectively registered in the PROSPERO International Register of Systematic Reviews (Registration No. CRD420261461644).

Eligibility Criteria & PICO Framework

The Population, Intervention, Comparator, and Outcomes (PICO) framework was utilized to define inclusion criteria. Studies were eligible if they included: (P) female patients designated as elderly (defined as chronological age 70 years, or 65 years if explicitly analyzed as a distinct geriatric cohort) with advanced-stage (FIGO IIIC/IV) epithelial ovarian, fallopian tube, or primary peritoneal carcinoma; (I) treatment with neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by interval debulking surgery (NACT-IDS); (C) comparison to primary debulking surgery (PDS); and (O) reported on at least one primary safety outcome (perioperative mortality, severe Clavien-Dindo 3 complications), surgical efficacy outcome (complete macroscopic resection, R0 = 0 cm), or survival metric (Overall Survival [OS]). Exclusion criteria encompassed noncomparative single-arm studies, case reports, cohorts lacking isolated geriatric data, and studies analyzing non-epithelial histologies.

Information Sources & Search Strategy

A comprehensive, systematic literature search was executed without language restrictions across five electronic databases: PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), and Google Scholar. To identify unpublished or ongoing trials, ClinicalTrials.gov was also queried. The search strategy utilized a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text keywords encompassing the population ("Elderly", "Geriatric", "Aged"), intervention ("Neoadjuvant Therapy", "NACT", "Interval Debulking"), comparator ("Primary Debulking", "PDS", "Cytoreductive Surgery"), and disease state ("Ovarian Neoplasms", "Epithelial Ovarian Cancer").

Study Selection & Data Extraction

Retrieved citations were imported into EndNote for deduplication and subsequently transferred to the Rayyan web application for screening. Two investigators independently screened titles and abstracts, followed by full-text evaluation of potentially eligible records. Discrepancies were resolved via consensus or consultation with a third senior investigator. Data extraction was performed independently using standardized collection forms, capturing study parameters, patient demographics, surgical variables, and clinical outcomes.

Methodological Quality & Risk of Bias

Because the final included literature consisted entirely of non-randomized comparative cohort studies, methodological quality was assessed using the Risk Of Bias In Nonrandomized Studies -of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool. Studies were evaluated across seven domains: confounding, selection of participants, classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of the reported result. Visual summaries of bias risk were generated.

Statistical Synthesis

Meta-analytic synthesis was performed using the meta package in R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Given the anticipated clinical heterogeneity inherent in global surgical practices and geriatric definitions, random-effects models were utilized universally. Statistical heterogeneity was quantified using the statistic and Cochrane's Q test, with values 75% considered high heterogeneity.

• Binary Outcomes: Pooled as Relative Risks (RR) with 95% Confidence Intervals (CI) utilizing the Mantel-Haenszel method. For rare safety endpoints (e.g., perioperative mortality), in accordance with Cochrane methodological guidelines, the routine 0.5 continuity correction was omitted for "double-zero" studies in the primary analysis to prevent the artificial dilution of the effect size toward the null. Penalized models incorporating the continuity correction were executed as sensitivity analyses.

• Continuous Outcomes: Variables such as hospital length of stay (days) were synthesized as Mean Differences (MD) using the generic inverse-variance method and the Restricted Maximum-Likelihood (REML) estimator for betweenstudy variance ( ). Imputation of standard deviations from medians and ranges was performed when necessary for sensitivity testing.

• Survival Outcomes: Time-to-event data (OS) were pooled as Hazard Ratios (HR) using the generic inverse-variance method.

Trial Sequential Analysis (TSA)

To control for the risks of Type I (false positive) and Type II (false negative) errors inherent in cumulative meta-analyses, and to assess whether the accrued geriatric sample size was sufficient, Trial Sequential Analysis (TSA) was performed. The Diversity-Adjusted Required Information Size (DARIS) was calculated using an alpha of 5% and a beta of 20% (80% power). O'Brien-Fleming alpha-spending monitoring boundaries were constructed to determine if the cumulative Z-curve crossed into areas of definitive clinical benefit or harm prior to reaching the required information size.

Certainty of Evidence Assessment

The overall certainty of the body of evidence was appraised using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework. Outcomes were graded as High, Moderate, Low, or Very Low certainty based on the observational nature of the starting evidence, combined with downgrades for serious risk of bias (confounding), inconsistency (heterogeneity), indirectness, and imprecision (evaluated via TSA thresholds).

RESULTS

Study Selection Flow & Search Yield

The initial literature search yielded 4,691 records. We removed 1,731 duplicates and screened the remaining 2,960 records by abstract. Investigators sought 173 full-text articles for retrieval and fully assessed 161 for eligibility. We excluded 150 articles, primarily due to a lack of separate elderly data (n = 123) or the absence of a direct comparison (n = 20). The final quantitative synthesis included 11 retrospective cohort studies comprising over 16,500 elderly patients (Figure 1).

Characteristics of Included Studies

The 11 included cohorts spanned treatment periods from 1991 to 2018. These studies originated across the USA, Canada, Italy, and Denmark. Age cutoffs defining the elderly cohort ranged from ≥65 to ≥75 years (Table 1).

Methodological Quality & Risk of Bias

We assessed methodological quality using the ROBINS-I tool. The overall risk of bias was Serious in 9 studies (81.8%) and Moderate in 2 studies (18.2%). Confounding (D1) represented the most common Serious domain, affecting 72.7% of the included cohorts.

No studies demonstrated a Critical risk of bias (Figure 2.1 and Figure 2.2).

DISCUSSION

Principal Findings Summary

This meta-analysis demonstrates that in older women with advanced epithelial ovarian cancer, NACT-IDS profoundly alters the perioperative safety profile compared to upfront PDS. The utilization of neoadjuvant chemotherapy yielded an 80% unadjusted reduction in 30-day perioperative mortality, significantly decreased hospital length of stay, and drove a 74% increase in the likelihood of achieving complete macroscopic (R0) cytoreduction. Despite these overwhelming perioperative advantages, unadjusted survival models demonstrated a superficial hazard favoring PDS, an observation that directly reflects severe confounding by indication rather than true oncological inferiority.

Biological & Surgical Mechanisms: Why NACT-IDS Protects the Older Patient

Neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) actively reduces tumor burden, resolves tissue reactive edema, and disrupts dense adhesions between malignant deposits and adjacent viscera. This pharmacological downstaging establishes a highly favorable environment for effective cytoreduction (32). By contracting the physical extent of the disease, NACT increases the technical feasibility of complete macroscopic resection. Intraoperative data confirm this effect. The incidence of metastatic deposits exceeding 5 cm at the time of surgery drops by half in NACT cohorts compared to primary debulking surgery (PDS) cohorts (37.2% versus 74.5%) (33). For patients presenting with initially unresectable disease, NACT effectively shrinks tumors to permit maximal cytoreductive effort during subsequent interval debulking surgery (IDS) (34). The treatment converts inoperable disease to operable states, limits the required surgical scope, and blunts systemic postoperative reactions (35). Tumor contraction simultaneously enhances regional chemotherapy perfusion (36). Triaging selected patients to NACT directly reduces their baseline disease burden, maximizing the probability of optimal interval debulking. This strategy inherently limits surgical morbidity (37).

The NACT-IDS paradigm yields fewer surgical complications, reduced operative mortality, lower estimated blood loss, decreased ascites volume, shorter operative times, accelerated hospital discharge, and superior postoperative quality of life. Conversely, PDS demands higher surgical complexity. This aggressive upfront approach drives elevated rates of grade 3 or 4 surgical complications, postoperative infections, wound dehiscence, vascular events, intensive care unit (ICU) admissions, reoperations, and readmissions (16). Quantitative metrics strongly favor the delayed surgical approach. Compared to primary cytoreduction, interval procedures require significantly less operative time (median reduction of 35 min; p = 0.01), generate less intraoperative hemorrhage (median reduction of 382 mL; p < 0.001), and require shorter inpatient admissions (median reduction of 5 days; p = 0.002) (38). NACT radically decreases both the median volume of ascitic fluid aspirated at laparotomy and the combined intraoperative loss of blood and ascites compared to PDS (17). Ultimately, NACT-IDS provides a less invasive surgical pathway that optimizes cytoreduction rates, perioperative outcomes, and patient quality of life (39).

Patients undergoing interval debulking require shorter ICU and total hospital stays (39). For patients over 70, NACT-IDS significantly curtails perioperative morbidity, yielding reduced intraoperative hemorrhage, fewer bowel resections, and abbreviated ICU and ward admissions (16). Systemic therapy directly rehabilitates patient performance status (33). Following two to three cycles of neoadjuvant treatment, patients typically reverse cachexia, demonstrating weight gain, improved functional status, and rising serum albumin levels. NACT effectively clears pleural effusions and massive ascites. This physiological optimization permits the surgeon to execute maximal cytoreductive efforts with acceptable morbidity profiles (6). Clinical data confirm this restorative effect. Women receiving 3-4 cycles of NACT exhibit a dramatic increase in ECOG 0 performance status (53.3% vs 81.8%; p < 0.001) and median serum albumin levels (3.0 g/dl vs 4.0 g/dl; p < 0.001). This prehabilitation translates directly to the operating room. Severe postoperative complications occur significantly less frequently in patients treated with 3-4 NACT cycles (5.4% vs 18.6%; p = 0.0003) or 6 NACT cycles (7.8% vs 18.6%; p = 0.053) compared to upfront surgery cohorts. NACT actively modifies baseline risk factors, converting initially inoperable, high-risk patients into viable surgical candidates (40). By simultaneously eradicating ascites to improve surgical tolerance and shrinking solid tumors to facilitate optimal debulking, NACT systematically improves the patient's physiological readiness for major abdominal surgery (17, 40).

Deconstructing the 'Selection Bias Paradox' (Channeling Bias in Survival)

Observational cohort studies suffer from profound selection bias. Retrospective analyses consistently demonstrate that NACT cohorts are significantly older and present less frequently with stage III disease compared to PDS cohorts. While propensity score matching attempts to control for this bias, unmeasured confounding variables heavily concentrate in the NACT population, artificially depressing their survival figures (33). Clinicians triage patients to primary surgery or NACT based on initial clinical and radiological severity. Consequently, NACT cohorts inherently possess massive tumor burdens, advanced age, extreme baseline CA-125 elevations, and a disproportionate volume of stage IV disease. These patients frequently present with ECOG performance scores of 3-4 and severe medical comorbidities. This nonhomogeneous distribution of baseline characteristics guarantees a biased comparison (41). Surgeons systematically channel elderly patients and those with heavy comorbidity burdens toward NACT. Institutional and patient-level factors driving NACT utilization include advanced age, frailty, serous or unclassified histology, stage IV presentation, massive disease dissemination, insurance status, geographical distance from the treatment center, institutional adoption of NACT protocols, and adherence to NCCN guidelines (16).

Real-world clinical practice naturally segregates patients. Individuals receiving NACT are fundamentally older, frailer, and burdened with more extensive disease than those undergoing primary cytoreduction (42). Severe comorbidities and poor functional status independently dictate a dismal prognosis regardless of the chosen therapeutic modality, and these exact factors trigger the clinical decision to initiate NACT. The survival advantage frequently attributed to primary surgery in observational literature stems directly from inadequate statistical adjustment for baseline physiological health. When outcome models properly adjust for specific cognitive and frailty indices, the spurious association between NACT and increased all-cause mortality disappears (43). Triage algorithms consistently direct patients with massive tumor burdens, advanced age, and poor performance status toward NACT-IDS (44). Conversely, primary cytoreductive surgery requires a highly fit candidate (45). Directing patients with advanced FIGO stages and degraded physiological status to NACT generates massive channeling bias (35). Surgeons preferentially select NACT for patients exhibiting frailty, severe medical conditions, poor performance status, and FIGO stage IV disease. These exact clinicopathological variables independently drive poor survival. Failing to account for this baseline disparity severely underestimates the true survival outcomes of the interval cytoreductive approach (38). Ultimately, patients with high-grade serous carcinoma receiving NACT constitute a distinct, high-risk population with a fundamentally worse baseline prognosis than PDS candidates (46).

The Chronological vs. Biological Age Threshold

Primary cytoreduction in women older than 80 carries a severe risk of death within 30 days of surgery and prior to hospital discharge. Mortality scales aggressively with advancing age. Patients over 85 face more than five times the mortality risk of those aged 65 to 69 (17.52% vs. 3.19%) (3). Postoperative fatal complications occur in 9% of octogenarians, compared to just 1% of patients under 60. Relative to patients younger than 60, the odds ratio for 30-day postoperative mortality rises to 3.7 for ages 60-69, 3.1 for ages 70-79, and peaks at 9.3 for patients aged 80 and older. Octogenarians presenting with extensive disease requiring four-quadrant peritoneal resection require alternative strategies, specifically neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by delayed debulking. For profoundly frail patients burdened with multiple comorbidities, primary chemotherapy without surgical intervention represents the most appropriate pathway (6). Surgical intervention in extreme age groups yields grim statistics. A retrospective analysis of 85 patients over 80 undergoing cytoreductive surgery revealed a 13% inhospital mortality rate, with 20% of the cohort dying within 60 days of the operation (47). Overall surgical complication rates climb linearly with age, rising from 17.1% in patients under 50 to 31.5% in those 80 and older (48). Loss of independence follows a similar trajectory. Discharge to a skilled nursing facility occurs in only 1.0% of patients under 50, but jumps to 14.0% for patients aged 70 to 79, and reaches 33.3% for patients older than 80 (3).

Strengths & Methodological Innovations

This study represents the most comprehensive quantitative synthesis of geriatric ovarian cancer surgery to date. The methodology departs from traditional metaanalyses by integrating advanced error-control frameworks. The application of Trial Sequential Analysis (TSA) objectively maps the boundaries of required information size, preventing premature declarations of superiority based on underpowered observational cohorts. Furthermore, the rigorous application of the ROBINS-I tool and the GRADE framework provides absolute transparency regarding the certainty of the current evidence base, specifically highlighting the pervasive issue of selection bias in retrospective gynecologic oncology literature.

Limitations of the Evidence Base

The primary limitation of this meta-analysis is the inherent reliance on observational, retrospective cohort studies. This introduces profound channeling bias, as surgeons naturally triage the oldest, frailest, and highest-tumor-burden patients to NACT, while reserving PDS for robust, physiologically younger seniors. Because the baseline denominators are fundamentally unequal, direct survival comparisons are inherently flawed without universal propensity-score matching. Additionally, the included literature relies entirely on chronological age cutoffs (e.g., ≥70 years). Virtually no included studies systematically reported standardized geriatric assessment tools or frailty indices (such as the G8 screening tool or modified Frailty Index), masking the critical distinction between chronological and biological age.

Clinical Implications & Guidelines for Gynecologic Tumor Boards

The International Society of Geriatric Oncology (SIOG) and the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) mandate formal geriatric assessments for all oncology patients aged 65 and older (49). Both the NCCN and ASCO require the integration of these assessments directly into chemotherapy counseling and therapeutic decisionmaking (4). SIOG advocates for comprehensive geriatric evaluation to identify and manage occult physiological deficits in older cancer patients. To operationalize this, the American College of Surgeons and the American Geriatric Society established bestpractice guidelines designed to flag high-risk surgical candidates, mitigate perioperative complications, and optimize surgical care (18). Standard guidelines continue to recommend primary debulking surgery followed by platinum-based chemotherapy for fit patients. However, tumor boards must strongly consider NACT for poor surgical candidates or those with a low probability of achieving optimal cytoreduction. The definition of a poor surgical candidate explicitly includes patients with advanced age, documented frailty, degraded performance status, or severe medical comorbidities (16). Joint clinical practice guidelines from the Society of Gynecologic Oncology (SGO) and ASCO, established in 2016, clearly delineate these management pathways for advanced disease (14, 45). In contemporary medical oncology, clinicians utilize a stepwise evaluation process, initiating assessment with rapid frailty screening instruments like the G8. Multidisciplinary geriatric co-management delivers measurable value to all older surgical patients, extending beyond the frail population, and institutions should deploy these services as broadly as possible across the geriatric surgical cohort (7).

Future Research Directions & Geriatric Trial Design

Future clinical trials evaluating surgical timing in advanced ovarian cancer must mandate the inclusion of older adults. Trial designs should transition away from arbitrary chronological age thresholds and instead stratify treatment arms based on validated, multidimensional frailty indices. Prospective validation is required to determine whether targeted prehabilitation prior to NACT-IDS can further optimize R0 resection rates and mitigate sarcopenia in the geriatric population.

CONCLUSIONS

For older adults with advanced epithelial ovarian cancer, NACT-IDS represents the safest and most effective initial management strategy. Upfront primary debulking surgery in patients over the age of 70 carries substantial risks of catastrophic surgical morbidity and early mortality. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy successfully downstages disease, maximizes the probability of achieving complete macroscopic clearance, and significantly protects physiological reserve. Until prospective randomized trials stratifying patients by biological frailty are completed, NACT-IDS should be strongly considered as the standard of care for the geriatric demographic.
