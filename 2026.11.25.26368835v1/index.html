What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Identifying reliable neural biomarkers of chronic tinnitus remains challenging because ageing and hearing loss independently influence auditory cortical activity. Here, we investigated auditory expectation processing in 440 adults with (n = 226) and without (n = 214) chronic tinnitus using electroencephalography during a local-global auditory paradigm across six centres. Although several electrophysiological group differences emerged in unadjusted analyses, most were substantially attenuated after adjustment for demographic and audiological factors. Two clusters remained significant within the unexpected-omission-versus-sound contrast (44-100 ms and 356-476 ms), but neither survived correction across eight contrasts.

Direct comparisons between expected and unexpected omissions revealed no significant group differences. Exploratory classification using selected EEG features achieved a cross-validated area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.624, increasing to 0.689 when combined with demographic and audiological predictors. However, this improvement was not reproduced in held-out validation, and EEG features showed limited predictive value for tinnitus burden or loudness. These findings highlight a fundamental distinction between electrophysiological group differences and clinically informative individual-level biomarkers, underscoring the importance of controlling for confounding factors and establishing independent predictive validity in tinnitus research.

Introduction

Tinnitus affects approximately 10-15% of adults [1] [2] [3] [4] [5] . In its chronic subjective form, it involves the persistent perception of sound without a corresponding external source. Despite its prevalence and clinical burden, its neural basis remains only partly understood, and objective physiological characterisation remains challenging 1, 6 . Tinnitus becomes more common with age and often co-occurs with hearing loss 2, [6] [7] [8] . Both ageing and hearing loss can alter cortical responses to sound 2, [6] [7] [8] . Differences in cortical auditory responses between people with and without tinnitus may therefore reflect, in part, differences in age and hearing sensitivity. It remains unclear which features of the cortical response to sound are associated with tinnitus after accounting for these factors.

Predictive-processing accounts propose that auditory perception depends on how the brain weighs incoming sensory evidence against prior expectations [9] [10] [11] [12] [13] [14] . When auditory input becomes less reliable, greater reliance on prior expectations may favour the perception of sound in the absence of an external source 15, 16 . If this altered weighting extends to external sounds, the influence of sequence context on the response to an identical auditory event may differ between people with and without tinnitus. The local-global auditory paradigm provides a way to test this possibility by varying which event listeners can expect at the end of a sound sequence 17 . Two comparisons are particularly informative. Comparing an unexpectedly omitted sound with the sound normally presented at that moment captures the combined effects of violating an expectation and withholding acoustic input. Comparing the same omission across contexts in which it is expected or unexpected holds the acoustic input constant and tests how the response to silence depends on what was anticipated.

Previous EEG studies suggest that tinnitus is associated with differences in how listeners process changes in sound 14, 18 . Even when an EEG response differs on average between people with and without tinnitus, individual responses may overlap substantially across the two groups. A separate question is whether the same response relates to how loud or distressing tinnitus is for an individual 19, 20.

Previous work illustrates this distinction by showing that auditory physiological differences associated with tinnitus and sound sensitivity do not necessarily track tinnitus-related distress 21.

Here, we investigated auditory expectation processing in 440 adults with chronic subjective tinnitus (n = 226) or without tinnitus (n = 214), using 64-channel electroencephalography during a local-global auditory oddball paradigm across six centres. Our primary aim was to determine whether group differences in responses to auditory deviance and sound omission persisted after accounting for site, age, sex and hearing thresholds. In exploratory analyses, we assessed how well selected EEG features distinguished participants with and without tinnitus, both alone and in combination with age, sex and hearing thresholds. Among participants with tinnitus, we also examined whether these features predicted tinnitus-related distress, subjective loudness and psychophysically matched loudness.

Methods

This study formed part of the Tinnitus Detection (TIDE) project, a prospective, multicentre case-control study conducted between September 2024 and October 2025 at seven centres in.

Europe and the United States: the University of Texas at Austin, University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, Trinity College Dublin, Brai3n Clinic in Ghent, University Hospital Zurich, University Clinic Tübingen and University Hospital Regensburg. The principal objective was to examine differences in auditory deviance-and omissionrelated responses between participants with and without chronic subjective tinnitus, accounting for site, age, sex and hearing thresholds. Local-global auditory paradigm and contrasts

The task comprised three protocols presenting five-event sequences: five consecutive 500-Hz pure tones (xxxxx), four tones followed by a white-noise burst (xxxxY), or four tones followed by omission of the fifth event (xxxx_). Stimuli lasted 50 ms, including 7-ms linear onset and offset ramps, with a 300-ms stimulus onset asynchrony (250-ms offset-to-onset interval) and a randomly jittered inter-trial interval of 1,300-1,500 ms (Figure 2A). Sitespecific acoustic calibration equipment and PsychoPy versions are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

The xxxxx and xxxxY protocols each comprised five blocks of 125 trials. Each block began with 25 repetitions of the protocol-specific standard, followed by 100 test trials comprising 75 standards, 15 deviants and 10 unexpected omissions. Only the 75 test-phase standards in each block were eligible for inclusion in the standard ERP, subject to artefact rejection. Standard and deviant sequence identities were reversed between the two protocols, whereas omissions were unexpected in both. A third protocol comprised 125 sequences with predictable omission of the fifth event. Protocol and test-trial order were pseudorandomised, yielding 1,375 trials overall. Participants could take self-paced breaks between blocks, with fatigue and comfort monitored between protocols (Figure 2A).

. Eight ERP contrasts were evaluated across four families (Figure 2B). Within-block deviance contrasts compared deviant with standard responses separately within each sound-present protocol, reflecting differences in terminal-event identity and sequence probability. Global contextual contrasts compared physically identical sequences across protocols in which they served as deviants or standards, assessing responses associated with global sequence context while holding sequence identity constant. Omission deviance contrasts compared unexpected omissions in each sound-present protocol with predictable omissions in the expected-omission protocol, examining omission-related activity across different expectation and block contexts.

Within-block omission contrasts compared unexpected omissions with sound-present standards within each protocol, capturing omission-related differences that also incorporate the response to the presented terminal sound in the standard condition.

All temporal measurements were referenced to sequence onset; where relevant, latencies were additionally expressed relative to the fifth event position at 1,200 ms.

EEG preprocessing and physical montage alignment

EEG preprocessing was performed using MNE-Python (v1.12.1), AutoReject (v0.4.3) and mne-icalabel (v0.8.1). Recordings acquired above 1,000 Hz were downsampled to 1,000 Hz before all datasets were resampled to 250 Hz and zero-phase band-pass filtered (1-40 Hz); sitespecific line-noise frequencies (50 or 60 Hz) were notch-filtered. Electrode locations were mapped to manufacturer-specific international 10-05 coordinates (Supplementary Table 1).

Illinois digitised electrode coordinates were co-registered to the standard 10-05 template using an affine 3D transformation based on periauricular and nasion fiducials. Signals were averagereferenced, and candidate bad channels were identified using Local Outlier Factor (threshold = 3.0), visually inspected by an analyst blinded to diagnostic group, and reconstructed using . spherical-spline interpolation. Continuous data were inspected for gross non-physiological movement and mechanical artefacts, which were manually annotated and excluded.

Physiological artefacts were addressed using Extended Infomax independent component analysis (ICA). Components identified by ICLabel as ocular/saccadic artefacts with probability >0.70 were removed, and the cleaned signals reconstructed. Data were epoched from -0.5 to 1.8 s relative to sequence onset and baseline-corrected using the -0.5 to 0 s pre-sequence interval. Epochs overlapping manually annotated artefacts were rejected. Before epoch-level correction, trigger counts were verified separately for each protocol against nominal trial totals, with a permitted deviation of 10% (approximately 500 standards, 75 deviants and 50 unexpected omissions per five-block protocol, and 125 trials for the expected-omission protocol); recordings outside this tolerance were excluded. Remaining epochs were processed using AutoReject, which estimated participant-and sensor-specific peak-to-peak thresholds to interpolate transient sensor artefacts and reject corrupted epochs.

Subject-level condition ERPs and subtraction contrasts were computed in each site's native montage. For group-level spatiotemporal permutation testing, datasets were restricted to the physical intersection of 52 scalp electrodes shared across recording caps (Supplementary Table 1); non-native channels were not synthesised or interpolated.

Spatiotemporal cluster-based permutation analysis and source localisation

Before group-level testing, sensor-space ERP waveforms from 0.0 to 1.8 s after sequence onset were harmonised across the six retained centres using mean-only NeuroComBat (v0.2.12) 31 . Harmonisation was fitted jointly across cases and controls to maximise the data available for estimating centre effects, while preserving variation associated with age, sex and PTA4. Tinnitus status was not included in the harmonisation model, so diagnostic group membership did not directly inform estimation of the harmonisation parameters 32 . Candidate clusters were formed at two-tailed uncorrected p < 0.05 across ≥2 spatially adjacent channels (Delaunay triangulation). Cluster voltage polarity (positive or negative) is defined relative to the sign of the covariate-adjusted group difference wave (Tinnitus -Control). A positive cluster indicates that event-related potential amplitudes in the tinnitus group are significantly more positive (or less negative) than in the control group within that spatiotemporal window, whereas a negative cluster indicates significantly more negative (or less positive) amplitudes in the tinnitus group.

. The eight contrasts were designed to investigate distinct, hierarchically organised aspects of auditory predictive processing within the local-global oddball paradigm, including local deviance detection, omission-related processing and global sequence violations (Figure 2B).

Previous ERP studies have demonstrated that auditory responses to omissions depend on the predictability of the omitted stimulus 33 , while tinnitus-related abnormalities have also been associated with altered responses to violations of auditory intensity predictions 18 . These findings support the investigation of distinct predictive processing mechanisms rather than treating all auditory deviance responses as a single neurophysiological phenomenon 17.

Statistical inference was performed separately for each contrast using spatiotemporal clusterbased permutation testing, with 10,000 Freedman-Lane permutations restricted within recording centre 34 . This nonparametric approach addresses the multiple-comparisons problem arising from mass-univariate testing across the 52 common EEG sensors and time points, controlling the family-wise error rate (FWER) within each contrast. Positive and negative clusters were incorporated into the same permutation-based null distribution, with the maximum absolute cluster statistic used to control the FWER across the spatiotemporal search space 35 . The reduced model included age, sex and PTA4, while the full model additionally included tinnitus status.

To assess multiplicity across the eight investigated ERP contrasts, we additionally applied a conservative Bonferroni correction to the contrast-specific cluster-level p-values. This correction supplemented the spatiotemporal permutation procedure, which controlled the family-wise error rate across electrodes, time points and cluster polarities within each contrast.

Experiment-wide statistical significance was evaluated at a Bonferroni-adjusted threshold of p < 0.05.

Exploratory cortical source estimates were computed using dynamic statistical parametric mapping (dSPM) on the fsaverage template with a three-layer boundary-element forward . model, loose orientation constraint (0.2), depth weighting (0.8) and regularisation 𝜆2 = 1/9.

Noise covariance was estimated using an ad-hoc diagonal noise covariance matrix computed from sensor geometries. Source estimates represented un-thresholded group differences in mean source amplitude averaged over the corresponding empirical sensor-cluster time windows; no source-space statistical inference was performed.

Electrodes and time windows for ERP feature extraction were selected using group-level contrasts from the full cohort before cross-validation. Consequently, participants in the heldout folds contributed to the initial feature-selection process, meaning that standard crossvalidation alone cannot eliminate potential circularity. The primary objective of these analyses was therefore exploratory: to assess the incremental predictive information provided by taskevoked ERP features beyond demographic and audiological baselines (age, sex and PTA4).

To assess the potential influence of feature-selection circularity, we performed a stratified random 85:15 discovery-test split, maintaining approximately equal proportions of participants with tinnitus and controls in each subset (Figure 5C-D). NeuroComBat was fitted using the discovery subset, and the resulting parameters were applied to the test subset without refitting.

Cluster-permutation testing and ERP feature selection were restricted to the discovery subset; the selected electrodes and time windows were then applied unchanged to the test subset.

. Models were trained in the discovery subset and evaluated in the held-out test subset. Given the relatively small test sample, this analysis was treated as an internal sensitivity assessment rather than definitive validation. The full-cohort analysis remained primary, with its performance estimates interpreted as exploratory.

Using hierarchical logistic regression, we evaluated EEG features alone and in combination with age, sex and PTA4 in 439 participants with complete data. We compared the age, sex and PTA4 baseline model with the EEG-augmented model using the same participants and identical stratified ten-fold cross-validation splits. Within each fold, we fitted ComBat to the training data and applied the fitted transformation to the held-out participants. We estimated 95% confidence intervals for model performance and for the difference between models using 1,000 bootstrap resamples of the paired out-of-fold predictions (random seed 42).

Probabilistic calibration (Brier score, calibration slope and intercept) and discrimination across sex, age and hearing-threshold subgroups (Sex, Age <50 vs ≥50, PTA4 <15 vs ≥15 dB HL) were evaluated following TRIPOD+AI guidelines (Supplementary Table 6). Multi-centre parameter stability was examined using leave-one-site-out (LOSO) sensitivity analyses, holding out each centre in turn and cross-validating across the remaining centres to assess sensitivity to cohort composition, distinct from external validation on an unseen centre. False discovery rate (FDR) was controlled across candidate predictors within each hierarchical model step using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure (𝑝 FDR < 0.05).

Results

Demographic and Clinical Cohort Profile

Across seven centres, 820 individuals were assessed for eligibility, 584 met study criteria, and 562 had complete task datasets (282 with tinnitus and 280 controls; Figure 1; Supplementary Table 2). Quality assessment excluded the Austin cohort (n = 82) because of elevated baseline noise and absent sensory-evoked responses (Supplementary Figures S1-S2).

A further 40 participants were excluded across the remaining six centres: 36 for sensory signalto-noise ratios below 1.0, 2 for missing clinical covariates, and 2 for corrupted task triggers.

The final analysis included 440 participants from six centres: 226 with chronic subjective tinnitus and 214 controls. In ERP-only analyses, sample size depended on contrast trial availability (𝑛 = 440 for the omission xxxxY-standard xxxxY contrast; 𝑛 = 439 for the xxxxx deviant-standard contrast due to insufficient trial counts for one tinnitus participant), while complete-case covariate-adjusted classification models included 439 participants due to missing biological sex data for one tinnitus participant.

Demographic and audiological adjustment substantially attenuates electrophysiological group differences.

We first examined whether electrophysiological differences between participants with and without chronic tinnitus persisted after accounting for demographic and audiological variation.

Unadjusted spatiotemporal analyses identified group differences across several auditory deviance and omission contrasts (Supplementary Table 3 and Supplementary Figure S3). However, these differences were substantially reduced after accounting for site, age, sex and hearing thresholds, indicating that much of the apparent electrophysiological variation was associated with these factors.

Following covariate adjustment and contrast-specific spatiotemporal family-wise error correction, only the comparison between unexpected omissions and sound-present standards in the xxxxY protocol yielded significant clusters (Supplementary Table 3). Relative to the expected onset of the fifth event, the tinnitus group showed a more positive omission-minusstandard response at 44-100 ms (peak t = 4.19, cluster p = 0.021) and a more negative response at 356-476 ms (peak t = -3.27, cluster p = 0.044; Figure 4). However, neither cluster remained statistically significant after additional correction across the eight investigated ERP contrasts. Conservative Bonferroni correction yielded adjusted pvalues of 0.168 and 0.352 for the early and late clusters, respectively. These findings therefore represent contrast-specific candidate effects rather than experiment-wide significant group differences. Neither comparison between unexpected and expected omissions yielded significant group differences (Figure 4A,B and Supplementary Table 3).

Candidate omission-related differences show limited individual-level discrimination

We next investigated whether the electrophysiological features identified at the group level could distinguish individuals with and without chronic tinnitus. Three candidate ERP features were evaluated using logistic regression with 10-fold cross-validation (n = 439; Figure 5; Supplementary Table 4) , with parallel classification results for the unadjusted (uncontrolled) ERP features reported in Supplementary Figure S4. Peak-to-peak amplitudes provided limited discrimination (combined ROC-AUC = 0.523, 95% CI 0.468-0.580), whereas temporally integrated ERP amplitudes achieved a ROC-AUC of 0.624 (95% CI 0.570-0.673).

The model containing age, sex and PTA4 achieved a cross-validated ROC-AUC of 0.608 (95% CI 0.551-0.662), compared with 0.689 (95% CI 0.640-0.738) after adding the three integrated ERP amplitudes. The paired difference in ROC-AUC (combined model minus baseline model) was 0.081 (95% CI 0.046-0.109). The model including the ERP features also showed better fit (Δ McFadden's pseudo-R² = 0.056, likelihood-ratio test p < .001). The preterminal contextual feature (OR = 0.65, 95% CI 0.52-0.81; FDR-adjusted p < 0.001) and early positive omission-versus-sound feature (OR = 1.39, 95% CI 1.11-1.74; FDR-adjusted p. = 0.016) remained associated with tinnitus status after mutual and covariate adjustment. The later negative omission-versus-sound feature did not survive FDR correction (p = 0.087).

Adding anxiety and depression scores yielded a ROC-AUC of 0.716 (95% CI 0.670-0.763).

Sensitivity analyses excluding each centre in turn yielded cross-validated ROC-AUCs of 0.659-0.707 within the remaining participants (Supplementary Table 5). The preterminal contextual feature remained associated with tinnitus status across all six exclusions, and the early omission-versus-sound feature across five. These analyses assessed sensitivity to cohort composition rather than prediction in an unseen centre. The out-of-fold calibration slope was 0.90 and the intercept was -0.08. Subgroup ROC-AUCs ranged from 0.65 to 0.70 (Supplementary Table 6).

These exploratory analyses indicate modest discrimination using the selected ERP features.

Physiological classification features do not reliably predict tinnitus burden or loudness.

Finally, we examined whether the candidate ERP features captured individual differences in tinnitus-related burden, subjective loudness and psychophysically matched loudness among participants with chronic tinnitus (Figures 6 and 7; Supplementary Tables 7 8 9 10 11 12 13 14).

Within the tinnitus cohort, the selected ERP features showed little predictive value for symptom burden or loudness. Models combining all three integrated ERP amplitudes yielded cross-validated correlations of r = -0.018 for THI scores and r = 0.033 for subjective loudness ratings (n = 217). The two clinical measures were more informative about each other.

Subjective loudness alone predicted THI scores with r = 0.438 and a mean absolute error (MAE) of 11.89 THI points (n = 218). A model including subjective loudness, age, sex, hearing thresholds and the late omission-versus-sound ERP feature yielded similar performance (r = 0.438, MAE = 11.93 points, n = 217). Conversely, THI scores alone predicted subjective loudness with r = 0.439 (n = 218) (Figure 6 and Supplementary Tables 9 10).

Age was the strongest individual predictor of pure-tone hearing thresholds (PTA4), with a cross-validated correlation of r = 0.570 and an MAE of 6.68 dB HL (n = 226). A model combining age, sex and the preterminal contextual ERP feature yielded r = 0.566 and an MAE of 6.61 dB HL (n = 217). ERP features alone also showed little predictive value for psychophysically matched tinnitus loudness, with cross-validated correlations near zero or negative. A model combining the late omission-versus-sound feature with age, sex, PTA4, THI scores and subjective loudness predicted matched loudness with r = 0.515 and an MAE of . 14.26 dB HL (n = 171). The ERP feature showed little independent association with matched loudness in this model (standardised β = -0.084, FDR-adjusted p = 0.404). Full model comparisons and site-exclusion analyses are reported in Supplementary Tables 13-14 (Figures 6 and 7).

Discussion

In this six-centre study of 440 adults, we investigated whether auditory event-related potentials distinguish individuals with and without chronic tinnitus after accounting for demographic and audiological variation. Although several group differences emerged in unadjusted analyses, most were attenuated following harmonisation and adjustment for age, sex and hearing thresholds. Two omission-versus-sound response clusters remained significant after contrast-specific spatiotemporal correction, but neither survived additional correction across the eight investigated contrasts. These findings therefore provide exploratory rather than confirmatory evidence for tinnitus-associated electrophysiological differences. Moreover, although the selected ERP features showed modest discrimination of tinnitus status in crossvalidation, their incremental predictive value was not reproduced in the internal held-out test subset, and they provided little information about tinnitus burden or loudness. Together, these results highlight the distinction between candidate electrophysiological group-level associations and neural measures that provide robust, generalisable information at the individual level.

Accounting for demographic and audiological variation narrowed the set of contrasts in which we detected tinnitus-control differences. Age and hearing impairment influence cortical auditory responses and are also associated with tinnitus, making their contributions difficult to separate in case-control comparisons [36] [37] [38] [39] . This sensitivity to adjustment may help contextualise heterogeneous findings across studies with different demographic and audiological . profiles 14,18,40 . However, attenuation does not establish that age or hearing loss caused the original differences, and persistence after adjustment does not demonstrate tinnitus specificity.

PTA4 provides a standardised measure of hearing sensitivity, while additional audiological measures could further clarify the contribution of peripheral auditory function. The adjusted analyses identified candidate electrophysiological differences that warrant further investigation, although none survived correction across all eight ERP contrasts. These exploratory findings provide a basis for testing whether similar response patterns can be reproduced in independent cohorts and distinguished from residual demographic and audiological influences.

In the xxxxY protocol, the same four tones preceded either a white-noise burst or its unexpected omission. The observed group difference could therefore reflect the response to the omission, the response to the burst, or both. Comparing expected and unexpected omissions provides a more direct test of auditory expectation because the fifth position is silent in both conditions 17, 33, [41] [42] [43] . Neither of the two expected-versus-unexpected omission contrasts yielded a significant group difference, leaving the contribution of expectation violation to the omissionversus-sound finding unresolved.

The temporal characteristics of the candidate omission-related responses may be compatible with different stages of auditory event processing, although their functional interpretation remains uncertain 17, 44 . Exploratory source reconstruction yielded spatial patterns compatible with activity in posterior cingulate, pregenual anterior cingulate, retrosplenial and parahippocampal regions, which have previously been implicated in tinnitus-related neural activity and contextual processing [45] [46] [47] [48] [49] . However, these anatomical estimates were derived from template-based source modelling without individual structural MRI or formal source-space statistical inference. Moreover, the underlying sensor-level clusters did not survive correction across all eight ERP contrasts. Consequently, these spatial patterns should be considered . descriptive and hypothesis-generating rather than evidence for specific cortical generators or neural mechanisms. Future studies combining electrophysiology with individual anatomical imaging and independent validation will be necessary to establish whether these regions contribute to altered auditory event processing in chronic tinnitus.

The individual-level analyses extended the central question raised in the introduction whether EEG differences associated with tinnitus remain informative when age and hearing sensitivity are considered. Given previous evidence that ageing and hearing loss alter cortical auditory responses [36] [37] [38] , evaluating these features alongside demographic and audiological measures is essential for assessing their potential contribution to individual characterisation.

Selected EEG features showed modest discrimination of tinnitus status within this cohort, with a neural-only cross-validated ROC-AUC of 0.62, increasing to 0.69 when age, sex and PTA4 were included. Furthermore, including anxiety and depression showed an increase to 0.72. This pattern highlights the relevance of the demographic, audiological context as well as mood in which tinnitus occurs. Adding the three selected ERP features to age, sex and PTA4 increased cross-validated ROC-AUC from 0.608 to 0.689 in this cohort. Temporally integrated ERP features performed better than peak-to-peak measurements, potentially reflecting differences in measurement reliability, noise sensitivity or the representation of sustained activity 50, 51.

These findings should nevertheless be interpreted in light of the exploratory featureselection procedure. Because the full cohort contributed to initial ERP feature selection, the primary cross-validated performance estimates may be optimistic and do not establish generalisable diagnostic performance. The additional 85:15 held-out sensitivity analysis restricted harmonisation and feature selection to the discovery subset. Although the same contrast-specific significant clusters were identified in this subset, the ERP-only classifier showed poor discrimination in the held-out participants (Supplementary Figure S5). The apparent improvement in discrimination following the addition of ERP features was not . reproduced in the held-out test subset, where the demographic and audiological model performed equally well with or without electrophysiological predictors. Although the relatively small test sample limits the precision of this comparison, these findings suggest that the incremental predictive value observed in the primary cross-validation analysis may partly reflect feature-selection optimism. Consequently, the present results do not establish that the selected ERP features provide generalisable diagnostic information beyond readily available demographic and audiological characteristics. Site-exclusion analyses further assessed sensitivity to individual centres. Together, these results motivate further evaluation of EEG for physiological characterisation of tinnitus, rather than establishing a clinically applicable diagnostic classifier.

The clinical prediction analyses clarify which dimensions of tinnitus are captured by the selected EEG features. Previous research has shown that elevated central auditory gain in individuals with tinnitus and sound sensitivity need not track symptom severity, whereas autonomic responses to emotionally evocative sounds can predict tinnitus-related burden 21.

Our findings extend this distinction to task-evoked ERP features: measures selected to distinguish tinnitus from controls contributed little to predicting THI scores or subjective loudness. The contribution is therefore to demonstrate, within the same multicentre cohort, that physiological features associated with tinnitus presence do not necessarily provide useful estimates of its clinical expression. This motivates selecting and validating neural measures specifically for the clinical outcome of interest. THI scores and subjective loudness informed prediction of one another, consistent with their related but distinguishable clinical characteristics 46, 52 . Psychophysically matched loudness showed a different predictor pattern, with greater contributions from hearing thresholds and age. Research likewise demonstrated that the relationship between loudness matching and tinnitus severity depends on hearing sensitivity and the measurement scale [53] [54] [55] . In the present study, conversion from dB SL to dB . HL explicitly incorporates the hearing threshold at the matched frequency, which may partly contribute to the observed audiological association. Together, these findings support treating matched loudness, subjective loudness and tinnitus-related burden as complementary outcomes requiring separate validation of candidate physiological measures.

Future studies can build on these findings by addressing several methodological limitations.

Harmonisation enabled pooled analysis, although exclusion of one centre for EEG quality reduced acquisition-system diversity, and generalisation to new recording environments remains to be tested. Although the initial analyses controlled the family-wise error rate across electrodes and time points within each contrast, neither of the two candidate omission-related clusters remained statistically significant after additional correction across all eight investigated contrasts. These findings therefore provide exploratory rather than confirmatory evidence for tinnitus-associated electrophysiological differences. Moreover, because the full cohort contributed to the initial selection of ERP features, the primary cross-validation estimates may be optimistic, as suggested by the absence of incremental predictive value in held-out validation. Although NeuroComBat was used to minimise centre-related variation, future studies should assess the robustness of these findings using alternative approaches to site adjustment, including regression models incorporating recording centre as a fixed effect without prior harmonisation. Source localisation was exploratory and relied on template-based anatomical modelling without individual MRI or formal source-space statistical inference, limiting the anatomical specificity of the observed patterns. More broadly, these methodological considerations illustrate a central challenge in biomarker development: statistical associations identified at the group level may be sensitive to confounding, analytical choices and sample characteristics, limiting their translation into reliable individual-level predictions.

. This large multicentre study highlights the challenges of translating electrophysiological group-level associations into reliable individual-level biomarkers of chronic tinnitus. Although candidate differences in auditory event-related potentials emerged following adjustment for demographic and audiological factors, these effects did not survive correction across all investigated contrasts. Moreover, the apparent improvement in tinnitus classification following the addition of EEG features was not reproduced in held-out validation, and these features provided little predictive information regarding tinnitus burden or loudness. These findings illustrate a broader methodological challenge in neuroscience: the identification of neural differences between clinical and control populations does not necessarily establish their diagnostic relevance or generalisability to individual patients. Similar challenges arise in biomarker research across neurological and psychiatric conditions, where group-level neural associations may not translate into robust clinical prediction. Rather than excluding the potential value of electrophysiological biomarkers, our findings emphasise the importance of rigorous confounder adjustment, appropriate control of multiple comparisons, independent validation and outcome-specific biomarker development. Such approaches are essential for moving from descriptive neural associations towards clinically informative measures of brain function.
