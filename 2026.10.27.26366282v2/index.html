Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Misinformation decreases public confidence in health technologies and willingness to use them. Psychological inoculation, or "prebunking", exposes people to misleading claims before they spread widely and refutes the claims and embedded persuasion tactics. Evidence on its effectiveness for HIV-related misinformation, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa and among young women (a population highly vulnerable to HIV), is limited.

We conducted a randomised controlled trial (July to October 2025) among 2,163 women aged 18-29 years recruited online in South Africa. Participants were randomly assigned to view two short TikTok-style inoculation videos addressing common myths about an HIV vaccine, or attention-matched control videos on nutrition and diabetes. All participants then viewed multiple false claims presented as social media posts. The primary outcome was intention to receive an HIV vaccine if available (1-10 scale), assessed immediately postintervention and at three-week follow-up. Secondary outcomes included likelihood of recommending the vaccine; and perceived credibility, emotional impact, and shareability of misinformation claims. We used linear regression models to estimate intervention effects.

Results

Misinformation exposure reduced vaccine intentions by 0.63 points in the control group, while intentions increased by 0.30 points in the intervention group. Controlling for covariates and baseline intentions, post-exposure vaccine intentions were 0.92 points higher in the intervention group (95% CI 0.78, 1.06; p<0.01), a 12% relative increase. Intervention participants were also more likely to recommend the vaccine (+0.74; 95% CI 0.61, 0.88; p<0.01). Inoculation reduced perceived credibility, emotional distress, and sharing intentions across all false claims. Effects persisted at three-week follow-up (vaccine intentions: +0.97; 95% CI 0.78, 1.17; p<0.01).

Conclusion

Brief, scalable psychological inoculation delivered via social-media-style videos protected young women's HIV vaccine intentions, with effects generalising to novel claims and persisting over three weeks. Prebunking campaigns may safeguard public confidence in other new HIV prevention technologies like lenacapavir.

Introduction

Expanded HIV prevention and treatment services across sub-Saharan Africa over the past 20 years has enabled millions to live longer, healthier lives, yet HIV incidence remains highparticularly among adolescent girls and young women. Many prevention efforts have had only modest success, with low uptake and low sustained use of effective prevention options like oral pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) among young women. 1 As a result, the development of new prevention options, including an HIV vaccine, remains important for ending the HIV epidemic.

The recent discovery of highly-effective long-acting injectable PrEP represents a major opportunity to accelerate progress toward an HIV-free generation. Among new modalities, lenacapavir is the first twice-yearly injectable PrEP shown to offer substantial protection against HIV acquisition. 2 Lenacapavir has received swift global support, including World Health Organization prequalification and record-speed approval in South Africa. 3, 4 While ensuring adequate supply of new biomedical innovations like lenacapavir is a prerequisite for altering the trajectory of the HIV epidemic, addressing the barriers to demand for such innovations is equally important. Prior experience with innovations like COVID-19 and human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccines has demonstrated how viral misinformationespecially regarding safety, ingredients, and scientific and policymaker intent -can erode public confidence and significantly undermine uptake. [5] [6] [7] Identifying effective and scalable approaches to countering misinformation about new health products -particularly before it becomes viral and widespread -is thus an important priority for policymakers and public health practitioners.

We investigated psychological inoculation as a strategy to proactively build resistance to HIV prevention misinformation, focusing here on a yet-to-be developed HIV vaccine. Analogous to medical inoculation, psychological inoculation entails exposing individuals to weakened versions of misinformation or misleading arguments, alongside refutations or explanations that highlight the common fallacies and manipulative tactics used in misinformation campaigns. 8, 9 This "prebunking" process aims to build cognitive resistance among individuals before they encounter stronger, more persuasive forms of misinformation -it gets ahead of misinformation before it can do damage. 8 Figure 1 depicts the theory of psychological inoculation. Prebunking may be more effective than debunking, which counters false claims after they've already spread and taken hold, because of the continued influence effect: the psychological phenomenon where outdated or false information continues to influence a person's thinking and decision-making even after it has been corrected or retracted. 11 Prior research in other domains has shown that brief (5-15 minute) interactive prebunking interventions, often delivered via online games, can reduce belief in misinformation and reduce willingness to share them. 12 Meta-analyses also support the efficacy of psychological inoculation. 12, 13 However, psychological inoculation has been studied primarily in highincome countries and for topics including COVID-19, 14, 15 climate change, 16 and politics. 17 There is limited evidence on the use of psychological inoculation for HIV-related misinformation, from low-and middle-income countries, and among young people. 18, 19 Furthermore, little is known about the effectiveness of brief, social-media-native video formats (e.g., TikTok's distinct portrait-orientation reels) in delivering psychological inoculation at scale, which is an important gap in light of high social media penetration globally. Critical evidence gaps also persist on the durability of these effects, 10, 20, 21 and the ability of inoculation interventions to confer generalised resistance to other forms of misinformation.

To address these gaps, we developed and tested two 2.5-minute videos that sought to inoculate viewers against common misinformation claims about a future HIV vaccine. We then conducted a pre-registered, two-arm randomised controlled trial with 2,163 women (18-29 years old) in South Africa. Our trial tested several pre-registered hypotheses: 1) participants who viewed the inoculation videos prior to exposure to misleading claims would report higher intentions to receive the HIV vaccine and be more likely to recommend the vaccine to a friend -an indicator of broader normative acceptance; 2) the inoculation videos would reduce the perceived credibility, emotional impact, and shareability of misinformation claims; 3) psychological inoculation would generalise to different misinformation claims and tactics not explicitly addressed in the videos; 4) the intervention would remain effective after 3-4 weeks.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a two-arm randomised controlled trial consisting of a main online survey (at study enrolment) and a follow-up survey approximately three weeks later. The main survey included: delivery of the intervention videos (or attention-matched control videos); exposure to multiple misinformation claims; pre-and post-intervention measurement of the primary outcome of vaccine intentions; and a study debrief highlighting that all misinformation claims were fabricated for research purposes. The follow-up survey included: exposure to additional misinformation claims; a re-assessment of vaccine intentions to examine the persistence of effects over time; and another study debrief. An outline of the study procedures and data collection is provided in appendix 1 (p 13, Figure S1). The study was reviewed and approved by independent research ethics committees at the University of Pennsylvania (854420), University of the Witwatersrand (reference number: 241118), University of Cape Town (reference number: 1018/2024), and Boston University (IRB number: H-45460).

Study setting and procedures

We recruited participants from Prolific, an online research platform that includes thousands of individuals from South Africa. 22 We enrolled participants who were female, aged 18-29 years, resident in South Africa, and able to read English (as indicated by Prolific as 'fluent in English'); and had had sex in the past 12 months and reported their HIV status as negative or unknown. Although Prolific participants tend to be more educated than the general population in South Africa, the platform enabled rapid recruitment of a large, relevant cohort of young women who would be eligible for an HIV vaccine. Prolific invited eligible users to participate and provided a link to the study survey, which was administered through Qualtrics. Participants could complete the survey on any internet-enabled device.

After providing informed consent, participants answered questions related to socio-economic characteristics, mental health, and prior health behaviours. The survey also assessed participants' HIV vaccine intentions and likelihood of recommending the HIV vaccine. To reduce the potential for demand-side characteristics to explain the results, all participants were provided with information about the HIV vaccine prior to randomisation to the intervention or control group: that any HIV vaccine available to the public in the future would have been tested and found to be safe and effective. Participants were then randomly assigned (1:1) to the intervention and control groups. Randomisation and survey administration were fully automated.

Intervention group

Participants in the intervention group viewed two short inoculation videos, which were developed in three steps. First, we conducted a rapid review of misinformation claims about HIV vaccines (details published elsewhere), 23 identifying concerns around side effects, fertility, and conspiracy theories. Second, we selected 54 unique claims from the review and surveyed young South African women (18-29 years; n=188) to determine which claims were most concerning to them. Safety-related claims -particularly those suggesting severe harm -were rated most concerning (details published elsewhere). 24 Third, we developed a new set of false HIV-vaccine safety claims incorporating commonly used persuasion tactics when promoting misinformation. 25 The claims mimic real-world statements that people could encounter. We tested their perceived credibility and influence on vaccine intentions and recommendations in a second online survey (n=195).

The two claims selected for the intervention scored highly in this second survey for how concerning, credible, and shareable they were; making them good candidates for inoculation. The first claim is "My sister got the HIV vaccine and her heart stopped the next day. Please don't get vaccinated-IT COULD KILL YOU! 😭". This claim uses the post hoc fallacy, a misinformation tactic that exploits the human tendency to infer a cause-and-effect relationship based solely on the temporal order of events. 25 The second claim is "HIV vaccine?! Yea, right, as soon as you can prove it's ZERO risk! #HIVvaccine #nomorejabs". This claim uses a misinformation tactic called "impossible expectations", which is based on demanding unrealistic standards before acting on scientific evidence. 25 Scripts for videos that inoculated against these two claims were developed following the three-part structure (see Figure 1): threat activation (alerting viewers that misinformation is common and about a specific false claim); refutation of the claim by explaining the common fallacies and manipulative tactics used in the claim; and a call to action (encouragement to look out for misinformation). The scripts were developed via participatory prototyping with young women (19-25 years old) in South Africa working as peer ambassadors for a mobile health clinic. Based on formative work assessing multiple modes or formats of inoculation content delivery (whiteboard animation, digital avatar, live video, or text), we then scripted and produced brief multi-character TikTok style videos filmed by a South African production company. Co-design workshops with peer health navigators and counselors from an HIV service delivery organisation helped ensure that the content, format, and actor choice were locally relevant, salient, and relatable. Inoculation Video 1 -post hoc fallacy -can be viewed at https://youtube.com/shorts/9Hs_36HS6Io?feature=share. Inoculation Video 2 -impossible expectations -can be viewed at https://youtube.com/shorts/viZUbEZJqfY?feature=share.

We also incorporated several strategies from the behavioural economics toolkit to strengthen message engagement, comprehension, retention, and to motivate action. Each video was followed by a short comprehension quiz (see appendix 2 (pp 16-19); incorrect answers required viewing the videos again since we sought to ensure that participants understood the tactics used in misinformation claims and thereby test the efficacy of psychological inoculation. 26 Other strategies included cognitive engagement with the material through selection of a digital sticker at the end of the main survey; priming a new identity for the participants as misinformation spotters; and reminder SMS messages between the main and follow-up surveys to prompt sharing their new expertise in countering misinformation.

Control group

Participants in the control arm viewed two attention-matched videos of identical length and style, and produced with the same actor, that focused on nutrition and diabetes, but with no inoculation content.

Misinformation exposure and outcome measurement

After viewing the videos, all participants were shown the two primary misinformation claims -those directly addressed in the inoculation videos -presented as Facebook posts (see appendix 3 (p 20). Immediately after each post, participants rated (a) its credibility, (b) the extent to which it made them anxious or worried about an HIV vaccine, and (c) their likelihood of sharing it on social media. HIV vaccine intentions were then measured a second time to assess the impact of misinformation exposure on the primary outcome.

Next, participants viewed four additional misinformation claims and one true claim, presented in random order. For each claim, the same three outcomes -credibility, anxiety, and shareability -were assessed. The additional false claims were included to assess the extent to which the psychological inoculation might generalise beyond the HIV claims that were specifically inoculated against to new claims that used different content and tactics. HIV vaccine recommendation was measured again at the end of this sequence to capture changes after exposure to the full set of claims.

The follow-up survey was administered approximately three weeks later (median: 21 days; average: 22.6; SD: 5.4). Participants were shown the two primary misinformation claims again, along with four new misinformation claims and one new true claim, and completed the same set of outcome measures, including HIV vaccine intentions and HIV vaccine recommendation. The study ended with a second debrief identical to the main survey debrief.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was participants' intention to get vaccinated. Specifically, participants were asked: "If an HIV vaccine became available, how likely would you be to get vaccinated?" (1-10 scale, where 1 is extremely unlikely and 10 is extremely likely). Given that an HIV vaccine is not yet available, our measure proxied actual demand for the vaccine.

A secondary outcome assessed participants' likelihood of recommending the vaccine to friends ("If an HIV vaccine became available, how likely would you be to recommend it to your friends?" ; 1-10 scale). Additional secondary outcomes measured on 1-7 scales assessed the extent to which participants believed the claims ("How much do you believe this post is true?") ; the anxiety induced by these claims ("How much does this post make you anxious or worried about the HIV vaccine?") ; and the likelihood of sharing the claims ("If you saw this post on social media, how likely would you be to share it?").

Statistical analyses

We used ordinary least squares regression (OLS) models to estimate the effect of the intervention on primary and secondary outcomes, controlling for demographic, socioeconomic and other factors that have previously been shown to influence vaccine intentions and the perceived credibility of misinformation. These were participants' age, race, education, province of residence (i.e., Gauteng or other), residential setting (i.e., informal settlement/township or other), employment status, and food insecurity. Province is relevant as the young people who co-designed the intervention resided in Gauteng province. While the intervention was designed for all young people in South Africa, it is possible it resonated more with people living in Gauteng. We also controlled for participant characteristics such as daily use of social media; symptoms of depression (i.e., PHQ-2 score ≥3); no receipt of a COVID-19 vaccine (proxy for being vaccine hesitant); ever used PrEP; HIV risk perception (binary variable identifying self-perceived high HIV risk); information avoidance behaviour (above the median); and self-reported confidence in scientists (1-4 scale). Finally, we also controlled for the pre-intervention values of the outcome variables in the regression specifications.

We also conducted pre-specified subgroup analyses to examine whether the effect of the intervention on study outcomes differed by age (<25 years vs. ≥25 years), prior PrEP use (never used vs. ever used), perceived risk of HIV (low vs. high), COVID-19 vaccination status (never received vs. received at least one dose), and information avoidance behaviour (low vs. high). For each sub-group characteristic, a separate analysis was conducted by including an interaction term between the treatment indicator and the corresponding binary subgroup indicator in a regression model that included the full sample. This interaction term tested whether the intervention effect differed significantly between a given subgroup and its complement. Effectiveness within a subgroup was defined as the magnitude and direction of the intervention effect in that subgroup, and a subgroup was characterised as showing greater effectiveness than its complement when the interaction term was statistically significant and indicated a larger treatment effect in that subgroup. All analyses were conducted using Stata version 16.1.

Results

Between 24 July and 16 October 2025 we enrolled 2,163 participants, representing 93% of eligible individuals invited to participate (See CONSORT diagram, Figure 2). Participant characteristics were similar in the study groups (Table 1). Overall, the mean age was 25 years (standard deviation (SD): 2.57) and participants were predominantly Black African (91%). Education levels were high with 99% having completed high school (grade 12), but other measures indicated variation in socioeconomic status: 11% reported household hunger (past 7 days), and over a third were living in a township or informal settlement. Nearly all participants reported daily social media use and about half resided in Gauteng Province, the largest province in South Africa. Participants' intentions to take the HIV vaccine were similar in both groups: mean of 7.81 and 7.77 in the control and intervention groups respectively.

HIV vaccine intentions decreased in the control group after seeing the misinformation claims (-0.63, SD:1.92). In contrast, intentions to take the vaccine increased in the intervention group after seeing the inoculation video and then being shown the misinformation claims (+0.30, SD:1.82). See Figure S2. Controlling for covariates and baseline vaccine intentions, vaccine intentions post misinformation exposure was 0.92 (12%) higher (p<0.01; 95%CI: 0.78,1.06) in the intervention group compared to the control group (Figure 3 Panel A and appendix 1 (p 1, Table S1).

Similarly, participants' self-reported likelihood of recommending the HIV vaccine to a friend declined in the control group after seeing the misinformation claims (-0.63, SD:1.90), whereas they increased in the intervention group (+0.11, SD:1.63). See Figure S3. Controlling for covariates (Figure 3 Panel A and appendix 1 (p 2, Table S2), intervention group participants were more likely to recommend the HIV vaccine to a friend than control group participants (+0.74, p<0.01, 95%CI: 0.61,0.88).

Sensitivity analysis excluding individuals who answered quiz questions incorrectly and were asked to rewatch either video (n=29), found substantively similar results (appendix 1 (pp 3-4, Table S3 and S4).

Inoculation messages also reduced (32-55%) how much participants believed all six false claims shown in the main survey (Figure 4 Panel A, appendix 1 (p 5, Table S5): someone's heart stopped after getting the HIV vaccine (Claim #1, specifically inoculated against, -1.7, p<0.01); that you should not get the HIV vaccine unless it is 100% safe (Claim #2, specifically inoculated against, -2.2, p<0.01); the HIV vaccine causes someone to get HIV (Claim #3, -1.0, p<0.01); the HIV vaccine is not worth getting unless 100% effective (Claim #4, -2.0, p<0.01); the COVID-19 vaccine was created by the government to sterilise Black women (Claim #5, -0.6, p<0.01); and a person should not get any vaccine unless there are no side-effects (Claim #6, -2.4, p<0.01).

The inoculation videos reduced negative emotional reactions to, and reduced likelihood of sharing the misinformation (a behavioural indicator of intent to engage with and propagate misinformation). 13 Compared to the control group, the intervention group reported feeling less anxious or worried about the claim (33-49% lower), with the difference in average scores ranging from -0.80 (p<0.01, 95% CI: -0.93, -0.66) to -2.12 (p<0.01, 95% CI: -2.28, -1.97). Intervention group reported being less likely than the control group to share the six misinformation claims with others if they saw them on social media (range: 𝛽:-0.57 (p<0.01, 95% CI: -0.68, -0.46) to 𝛽:-1.90 (p<0.01, 95% CI: -2.06, -1.74)).

The 3-week follow-up survey re-interviewed 1,936 (89.5%) participants, with attrition similar in the intervention group (114/1083 (10.5%)) and control group (113/1080 (10.5% )). At 3week follow-up, the intervention group continued to report stronger intentions to accept an HIV vaccine than the control group after viewing additional misinformation claims (+0.97, p<0.01, 95% CI: 0.78, 1.17). Descriptive analysis showed that in the control group, HIV vaccine intentions were reduced by 13% (from 7.8/10 to 6.8/10), while in the treatment group there was no change in vaccine intentions (7.8/10 vs 7.8/10). The intervention group remained more likely to recommend the HIV vaccine to a friend than the control group (𝛽: 0.83, p<0.01, 95% CI: 0.64, 1.03) (Figure 3 Panel B); continued to perceive misinformation to be less credible, were less likely to report that misinformation made them anxious or worried, and were less likely to share misinformation (Figure 4 Panel B). A logistic regression analysis of factors associated with attrition between the surveys found that no covariates were significantly associated with attrition (appendix 1 (p 6, Table S6).

To assess the possibility that the inoculation intervention also increased skepticism about true claims, 12 we asked participants to view a true claim in the main survey (that the vaccine will only be offered to the public once it is tested and approved) and evaluate its credibility. The intervention group expressed slightly higher (rather than lower) belief in the true statement than the control group (5.59 vs 5.75; 𝛽: 0.16, p<0.05, 95% CI: 0.02, 0.30). The follow-up survey also assessed responses to a true claim: "Smoking literally increases your risk for lung cancer for sure --that's been proven! But that doesn't mean everyone who smokes gets lung cancer." Counter to expectations, the intervention group was less likely to believe this claim than the control group (𝛽: -0.29, p<0.01, 95% CI: -0.47,-0.11).

Pre-specified sub-group analyses (see appendix 1 (pp 8-11, Tables S8-S11) found that the inoculation messages were most effective among participants who had not received the COVID-19 vaccine at enrolment: HIV vaccine intentions in the follow-up survey (𝛽 for interaction term: +0.83, p<0.01, 95% CI: 0.39, 1.30); HIV vaccine recommendation at main survey (𝛽 for interaction term: +0.56, p<0.01, 95% CI: 0.25, 0.87); and HIV vaccine recommendation at follow-up (𝛽 for interaction term: +0.75, p<0.01, 95% CI: 0.31, 1.19). For three other pre-specified sub-groups we found little evidence of subgroup-specific intervention effects: age, PrEP use, perceived HIV risk, and information avoidance. An additional subgroup analysis conducted to assess potential differences in intervention effect by socioeconomic status found that our intervention had similar effects on HIV-vaccine intentions among those residing in an informal settlement/township and those living elsewhere (see appendix 1 (p 12, Table S12).

Discussion

In this randomised controlled trial with young women in South Africa, brief psychological inoculation messages delivered through short videos were effective in protecting participants from the negative effects of misinformation about a yet-to-be-developed HIV vaccine. The inoculation messages prevented the substantial decline in intentions to accept an HIV vaccine observed in the control group after encountering misinformation. The intervention also reduced the perceived credibility of multiple misinformation claims, lowered negative emotional reactions to misinformation, and decreased participants' likelihood of sharing false claims. Importantly, these protective effects persisted three weeks later, even after participants saw a broader range of misinformation claims. Together, these findings show that inoculation messages can meaningfully preserve vaccine intentions and reduce susceptibility to misinformation in a population particularly at risk of acquiring HIV. Furthermore, the protective effects of the inoculation videos extended beyond the two specific claims about the HIV vaccine addressed in the videos. The additional claims included ones using the same persuasion tactics as in the main claims (post hoc fallacy and impossible expectations) but with different content and in relation to vaccines in general, the flu vaccine, and different health topics. They also included claims using different persuasion tactics and about other vaccines or HIV prevention methods. Inoculated participants also rated these new claims as less credible, experienced lower levels of worry in response to them, and reported a lower likelihood of sharing them compared to the control group. This is an encouraging finding given how rapidly false claims evolve and how difficult it is to preemptively counter every claim that may emerge. It points to the potential for concise, scalable inoculation interventions to offer broader protection against misinformation.

While we found no evidence that the inoculation videos made participants more suspicious of accurate health claims immediately after receiving the intervention, at the 3-week follow-up the intervention group was slightly less likely than the control group to believe that smoking increases risk of lung cancer but that not everyone who smokes gets lung cancer. This result could indicate a small backfire effect of inoculation that should be considered in future studies and intervention delivery. One explanation for this finding, however, is that the claim included language that might have been seen to be consistent with the persuasion technique of impossible expectations ("increases your risk of lung cancer for sure"), which was the focus of the second inoculation video. The intervention group may have become more skeptical about posts that make absolute claims. Another explanation is that the inoculation videos' call to action -to trust only credible sources -may have led participants to distrust a Facebook post by "Slay Queen." Finally, there is potential that the intervention group found this claim suspicious given the true claim had nothing to do with the intervention or misinformation topic (the HIV vaccine). Our main survey results are consistent with findings of a recent meta-analysis, 12 which found that psychological inoculation improved the ability to identify manipulative content specifically rather than increasing generalized scepticism.

There are several mechanisms through which brief, engaging inoculation videos may have combatted misinformation. First, consistent with the inoculation (prebunking) approach and given the HIV vaccine is not available yet, participants most likely had not yet formed strong opinions or preferences related to the HIV vaccine nor had been exposed to many claims about it. This highlights the importance of getting out ahead of misinformation when a new health technology is being introduced. Second, the protection/immunity found in our study beyond the specific claims we inoculated against is consistent with the theoretical proposition that inoculation against underlying argumentative tactics, rather than against specific factual assertions, can build a broader cognitive immune response. 12 Third, the intervention videos used a familiar, highly engaging social-media format and incorporated multiple behavioural insights to increase attention, salience, and relatability. This points to the importance of ensuring the potency and relatability of brief video interventions like ours to enable broad dissemination.

To our knowledge, this is the first psychological inoculation study to focus specifically on HIV prevention in sub-Saharan Africa -an important context given the high burden of HIV and the rapid diffusion of health misinformation through social media. Our findings contribute to an emerging literature on the effectiveness of prebunking interventions in mitigating the influence of misinformation, while extending this literature into the domain of HIV prevention in a middle-income country setting. Two previous studies have assessed the impact of psychological inoculation on condom use and disclosure among people living with HIV. 18, 19 While other inoculation studies demonstrating significant effects typically have more intensive and active inoculation (e.g., playing an interactive game), our light touch, passive inoculation intervention (watching two 2.5-minute videos) achieved similar results in a format that is easily scalable. Our results align with another study evaluating short inoculation videos: in standardized terms, our effect (Cohen's d = 0.33) falls within the range reported across the seven inoculation video substudies targeting misinformation more broadly (d = 0.28-0.68). 27 The durability of the intervention's effects over a three-week period also compare favourably to other inoculation studies, 10, 12, 20 and could potentially be considered an underestimation if the study debrief at the end of the main survey increased scepticism of misinformation among both the control group and treatment group.

This study has several limitations. First, we assessed participants' vaccine intentions rather than actual health behaviour. Because an HIV vaccine does not yet exist, measuring stated demand is the most feasible proxy for potential uptake, and the hypothetical context is valid for the intervention and control groups. The use of intention measures is common in online experimental studies, but the extent to which intentions translate into real-world behaviour remains uncertain. We do however know that the study intervention reduced how much people believed misinformation, and misinformation has been found to be associated with reduced vaccine uptake. 5, 28 Second, participants were recruited through an online research platform and may not fully reflect the broader population of adolescent girls and young women in South Africa. Although nearly all participants were daily social media users the generalisability of our findings to offline or harder-to-reach populations requires further study. Our findings may nonetheless apply more broadly as psychological inoculation effects have been found to be similar across education levels. 29 In addition, we found that our intervention had similar effects on HIV-vaccine intentions among those residing in an informal settlement/township and those living elsewhere. This finding provides some confidence that results may apply to young women across a broader socioeconomic spectrum. We also found that 36.3% of our study participants reported a "moderate" or "great risk" of HIV infection, indicating our sample is the population of interest when rolling out HIV prevention services. Third, we cannot isolate the effect of the inoculation videos completely from the effect of the other intervention components. One concern with the main study findings is that this could be driven by intervention participants answering the quiz questions incorrectly and being asked to rewatch the video. We are confident that the effects found in the main survey are caused by the intervention given that only 29 participants were asked to rewatch the video, and that the main survey results are robust to the exclusion of these participants. The identity prime at the end of the main survey and SMS reminder before the follow-up might have boosted the intervention effect slightly at follow-up. While future research should isolate the inoculation impact, it is likely that nudges such as these have relatively small effects in comparison to the main intervention. Fourth, our follow-up period was limited to approximately three weeks (which is longer than many inoculation studies that do not include a follow-up). While we observed encouraging durability over this interval, longer-term assessments are needed to determine whether the protective effects of inoculation persist as misinformation evolves. Fifth, while the control group received the same information about the HIV vaccine as provided to the intervention group in the inoculation videos -any HIV vaccine available to the public in the future would have been tested and found to be safe and effective -it is possible that the results could, in part, be explained by demand-side characteristics. Sixth, we do not know whether our results would generalise to other country contexts with lower prevalence of HIV.

Although an HIV vaccine is not yet available, there are ongoing efforts to develop a safe and effective vaccine. Equipping people with the ability to detect and reject false claims before health products become widely available is essential for ensuring high demand, given mounting evidence that debunking misinformation after it has spread is much more difficult and often less effective. 30 Our findings are also extremely timely in light of the imminent rollout of lenacapavir, a groundbreaking long-acting medication for HIV prevention that is very likely to be the subject of considerable misinformation about its safety, biological mechanisms, and the populations for which it is intended.
