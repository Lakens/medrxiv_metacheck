Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

It failed to detect 27 statements. Thus, among all statements flagged by the module, 63% were genuine cases (positive predictive value). However, the module missed 42% of all true statements (false negative rate).

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Introduction

Electronic health records (EHRs) have become a cornerstone of modern healthcare delivery, serving both as repositories of patient information and as tools to improve care coordination, quality of care, and patient outcomes. Documentation within EHRs typically falls along a spectrum, from structured (coded fields, templates, and standardized terminologies) to unstructured (narrative free text, dictated notes, and clinician comments).

Understanding the strengths, limitations, and interplay of these two forms is critical for optimizing data usability, supporting clinical workflow, and enabling secondary uses such as research, quality measurement, and public health surveillance.

Structured documentation 1 refers to data recorded in pre-defined formats such as drop-down menus, checkboxes, and templates, often using standardized terminologies such as SNOMED CT 2 , the Systematized Nomenclature for Dental Diagnostic System (SNODDS) 3 , ICD 4 , and CDT. Because these elements are coded and standardized, they reduce variability and can be readily queried, aggregated, and analyzed; they also facilitate clinical decision support (CDS) and interoperability across disparate health information systems. These same features, however, carry costs. Recording findings into rigid templates can be time-consuming and forces clinicians to adapt their workflow to the EHR design rather than their own clinical reasoning, increasing cognitive load and reducing face-to-face patient interaction 5, 6 . Structured fields may also fail to capture the subtleties of a patient's condition, with important clinical narratives, rationales for decisions, and unique circumstances lost or oversimplified 7 . Unstructured documentation, by contrast, appears as narrative text produced through typing, speech recognition, or transcription 8 . It offers greater flexibility and expressiveness, often mirroring the way clinicians think and communicate, and supports the recording of subtle details, rationales, and complex decision-making that do not fit neatly into pre-defined categories; many clinicians therefore find free-text entry more natural and less disruptive to the patient encounter. These same narratives, however, are noisy and heterogeneous, and not readily machine-processable without natural language processing (NLP) or text-mining techniques 6, 9 . Variation in language, abbreviations, misspellings, and documentation style limits the comparability and reliability of narrative data, and the absence of inherent structure makes it difficult to de-identify, share, integrate, and repurpose across systems, impeding large-scale analytics and decision support 10.

Regardless of format, the secondary use of EHR data offers substantial potential to reduce research costs, enable patient-centered studies, and accelerate medical discovery 11 . This premise underlies the learning health system, in which data generated during routine care are translated into knowledge and returned to the point of care as improved practice 12 . Such a cycle is only as reliable as the documentation that seeds it: what is mis-recorded, or not recorded at all, at the chairside propagates into every inference the system subsequently draws. Yet this reuse faces persistent challenges, particularly around data quality and suitability for research: because clinical care and research have differing priorities, clinical data are often not recorded with the precision and rigor of data collected for research 13 , and prior assessments of EHR data quality have yielded highly variable results 14 . Among the dimensions proposed for evaluating suitability for secondary use, completeness, accuracy, and consistency are central 14 . Completeness refers to the extent to which the data required for a given purpose are present and adequately recorded, including the presence or absence of missing values 15, 16 ; accuracy is the degree to which recorded values truly represent the real-world events or conditions they describe 17, 18 ; and consistency is the degree of agreement among data recorded for the same patient across sources or formats.

Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) and natural language processing have markedly improved the ability to process and analyze unstructured clinical data, creating new opportunities to incorporate narrative information into research and, potentially, clinical use. Dentistry is actively building the infrastructure such a cycle requires, including standardized diagnostic terminologies, integrated diagnostic entry, and multi-institutional data networks 3, 19 . Yet these documentation trade-offs and their consequences for data quality remain largely unexamined in dentistry, where structured diagnostic terminologies are still relatively new. In this study, we comparatively evaluate the quality of structured and unstructured data within a dental EHR and assess its fitness for the secondary uses on which a learning health system depends, focusing on the dimensions of completeness, accuracy, and consistency. We use periodontal diagnosis as a representative case to evaluate the reliability of dental EHR data for the analytics, quality measurement, and decision support through which such a system closes the loop.

Research Interests

Using periodontal diagnosis as a representative case, and analyzing two academic dental centers in parallel to distinguish systematic patterns from site-specific ones, we asked whether structured (coded) and unstructured (narrative) documentation differ in three dimensions of data quality: • Completeness: Among encounters for which a periodontal diagnosis was clinically indicated, how often is a diagnosis documented in structured fields versus narrative notes, and does either format capture diagnoses more completely?

• Consistency: Among encounters documented in both formats, how well do the structured and narrative diagnoses agree, and when they disagree, in what magnitude and direction?

• Accuracy: Among encounters with a documented diagnosis, how often does each format match an expert gold-standard diagnosis, and does either format align more closely with expert adjudication?

Methods

Ethical Considerations

Institutional Review Board approval was obtained. Given its retrospective design using pre-existing data, a waiver of patient informed consent was granted. All data were deidentified, stored on secured institutional servers, and accessible only to authorized study personnel.

Study Design and Setting

This retrospective study was conducted at two academic dental institutions that share the same EHR platform (axiUm, Exan, Vancouver, Canada) and the same integrated diagnostic terminology, the Systematized Nomenclature for Dental Diagnostic System. The primary objective was to compare the completeness and accuracy of periodontal diagnoses recorded in structured EHR fields against those documented as unstructured free text in the progress notes. Where a diagnosis was recorded in both sources for the same patient at the same appointment, we additionally evaluated the consistency between the two formats.

The two sites were analyzed in parallel rather than against each other: instead of ranking institutions or testing for inter-site differences, we asked whether the same structuredversus-unstructured patterns recurred across independent settings on a common platform, since convergent findings would indicate systematic features of the two documentation formats rather than the idiosyncrasies of one institution. Our overall approach is summarized in Figure 1.

Data Source and Population

We identified a cohort of patients who received a qualifying periodontal examination during the study period (January 1, 2021, to December 31, 2021). Eligibility criteria included having at least one encounter in which a periodontal evaluation or treatment was documented. Cases were limited to adult patients with a minimum of 10 natural teeth present. For accuracy, cases additionally had to have comprehensive periodontal charting including pocket depth, clinical attachment level, and the distance from the free gingival margin to the cemento-enamel junction, and recent bitewing radiographs (within 6 months).

These additional criteria defined the gold-standard-eligible subset used for the accuracy analysis, which was necessarily smaller than the full completeness cohort.

Structured Data Extraction

Structured data pertaining to periodontal diagnoses were extracted using SQL scripts that queried specific EHR fields and/or coded diagnoses SNODDS -that uses the American Academy of Periodontology 2017 World Workshop https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/edentulism diagnostic criteria 20.

Our multidisciplinary team of clinicians, informaticians, and data specialists reviewed the EHR data model to pinpoint fields capturing structured periodontal diagnosis information.

Our team has extensive experience extracting data from EHRs and has iteratively developed custom SQL scripts. These scripts were validated through manual review by comparing query outputs with patient encounters in the EHR, as described in our previous work 19.

Unstructured Data Extraction and NLP Processing

For the same encounters, the free-text clinical notes associated with each visit were extracted to capture periodontal diagnoses documented in narrative form. Notes were preprocessed by removing extraneous formatting characters. To develop the extraction model, a representative subset of 45 de-identified notes was selected and manually labeled by the study team. Each labeled note then served as a template from which the OpenAI GPT-4 API generated 10 synthetic notes, yielding 450 synthetic notes for model training. Prompts were designed to preserve the clinical nuances specific to periodontal documentation, and sentence structure was varied to mirror real-world charting; all generation was performed on the HIPAA-compliant Azure OpenAI platform. These synthetic notes were used exclusively to fine-tune the RoBERTa 21 (base) model and were not part of the analytic cohort.

The fine-tuned model was then applied to the real clinical notes in the study cohort to identify and categorize periodontal diagnoses along the dimensions of stage, grade, and extent. Model performance was evaluated against manually labeled real notes, with full development and validation methods reported separately 22 . On the gold-standard evaluation sets from both sites, the fine-tuned model achieved weighted-average F1 scores of 0.84-0.99 across the five diagnostic dimensions (periodontal status, stage, grade, extent, and subtype), with the lowest performance on extent and the highest on stage, grade, and subtype.

Gold-Standard (GS) Review

A reference set was generated from a random sample (about 20% of the sampling frame). Two primary reviewers independently reviewed the included charts and then discussed cases in which their assessments varied to achieve a consensus in every case.

Both are clinician-scientists with an in-depth knowledge in the etiology and treatment of periodontal disease. They are certified by the American Board of Periodontology and have extensive experience (>15 years) in providing complex patient care and conducting clinical research as full-time faculty. The reviewer-assessed diagnoses thereby created a goldstandard dataset against which the structured and unstructured diagnoses could be compared.

Outcome and Statistical Analysis

We evaluated three dimensions of data quality: completeness, consistency, and accuracy. Because periodontal diagnoses are ordinal (health/gingivitis through Stage IV), agreement was quantified throughout with quadratic weighted κ, which penalizes disagreements in proportion to the square of the number of stages separating them, so that larger stage discrepancies, which carry disproportionately greater clinical consequence, contribute more heavily to the coefficient than single-stage discrepancies. a) Completeness was the proportion of qualifying encounters, those for which a periodontal diagnosis was clinically indicated, with a documented diagnosis in a given data source. It was computed separately for structured and unstructured data across the full qualifying cohort, and we cross-tabulated how often the two formats co-occurred (both present, structured only, unstructured only, or neither). b) Consistency was the agreement in assigned periodontal stage among encounters documented in both formats, reported as raw agreement (the proportion of concordant encounters) and quadratic weighted κ. For discordant encounters, we summarized the magnitude (single-stage versus ≥2-stage mismatch) and direction (whether the structured field assigned a milder or more severe stage than the narrative note) of disagreement. c) Accuracy was the proportion of documented diagnoses whose recorded stage matched the single expert-adjudicated diagnosis for that chart, computed separately for structured and unstructured data within the gold-standard-eligible subset, with 95% confidence intervals and quadratic weighted κ. Both formats were scored against the same fixed set of adjudicated charts, but a format could be scored only where it carried a diagnosis, so the two estimates drew on overlapping but not identical subsets. The formats were therefore compared as independent proportions, with the between-format difference in raw agreement reported alongside its 95% confidence interval rather than a paired test.

All analyses were performed in R (RStudio, Inc., Boston, MA).

Results

A total of 4,542 encounters met the inclusion criteria, comprising 2,055 at Site 1 and 2,487 at Site 2 (Table 1). A structured periodontal diagnosis was recorded in 1,650 encounters (80.3%) at Site 1 and 1,253 (50.4%) at Site 2, while an unstructured diagnosis was present in 1,736 (84.5%) and 863 (34.7%), respectively. The more complete format differed by site: at Site 1, unstructured notes carried a diagnosis somewhat more often than structured fields (84.5% vs 80.3%), whereas at Site 2 the structured field was the more complete source (50.4% vs 34.7%). The two sites also differed in how often both formats cooccurred: dual-documentation was present in 1,422 encounters (69.2%) at Site 1 but only 519 (20.9%) at Site 2. Single-format documentation followed the opposite pattern, being more common at Site 2, where 734 encounters (29.5%) carried a structured entry only and 344 (13.8%) an unstructured entry only, compared with 228 (11.1%) and 314 (15.3%) at Site 1. Overall, 890 encounters (35.8%) at Site 2 had no documented periodontal diagnosis in either format, versus 91 (4.4%) at Site 1.

Discussion

Our central question was whether structured (coded) and unstructured (narrative) documentation differ in completeness, accuracy, and consistency, and whether any such differences recur across sites. Three findings stand out. First, neither format was consistently more complete: which one more often carried a diagnosis varied by site, so the documentation discipline imposed by structured templates did not translate into a reliable completeness advantage. Second, two patterns did recur across both independent settings. Both structured and unstructured diagnoses matched the expert gold standard only about half the time, and when both formats were present they agreed substantially but imperfectly. Against this shared backdrop, narrative notes held a small, severity-weighted accuracy edge that was clear at one site and borderline at the other. Together, these results describe a hybrid data asset that is neither reliably complete nor consistently correct. Yet this is the very record a learning health system would draw on to generate knowledge and return it to the point of care. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first comparative analysis of structured versus narrative diagnoses within a dental EHR. Because we analyzed the two sites in parallel rather than against each other, our aim was not to rank institutions but to identify shared, systematic patterns.

A primary dimension we assessed was completeness, defined as the presence of a documented periodontal diagnosis among qualifying patients. Contrary to a simple "structured captures more" narrative, the more complete format differed by site: narrative notes carried a diagnosis slightly more often at Site 1, whereas the structured field was the more complete source at Site 2. Thus, no format was consistently more complete. What did converge across sites, and what we regard as the more concerning completeness finding, was outright missingness: over one-third of qualifying encounters at Site 2 had no documented periodontal diagnosis in either format. Where structured fields did outperform notes, as at Site 2, the mechanisms proposed in prior work, such as mandatory fields and guided templates that prompt clinicians to record essential data points, offer a plausible explanation 23, 24 . Such prompts can be beneficial in busy clinical settings by reducing inadvertent omissions, but our data indicate they do not guarantee capture and do not confer a uniform completeness advantage across institutions.

In terms of accuracy, unstructured notes, when present, were numerically more accurate than structured fields at both sites, but this directional advantage warrants caution: the raw differences were modest and non-significant at either site, though clearer on the severity-weighted κ. This is consistent with the view that rigid predefined fields lead clinicians to select the "closest fit" rather than the most precise descriptor, or to "checklist fatigue" in which fields are populated without capturing clinical nuance 25, 26.

Correspondingly, the coded field more often carried the milder stage when formats disagreed. Crucially, absolute accuracy was modest for both sources (all estimates near 50%), pointing to a challenge in translating clinical assessment into recorded data regardless of format. This may partly reflect the learning curve of the periodontal classification introduced only a few years earlier 27 , an interpretation supported by a contemporaneous study reporting comparable accuracy 28.

Lastly, we evaluated consistency, defined as agreement between structured and unstructured diagnoses for the same encounter when both were present. Agreement was substantial but imperfect, and lower at Site 2 than at Site 1. Two features of the disagreements are more informative than the overall agreement rate. First, discordance was more often large than small: among dual-documented encounters, more differed by two or more stages than by a single adjacent stage. Second, disagreement was directional; the structured field assigned a milder stage than the narrative note in roughly two-thirds of discordant encounters. In other words, when the two sources diverged, they tended to diverge by a clinically meaningful margin, and the coded field was the milder of the two in about two-thirds of those cases. We did not adjudicate discordant encounters individually against the gold standard, so we cannot attribute these mismatches to either source; given that absolute accuracy was near 50% for both formats, some discordant pairs likely contain no correct value at all. The directional asymmetry is nonetheless consequential: analytics, population health surveillance, and CDS that rely predominantly on coded data will inherit a coded stage that is systematically the milder of the two available records, and because larger stage discrepancies carry disproportionately greater clinical consequence, this skew is a direct threat regardless of which source is nearer the truth. Current EHR designs often physically separate structured entry points from narrative note sections, which may contribute to clinicians inadvertently misaligning information between the two. While some might argue that redundant dual entry could serve as a dataquality check, the additional burden on clinicians already facing significant workloads is a serious concern that can contribute to burnout 29 . These challenges underscore the need for more integrated and intelligent EHR documentation workflows, interventions at the practice end of the cycle, aimed at the point where knowledge is meant to re-enter care. Future systems could more seamlessly blend structured and unstructured entry. Dynamic templates 30 could guide clinicians through comprehensive periodontal assessments, incorporating parameters such as pocket depth, clinical attachment level, and radiographic findings, while offering embedded narrative fields within the same interface. Real-time NLP could parse free-text entries, identify key clinical concepts including diagnoses, and flag discrepancies between the narrative and structured fields for immediate reconciliation. This capability speaks directly to our central discordance finding: because coded fields so often diverged from notes, and diverged by two or more stages in more than half of discordant cases, an NLP-driven check is well positioned to surface precisely the mismatches we observed. For example, if a provider types "Stage III, Grade B periodontitis" in a note but selects a milder or less specific code in a dropdown, an alert could surface the conflict at the point of care, where the clinician, who alone has the patient in front of them, can resolve it. Such reconciliation would improve internal consistency directly; whether it also improves accuracy depends on the clinician resolving the conflict correctly, which our data cannot establish. Semantic enrichment of narrative text with standardized terminologies such as SNODDS or SNOMED CT can further bridge narrative richness and computational tractability, improving retrieval and interoperability.

Our study has several strengths, including the inclusion of two large academic dental institutions using the same EHR platform and diagnostic terminology, which allowed a focused comparison of data types. Analyzing the sites in parallel rather than against each other let us distinguish systematic patterns that recurred across settings (modest accuracy, directional discordance) from site-specific ones (which format was more complete), a distinction that matters for any institution deciding whether these risks are ones it can engineer around locally or ones inherent to the documentation formats themselves. The use of experienced, board-certified periodontists for the gold-standard review and the application of NLP methodology to systematically analyze unstructured text add further rigor.

We acknowledge several limitations. First, this was a retrospective study and thus reliant on the quality and completeness of existing documentation. Second, our findings are from academic dental institutions, and their generalizability to private practice or to institutions using different EHR systems may be limited; relatedly, the direction of the completeness difference diverged by site, so our data do not support a general claim about which format is more complete. Third, the use case was specific to periodontal diagnoses, and data-quality characteristics might differ for other dental or medical conditions.

Relatedly, discordant encounters were not individually adjudicated against the gold standard, so we can describe the magnitude and direction of structured-narrative disagreement but cannot say which format was correct in any given mismatch. Fourth, because each format's accuracy could be scored only where that format carried a diagnosis, the two estimates rested on overlapping rather than identical subsets of the same adjudicated charts and were compared as unpaired proportions. Combined with raw differences that did not reach significance at either site, the narrative advantage should be read as suggestive rather than definitive. Fifth, although our NLP model was fine-tuned and evaluated, all NLP models have inherent limitations in interpreting complex clinical narratives, and the use of synthetic data for fine-tuning, while useful for augmenting training data, may introduce its own biases. Finally, the gold-standard review, though robust, was based on a sample of the data, and expert interpretation retains a degree of subjectivity.

The distinct advantages and limitations of structured and unstructured EHR data necessitate a balanced approach. Structured data offer consistency in format and computability crucial for CDS and large-scale analytics, while unstructured narratives provide contextual richness often missing from coded entries. Our findings suggest that relying solely on one data type may yield an incomplete or inaccurate understanding of patient conditions. Hybrid strategies that leverage the strengths of both, supported by advanced informatics techniques such as NLP and intelligent EHR design, are therefore essential. Combining unstructured with structured data has already been shown to improve prediction-model performance relative to using either source alone 31.

In two dental centers, both structured and unstructured diagnoses matched expert adjudication only about half the time, and neither format was consistently more complete, with up to one-third of qualifying encounters at one site missing any documented diagnosis.

Narrative notes held a small severity-weighted accuracy advantage, and roughly one in seven dual-documented encounters (14.8%) held discordant diagnoses, with 8.4% differing by two or more stages and the coded field carrying the milder stage in 67.9% of disagreements. Improving data quality will require interfaces that marry the computability of coded entries with the fidelity of free text, ideally through integrated templates, real-time NLP, and, eventually, ambient AI documentation support. Until then, a dental learning health system will be learning from a record that is systematically milder than the disease in front of the clinician.
