Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

P-Value Text
p-value <0.05 Because the dependence between a proportion and its variance can produce funnel-plot asymmetry unrelated to publication bias, these tests were interpreted cautiously [9, 10], and a p-value <0.05 was considered evidence of asymmetry.

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 6 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Tuberculosis (TB) remains a major public health challenge in Cameroon, yet national evidence on treatment outcomes across the full spectrum of routine care is limited. This systematic review and meta-analysis estimated pooled proportions of treatment success, mortality, treatment failure and loss to follow-up (LTFU) among TB patients in Cameroon, and identified predictors of unfavorable outcomes. Methods: PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, African Journals Online and the Cochrane Library were searched, supplemented by Google Scholar and reference-list screening. Observational studies conducted in Cameroon reporting at least one WHOdefined treatment outcome were eligible. Pooled proportions and adjusted odds ratios (AOR) were estimated with random-effects models meta-analysis. Data were analyzed using R Statistics version 4.6.1.

Results: Thirty-two studies (25 cohorts, 7 cross-sectional), conducted between 1998 and 2022 across six of Cameroon's ten regions, were included. Pooled treatment success was 78.2% (95% CI: 73.8-82.1; 27 reports; 71,784 participants; I² = 98.2%), mortality 8.1% (95% CI: 5.5-11.7; 25 reports; 71,870 participants; I² = 97.8%), treatment failure 0.9% (95% CI: 0.5-1.4; 26 reports; 71,389 participants; I² = 89.1%) and LTFU 6.8% (95% CI: 5.0-9.1; 26 reports; 70,768 participants; I² = 98.6%). Mortality was highest in the South-West (16.2%) and among TB/HIV-coinfected patients (30.5%), and lowest among children (5.0%).

Treatment success was lowest in the Littoral (71.1%) and among TB/HIV-coinfected patients (60.8%) and highest among multidrug-resistant TB (89.3%) and pediatric patients (86.8%).

Treatment failure significantly declined from 2.9% before 2005 to 0.5% from 2020 onward (p < 0.001). Significant predictors of unfavorable outcomes were HIV positivity (AOR = 2.57; 95% CI: 1.86-3.55), previous TB treatment (AOR = 1.99; 95% CI: 1.40-2.84), male sex (AOR = 1.67; 95% CI: 1.45-1.92), age ≤40 years (AOR = 1.45; 95% CI: 1.06-1.98) and absence of antiretroviral therapy (AOR = 1.71; 95% CI: 1.15-2.54).

Conclusions: TB treatment outcomes in Cameroon remain suboptimal, with nearly one in five patients not achieving treatment success and high mortality among TB/HIV-coinfected individuals. Strengthening TB/HIV service integration, early antiretroviral therapy initiation, targeted support for previously treated patients and men, and improved retention in care are needed.

1. Background

Tuberculosis (TB) remains one of the world's leading infectious causes of illness and death, despite being preventable and curable when timely diagnosis and effective treatment are available. In 2024, tuberculosis was estimated to cause 10.7 million cases and 1.23 million deaths, including 150,000 among people living with HIV. Only 40% of patients with drugresistant TB received treatment, even though major advances since 2000 have saved 83 million lives [1]. The burden is distributed unevenly, with low-and middle-income countries accounting for most cases and deaths. Sub-Saharan Africa continues to experience a particularly high burden because TB transmission and outcomes are shaped by delayed diagnosis, poverty, undernutrition, overcrowding, HIV infection, limited access to rapid diagnostics, and weaknesses in health-service delivery. Despite regional and global advancements, the rate of decline is still too slow to meet the objectives of the Sustainable Development Goals relating to tuberculosis and the World Health Organization's (WHO) End TB Strategy [2, 3].

Cameroon remains an important setting for TB control in Central Africa. Available estimates indicate that TB incidence remained approximately 132 cases per 100,000 population in 2024, demonstrating continued transmission and a substantial need for diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up services [4]. The country's response is implemented through the National Tuberculosis Control Programme and a network of diagnostic and treatment centres [2]. However, treatment coverage, equitable access to diagnostic services, continuity of care, and detection of drug-resistant disease remain important programmatic concerns [5]. Additionally, Cameroon's 10 regions differ significantly in terms of geography, socioeconomic status, and health systems. As a result, significant differences between urban referral hospitals and decentralized treatment facilities, as well as between areas impacted differently by poverty, population mobility, insecurity, a lack of medical professionals, and disruptions in access to medications and laboratory services, may be hidden by national aggregate indicators.

Treatment-outcome monitoring is a core component of TB surveillance because it indicates whether patients who begin therapy are retained in care and achieve favorable clinical results. WHO reporting frameworks classify treatment outcomes using standardized categories, including cured, treatment completed, treatment failed, died, lost to follow-up, not evaluated, and treatment success, the latter generally combining cure and treatment completion [6, 7]. These definitions facilitate comparison across programmes and periods.

Treatment for tuberculosis that is successful lowers the chance of developing drug resistance as well as morbidity, mortality, recurrence, and continued transmission. On the other hand, the severity of the illness, delayed diagnosis, insufficient regimens, poor adherence, medication resistance, severe drug responses, or obstacles to continuity of care may be reflected in death, treatment failure, and loss to follow-up [7]. Age, socioeconomic position, prior TB treatment, HIV status, comorbidities, treatment support, and health-system capability all have an impact on these treatment outcomes [8].

The clinical form of TB is another potential source of outcome variation. Pulmonary TB (PTB), which affects the lungs, is the principal transmissible form and is usually evaluated through bacteriological examination of respiratory specimens, supported by clinical and radiological findings [9]. Extrapulmonary TB (EPTB) involves organs other than the lungs and may affect lymph nodes, pleura, bones and joints, the abdomen, genitourinary tract, or central nervous system. EPTB is often more difficult to diagnose because symptoms may be nonspecific and confirmation may require imaging, tissue biopsy, histopathology, or testing of paucibacillary non-respiratory specimens [10]. Evidence from Cameroon's Littoral Region has shown that bacteriologically confirmed EPTB presents diagnostic and therapeutic challenges under routine programme conditions [11].

HIV infection is particularly relevant to TB presentation and prognosis in Cameroon.

Immunosuppression increases the risk of progression from infection to active disease and is associated with disseminated or extrapulmonary forms, diagnostic difficulty, and increased mortality. Outcomes among people with TB/HIV coinfection may also depend on the timing and continuity of antiretroviral therapy, degree of immunosuppression, opportunistic infections, drug interactions, pill burden, and retention in care. A study in the Fako Division of Cameroon identified variation in TB treatment outcomes among people living with HIV and highlighted the importance of patient and health-service factors [12].

Drug-resistant TB further complicates treatment and threatens progress toward national and global targets [13, 14]. Multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB) is defined as disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis resistant to at least isoniazid and rifampicin-resistant TB includes any rifampicin resistance, with or without resistance to other medicines [13]. MDR/Rifampicin resistant (RR)-TB has historically required longer, more toxic, and more expensive regimens than drug-susceptible TB, although WHO now prioritizes shorter all-oral regimens for eligible patients. Nevertheless, drug-resistant disease remains associated with adverse drug effects, demanding monitoring requirements, and a greater risk of unfavorable outcomes. An individual patient-data meta-analysis of 9,153 patients demonstrated the complexity and generally poorer outcomes of MDR-TB treatment, [15] while a comparative cohort study found a markedly higher risk of death among patients with MDR-TB than among those with drug-susceptible TB [16].

Cameroon-specific evidence similarly demonstrates that outcomes among patients with MDR/RR-TB are influenced by both clinical and programme factors. A study of rifampicin-resistant pulmonary TB in Yaoundé evaluated unfavorable outcomes among patients treated with a standardized shorter regimen and identified locally relevant predictors of poor outcome [17]. More recently, a Cameroon-focused systematic review and metaanalysis synthesized drug-resistant TB studies and reported pooled estimates for treatment success, death, loss to follow-up, and treatment failure [14]. That review provides an important synthesis of drug-resistant disease; however, it does not replace the need for a broader review that incorporates drug-susceptible TB and directly examines pulmonary and extrapulmonary disease across the full continuum of routine TB care.

A systematic review and meta-analysis is therefore warranted to identify, critically appraise, and quantitatively synthesize available evidence on TB treatment outcomes in Cameroon. The review will estimate pooled proportions of treatment success or recovery, death, loss to follow-up, and treatment failure. This synthesis will provide a clearer national evidence base, identify populations and contexts associated with unfavorable outcomes, reveal gaps in the published literature, and support the Cameroon National Tuberculosis Control Programme and other stakeholders in strengthening diagnosis, treatment delivery, patient retention, surveillance, and resource allocation.

2. Methods

>Methods

2.1. Study design and registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [18]. The protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO identifier: CRD420261404490). Exclusion criteria: Case reports, narrative reviews, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, editorials, opinion pieces, conference abstracts, animal studies and in vitro or laboratory-only studies were excluded. We also excluded studies that did not report patient-level treatment outcomes (for example, diagnostic accuracy or drug-resistance surveillance studies without outcome data), studies in which outcome counts for the population of interest could not be extracted (aggregated data or percentages without denominators), and studies with corrupted or incomplete outcome data. Duplicate publications of the same dataset were excluded, and overlapping cohorts were handled as described under data extraction.

>Eligibility criteria

2.3. Outcomes measurement and definition

The primary outcomes were the pooled proportions of treatment success, death, treatment failure and loss to follow-up (LTFU) among patients registered for treatment. Outcomes were classified according to the WHO definitions [7]. Treatment failure was defined as a patient whose treatment regimen needed to be terminated or permanently changed to a new regimen or treatment strategy. Cured was defined as a patient with pulmonary TB with bacteriologically confirmed TB at the beginning of treatment who completed treatment as recommended by the national policy, with evidence of bacteriologic response and no evidence of failure. Treatment completed was defined as a patient who completed treatment as recommended by the national policy but whose outcome did not meet the definition for cure or treatment failure. Mortality was defined as a patient who died before starting treatment or during the course of treatment. Lost to follow-up (LTFU) was defined as a patient who did not start treatment or whose treatment was interrupted for two consecutive months or more. Treatment success was defined as the sum of all patients cured and treatment completed. The proportion for each outcome was the number of patients with the outcome divided by the number of patients registered for treatment in the study. The secondary outcome was the association between patient characteristics and an unfavorable outcome, defined as death, treatment failure or LTFU [19] [20] [21].

2.4. Search strategy

A comprehensive search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, African Journals Online (AJOL) and the Cochrane Library. Grey literature was searched on Google Scholar, and the reference lists of all included studies were hand-searched. The strategy combined controlled vocabulary (MeSH and Emtree) and free-text terms with Boolean operators around three concepts (tuberculosis, treatment outcome and Cameroon), without restriction on the type of tuberculosis. The PubMed strategy was: ("tuberculosis" [tiab] OR "TB"[tiab] OR "Mycobacterium tuberculosis"[tiab]) AND ("treatment outcome"[tiab] OR "treatment success"[tiab] OR "cure"[tiab] OR "treatment completion"[tiab] OR "mortality" [tiab] OR "death"[tiab] OR "loss to follow-up"[tiab] OR "default"[tiab] OR "treatment failure"[tiab]) AND ("Cameroon" [tiab]). The full strategy for each database is presented in the Supplementary Materials (Table S1). The last search was performed on April 15, 2026.

All retrieved records were deduplicated and then screened against the eligibility criteria. Full texts of all potentially eligible articles were independently screened by two reviewers (FZLC and RT). Disagreements were resolved through discussion or by consulting a third reviewer (JTZ).

2.5. Data extraction

Data were extracted independently by two reviewers (FZLC and JTZ) using a standardized form. For each study we extracted the first author, year of publication, year of study completion, design, region, participant group (unselected TB patients, previously treated patients, RR-TB, MDR-TB, TB/HIV co-infected patients or children), TB localization (pulmonary, extrapulmonary or mixed), treatment regimen (first-line or second-line), the number of patients registered for treatment, the number with each outcome (success, death, failure and LTFU) and the adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals for predictors of unfavorable outcomes. Studies are identified by first author last name and year of study completion, with a numeric suffix when an author contributed several studies in the same year. The year of study completion was grouped into five periods (<2005, 2005-2009, 2010-2014, 2015-2019 and ≥2020) that follow the successive National Strategic Plans against tuberculosis, and studies were classified as single-region (by region) or multi-region.

Disagreements were resolved through discussion or by a third reviewer (CA).

When several publications reported the same patients (same site and overlapping study period), or when one cohort was nested in a larger one, a single publication was retained, namely the one with the largest sample and the most complete outcome data, so that no patient contributed more than once to a pooled estimate.

2.6. Quality assessment of included reports

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed independently by two reviewers (FZLC and CA) using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklists matched to the study design : the 11-item checklist for cohort studies and the 9-item checklist for prevalence studies [22]. For cohort studies, the checklist contains eleven items assessing: the two groups were similar and recruited from the same population; exposures were measured similarly to assign people to exposed and unexposed groups; the exposure was measured in a valid and reliable way; confounding factors were identified and strategies to deal with them stated; whether the groups/participants were free of the outcome at the start of the study (or at the moment of exposure); outcomes were measured in a valid and reliable way; whether the follow-up time was sufficient for outcomes to occur; follow-up was complete, with reasons for loss to follow-up described and explored; strategies to address incomplete follow-up were utilized; and appropriate statistical analysis was used. Each item was scored 1 (yes) or 0 (no or unclear), and the overall risk of bias was classified as low (≥70% of items scored "yes"), moderate (50-69%) or high (<50%). Disagreements were resolved by discussion or by a third reviewer (RT).

2.7. Statistical analysis

Study-level proportions are presented with exact (Clopper-Pearson) 95% confidence intervals. Pooled proportions were estimated with a random-effects generalized linear mixed model (GLMM; random-intercept logistic regression with a logit link, fitted by maximum likelihood), implemented with the "metaprop" function of the "meta" package in R (method = "GLMM", transformation "PLOGIT") [23]. This approach models the binomial within-study variability directly and accommodates studies with 0% or 100% event rates without continuity corrections.

Heterogeneity was assessed with the I² statistic, classified as low (<25%), moderate (25-75%) or high (>75%). Because the between-study heterogeneity was high, the pooled proportion represented the mean of the distribution of true proportions across the heterogeneous studied settings and not a single common value.

Subgroup analyses were conducted for each outcome by study period, study design, region (each region or multi-region), participant group, TB localization and treatment regimen, and differences between subgroups were tested with the Q test for subgroup differences. Subgroup estimates were pooled when at least two reports were available, and categories with a single report are shown for completeness only. Subgroup analyses were exploratory and were not adjusted for multiple testing.

For predictors of unfavorable outcomes, adjusted odds ratios (AOR) and their 95% confidence intervals from multivariable models were pooled by inverse-variance randomeffects meta-analysis when at least two independent studies reported the same predictor. Reference categories were harmonized across studies (male vs. female; HIV-positive vs.

HIV-negative; previous TB treatment vs. new case; age ≤40 vs. >40 years; extrapulmonary vs. pulmonary TB; no vs. yes for co-trimoxazole prophylaxis and for antiretroviral therapy), and odds ratios reported in the opposite direction were inverted. Crude odds ratios and predictors reported by a single study were not pooled. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value <0.05. All analyses were conducted in R version 4.6.1 [24].

2.8. Publication bias and sensitivity analysis

Small-study effects were examined with funnel plots and with Egger's regression and Begg's rank correlation tests for outcomes with at least 10 reports. Because the dependence between a proportion and its variance can produce funnel-plot asymmetry unrelated to publication bias, these tests were interpreted cautiously [9, 10], and a p-value <0.05 was considered evidence of asymmetry. Robustness was assessed with a leave-one-out analysis.

This systematic review and meta-analysis brought together evidence from 32 studies 125 conducted in Cameroon between 1998 and 2022 to examine tuberculosis treatment 126 outcomes and their associated factors. The pooled treatment success rate was 78.2%, while 127 mortality, treatment failure and loss to follow-up were 8.1%, 0.9% and 6.8%, respectively. 128 Although some improvement was observed over the years, the findings suggest that a 129 considerable proportion of patients in Cameroon still experience poor treatment outcomes. 130 Important differences were also observed across regions and patient groups, with HIV 131 coinfection, previous tuberculosis treatment, male sex and absence of antiretroviral therapy emerging as significant factors associated with unfavorable outcomes.

Our finding of a pooled treatment success rate of 78.2% was consistent with findings from previous studies conducted across Africa. For instance, a systematic review and metaanalysis involving 31 studies and 18,194 participants from sub-Saharan Africa reported a treatment success rate of 76.2% [53]. Similar findings were also documented in a metaanalysis of 26 African studies [54], which reported a pooled treatment success rate of 79.0%.

Our findings suggest that the challenges affecting tuberculosis treatment in Cameroon are also being experienced in other African countries. However, our estimate was lower than the 83.7% reported in Ethiopia [53] and the global treatment success rate of 88% for drugsusceptible tuberculosis reported by the World Health Organization [55]. Our findings should be interpreted with caution, given differences in study populations and treatment regimens.

The results suggest that there is still room to improve tuberculosis treatment outcomes in Cameroon. Differences in access to healthcare, patient follow-up, treatment adherence and the organization of tuberculosis services may help explain some of these variations.

Mortality remains an important concern in this review, with a pooled mortality rate of 8.1% indicating that a substantial proportion of patients die during tuberculosis treatment.

The burden was particularly high among patients coinfected with TB and HIV, for whom mortality reached 30.5%. This was considerably higher than the 6.9% observed among patients in the general tuberculosis category. The finding is consistent with previous studies showing that HIV infection remains a major contributor to poor tuberculosis treatment outcomes in sub-Saharan Africa. An Ethiopian study reported that HIV-coinfected patients had nearly twice the odds of unsuccessful treatment compared with HIV-negative patients (OR = 1.98; 95% CI: 1.56-2.52) [56]. Similarly, a more recent meta-analysis found that HIV coinfection increased the odds of unsuccessful tuberculosis treatment by approximately 2.65 times (95% CI: 2.1-3.3) [57]. The consistency of these findings with our results highlights the continuing challenge of managing tuberculosis among people living with HIV and highlights the importance of integrating TB management into routine HIV care.

Several factors may contribute to the high mortality observed among TB/HIVcoinfected patients, particularly advanced immunosuppression, opportunistic infections and delayed diagnosis. A systematic review and meta-analysis conducted in Ethiopia found that patients with CD4 counts below 50 cells/mm³ had a significantly higher risk of mortality (HR = 3.37; 95% CI: 2.18-5.22), while the presence of opportunistic infections was also associated with increased mortality (HR = 1.75; 95% CI: 1.30-2.34) [58]. Similarly, WHO has highlighted the increased risk of death among patients with advanced HIV disease, particularly those presenting with tuberculosis and other opportunistic infections [59].

Patients presenting with advanced HIV disease may already have severe tuberculosis or other infections by the time treatment is initiated, further complicating their management.

Although our review could not establish the contribution of these individual factors, the findings highlight the importance of early identification of TB/HIV coinfection and timely initiation of appropriate treatment. In particular, the absence of cotrimoxazole chemoprophylaxis initiation emerged as a significant predictor of poor outcomes among TBtreated patients. Better integration of tuberculosis and HIV services, regular clinical monitoring, and continued access to antiretroviral therapy could therefore help improve survival among these patients, in line with WHO recommendations [59].

Furthermore, the Mortality rate in our study also varied according to the type of tuberculosis. Patients with rifampicin-resistant tuberculosis had a pooled mortality rate of significantly higher than those with multidrug-resistant tuberculosis. Although this difference may seem unexpected, it should be interpreted with caution, as the estimates were derived from a small number of studies involving different patient populations. Patients receiving treatment for multidrug-resistant tuberculosis may benefit from more specialized care and closer clinical follow-up, while differences in disease severity and treatment history may also influence outcomes. These findings should therefore not be taken to suggest that multidrugresistant tuberculosis carries a lower risk of death. Rather, they highlight the importance of considering patient characteristics and treatment settings when interpreting subgroup differences.

The pooled treatment failure rate was relatively low at 0.9% (95% CI: 0.5-1.4). A significant difference was observed across study periods, with treatment failure declining from 2.9% before 2005 to 0.5% after 2020. This finding is encouraging and may reflect improvements in tuberculosis management, particularly in diagnosis, treatment monitoring and the identification of patients who do not respond adequately to treatment. However, a low recorded failure rate does not necessarily mean that treatment outcomes are optimal.

Patients who die or are lost to follow-up may not complete the assessments required to determine treatment failure, potentially leading to underestimation. The higher failure rate observed among patients with pulmonary tuberculosis compared with those with extrapulmonary tuberculosis should also be interpreted cautiously, as monitoring treatment response in extrapulmonary disease can be more challenging. Differences in bacteriological confirmation and outcome assessment may therefore have influenced the reported rates.

Loss to follow-up was another important finding, affecting an estimated 6.8% (95% CI: 5.0-9.1) of patients. Although the rate declined from 14.6% in 2005-2009 to 4.9% in 2015-2019, it increased slightly to 5.7% from 2020 onwards. The overall pattern suggests that retaining patients in treatment remains an important challenge. Tuberculosis treatment requires sustained engagement with healthcare services, and patients may face difficulties related to transportation, treatment-related costs, competing responsibilities, and the burden of prolonged treatment. These challenges may be particularly important in settings where access to healthcare is limited.

Loss to follow-up was highest among previously treated patients, reaching 11.8%. This finding suggests that patients with a history of tuberculosis treatment may face additional difficulties completing subsequent treatment episodes. Such patients may have previously experienced treatment interruption, drug-related adverse effects, or challenges accessing healthcare. The findings are consistent with those observed in an African study who reported that previously treated tuberculosis patients had a higher risk of unsuccessful treatment than newly diagnosed patients (RR = 1.48; 95% CI: 1.14-1.94) [54]. Our analysis also found lower loss-to-follow-up rates among patients receiving second-line treatment (3.6%) than among those receiving first-line regimens (7.9%). One possible explanation is that patients receiving second-line treatment are more likely to be managed in specialized centres where monitoring and adherence support may be more intensive. However, differences in patient characteristics and treatment settings make it difficult to attribute this finding to the treatment regimens themselves.

Beyond the individual treatment outcomes in our study, the analysis identified several factors associated with unfavorable outcomes. HIV coinfection was one of the strongest, with HIV-positive patients having more than twice the odds of an unfavorable outcome compared with HIV-negative patients (AOR = 2.57; 95% CI: 1.86-3.55). This estimate was close to the findings from Ethiopia, where HIV coinfection was associated with an odds ratio of 2.65 [57].

The similarity between these findings strengthens the evidence that HIV remains an important determinant of tuberculosis treatment outcomes across African settings. In addition, patients who were not receiving antiretroviral therapy had higher odds of unfavorable outcomes (AOR = 1.71; 95% CI: 1.15-2.54). Together, these results reinforce the importance of early HIV diagnosis, timely initiation of antiretroviral therapy and closer follow-up of patients with TB/HIV coinfection.

Previous tuberculosis treatment was also significantly associated with unfavorable outcomes (AOR = 1.99; 95% CI: 1.40-2.84). This finding corroborated findings from an Ethiopian meta-analysis which reported approximately twice the odds of unsuccessful outcomes among previously treated patients (OR = 2.17; 95% CI: 1.55-3.03) [56]. Patients with a previous history of tuberculosis may be more likely to have underlying drug resistance, recurrent disease or difficulties with treatment adherence [13]. Although these explanations require further investigation, the findings suggest that previously treated patients should receive particular attention during treatment assessment and follow-up.

Male sex was another factor associated with unfavorable outcomes (AOR = 1.67; 95% CI: 1.45-1.92). Differences in health-seeking behavior, work-related mobility, social circumstances and treatment adherence may partly explain this association. In some settings, men may delay seeking care or encounter difficulties attending regular treatment appointments because of occupational or financial responsibilities. However, these explanations were not directly assessed in the present review and should be explored in future studies. Understanding the barriers experienced by different patient groups could help tuberculosis programmes provide more appropriate and targeted support.

Treatment outcomes also varied across geographical regions, with significant differences observed in treatment success and mortality. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution, as some regions were represented by only one or two studies, which limits the reliability of regional comparisons. More geographically representative studies are needed to better understand variations in tuberculosis treatment outcomes across Cameroon.

Finally, substantial heterogeneity was observed across the pooled estimates. Similar findings have been reported in previous African systematic reviews [53, 54], suggesting that tuberculosis treatment outcomes vary considerably between populations and healthcare settings. Differences in study design, treatment regimens, HIV status, disease presentation and outcome reporting may have contributed to this variation. Although the pooled estimates provide an overview of the available evidence, they should be interpreted in light of these differences rather than as uniform outcomes across all tuberculosis treatment settings in Cameroon.

5. Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, considerable heterogeneity was observed across the included studies, probably reflecting differences in patient characteristics, study settings, treatment regimens and periods of data collection. Although subgroup analyses were conducted to explore these differences, they did not fully explain the observed variation. The inclusion of different tuberculosis populations, including patients with drug-resistant TB and those coinfected with HIV, may also have influenced the pooled estimates. Another limitation was the uneven geographical distribution of the included studies. Some regions of Cameroon were represented by only one study, while others had no available data. This limits the extent to which the findings can be generalized to the entire country. Similarly, some subgroup analyses were based on relatively few studies, making their estimates less precise.

Most of the included studies were observational, which limits our ability to draw causal conclusions about the factors associated with treatment outcomes. Differences in how treatment outcomes were reported, along with incomplete data in some studies, may also have affected the results. Furthermore, funnel plot asymmetry and the significant Egger's test for treatment success suggest possible small-study effects or publication bias. Despite these limitations, this review brings together available evidence on tuberculosis treatment outcomes in Cameroon and identifies important areas where patient care and follow-up could be improved.

6. Conclusions

This systematic review and meta-analysis found that tuberculosis treatment outcomes in Cameroon remain suboptimal, with a pooled treatment success rate of 78.2%, mortality of 8.1%, treatment failure of 0.9% and loss to follow-up of 6.8%. Although some improvements were observed across study periods, considerable differences remained between patient groups and study settings. HIV coinfection, previous tuberculosis treatment, male sex and absence of antiretroviral therapy were associated with increased odds of unfavorable outcomes. These findings highlight the need to strengthen tuberculosis care in Cameroon, particularly through better integration of TB and HIV services, closer patient follow-up and improved treatment support for those at greater risk of poor outcomes. Addressing barriers to treatment completion, strengthening routine surveillance and improving the quality of care across different regions could help reduce preventable deaths and improve tuberculosis treatment outcomes in Cameroon.
