Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Alzheimer disease (AD) is characterized by amyloid-β plaques and neurofibrillary tangles of tau. Suvorexant, a dual orexin receptor antagonist, was previously shown to acutely reduce the ratio of phosphorylated tau-181 to unphosphorylated tau-181 (pT181/T181) in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). However, its effects on plasma pT181/T181 and broader proteomic networks remain unknown. Here, we conducted a secondary analysis of samples from a randomized, placebo-controlled trial of 25 participants receiving placebo or suvorexant 20 mg. Paired CSF and plasma samples were collected longitudinally via indwelling intrathecal and intravenous catheters. Plasma AD biomarkers were quantified by mass spectrometry, and proteomic profiling was performed using the SomaScan 11K assay on samples from hours 0, 16, and 24. Suvorexant increased pT181/T181 in plasma while decreasing it in CSF between 08:00-16:00. At the pathway level, suvorexant increased the extracellular abundance of proteins associated with translation, RNA binding, cytoskeletal organization and endosomal sorting, centrally at 16 hours and peripherally at 24 hours, and decreased cytokine and receptor-ligand signaling and serine protease activity in plasma at 24 hours. As plasma biomarkers are increasingly used for clinical screening and trial enrollment, factors that influence their levels, including medications, must be identified and their effects on interpretation understood.

INTRODUCTION

Alzheimer disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder marked by the accumulation of extracellular amyloid-β (Aβ) plaques and intraneuronal tau tangles, leading to synaptic dysfunction, cognitive decline, and eventually death. 1 Recent advances in biofluid biomarker development have enabled increasingly sensitive and specific detection of AD pathology using blood-based markers, complementing wellestablished cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and neuroimaging modalities. 2, 3 CSF biomarkers such as Aβ42, phosphorylated tau (p-tau) and total tau (t-tau) reflect key aspects of AD pathophysiology and are widely used in both clinical and research settings. These markers, however, require lumbar puncture and are not always feasible for large-scale or longitudinal studies. In contrast, plasma-based biomarkers, including Aβ42 and p-tau forms, are minimally invasive and reach a level of analytical performance that allows for reliable detection of early pathological changes in AD. 4, 5 However, questions remain about the concordance and timing of changes in AD biomarkers between plasma and CSF, particularly in response to pharmacological interventions.

Growing evidence suggests the orexinergic system is a critical regulator in modulating

Aβ and tau dynamics. 6, 7 Orexins (also known as hypocretins) are hypothalamic neuropeptides that promote arousal and regulate sleep-wake transitions. 8 In animal models, orexin antagonism reduces Aβ levels and plaque formation while orexin administration increases interstitial Aβ levels. 9 Furthermore, blocking orexin also decreases tau-mediated neurodegeneration through reduced phosphorylation of tau. 10 Suvorexant, a dual orexin receptor antagonist (DORA), is approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of insomnia. 11 A recent randomized controlled trial in cognitively unimpaired adults demonstrated that a single dose of suvorexant 20 mg acutely reduced CSF Aβ levels approximately 10-20% and decreased the ratio of phosphorylated tau-threonine-181 to unphosphorylated tau-threonine-181 (pT181/T181), a site linked to early AD pathology, approximately 10-15% compared to placebo. 12 These changes occurred in the absence of measurable differences in total sleep time or sleep architecture, suggesting a direct pharmacodynamic effect of orexin receptor antagonism on CSF AD biomarkers. While these results provide compelling evidence that suvorexant modulates key AD biomarkers in the central nervous system (CNS), a number of critical questions remain such as suvorexant's effect on plasma AD biomarkers, the degree to which the CNS effects translate to peripheral compartments, and the molecular breadth of suvorexant's effects. In addition to understanding the effects of suvorexant on AD biomarkers, understanding the systems-level proteomic effects could reveal mechanistic insights into orexin modulation and inform future prevention strategies.

High-throughput proteomic technologies offer a powerful approach to characterize the broad biological effects of CNS-active agents such as suvorexant. SomaScan, developed by SomaLogic, uses modified DNA aptamers (SOMAmers) to quantify 11,000 aptamers with high specificity and sensitivity from small-volume plasma or CSF samples.

It covers a broad range of biological processes including inflammation, synaptic function, metabolism, vascular health, and phosphorylation cascades. Complementary to this, NUcleic acid Linked Immuno-Sandwich Assay (NULISA) is a bead-based, highsensitivity immunoassay platform optimized for low-abundance protein detection.

Together, these platforms enable accurate measurement of core AD biomarkers, deep profiling of known AD-relevant pathways, and the discovery of novel protein networks altered by suvorexant in both CSF and plasma.

This study tests the acute effect of suvorexant 20 mg on pT181/T181 and other AD biomarkers in paired CSF and plasma samples compared to placebo. The results will establish the degree to which peripheral changes in AD biomarkers reflect central dynamics, a critical issue for blood-based diagnostic development and drug monitoring.

Then, we will determine whether suvorexant 20 mg alters individual protein levels and proteomic networks in plasma and CSF relative to placebo. We aim to identify proteomic networks whose response to suvorexant may be compartment-specific or correlated with changes in p-tau levels. By integrating targeted biomarkers and untargeted systems proteomics, this study aims to develop a comprehensive molecular map of suvorexant's acute effects on the human proteome in both central and peripheral compartments.

Findings from this work will inform both the utility of suvorexant as a repurposed diseasemodifying therapeutic and the development of fluid biomarkers for early pharmacodynamic effects in prevention trials.

RESULTS

Differential Effects of Suvorexant on CSF and Plasma pT181/T181

Suvorexant 20 mg was previously reported to decrease pT181/T181 in CSF compared to placebo. 12 In plasma, pT181/T181 initially decreased in both placebo and suvorexant groups before rebounding after hour 6 in the suvorexant treated participants (Figure 1). The greatest group difference in plasma pT181/T181 occurred approximately 15 hours post-dose (12:00), approximately when the greatest group difference was seen in CSF. Changes in CSF and plasma were seen with the pT181/T181 ratio rather than individual pT181 or T181. There were no group differences in CSF or plasma T181, strongly supporting that suvorexant is associated with decreased tau phosphorylation occupancy at threonine-181 in CSF and increased occupancy in plasma. Further, there were no group differences in plasma unphosphorylated tau at threonine-217 (T217) which showed similar change as plasma T181 (Supplementary Figure 1). Plotting the area under the curve (AUC) of CSF and plasma pT181/T181 percent change from hour 0 between hours 12-20 (08:00-16:00) demonstrated a significant inverse correlation (r = -0.42, p = 0.039; Figure 2) indicating that as pT181/T181 decreased in CSF, the ratio increased in plasma. Unlike in CSF, plasma Aβ40 and Aβ42 levels did not change significantly after treatment with suvorexant.

The temporal pattern of biomarker changes between biofluid compartments suggested at least three independent hypotheses: 1) suvorexant increases clearance of pT181 from the central to the peripheral compartment; 2) suvorexant exerts different effects on tau phosphorylation in CSF and plasma; and 3) suvorexant alters peripheral processing of tau such that phosphorylated and unphosphorylated T181 are differentially changed in plasma.

To investigate these hypotheses and other potential mechanisms, proteomics was measured using SomaScan and NULISA. Although individual protein levels changed at both time points in CSF and plasma, none of these remained significant after multiple test correction using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure (Figure 3; Supplementary Figures 2 and 3). There were several individual proteins with similar changes by both assays at hour 24 in CSF (C-C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2), interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-8 (IL-8), C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 1 (CXCL1)) and plasma (malate dehydrogenase 1, peroxiredoxin 6, ⍺-synuclein (SNCA) (total), ⍺-synuclein oligo-SNCA (oligomeric)). C-C motif chemokine ligand 13 (CCL13), however, decreased in CSF at hour 24 when measured by SomaScan and increased when measured by NULISA.

Given that no individual proteins were significant after FDR-adjustment, we tested group differences in pathways at hour 16 and 24 compared to baseline in two complementary analyses. To visualize the data and provide additional biological context for the changes associated with suvorexant compared to placebo at each time point, pathway and process enrichment analysis was performed through Metascape for up-and down-regulated pathways, protein-protein interactions, and Molecular Complex Detection (MCODE) subnetworks. During this exploratory analysis, pathways were included if their unadjusted pvalue was < 0.05. Gene set enrichment analysis by the GAGE method 13 compared the mean log fold change (logFC) of expressed genes in each pathway with the background genes across three databases: Gene Ontology (GO)-Biological Processes, GO-Molecular Function, and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) Signaling and Metabolism. Then, the resulting enrichment statistic and its p-value were adjusted for multiple comparisons within each database, time point, and compartment. All pathways in CSF and plasma that were significant after FDR correction are shown in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2. Since GO terms have gene sets that overlaps, the number of significant terms exceeds the number of independent signals.

Suvorexant Alters Different Protein Pathways in CSF and Plasma CSF -Hour 16

Exploratory Metascape analysis of proteins with increased abundance in CSF in the suvorexant group at hour 16 compared to placebo identified clusters corresponding to cytoskeletal remodeling and cell motility (actin filament-based processes, regulation of lamellipodium organization, and spindle localization), synaptic and neuronal plasticity (modification of postsynaptic structure and synaptic vesicle endocytosis), immune cell trafficking and neuroinflammatory regulation (regulation of leukocyte cell-cell adhesion, positive regulation of leukocyte migration), and receptor-mediated signaling cascades (enzyme-linked receptor protein signaling pathway, regulation of G protein-coupled receptor signaling pathway) (Figure 4; Supplementary Figure 4). The protein-protein interaction network showed functional groupings with densely connected hubs corresponding to the cytoskeletal, synaptic, and receptor-signaling clusters. Four MCODE subnetworks were also identified: a GPCR-signaling module, an actin-regulatory module, a translation and RNAprocessing module, and a cell-cycle-associated module. Among decreased enrichment pathways in CSF at hour 16 (Figure 5), identified clusters included humoral immune response, enzyme-linked receptor protein signaling pathway, regulation of angiogenesis, and miRNA targets of extracellular matrix and membrane receptors. There were too few significant pathways to perform MCODE analysis of proteins with decreased enrichment.

After correction for multiple comparisons, 8 GO-Biological Process terms, 24 GO-Molecular Function terms, and 1 KEGG pathway were FDR < 0.05 (Supplementary Table 1). All were enriched among proteins increased in the suvorexant group. The most significant terms were RNA related such as RNA binding (GO:0003723), mRNA binding (GO:0003729), translation (GO:0006412), and others (see Supplementary Table 1). These enrichment terms are not independent and have marked overlap. Multiple terms related to structural integrity and cytoskeletal dynamics were increased: cytoskeleton organization (GO:0007010), cellular component biogenesis (GO:0044085), organelle organization (GO:0006996), cellular component assembly (GO:0022607), and actin filament-based process (GO:0030029). These enrichment terms overlap and indicate that suvorexant is associated with greater abundance of proteins involved in the maintenance and remodeling of cellular architecture in CSF. Cadherin binding (GO:0045296), cytoskeletal protein binding (GO:0008092), phosphatidylinositol binding (GO:0035091), and ligase activity (GO:0016874) were also significantly enriched.

The endocytosis pathway (hsa04144) was the sole KEGG pathway to reach significance.

There are several potential explanations for enrichment of the endocytosis pathway. First, neurons and glial cells rely on endocytosis for synaptic vesicle recycling, neurotransmitter receptor turnover, and clearance of cell-surface proteins. 14, 15, 16 Enrichment suggests active membrane remodeling within the CNS. Second, exosomes originate from multivesicular endosomes, 17 therefore enrichment of endocytic machinery may be associated with increased production of extracellular vesicles released into CSF 18 by choroid plexus epithelium 19 and neurons. 20 Third, altered endosomal-lysosomal pathways are observed in neurodegenerative diseases where clearance is impaired including Alzheimer disease, Parkinson disease, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis. 21, 22, 23 Fourth, enrichment of endocytosis may also reflect altered choroid plexus or blood-CSF barrier transport activity.

Although plausible, each of these mechanisms needs further study in preclinical models and human participants to confirm.

CSF -Hour 24

Metascape enrichment analysis of CSF proteins increased in the suvorexant group at hour 24 compared to placebo found clusters involving immune trafficking and inflammatory signaling (positive regulation of leukocyte migration, regulation of humoral immune response), vascular and endothelial processes (angiogenesis, VEGFA VEGFR2 signaling), and intracellular signaling and proteostasis pathways (regulation of catalytic activity, regulation of proteolysis) (Figure 6; Supplementary Figure 5). MCODE subnetworks included cell-polarity and adhesion, cell cycle, integrin-cytoskeletal, chemokine-cytokine, nucleolar/metabolic, and Rho-GTPase signaling modules. After FDR correction, however, a single GO-molecular function term remained significant at this time point: RNA binding (GO:0003723).

Of the CSF proteins decreased at hour 24, Metascape identified clusters associated with receptor-driven proliferative and cytotoxic responses (enzyme-linked receptor protein signaling pathway, cell killing, and cell activation), cytokine-mediated immune cell effector functions and phagocytic clearance (phagocytosis, cellular response to cytokine stimulus), and tissue morphogenesis and vascular remodeling (Figure 7; Supplementary Figure 6). MCODE subnetworks included collagen-integrin adhesion, MHC/antigen-presentation, interleukin-receptor signaling, chromatin-remodeling, chemokine module, and cell cycle modules. No pathway was significantly decreased in CSF after adjustment for multiple comparisons at hour 24. Therefore, suvorexant increased 34 FDR-significant terms in CSF overall with only one significant term at hour 24.

Plasma -Hour 16

There were insufficient enriched pathways to perform Metascape analysis in plasma at hour 16. However, 12 GO-Biological Process terms, 1 GO-Molecular Function term, and 9 KEGG pathways were FDR < 0.05 at this time point (Supplementary Table 2). The most significantly enriched terms in the suvorexant group in plasma at hour 16 compared to placebo were organic acid metabolic process (GO:0006082), oxoacid metabolic process (GO:0043436), and carboxylic acid metabolic process (GO:0019752). The constituent proteins in these pathways are highly overlapping and represent a single signal. Organic acid catabolic process (GO:0016054) and carboxylic acid catabolic process (GO:0046395), which have identical gene sets, were also significantly enriched. These findings may indicate: 1) upregulation of proteins involved in the synthesis, transformation, and catabolism of organic and carboxylic acids in the plasma of suvorexant-treated participants; 2) release of these proteins in plasma that are not used or needed.

Small molecule catabolic process (GO:0044282) and small molecule metabolic process (GO:0044281) associated with the turnover of low-molecular-weight metabolites were both enriched in the suvorexant group compared to placebo. Multiple amino acid metabolic pathways reached significance as well, such as alpha-amino acid metabolic process (GO:1901605), L-amino acid metabolic process (GO:0170033), proteinogenic amino acid metabolic process (GO:0170039), and amino acid metabolic process (GO:0006520).

Increased valine, leucine, and isoleucine degradation (hsa00280) and glycine, serine, and threonine metabolism (hsa00260) further support broad upregulation of amino acid catabolic and metabolic flux following suvorexant treatment. Carbon metabolism (hsa01200), pyruvate metabolism (hsa00620, fatty acid degradation (hsa00071), and metabolic pathways (hsa01100) were also significant. Peroxisome (hsa04146), an organelle central to fatty acid β-oxidation and reactive oxygen species metabolism, was enriched as was oxidoreductase activity (GO:0016491), which encompasses proteins that catalyze oxidation-reduction reactions.

In contrast with the CSF findings, the endocytosis pathway (hsa04144) exhibited significantly lower abundance in the suvorexant group relative to placebo at hour 16. While metabolic and catabolic pathways were broadly enriched, cellular vesicular uptake processes were relatively suppressed in plasma at 16 hours following suvorexant administration.

Plasma -Hour 24

In plasma at hour 24, Metascape enrichment analysis found an increased cluster in the suvorexant group compared to placebo involving cellular metabolic reprogramming with nucleoside phosphate metabolic process and carboxylic acid metabolic process as the top two terms (Figure 8; Supplementary Figure 7). A second cluster focused on protein homeostasis and intracellular degradation (regulation of translation, endocytosis, and autophagy). Others implicated vascular, immune, and stress-response signaling (VEGFA-VEGFR2 signaling, membrane trafficking, response to oxidative stress). MCODE subnetwork modules included a large ribosomal/translational module, nucleotide-metabolism, carboxylic acid metabolism, and cellular stress modules, and smaller nodes for VEGFA-VEGFR2 signaling, neutrophil degranulation, and oxidative stress response.

Of the proteins decreased in plasma at hour 24 in the suvorexant group compared to placebo, Metascape identified matrisome secretion, epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, and receptor-tyrosine kinase cascades, a developmental and morphogenetic cluster including vasculature development and tissue morphogenesis, and a cluster implicated in immune regulation and inflammatory signaling (Figure 9; Supplementary Figure 8). The proteinprotein interaction network organized the terms into a large central hub connecting cell activation, immune system development, cytokine signaling, and multicellular organismallevel homeostasis, with branches to enzyme-linked receptor protein signaling, vasculature development, NABA core matrisome, neuron projection development, cell-cell adhesion, and Hallmark epithelial mesenchymal transition. MCODE subnetworks included collagen-extracellular-matrix, Wnt developmental signaling, cell-surface receptor, MHC/immunerecognition, and G-protein signaling modules.

One hundred and thirty-five GO-Biological Process terms, 55 GO-Molecular Function terms, and 27 KEGG pathways were FDR < 0.05 in plasma at hour 24 in the suvorexant group compared to placebo. Multiple pathways linked to translation were significantly enriched in participants receiving suvorexant including: translation (GO:0006412), translational initiation (GO:0006413), regulation of translational initiation (GO:0006446), and others. Processes related to upregulation of ribonucleoprotein complexes, such as RNA binding (GO:0003723) and mRNA binding (GO:0003729), were also significant. Multiple interconnected pathways related to nucleotide biosynthesis and catabolism (nucleobase-containing small molecule metabolic process (GO:0055086), nucleotide metabolic process (GO:0009117), nucleotide biosynthetic process (GO:0009165), ATP binding (GO:0005524), nucleotide metabolism (hsa01232)) were also enriched. Additional pathways reflecting intracellular protein degradation, such as catabolic process (GO:0009056), proteolysis involved in protein catabolic process (GO:0051603), protein catabolic process (GO:0030163), and others, were increased. The ubiquitin-proteasome system was implicated through ubiquitin-dependent protein catabolic process (GO:0006511), ubiquitin-like protein ligase binding (GO:0044389), and ubiquitin protein ligase binding (GO:0031625). Autophagic pathways were also increased with enrichment of macroautophagy (GO:0016236), autophagy (GO:0006914), autophagosome maturation (GO:0097352), and autophagy -animal (hsa04140).

A cluster of vesicular transport pathways showed significantly increased abundance.

Endosome organization (GO:0007032), vesicle-mediated transport (GO:0016192), multivesicular body assembly (GO:0036258), endocytosis (hsa04144), among others, were enriched. Interestingly, endocytosis was decreased in plasma while increased in CSF at hour 16 but increased in plasma at hour 24. There were also multiple cytoskeleton remodeling pathways upregulated. Cytoskeleton organization (GO:0007010), actin filament organization (GO:0007015), microtubule cytoskeleton organization (GO:0000226), and several others were significant. Finally, protein quality control pathways were significantly enriched, including protein folding (GO:0006457), chaperone-mediated protein folding (GO:0061077), and response to heat (GO:0009408).

These gene sets overlap substantially. Mitophagy (hsa04137), protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum (hsa04141), glutathione metabolism (hsa00480), purine metabolism (hsa00230), nucleotide metabolism (hsa01232), and several metabolic pathways (metabolic pathways (hsa01100), carbon metabolism (hsa01200), pyruvate metabolism (hsa00620), biosynthesis of amino acids (hsa01230)) were also significant. Tau protein binding (GO:0048156) and phosphoprotein binding (GO:0051219) reached significance among molecular function terms.

Thirteen terms were significantly decreased in the suvorexant group in plasma at hour 24 and comprise two groups. The first is a receptor and cytokine signaling group: cytokinecytokine receptor interaction (hsa04060) was the most strongly suppressed KEGG pathway, followed by neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction (hsa04080), signaling receptor activity (GO:0038023), molecular transducer activity (GO:0060089), transmembrane signaling receptor activity (GO:0004888), signaling receptor regulator activity (GO:0030545), receptor ligand activity (GO:0048018), signaling receptor activator activity (GO:0030546), and cytokine activity (GO:0005125). The second is a proteolytic and matrix group: serine-type peptidase activity (GO:0008236) and serine hydrolase activity (GO:0017171) were both significantly decreased, as were protein digestion and absorption (hsa04974) and extracellular matrix structural constituent (GO:0005201). The direction of the immune signal at this time point is therefore mixed rather than uniformly increased: circulating cytokine and receptor-ligand signaling was suppressed, while the intracellular Fc-receptor and antigenreceptor effector modules noted above were modestly enriched and sit among the weakest significant KEGG terms.

Cross-Compartment Pathway Enrichment

Thirty-two GO and KEGG pathways achieved FDR < 0.05 in both CSF and plasma. All of them were significant in CSF at hour 16 and in plasma at hour 24 with only 1 pathway reaching significance in both compartments at the same timepoint (RNA Binding). All 24 CSF molecular function terms recur in plasma at hour 24, as do 7 of the 8 biological process terms; only mRNA metabolic process (GO:0016071) fails to recur. Endocytosis (hsa04144) was the sole KEGG term in this shared set. RNA binding (GO:0003723) was the only term significant in three of the four comparisons (CSF hour 16, CSF hour 24, plasma hour 24).

DISCUSSION

This study found that suvorexant increases plasma pT181/T181 and peaks approximately 15 hours post-dose. In contrast, we previously reported that suvorexant decreases CSF pT181/T181. In CSF, every FDR-significant pathway at both hours 16 and 24 was enriched among proteins that increased in response to orexin antagonism; no pathway was significantly decreased centrally at either time point. The CSF signature at hour 16 consisted of increased RNA and nucleotide binding, translation, cytoskeletal organization, cadherin and phosphatidylinositol binding, guanyl-nucleotide and G-protein modules, and KEGG endocytosis; at hour 24 only RNA binding remained significant. In plasma, hour 16 was dominated by hepatic and catabolic metabolism pathways, and hour 24 carried the great majority of the study's significant terms, comprising translation and RNA handling, nucleotide metabolism, ubiquitin-proteasome and autophagic catabolism, endosomal and multivesicular body sorting, cytoskeletal organization and chaperone-mediated folding, together with a smaller set of significantly decreased terms covering cytokine and receptor-ligand signaling, serine protease activity and extracellular matrix constituents.

The divergent CSF and plasma pT181/T181 trajectories are consistent with altered intercompartmental handling of phosphorylated tau, but the present data cannot distinguish selective CNS-to-blood export from altered peripheral clearance, differential tau processing, assay-related effects, or compartment-specific changes in phosphorylation. We hypothesize that selective export from the central to the peripheral compartment is one mechanism to explain the increase in plasma pT181/T181. The absence of a significant effect on plasma Aβ40, Aβ42, or unphosphorylated T181 and T217 argues against a simple increase in bulk transfer of CNS-derived species into blood since a clearance effect of that kind should also affect Aβ and unphosphorylated tau.

Further, the CSF pool of tau is approximately 20-fold more concentrated than the plasma pool. 24 Therefore, exporting a smaller relative fraction of CSF p-tau would be predicted to increase the plasma p-tau pool by a greater relative amount. Proteins are cleared from CSF by several routes: bulk outflow via glymphatic exchange along perineural and dural lymphatic pathways to cervical lymph nodes and blood, 25, 26, 27, 28 receptor-mediated transport at the blood-CSF and blood-brain barriers for specific proteins, 29, 30 and local proteolytic degradation. 31 However, our data cannot measure any of these processes directly.

There are alternative hypotheses, however, including reduced peripheral proteolytic capacity for phosphorylated tau and/or central versus peripheral differences in kinase engagement with tau. Both alternatives are difficult to assess since the proteomic data does not measure activity or concentration, although pathways that would support these hypotheses also do not align with the biomarker changes. For example, tau is a substrate for circulating serine proteases, 32 and phosphorylation prevents cleavage with paired helical filament tau unless first dephosphorylated. 33 A fall in circulating serine protease capacity would therefore lengthen the residence time of the phosphorylated species preferentially, raising the measured ratio of phosphorylated to unphosphorylated T181 without any kinase acting on tau in blood. Although serine-type peptidase activity (GO:0008236) and serine hydrolase activity (GO:0017171) were both significantly decreased in plasma in the suvorexant group, this occurred at hour 24 rather than hour 16, when group differences in pT181/T181 were greatest. Multiple pathways associated with the ubiquitin-proteasome-chaperone system that degrades tau were also enriched at hour 24 in plasma but not at hour 16 or in CSF: modification-dependent protein catabolic process (GO:0019941), ubiquitin-dependent protein catabolic process (GO:0006511), regulation of proteasomal protein catabolic process (GO:0061136), and others. DORAs, such as suvorexant and lemborexant, reduce pT181/T181 in human CSF 12 and tau phosphorylation in transgenic mice. 10 In mice, lemborexant decreased phosphorylated tau and tau-mediated neurodegeneration via protein kinase A (PKA) and microglia changes. 10 The CSF signature, however, does not contain pathways directly associated with tau phosphorylation that reached FDR < 0.05 at either time point. In contrast to CSF, multiple pathways potentially related to tau phosphorylation were enriched in plasma at hour 24 but not hour 16. KEGG pathways, for example, containing the tau kinases were increased: 34, 35 neurotrophin signaling (hsa04722), ErbB (hsa04012), vascular endothelial growth factors (VEGF) (hsa04370), mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) (hsa04150) 36 and insulin signaling (hsa04910). The neurotrophin signaling pathway includes extracellular signalregulated kinase (ERK) kinases that phosphorylate tau at threonine-181. 37, 38 Again, the timing of these proteomic changes does not align with the group differences in pT181/T181. This study represents one of the first attempts to measure the effect of DORAs on plasma AD biomarkers and to map the proteomic effects of orexin antagonism across CSF and plasma in cognitively unimpaired adults. However, there are several important limitations. Suvorexant-mediated changes were measured over hours in a small cohort of cognitively unimpaired middle-aged adults. We identified several pathway-level changes significant after FDR-correction despite the limited power to detect individual protein changes. SomaScan and NULISA measure relative protein abundance, not activity or concentration, and both assays assess the extracellular compartment, so terms describing intracellular processes reflect the origin and annotation of the proteins detected rather than processes occurring in CSF or plasma. Further, measurement of additional forms of phosphorylated tau in CSF and plasma as well as confirmation of proteomic changes with dedicated mass spectrometry or immunoassays are needed. We emphasize that no published work connects orexin signaling to all of the mechanisms discussed and that the link from suvorexant to these plasma changes is inferred from the enrichment pattern and needs further testing. Given the rapid adoption of plasma AD biomarkers for clinical care and research, factors that influence their levels, such as medications like suvorexant, must be identified and their effects on interpretation understood.

METHODS

Participant Characteristics and Study Design

CSF and plasma samples collected from twenty-five participants aged 45 to 65 years in a previously published randomized placebo-controlled trial were included in the study. 12 Inclusion criteria included a Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score ≥27 (i.e., cognitively unimpaired), 39 absence of neurological or sleep disorders, and actigraphic sleep efficiency < 85%. Participants were randomized to placebo (N=13) and suvorexant 20 mg (N=12). There was also a suvorexant 10 mg group which was not included in our analyses. Participant characteristics are shown in Table 1. The study was conducted at the Washington University School of Medicine in St Louis, Missouri. The study protocol was approved by the Washington University Institutional Review Board. The Clinical Trials number was NCT03077620. All participants completed written informed consent and were compensated for their participation in the study.

Participants were admitted to the Clinical Translational Research Unit (CTRU). Both an intravenous catheter (IV) and an intrathecal lumbar catheter were placed. Collection of paired CSF and blood samples started in all participants at 20:00. Participants, research staff, and laboratory personnel were blinded to treatment status. CSF was collected every 2 hours for 36 hours and blood collected at the following hours was used in the analyses: 0, 6, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, 28, 32, and 36. CSF and blood samples were processed as previously described and stored at -80°C until analysis. 12, 40 After acclimation to the lumbar catheter and collection of the initial samples, participants received their first dose of placebo or suvorexant 20 mg at 21:00 (hour 1). The participants received their second dose of the same intervention from the first night at 21:00 (hour 25) on the second night.

The IV and lumbar catheter were removed on day 3 at 08:00. Participants had meals served at 09:00, 13:00, and 18:00. Polysomnography was performed as previously reported throughout each participant's admission to the CTRU.

Alzheimer Disease Biomarker Quantification

All CSF samples were processed and measured for CSF Aβ40, Aβ42, T181, pT181, T217, and phosphorylated T217 (pT217) as previously described. 12 Plasma Aβ40, Aβ42, T181, pT181, T217, and pT217 concentrations were quantified by C2N Diagnostics, LLC (St. Louis, MO) using the immunoprecipitation liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) assays. 41, 42, 43 Plasma pT217 was below the limit of quantitation in 64.6% of samples and was not included in the analysis. Plasma tau and p-tau could not be quantified in one placebo participant at hour 0 and this participant was excluded from the analyses.

SomaScan Assay

Human CSF and plasma samples from participants treated with suvorexant 20 mg or placebo from t=0 (20:00 on Day 1), t=16 (12:00 on Day 2), and t=24 (20:00 on Day 2) timepoints were assayed using the SomaScan 11K kit (SomaLogic, Boulder, CO), which is a high-scale aptamer-based proteomic platform that measures relative concentrations of ~11,000 proteins. These timepoints were chosen to capture both the greatest AD biomarker change (hour 16) and circadian-matched comparisons (hour 24) relative to baseline. CSF and plasma samples were available from 13 participants in the placebo group and 12 participants in the suvorexant group at hours 0 and 16. At hour 24, there was one participant in each group without CSF available for analysis. All CSF and plasma samples were analyzed per manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, we input 50 μL of plasma samples and prepared in 3 separate plate wells at 40%, 1%, and 0.005% dilution, respectively, and a single 20% dilution across all CSF sample wells on the SomaScan assay plate. Samples were hybridized into Agilent Surescan MicroArray slides and then scanned at a 5-μm resolution to detect CY3 fluorescence. Gridding and image analysis were done using Agilent Feature Extraction v10.7.3.1. Raw signals were standardized according to SomaScan. 44

NULISA

Ultra-sensitive quantification of 120 proteins in CSF and plasma was performed using NULISAseq CNS Disease Panel 120 (Alamar Biosciences, Fremont, CA), a bead-based multiplex immunoassay that amplifies detection signals via nucleic acid ligation and nextgeneration sequencing readout. 45 Plasma samples were available from all 25 participants. CSF samples from 12 participants who received placebo and 11 participants who received suvorexant 20 mg were available for analysis. NULISA minimizes assay background and increases sensitivity at the attomole level. Ten µl of samples was used at each time point for each participant and concentration is reported as NULISA Protein Quantification (NPQ) units. NPQ units are the result of normalizing the sequence quantification counts by intraplate and intensity variability and then are log2-transformed to approximate normality. 46

Differential Abundance Analysis

Vendor-standardized SomaScan signal data were subjected to differential expression analysis using the limma package in R. For each compartment (CSF and plasma), two comparisons were performed: suvorexant 20 mg versus placebo at hour 16 versus baseline (T16 vs. T0) and at hour 24 versus baseline (T24 vs. T0). A participant-paired moderated t-test with empirical Bayes shrinkage of variance was used to identify differentially expressed proteins. The limma package generates both nominal p-values and multiple testing-corrected adjusted p-values using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. Given that pathway-level genes tend to be co-regulated, proteins with nominal p < 0.05 were included in downstream pathway enrichment analyses to reduce false negative rates at the individual protein level.

Network and Pathway Analysis

Upregulated and downregulated protein gene lists based on the SomaScan data were processed through the web interface Metascape for the gene ontology, biological function, and pathway analyses. The Metascape utilizes the well-adopted hypergeometric test to identify statistically enriched ontology terms. Significant terms were defined as those with a minimum count of three genes and an enrichment factor more than 1.5 (i.e., the ratio between the observed counts and the counts expected by chance) at p < 0.05 or Benjamini-Hochberg FDR corrected p < 0.05. The Metascape also uses Kappa similarity algorithm to absorb most redundancies of biological terms into representative clusters. Clusters were named based on the most statistically significant term within the cluster. Analysis in Metascape provided the top upregulated and downregulated gene ontology clusters with their representative enriched terms for illustration and discussion.

To capture the relationships between terms, we selected the top 15 enriched terms from each of the 20 top cluster terms and rendered them as a network plot visualized with Cytoscape. The terms with the best p-values had no more than 15 terms per cluster and no more than 250 terms in total. Circle node color is paired with the enriched terms that represent cluster identity (text written in the same color in the figure) and node size is proportional to the number of input genes included in the selected terms. Terms with a Kappa similarity score > 0.3 are linked by a branch whose thickness represents the similarity score. Finally, we use STRING and BioGrid to reveal enriched protein-protein physical interactions. Protein interaction networks containing between 3 and 500 proteins were included and analyzed through the MCODE algorithm to identify densely connected network components. The three best-scoring terms by p-value were retained as a functional description of the corresponding components.

Statistics

Serial CSF Aβ, tau, and phospho-tau data were analyzed using general linear mixed models to account for dependencies among longitudinal measurements. The mixed model used a compound symmetry covariance matrix and was fit using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) as implemented in GraphPad Prism 8.0. Intervention group, time of day, and intervention × time interaction were treated as fixed effects; random intercepts and slopes accommodated individual variation. Plasma AD biomarkers (Aβ40, Aβ42, pT181, T181, T217) were analyzed using the same mixed model framework. Two-sided t-tests were used to test for group differences in individual proteins measured by SomaScan and NULISA. For SomaScan proteomic analyses, differential expression was performed using the limma package in R with a participant-paired moderated t-test and empirical Bayes shrinkage of variance. Multiple testing correction was applied using the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR procedure. Individual proteins with nominal p < 0.05 were used for downstream pathway enrichment analyses. For pathway analyses, statistical significance was defined as FDR < 0.05. For the primary outcome, pT181/T181, significance was set at p < 0.05.
