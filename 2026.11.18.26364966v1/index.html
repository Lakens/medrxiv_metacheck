Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

P-Value Text
P<0.05 Functional enrichment analysis was performed to characterize biological pathways and processes represented by proteins significantly associated with incident epilepsy in the Cox proportional hazards analyses (Bonferroni-adjusted P<0.05).

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 6 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Epilepsy affects approximately 50 million people worldwide, yet biomarkers capable of identifying individuals at risk before clinical onset are lacking, and the molecular mechanisms preceding disease onset remain poorly understood. Here, we integrated plasma proteomic, metabolomic, and genomic data from the UK Biobank to characterize molecular changes preceding epilepsy and evaluate their predictive and causal relevance.

We quantified 2,923 plasma proteins in ~53,000 participants and followed incident epilepsy for up to 15 years before epilepsy diagnosis. Cox proportional hazards analyses identified 116 proteins associated with incident epilepsy, led by neurofilament light chain (NEFL; HR=2.03) and growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15; HR=1.73). These proteins were enriched for extracellular signaling, immune regulation, and cell-death pathways and displayed distinct subtype-and age-specific signatures. A proteomic risk score comprising 32 proteins stratified epilepsy risk, with individuals in the highest quintile exhibiting a 3.5-fold greater risk than those in the lowest quintile (AUC=0.70). In parallel, 154 of 249 circulating metabolites were associated with incident epilepsy, with the strongest inverse association observed for citrate (HR=0.72) and positive associations across HDL-related metabolites (HR=1.16-1.21), implicating dysregulated energy and lipoprotein metabolism. A metabolomic risk score showed more modest discrimination (AUC=0.63). Integrating proteomic, metabolomic, polygenic, and demographic information yielded an overall AUC of 0.71 for incident epilepsy; in a separate cross-sectional analysis of individuals with earlyonset epilepsy discrimination reached an AUC of 0.79.

Proteome-wide cis-pQTL Mendelian randomization identified 12 proteins associated with epilepsy after false discovery rate correction, predominantly for genetic generalized epilepsy. O-GlcNAcase (OGA), a regulator of protein O-GlcNAcylation with established roles in neuronal function and synaptic plasticity, showed the strongest protective association (OR=0.71, FDR=2.7×10 ).

Serine protease 53 (PRSS53), a poorly characterized serine protease, showed strong evidence for a potential shared causal variant with epilepsy (colocalization PP.H4=0.95).

Together, these findings demonstrate that circulating molecular alterations precede epilepsy diagnosis by years and reveal complementary proteomic, metabolic, and genetic determinants of disease susceptibility, providing a framework for biologically informed risk stratification and therapeutic target prioritization.

Introduction

Epilepsy comprises a heterogeneous group of neurological disorders characterized by a persistent predisposition to generate unprovoked seizures and affects approximately 50 million individuals worldwide, contributing significantly to the global disease burden. 1, 2 Despite major advances in neuroimaging, electrophysiology, and molecular genetics, epilepsy diagnosis remains largely symptom-driven and frequently occurs only after seizure onset.

Early identification of individuals at increased risk of developing epilepsy could facilitate preventive interventions, improve disease management, and ultimately enable more personalized therapeutic strategies 3 . Consequently, identifying objective molecular biomarkers that capture biological processes preceding clinical disease onset remains a major unmet need. 4, 5 Epileptogenesis is a complex and dynamic process involving interactions between inherited genetic susceptibility, environmental exposures, neuroinflammation, metabolic dysregulation, neuronal injury, and alterations in neuronal signaling. [6] [7] Individual molecular layers provide only a partial view of the biological complexity. Recent advances in high-throughput molecular profiling now enable comprehensive characterization of complementary biological processes before disease onset. Circulating plasma proteomics reflects immune activation, neuronal injury, extracellular signalling, and tissue-specific responses, whereas plasma metabolomics captures alterations in intermediary metabolism, mitochondrial function, oxidative stress, lipid metabolism, and energy homeostasis. [8] [9] [10] Integrating these complementary molecular layers therefore offers a unique opportunity to characterize systemic biological changes that precede epilepsy development. [11] [12] [13] Large population-based studies have demonstrated that circulating molecular profiles can predict future disease years before clinical diagnosis. Plasma proteomics has successfully identified biomarkers associated with incident cardiovascular disease, neurodegenerative disorders, inflammatory bowel disease, and multiple chronic diseases, while recent proteogenomic studies have further elucidated the genetic architecture of the circulating proteome and its relationship to human disease. [14] [15] [16] Likewise, metabolomic profiling has revealed predictive signatures across neurological and cardiometabolic disorders, highlighting its ability to capture dynamic biological processes that are not reflected by inherited genetic variation. 17, 18 In epilepsy, perturbations of energy metabolism are biologically plausible because the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle intermediate citrate links mitochondrial function to glutamate and γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) synthesis, while lossof-function variants in the citrate transporter gene SLC13A5 cause developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (DEE). [19] [20] [21] Nevertheless, the circulating metabolome has not been systematically examined in relation to incident epilepsy.

Within epilepsy, biomarker research has expanded considerably during recent years.

Circulating neurofilament light chain (NEFL), glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), inflammatory mediators, and several metabolic pathways have been associated with seizure burden, neuronal injury, pharmacoresistance, and disease progression. [22] [23] [24] [25] [26] More recently, Zhang et al. demonstrated in a large prospective population-based study that plasma proteomic profiles are associated with incident epilepsy and identified molecular alterations preceding clinical disease onset, highlighting pathways related to immune regulation, inflammation, extracellular matrix remodeling, and cellular stress responses. 27 While these findings established plasma proteomics as a promising approach for epilepsy biomarker discovery, they also raise the question: which circulating molecules merely predict future epilepsy and which are likely to contribute directly to disease pathogenesis. Furthermore, the complementary contributions of inherited genetic susceptibility, circulating proteins, and metabolic alterations to epilepsy risk remain largely unexplored.

Human genetics provides an orthogonal perspective on disease biology because germline genetic variants are fixed throughout life and are therefore largely unaffected by disease progression or reverse causation. Polygenic scores (PRSs) aggregate the effects of thousands of common variants and have emerged as a powerful tool for quantifying genetic liability across complex diseases, including generalized genetic epilepsy (GGE) and focal epilepsy (FE). However, PRSs capture inherited susceptibility rather than downstream biological processes and their predictive performance depends strongly on phenotype definition, ancestry, and the underlying genetic architecture. [28] [29] [30] [31] Conversely, circulating molecular profiles integrate both inherited and acquired biological processes occurring during disease development. Combining these complementary layers has already improved prediction and biological interpretation in several complex diseases, including coronary artery disease and type 2 diabetes. 32, 33 Beyond risk prediction, human genetics can also prioritize molecular mechanisms. Mendelian randomization (MR) exploits naturally occurring genetic variation to distinguish proteins that are more likely to contribute causally to disease from proteins that represent downstream responses, while Bayesian colocalization (BC) evaluates whether protein quantitative trait loci (pQTLs) and disease-associated variants share the same underlying causal signal. [34] [35] [36] Integrating prospective molecular profiling with these genetic approaches therefore provides an opportunity to separate predictive biomarkers from genetically supported molecular determinants of epilepsy.

Here, we integrated prospective plasma proteomics, metabolomics, PRSs, and proteome-wide MR within the UK Biobank (UKB) to characterize molecular alterations preceding incident epilepsy. We first identified circulating proteins and metabolites associated with future epilepsy risk, then evaluated their predictive performance individually and in combination with inherited genetic susceptibility, and finally prioritized genetically supported molecular determinants using MR and BC (Figure 1). By integrating complementary molecular layers with human genetics, our study independently confirms and substantially extends recent prospective proteomic analyses of epilepsy by moving beyond biomarker discovery toward distinguishing predictive biomarkers from putatively causal disease mechanisms, thereby providing a comprehensive framework for biologically informed risk stratification, mechanistic discovery, and improving the prevention and management of epilepsy. circulating metabolomics, and genome-wide imputed genotyping -in epilepsy. The workflow proceeds through two linked stages.

In the association and molecular risk prediction stage, Cox proportional-hazards models tested circulating protein and metabolite levels against incident epilepsy, followed by construction and comparison of proteomic (ProRS), metabolomic (MetRS), and polygenic (PRS) risk scores for predictive performance. In the genetic causal prioritization stage, cis-pQTLs and metabolite QTLs served as instruments for two-sample Mendelian randomization (Wald ratio, with Steiger filtering and FDR correction), and markers surviving correction were carried forward to Bayesian colocalization and SuSiE-based multi-signal finemapping to confirm a shared causal variant between molecular trait and epilepsy risk. This staged design allows observational associations to be distinguished from genetically supported causal relationships.

Materials and methods

Study cohort

This study utilized data from the UK Biobank (UKB), a prospective population-based cohort of ~500,000 individuals aged 40-69 at recruitment between 2006 and 2010. Participants underwent extensive baseline phenotyping, including demographic, lifestyle, clinical, genetics, and molecular assessments, and were subsequently followed longitudinally through linked hospital admission records, death registries, and primary care data. All participants provided written informed consent. UKB received ethical approval from the North West Multi-centre Research Ethics Committee (MREC) and all analyses were performed under the relevant UKB application in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. 37

Definition of epilepsy phenotypes

Epilepsy diagnosis was defined using linked hospital records and self-reported medical history. Cases were defined by the presence of epilepsy diagnosis recorded using ICD-10 codes G40 and G41, ICD-9 code 345, or self-reported epilepsy (UKB Data-Field 20002, code 1264). 38 Generalized genetic epilepsy (GGE) was defined using ICD-10 codes G40.3, G40.4, G40.6, and G40.7 and the corresponding ICD-9 codes 345.0-345.3. Focal epilepsy (FE) was defined using ICD-10 codes G40.0, G40.1 and G40.2 with corresponding ICD-9 345.4, 345.5, and 345.7. Participants without any epilepsy diagnosis recorded at baseline and at any follow-up were classified as controls.

Definition of incident recorded diagnosis of epilepsy

The primary analyses focused on incident epilepsy. Incident epilepsy cases were defined as participants who received their first recorded epilepsy diagnosis after the baseline blood collection used for plasma proteomic and metabolomic profiling. Individuals with an epilepsy diagnosis preceding baseline assessment were considered prevalent cases and excluded from the primary analyses. This study design ensured that circulating molecular profiles preceded clinical disease onset, allowing evaluation of biomarkers associated with future epilepsy risk rather than established disease.

Plasma proteomics

Plasma proteomic profiles were generated within the UKB Pharma Proteomics Project (UKB-PPP) using the Olink Explore 3072 platform based on proximity extension assays (PEA), enabling multiplexed protein quantification of 2,923 unique proteins across approximately 53,000 participants. 34 Participants with > 50% missing protein measurements (n=3,553) and proteins >50% missing values (n=3) were excluded. Remaining missing values were imputed using the k-nearest neighbors (k=10) method with the R package impute. 13 Protein abundances were analyzed as normalized protein expression (NPX) values provided by UKB-PPP.

Plasma metabolomics

Plasma metabolomic data were generated using the Nightingale Health high-throughput nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) metabolomics platform, which quantifies 249 circulating metabolites spanning lipoprotein subclasses, fatty acids, amino acids, ketone bodies, glycolysis-related metabolites, and other intermediates. 18, 39 Participants with >50% missing in metabolite measurements were excluded. The remaining missing values were imputed using the k-nearest neighbors imputation (k=10). Metabolite concentrations were subsequently log-transformed to reduce skewness and standardized (mean = 0, SD = 1) prior to downstream association analyses and risk score modeling.

Prospective association analysis

Associations between baseline plasma protein abundance and incident epilepsy were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards regression models, with protein normalized protein expression (NPX) values included as continuous predictors. Hazard ratios (HRs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated for each protein. All models were adjusted for age at assessment, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, alcohol consumption, history of stroke, and the first four genetic principal components (PCs) to account for population stratification. To account for multiple testing across all proteins, statistical significance was determined using Bonferroni correction, with a Bonferroniadjusted P-value < 0.05 considered significant.

To evaluate whether identified biomarkers were also associated with established disease, we performed secondary logistic regression analyses of prevalent epilepsy, GGE and FE using the same covariates. Additional subgroup analyses stratified cases by age at diagnosis into childhood-onset (<10 years), juvenile-onset (10-18 years), and adult-onset (>18 years) groups. Associations are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs. Sex-stratified Cox regression analyses were additionally performed to identify sex-specific prospective associations.

Associations between baseline metabolite concentrations and incident reported epilepsy were assessed in the full eligible UK Biobank dataset with available metabolomics measurements, including 3,418 incident reported epilepsy cases, using the same Cox regression framework and covariate adjustment as the proteomic analyses. Bonferroni correction was applied across all 249 metabolites.

Construction of molecular risk scores

A proteomic risk score (ProRS) for incident epilepsy was constructed using Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression implemented via the glmnet R package. LASSO simultaneously performs variable selection and coefficient regularization, making it particularly well suited for high-dimensional molecular datasets by shrinking noninformative coefficients toward zero while retaining most predictive features. Prior to model fitting, NPX values were standardized (mean = 0, SD = 1).

The dataset was randomly divided into equally sized training and testing subsets. Cox LASSO regression was used, adjusting for age, sex, and the first four genetic principal components (PCs). Missing-value imputation and standardization were performed within the training data and cross-validation folds to prevent information leakage, and the optimal λ was selected using five-fold cross-validation. The final model was refitted on the training set and applied unchanged to the independent testing set to calculate individual-level ProRS.

A metabolomic risk score (MetRS) for incident epilepsy was generated using the same analytical framework. Standardized metabolite concentrations were used as input, the dataset was split into equally sized training and testing subsets, and the covariate-adjusted LASSO regression with five-fold cross-validation was performed to identify the optimal λ before calculating MetRS values for the independent test cohort.

Construction of polygenic risk scores

Polygenic risk scores (PRSs) for epilepsy were computed for UKB participants using the summary statistics from the latest genome-wide association study (GWAS Catalog:

GCST90271611) from the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) Consortium on

Complex Epilepsies (GWAS). 30 We used the ILAE summary statistics that did not include UKB participants in the meta-analysis, thereby ensuring independence between the discovery GWAS and the UKB target dataset and minimizing potential overfitting and sample overlap. PRS were generated using PRS-CS, 40 a Bayesian regression framework that infers posterior SNP effect sizes using continuous shrinking priors while accounting for local linkage disequilibrium (LD) patterns based on an external reference panel (1000 Genomes).

Compared with conventional clumping-and-thresholding approaches, PRS-CS jointly models genome-wide marker effects and typically improves prediction for highly polygenic traits.

The resulting PRSs were standardized (mean = 0, SD = 1) prior to downstream analyses and subsequently evaluated both individually and in combination with the ProRS and MetRSs.

Functional and pathway enrichment analysis

Functional enrichment analysis was performed to characterize biological pathways and processes represented by proteins significantly associated with incident epilepsy in the Cox proportional hazards analyses (Bonferroni-adjusted P<0.05). Gene set enrichment was performed using g:Profiler, 41 incorporating annotations from Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and Reactome pathway databases.

Enrichment significance was assessed using g:Profiler's default multiple-testing correction, and significantly enriched terms were ranked according to adjusted P-values. Significantly enriched pathways were ranked according to adjusted P-values, and the four most significant terms from each annotation database were visualized.

Genetic prioritization of putatively causal proteins

To distinguish proteins that may contribute causally to epilepsy development from proteins representing downstream disease-associated biological processes, we performed two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) using independent cis-protein quantitative trait loci (cis-pQTL) as instrumental variables.

Exposure data were obtained from the UKB-PPP, which quantified 2,923 plasma proteins in 54,219 individuals and identified 14,287 significant cis-pQTLs after multiple-testing correction. 34 Genetic instruments were required to satisfy the following criteria: (i) genomewide significance (P < 5 × 10), (ii) localization outside the major histocompatibility complex (MHC; chromosome 6: 26-34 Mb), (iii) independence following LD clumping (r² < 0.001), (iv) localization within ±1 Mb of the corresponding protein-coding gene, and (v) an F-statistic >30.

Epilepsy GWAS summary statistics from the ILAE Consortium 30 served as the outcome dataset. Because all proteins were instrumented by a single independent cis-pQTL, causal effects were estimated using the Wald ratio. Instrument validity was evaluated using the Fstatistic and the Steiger directionality test, 35 and false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied across all tested proteins within each epilepsy outcome.

For proteins remaining significant after FDR correction, Bayesian colocalization was performed using the approximate Bayes factor method (coloc.abf) implemented in the coloc R package. 36 Analyses were conducted within ±500 kb of the lead cis-pQTL using default prior probabilities. Posterior probabilities for hypothesis 4 (PP.H4) were interpreted as evidence for a shared causal variant, with PP.H4 ≥ 0.80 indicating strong evidence for colocalization, PP.H4 = 0.50-0.79 suggestive evidence, and PP.H4 < 0.50 indicating little or no evidence of a shared signal.

To account for loci containing multiple independent association signals, we additionally performed multi-signal colocalization using the SuSiE fine-mapping framework. 42 Exposure and outcome datasets were independently fine-mapped prior to coloc.susie analysis, and the highest PP.H4 across all credible-set pairs was reported as a sensitivity analysis that relaxes the single-causal-variant assumption.

Metabolite Mendelian randomization

To evaluate whether circulating metabolic traits contribute causally to epilepsy risk, we performed a parallel two-sample MR analysis using metabolite quantitative trait loci (mQTLs) as instrumental variables. Exposure data were obtained from a UKB NMR metabolomics fine-mapping study comprising 249 Nightingale metabolic traits, in which fine-mapped credible sets generated using SuSiE and supported by FINEMAP were represented by their highest posterior inclusion probability (PIP) variants. 43 Genetic instruments were required to reach genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10 8 ) and an F-statistic >10. Within each metabolite, correlated variants (r² ≥ 0.001) were pruned using a European 1000 Genomes reference panel, retaining at least five independent instruments per metabolite. GWAS summary statistics from the ILAE Consortium for all epilepsy, GGE), and FE served as outcome datasets. 30 Inverse-variance weighted (IVW) Mendelian randomization was used as the primary analysis, with MR-Egger, weighted median, and weighted mode serving as sensitivity analyses. Heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy were assessed using Cochran's Q statistic, the MR-Egger intercept, and MR-PRESSO. Instrument orientation was confirmed using Steiger filtering. False discovery rate correction was applied across all metabolites within each outcome. Because no metabolite remained significant after multiple-testing correction, colocalization analyses were not performed.

Integration of genetic and molecular risk scores

To evaluate the complementary predictive value of genetic and molecular biomarkers for incident epilepsy, we systematically compared ten prediction models incorporating combinations of polygenic (PRS), proteomics (ProRS), and metabolomic (MetRS) risk scores.

Ten logistic regression models were evaluated, including a baseline model containing age, sex, and the first four genetic principal components; models including each individual risk score; baseline-adjusted models; pairwise combinations of risk scores; and a fully integrated model combining PRS, ProRS, MetRS, and demographic covariates. A complete description of all prediction models is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

For analyses of epilepsy onset at ≤18 years, because UK Biobank blood samples were collected at baseline when participants were aged 40-69 years, the molecular measurements were obtained years after disease onset and were therefore treated as cross-sectional sensitivity analyses, separate from the prospective incident-epilepsy analyses.

Model discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) with corresponding 95% CIs, enabling direct comparison of the predictive performance of individual molecular layers and their combinations relative to the baseline model.

To evaluate clinical risk stratification, participants were classified into high-risk (>80th percentile) and low-risk (≤20th percentile) groups according to their PRS and ProRS. Cox proportional hazards regression was then used to estimate HRs and 95% CIs for incident epilepsy during follow-up.

Results

Cohort characteristics

A total of 49,461 UKB participants with matched plasma proteomic, plasma metabolomic, and genomic data were included in the analyses. During the prospective follow-up, 400 individuals developed incident epilepsy, while 609 participants had prevalent epilepsy at baseline and were excluded from the primary prospective analyses. Incident cases were older at baseline than controls (mean = 59.0 vs. 56.8 years) and included a slightly higher proportion of males (49.0% vs. 45.9%) (Table 1). Among incident epilepsy cases 55 (13.8%) were classified as generalized genetic epilepsy (GGE) and 31 (7.8%) as focal epilepsy (FE), while the remaining cases represented other or unspecific epilepsy subtypes.

Together, these findings demonstrate that circulating plasma proteins capture a broad spectrum of biological alterations preceding epilepsy diagnosis with evidence for pronounced sex-, subtype-, and age-specific molecular signatures.

Biological pathways underlying epilepsy risk

Functional enrichment analysis demonstrated that proteins associated with incident epilepsy predominantly participate in extracellular signaling, immune regulation, and receptor-mediated communication (Figure 2B). The strongest enrichments were observed for extracellular region and extracellular space components, together with signaling receptor activity and molecular transducer activity, indicating that early epilepsy-associated molecular alterations are largely mediated through secreted proteins and cell-surface signaling.

These findings are consistent with accumulating evidence implicating neuroinflammation, immune activation, and extracellular signaling as central processes during epileptogenesis. 44, 45 Rather than reflecting isolated molecular changes, the prospective proteomic signature therefore suggests coordinated activation of biological pathways involved in neuronal injury, immune regulation, and intercellular communication years before clinical disease onset.

Mendelian randomization identifies proteins associated with epilepsy risk

To distinguish proteins that may contribute causally to epilepsy development from proteins representing downstream disease-associated biomarkers, we performed proteome-wide twosample MR using independent cis-pQTLs as genetic instruments.

Among the 2,923 proteins evaluated, 13 protein-epilepsy associations, involving 12 unique proteins, reached significance after false discovery rate (FDR) correction, indicating that only a small subset of circulating proteins showed genetic support for a potential causal role (Figure 3, Supplemental Table S3). All significant associations passed the Steiger directionality test, supporting the inferred direction of causality from genetically predicted protein abundance to epilepsy risk. The strongest association was observed for OGA, where higher genetically predicted plasma protein levels were associated with a substantially lower risk of GGE (OR = 0.71, 95% CI = 0.64-0.78, FDR = 2.7 × 10). OGA also showed a protective association with the 'all epilepsy' group (OR = 0.80, 95% CI = 0.73-0.87, FDR = 2.2 × 10).

Increased genetically predicted levels of PRSS53, LGALS1, ITIH3, ITIH4, DNER, VAT1, and GALNT3 were associated with increased epilepsy risk, whereas ITIH1, PYDC1, ITGAM, and AARSD1 showed protective associations (Figure 3A; Supplementary table S3).

Overall, genetically supported associations were predominantly observed for GGE, consistent with its stronger genetic architecture and higher heritability.

To evaluate whether the pQTL and epilepsy GWAS signals were driven by a shared causal variant, we performed Bayesian colocalization (BC) analyses (Figure 3B). We observed a clear hierarchy of evidence across the genetically prioritized proteins. PRSS53 was the only association showing strong evidence for colocalization, with high posterior probability of a shared causal variant using both the conventional approximate Bayes factor approach (PP.H4 = 0.95) and multi-signal SuSiE fine-mapping (PP.H4 = 0.91). OGA and five additional associations showed suggestive evidence for colocalization (PP.H4 = 0.53-0.75), including OGA, LGALS1, ITIH1, ITIH3, and PYDC1 for GGE, and OGA for 'all-epilepsy'. In contrast, six MR associations (ITIH4, ITGAM, DNER, AARSD1, VAT1, and GALNT3) showed no evidence of a shared causal variant (PP.H4 < 0.50), indicating that the observed protein-epilepsy associations may reflect distinct variants in linkage disequilibrium rather than a common causal signal.

Together these findings demonstrate that only a subset of proteins associated with prospective epilepsy risk also exhibit genetic evidence supporting a potential causal role in disease pathogenesis. The combination of MR, directionality testing, and BC therefore distinguishes predictive biomarkers from genetically supported molecular determinants, providing a framework for potentially prioritizing candidate therapeutic targets.

Plasma metabolome captures prospective epilepsy risk

Consistent with the proteomic findings, prospective metabolomic profiling revealed widespread systemic alterations preceding epilepsy diagnosis. Among the 249 NMRquantified metabolites, 154 were significantly associated with incident epilepsy after Bonferroni correction (Figure 4; Supplementary Table S4), suggesting that metabolic dysregulation is detectable prior to disease onset. The significant associations predominantly involved pathways related to energy metabolism, lipoprotein metabolism, ketone body metabolism, and amino acid homeostasis, suggesting broad perturbations of systemic metabolism before epilepsy develops.

The strongest signal was observed for citrate (HR=0.72; P=1.41×10 172 ), a central intermediate of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, with lower circulating citrate levels associated with increased epilepsy risk. Several HDL-related measures, including phospholipids in very large HDL (HR=1.21; P=1.43×10 65 ) and mean HDL particle diameter (HR=1.21; P=1.30×10 62 ), were positively associated with incident epilepsy, whereas multiple LDL-and VLDL-associated cholesterol measures showed inverse associations (HR=0.91-0.94). Among the small-molecule metabolites, the ketone body acetone (HR=1.08; P=1.50×10 36 ) was positively associated with epilepsy risk, while the amino acid histidine showed a protective association (HR=0.87; P=3.48×10 31 ). Notably, the observed metabolomic signatures converge on pathways involved in mitochondrial energy metabolism, lipid transport, and intermediary metabolism, complementing the inflammatory and neuronal injury signatures identified in the plasma Two-sample MR analysis found no metabolite surviving FDR correction in any of the three epilepsy outcomes (Supplementary Figure S5).

Comparative performance of genetic and molecular risk scores

To evaluate the predictive performance of the different molecular layers, we compared models incorporating the polygenic risk score (PRS), proteomic risk score (ProRS), metabolomic risk score (MetRS), and their combinations (Figure 5; Supplementary Table S1).

Among the individual molecular predictors (Figure 5A), the ProRS achieved the highest discriminatory performance for epilepsy (AUC = 0.70, 95% CI 0.68-0.73), substantially outperforming both the MetRS (AUC = 0.63, 95% CI 0.61-0.66) and the PRS (AUC = 0.52, 95% CI 0.49-0.54). Incorporating demographic covariates resulted in only modest improvements in predictive performance, while the fully integrated model combining PRS, ProRS, MetRS, age, sex, and genetic principal components achieved the highest overall performance (AUC = 0.71, 95% CI 0.68-0.73). Prediction performance was substantially higher among individuals with epilepsy onset at ≤18 years (Figure 5B).

To further evaluate clinical risk stratification, participants were grouped according to their molecular risk scores. Individuals in the high ProRS category had a 3.5-fold increased risk of epilepsy compared with those in the lowest category (Supplementary Figure S6), whereas the PRS alone showed more limited discrimination between risk groups. These findings suggest that the ProRS may provide additional predictive value beyond common genetic risk for future epilepsy in this population.

Overall, the comparison of prediction models indicates that proteomic biomarkers provide the strongest prospective predictive signal in this population. Although metabolomic and polygenic scores captured complementary dimensions of risk, their addition resulted in only modest improvent over the ProRS alone suggesting that prospective plasma proteomics captures much of the predictive information available before clinical epilepsy onset.

Biological architecture of the proteomic risk score

Given the superior predictive performance of the ProRS, we next examined the proteins retained by the LASSO model to characterize the biological processes underlying its discriminative ability (Supplementary Table S5). Rather than relying on a single biomarker, the ProRS integrated 32 proteins representing multiple complementary aspects of epilepsy biology, including neuronal injury, glial activation, immune regulation, lysosomal function, cellular stress responses, and vascular biology.

NEFL contributed the largest positive coefficient in the model (weight = 0.443), consistent with its role as sensitive marker of neuroaxonal injury and its strong prospective association with incident epilepsy. Other highly weighted proteins included RNF149 (0.234), CPVL (0.140), CTSL (0.135), and VSIG4 (0.115), implicating immune modulation, lysosomal activity, and neuroinflammatory pathways as major contributors to epilepsy risk prediction.

Additional positively weighted proteins, including GDF15, GFAP, SPINK1, and ACE2, further highlighted the contribution of cellular stress responses, astroglial activation, and epithelial defense, and vascular regulation to the prospective molecular signature.

Conversely, RAB6A (-0.211), TNR (-0.156), and NRXN3 (-0.112) were assigned negative coefficients, indicating that higher circulating levels of these proteins contributed negatively to the prediction model. These proteins are involved in vesicle trafficking, extracellular matrix organization, and synaptic function, suggesting that preserved neuronal homeostasis may characterize individuals at lower risk of developing epilepsy.

Discussion

Using prospective multi-omic profiling integrated with human genetics, we demonstrate that circulating plasma proteins and metabolites capture biological processes preceding epilepsy diagnosis and provide information complementary to inherited genetic susceptibility.

Our findings independently confirm and substantially extend the recent prospective plasma proteomic analysis by Zhang et al., 27 which similarly identified NEFL and GDF15 as the strongest circulating predictors of incident epilepsy. Our study extends this work by integrating proteomics with metabolomics, polygenic score (PRS) prediction, MR, and Bayesian colocalization (BC) to distinguish predictive biomarkers from genetically supported molecular determinants and to evaluate their complementary value for prospective epilepsy risk stratification.

Consistent with both studies, NEFL and GDF15 emerged as biologically compelling biomarkers among the 116 proteins associated with incident epilepsy. NEFL is a wellestablished indicator of neuroaxonal injury 46 , and has consistently been linked to seizure burden 22, 23 , drug-resistant epilepsy 24, 25 , cognitive impairment, supporting the concept that neuronal damage is already detectable during the preclinical phase of epilepsy. 26 GDF15, a stress-response cytokine induced by mitochondrial dysfunction and cellular stress, has emerged as a promising biomarker across neurological disorders 47, 48 . Recent studies reporting elevated GDF15 levels in status epilepticus associated with fever 49 and in epilepsy patients with primary mitochondrial disorders 50 further support a role for systemic mitochondrial and cellular stress responses during epileptogenesis. Beyond these individual markers, the predominance of proteins involved in immune regulation, lysosomal biology, vascular function, and glial activation suggests that epilepsy development is accompanied by coordinated systemic biological responses well before clinical onset. The pronounced sexspecific signatures observed in our study indicate that biological mechanisms preceding epilepsy may differ between males and females.

The proteomics risk score (ProRS), derived from only 32 proteins, represented the strongest predictor of incident epilepsy, stratifying individuals in the highest quintile into a 3.5-fold higher risk group than those in the lowest quintile. The ProRS outperformed both the metabolomic risk score (MetRS) and the current epilepsy PRS, while integration of additional molecular layers provided only modest improvements in discrimination. Discriminative performance was notably stronger when analyses were restricted to individuals with earlyonset (<18 years) epilepsy (AUC=0.79). As molecular profiling in UKB occcured later in adulthood, this analysis should not be interpreted as prospective prediction of childhoodonset epilepsy. Rather, it suggests that the combined molecular and genetic profile more strongly distinguishes individuals with a history of early-onset epilepsy, a group expected to contain a greater proportion of genetic epilepsies. Importantly, proteomic and genetic risk contributed independently and additively, with individuals carrying both high ProRS and high PRS exhibiting the highest cumulative incidence of epilepsy. These findings are consistent with studies from other complex diseases such as stroke, 15 Alzheimer's, 51 and cardiovascular disease 52 , where circulating protein signatures outperform polygenic risk scores for shortterm disease prediction while capturing ongoing pathophysiological processes.

The substantially higher discriminatin achieved by the ProRS compared with the PRS should not be interpreted as indicating a limited contribution of inherited susceptibility to epilepsy.

The two scores capture fundamentally different dimensions of risk; the PRS represents lifelong inhertited susceptibility, whereas circulating proteins reflect dynamic biological states at the time of sampling and may capture age-related, acquired, and prodomal processes preceding diagnosis. The superior predictive performance of the ProRS should therefore not be interpreted as evidence that all constituent proteins are causal mediators of epilepsy.

Rather, circulating signatures likely integrate multiple downstream biological processes during the prodromal phase of disease. Our MR analyses support this interpretation by identifying a subset of proteins with genetic evidence consistent with potential causal involvement. Together, these complementary analyses illustrate how prospective molecular profiling and human genetics can distinguish predictive biomarkers from putative therapeutic targets.

To identify proteins more likely to contribute to disease pathogenesis, we performed proteome-wide cis-pQTL MR combined with BC and fine-mapping analyses. Twelve proteins showed genetic support for causal involvement in epilepsy, predominantly in genetic generalized epilepsy (GGE), consistent with the higher heritability and stronger contribution of common genetic variation in this subtype. 53 Among these, PRSS53 emerged as the highest-confidence candidate, supported by convergent evidence from MR, colocalization, and SuSIE fine-mapping. Although PRSS53 has not previously been implicated in epilepsy, it belongs to a family of serine proteases with established roles in epithelial sodium channel regulation 54 and has been associated with several neurological disorders 55, 56 , making it an attractive candidate for future functional studies. Conversely, OGA represented the strongest genetically supported protective association across epilepsy outcomes. O-GlcNAcylation has increasingly been implicated in neuronal function and synaptic plasticity 57 , suggesting that altered O-GlcNAc signaling may represent another promising avenue for future mechanistic studies. More broadly, the limited overlap between predictive proteins and genetically supported proteins highlights the importance of integrating longitudinal molecular profiling with human genetics. While many circulating proteins predict future disease, only a subset shows genetic evidence consistent with potential involvement in epilepsy pathogenesis.

Subtype-specific analyses further highlighted the biological heterogeneity of epilepsy. GGE showed substantially broader proteomic dysregulation than focal epilepsy (FE), with prominent signals involving immune regulation, blood-brain barrier integrity (PIGR 58 and CEACAM1 59 ), neuroinflammation (ANPEP 60 ), and lysosomal function (LAMP2 61 ). In contrast, FE was characterized by comparatively fewer associations involving IL1R1, CSF1, 62, 63 RAB6A, and SELENOP 64 , reinforcing the importance of neuroinflammatory and oxidative stress pathways in focal epileptogenesis while suggesting distinct systemic molecular responses across epilepsy subtypes. Age-at-onset analyses further identified unique molecular signatures in childhood-and juvenile-onset epilepsy. In childhood-onset cases, ATRN and LEP were uniquely significant; whereas in juvenile-onset cases, unique associations included A1BG, CFB, COL9A1, DPP4, LAG3, and TGFBI. These findings align with previous evidence linking adrenergic signaling 65 , complement activation 66 , DPP4mediated microglial changes 67 , and TGFB-driven 68 blood-brain barrier dysfunction to epilepsy pathophysiology. Together, these findings support the concept that molecular processes preceding epilepsy differ according to both disease subtype and age at onset, reinforcing the biological heterogeneity of epileptogenesis.

Extending the proteomic analyses, we identified 154 circulating metabolites associated with incident epilepsy, indicating that systemic metabolic remodeling also precedes clinical disease onset. The strongest association was observed for citrate, a central metabolite linking mitochondrial energy metabolism with glutamate and GABA biosynthesis 69 . Particularly noteworthy is the established role of loss-of-function variants in the neuronal citrate transporter gene SLC13A5, which cause an early-infantile developmental and epileptic encephalopathy 20, 21 , providing a direct genetic link between citrate metabolism and seizures.

Although numerous lipid and lipoprotein measures were also associated with epilepsy risk 70 , metabolomic prediction performance remained modest (MetRS AUC=0.63) and contributed little beyond the ProRS, suggesting that the circulating proteome captures a larger proportion of biologically relevant variation in this population than the metabolome.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, although UK Biobank (UKB) offers one of the largest prospective multi-omic data sets at population scale, the relatively small number of incident epilepsy cases limited statistical power for subtype-specific analyses. Second, in UKB participants are predominantly of European ancestry and are affected by healthy volunteer bias, potentially limiting generalizability of our findings to other populations 71.

Third, epilepsy ascertainment relied primarily on hospital records using ICD coding and selfreported diagnoses, resulting in a heterogeneous mixture of epilepsy phenotypes, including GGE and FE together with structural, vascular, inflammatory, metabolic, and other acquired epilepsies. Consequently, the incident epilepsy phenotype represented in UKB differs substantially from the clinically characterized cohorts used to derive current epilepsy GWAS and PRSs. This difference in phenotype composition is likely to explain the relatively modest performance of the epilepsy PRS observed in our study. Fourth, the UKB predominantly includes middle-aged and older adults. Current epilepsy PRSs are primarily based on large case-control GWAS enriched for individuals with epilepsy onset during childhood, adolescence, or early adulthood, where inherited genetic susceptibility plays a particularly prominent role. In contrast, incident epilepsy in UKB occurs at considerably older ages and includes a substantial proportion of acquired epilepsies, for which currently available epilepsy PRSs are not expected to capture fully the underlying disease risk. This phenotype and-at-onset mismatch is likely to attenuate PRS performance in the present prospective setting. Consistent with this interpretation, predictive performance improved markedly in participants with epilepsy onset at ≤18 years of age, a subgroup expected to contain a greater proportion of genetically determined epilepsies.

Importantly, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence against a major genetic contribution to epilepsy. GGE is among the most heritable common neurological disorders, and the predominance of MR signals observed for GGE in our study further supports the strong influence of inherited susceptibility in this subtype. Rather, our results suggest that circulating proteomic biomarkers capture dynamic biological processes that are shared across genetically determined and acquired epilepsies and therefore provide complementary information to inherited genetic risk in population-based cohorts. This distinction between inherited susceptibility and dynamic disease-associated biology is likely to become increasingly important as multi-omic approaches are integrated into epilepsy risk prediction and precision medicine. Finally, although MR and colocalization analyses strengthened causal inference for several proteins, experimental validation will be required to confirm the biological mechanisms underlying the identified associations. In addition, independent replication in external prospective cohorts, including more diverse populations and larger numbers of clinically well-characterized epilepsy subtypes, will be essential to establish the generalizability and clinical utility of the proposed molecular risk scores.

By extending prospective plasma proteomics with metabolomics and human genetic analyses, our study demonstrates that prospective multi-omic profiling captures complementary dimensions of epilepsy biology that are not fully reflected by inherited genetic susceptibility alone. Circulating proteomic signatures provide the strongest predictive information, metabolomic profiles reveal systemic metabolic alterations preceding disease onset, and human genetics identifies a subset of proteins with evidence supporting causal involvement in epilepsy. Together, these findings establish a framework that combines prediction with mechanistic prioritization, advancing molecular risk stratification and potential therapeutic target discovery for epilepsy while illustrating the broader potential of prospective multiomic profiling for precision medicine in complex neurological disorders.
