Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. The 1984 Bhopal Gas Leak is widely considered the worst industrial disaster in history, and "big events" can have durable impacts on infectious disease transmission. Among men who have sex with men, we examined whether meeting sexual partners near the original gas leak site was associated with HIV, HIV-associated risk behaviors, and sexual stigma.

We used respondent-driven sampling to enroll MSM in Bhopal during 2023-24 (n=750).

Introduction

Large-scale events and policies are increasingly recognized as structural drivers of HIV transmission. Systematic economic disinvestment in Black communities in the United States and South Africa, facilitated through policies of residential segregation, created environments of concentrated poverty and limited healthcare infrastructure, which still impact the spatial distribution of HIV infection and viral suppression outcomes decades after those policies ended (1) (2) (3). For people who inject drugs, "big events" such as economic recessions and the COVID-19 pandemic are recognized as key drivers of HIV outbreaks, shaping injection networks and risk behaviors (4) (5) (6). Similarly, armed conflicts, like in Ukraine, can accelerate HIV transmission through population displacement and mixing of injection and sexual networks, reshaping the geospatial distribution of infections (7). These events and policies often operate through placebased mechanisms, reshaping physical and social structures in ways that concentrate harm among already-marginalized populations and embed effects that persist for decades.

The literature on the legacy effects of industrial and natural disasters on HIV and other infectious diseases is limited. One study found elevated incidence rates of tuberculosis in areas more affected by the Chernobyl disaster, and noted that the results were probably not a direct impact of radiation but rather due to a "set of negative factors that have a pernicious influence on the general health of the population" (8). Studies have linked hurricanes and other extreme weather events to short-term disruptions in HIV services and increases in HIV risk behaviors, especially among vulnerable populations (9) (10) (11) (12) (13) (14), though few studies have captured how these events influenced the burden of HIV over the following decades.

The Bhopal Gas Disaster is widely regarded as the worst industrial disaster in human history (15, 16). On December 2 nd , 1984, approximately 40 tons of methyl isocyanate gas leaked from the Union Carbide pesticide plant in Bhopal, India, engulfing the surrounding neighborhoods, including informal settlements. Approximately 10,000 people died within three days of the leak, and more than 25,000 deaths have since been attributed to the disaster (15, 16). In total, over half a million people were exposed to the gas (16). Long-term health consequences, including greater risk of chronic respiratory disease, ocular issues, immunotoxicity, neurological and psychiatric conditions, and adverse reproductive and birth outcomes, have been documented even amongst subsequent generations (15, (17) (18) (19) (20). Significant contamination around the plant persists, with groundwater showing elevated levels of heavy metals and chlorinated organic pollutants (21), but the last comprehensive contamination report was completed over a decade ago (22). The response to the disaster was widely criticized as inadequate. Union Carbide's 1989 settlement of 470 million USD, which amounted to 25,000 rupees (approximately 500 USD) per victim, was insufficient, and ongoing efforts to seek justice from the individuals responsible have proven unsuccessful (16). To date, there has been no comprehensive operation to remove the toxic waste from the site (23). Like many large-scale events and policies, the impact of the Bhopal disaster has been spatially and socially heterogeneous, with disproportionate burden falling upon impoverished communities close to the original leak site (16, 20). While many long-term consequences of the Bhopal disaster have been extensively documented, its potential role in shaping the burden of HIV has not been examined.

Overall HIV incidence in India has been steadily declining (24, 25), yet HIV burden among key populations, including men who have sex with men (MSM), remains disproportionately high (26): an estimated 2.4% of MSM were living with HIV compared to 0.2% among all adults aged 15-49 years (27). A study conducted across 10 Indian cities from 2016-17 using RDS found a weighted HIV prevalence of 17.6% among MSM overall and 16.3% among MSM in Bhopal (28).

Indian MSM face unique sociocultural and psychosocial pressures and remain a largely hidden population (29). Same-sex relations between men were criminalized under Section 377 of the Indian Penal Code until 2018, and its legacy continues to shape the social and structural context, including access to education and income-generating activities and vulnerability to poverty and violence (30). Approximately 30.6% of MSM in India are married to a woman (31), with many reporting societal stigma and familial pressures to marry, have children, and conceal their sexual practices. These social pressures contribute to experiences of living a double life, elevated rates of alcohol dependence (32), adverse childhood experiences, depression, and suicidality (33). MSM in India further contend with discrimination, internalized stigma, and enacted stigma by family members and healthcare providers (33, 34), influencing healthcare access, utilization, and retention in care.

Stigma and structural exclusion also influence sexual behaviors among MSM, including the locations where MSM find and meet partners (35). Among MSM in India, compounding pressures of stigma and social marginalization channel partner-seeking into hidden, semi-public spaces with documented venues including parks, public toilets, bus and railway stations, among others (36). Risk tolerance, or the willingness to accept situations or behaviors that may increase exposure to HIV/STI infection, violence, or other negative experiences, is often influenced by personal beliefs and life context (37) (38) (39) (40). Thus, within stigmatizing settings, desire to remain hidden and risk tolerance to engaging in same-sex practices may increase. Venue typology influences HIV risk and vulnerability, shaping condom negotiation power, access to outreach services, and exposure to violence (41). Methods

Study Design

Enrollment for this cross-sectional study of MSM in Bhopal, India, was done between 2023 and 2024 using respondent-driven sampling (44, 45), which is commonly used for sampling key populations where no sampling frame exists (46). Recruitment began with the selection of 2 seeds who were well connected and influential among MSM in Bhopal. Seeds were given two paper recruitment coupons to distribute to other MSM whom they knew and asked to encourage recipients to attend the study site and present the coupon. Those eligible were enrolled and given two recruitment coupons. This iterative peer recruitment continued until the desired sample size of 750 (excluding seeds) was reached. Eligibility criteria included: at least 18 years old, self-identified as male, reported oral or anal sex with a man in the prior 12 months, and presented a valid RDS referral coupon (except seeds). People who identified as women, transgender, or hijra were excluded. All enrolled participants provided a blood sample and completed an interviewer-administered electronic survey including sections on sociodemographics, sexual behavior, uptake of HIV treatment and prevention interventions, and stigma. Biometrics (i.e., iris) were used to ensure people did not enroll twice in the study.

Participants were compensated for completing RDS study procedures (300 Indian rupees (INR))

and for each eligible and enrolled participant they referred (100 INR), up to two total.

HIV Status and Viremia

HIV status was determined through on-site rapid testing and counseling under Indian standards.

Participants were provided with pre-and post-test counseling, and those living with HIV were referred to HIV care/treatment at local government HIV care/treatment centers. Plasma HIV-1 RNA viral loads were determined using the Abbott RealTime HIV-1 assay (Abbott Park, IL, USA), and participants were considered virally suppressed with viral loads <200 copies/mL and viremic otherwise. Population prevalence of viremia was computed using all participants regardless of HIV status as the denominator.

Survey Data

In the survey, participants reported up to four areas of Bhopal where they had most often gone to meet sexual partners in the past 12 months by providing a place name and approximate location using Google Maps on a tablet. Participants also reported socio-demographic and sexual behavior characteristics. Sociodemographic variables included age (years), sexual orientation (heterosexual, homosexual, bisexual), marital status (never married, currently married, widowed or divorced), education (primary, secondary or high school, post-secondary), wage earning pattern (monthly or weekly, daily, intermittent employment, not employed, student), average monthly personal income in the last 12 months (rupees), living situation (own, rent home, rent room, other), number of children, running out of basics (e.g. food) in the last 12 months (yes, no), sleeping outside in the last 12 months (yes, no), and access to a smartphone (yes, no). Sexual partner variables corresponded to the previous six months and included number of regular partners, number of casual partners, and condom use among regular and casual partners. Here, we examined use only among casual partners (consistent/perfect, inconsistent, never, no casual partners), as condom use among casual partners was expected to be a more relevant determinant of transmission risk.

Guided by established stigma frameworks (47-49), we measured multiple dimensions of sexual stigma using a structured 25-item survey capturing three stigma mechanisms (enacted, anticipated and internalized) across various sources (healthcare workers, family, and society).

Items were adapted for the Indian context, informed by existing stigma scales (49-52), and supported by existing qualitative data indicating that healthcare workers, family, and society are salient sources of stigma for MSM and other key populations in India (33, 53, 54). The survey included seven subscales: enacted healthcare stigma (3 items; α=0.70), enacted family stigma (3 items; α=0.88), enacted societal stigma (4 items; α=0.87), anticipated healthcare stigma (3 items; α=0.71), anticipated family stigma (3 items; α=0.76), anticipated societal stigma (4 items; α=0.86), and internalized stigma (5 items; α=0.71). Enacted stigma items paralleled anticipated stigma items, where enacted stigma items assessed the frequency of past discriminatory experiences and anticipated stigma assessed perceived likelihood of future mistreatment from a given source for being MSM. Internalized stigma measured current negative feelings about being MSM. Responses were recorded on Likert scales (enacted stigma:0 = never to3 = frequently; anticipated stigma:0 = very unlikely to3 = very likely; internalized stigma:0 = not at all to3 = a great deal), and mean scores were calculated for each subscale (theoretical range: 0-3), with higher scores indicating greater stigma. Individual survey questions can be found in the Supplemental Methods.

Spatial Data Processing

To identify unique sexual partner meeting locations reported by MSM, all reported locations were first cleaned and deduplicated before final geocoding. First, we identified groups of named destinations likely representing the same location and assigned a unique and consistent place name for all observations representing that location. We then geocoded those unique names with the Google Maps API. We compared those geocoded locations to those reported by participants attending that location. When the location obtained through the Google Maps API differed substantially from participant-reported locations, these errors were resolved manually.

Most locations (303/327; 92.7%) were accurately geocoded by Google API after name cleaning and harmonization, while the remaining 24 locations were geocoded by hand. We categorized each location into one of commercial (shop, market, mall, cineplex), neighborhood (hotel, house, unknown spot in neighborhood), transportation-related (bus stop/terminal, train stop/station, road, fuel pump), park (park, fairgrounds, trail, wedding grounds), and other (e.g., school, temple, factory, hospital).

Statistical Analyses

"Seed" participants were removed from all analyses. RDS-II weights were created using the inverse of reported network size (55). Network sizes were determined by the answer to the question "Of the [total number of MSM known by the participant] people who live in Bhopal, who you personally know are MSM, how many have you seen at least once in the past 30 days?"

We calculated weighted summary statistics and used the tree bootstrap method (100,000 replicates) to calculate 95% confidence intervals (56). We summarized characteristics overall and stratified by HIV status using both RDS-II weighted and unweighted percentages for categorical variables, and medians and first and third quartiles for continuous variables. We also calculated the prevalence of experiencing any sexual stigma (i.e., score>0) across mechanisms and sources, stratified by HIV status. We calculated the Euclidean distance from each meeting place to the original gas leak site and categorized participants according to their closest reported meeting place into the following categories: less than 1 kilometer (km), between 1 and 2 km (inclusive), and over 2 km. We then calculated summary statistics stratified by distance, as well as the weighted prevalence of HIV and HIV viremia. Because reporting more partner meeting locations may be associated with increased sexual activities (and therefore HIV), while also providing additional opportunities to meet a partner near the 1984 site, we conducted a sensitivity analysis where we restricted to participants who reported the maximum of four meeting locations to recalculate the HIV prevalence gradient across distance to the leak site.

Finally, we fit univariable weighted logistic regression models to estimate the odds of meeting a sexual partner within 2 km (dichotomized for modeling simplicity) of the gas leak site. We used sexual stigma measures as the exposure, and a separate regression was fit for each stigma type and source. We conducted a sensitivity analysis where we repeated our logistic regression analyses, but restricting only to people who did not live within 2 km of the leak site, to ensure that our results are not just identifying correlations among those living and meeting within that radius, but also among people traveling to the area. For both main and sensitivity analyses, we 228 also fit adjusted logistic regressions to account for potential confounding by age, sexual 229 orientation, marital status, education, personal income (rupees per month divided by 10,000), 230 and HIV status. 231

Results

Participant Characteristics

Of 750 MSM enrolled in the study, the overall weighted HIV prevalence was 11.6% (95% CI: 6.9%-17.9%), and the weighted population prevalence of HIV viremia was 4.4% (95% CI: 1.4%-8.5%). The median age was 24 years (IQR 21-30), with participants living with HIV tending to be older (median 30; IQR 24-38) (Table 1). Almost every participant lived in Bhopal for over a year (99.8%) and completed secondary school (99.5%). Though income did not vary widely by HIV status, participants living with HIV were more likely to identify as homosexual (66.9% vs 49.8%), own a home (79.4% vs. 68.8%), and to have slept outside (16.6% vs 7.9%) in the last 12 months. However, only one participant reported being currently homeless. Overall, 97.8% had at least one casual sexual partner in the last six months, and 40.7% reported ten or more casual partners. Reporting ten or more casual partners was more common among participants with HIV (64.4% vs 37.6%). Condom use with casual partners was typically inconsistent (87.6%). Over two-thirds reported selling sex or exchanging sex for goods (67.1%), and 29.6% bought or exchanged goods for sex. Almost half (45.1%) of those living without HIV had been tested for HIV, but only 8.4% had tested in the last year. Unweighted data are available in Supplemental Table 1.

Sexual Partner Meeting Locations

Participants reported 2,715 sexual partner meeting locations, and 94% of participants reported three or four locations (mean 3.6 per participant). This corresponded to 327 unique places, including 189 neighborhoods, 63 transportation-related locations, 29 commercial locations, 28 parks and grounds, and 18 other locations. Most (n=291, 89.0%) locations were outside of the 2-kilometer radius of the original site of the gas leak, but the two most reported transportationrelated locations were between 1-2 km of the site (Figure 1). Participants reporting four locations were more likely to report a meeting place within 2 km of the gas site compared to participants reporting three (39.4% vs. 28.4%).

HIV prevalence was highest among participants meeting partners at transportation-related locations (14.8%, 95% CI: 2.7%-26.4%) and lowest at commercial locations (6.8%, 1.7%-15.7%), compared to 11.4% (6.4%-18.7%) for neighborhoods and 10.4% (2.5%-24.6%) for parks. A similar result was observed for HIV viremia: 6.4% (0.0%-15.8%) for transportationrelated vs. 3.5% (0.0%-11.9%) for commercial, 4.3% (1.1%-8.8%) for neighborhoods, and 4.5% (0.0%-16.1%) for parks (Figure 2). HIV prevalence was elevated when a participant met sexual partners near the leak site: 21.7% (2.4%-58.8%) within 1 km, 14.4% (6.1%-24.3%) between 1-2 km, and 9.7% (5.0%-17.1%) over 2 km away (Figure 3). HIV viremia was elevated among participants meeting within 1 km (7.4% [0.0%-32.2%]) and between 1-2 km (6.9% [1.0%-17.1%]) compared to those meeting over 2 km away (3.2% [0.5%-7.3%]). These gradients remained after restricting to only participants reporting the maximum of four meeting locations: 34.3%, 15.9%, and 11.1% for HIV prevalence and 12.1%, 7.3%, 3.6% for HIV viremia for <1, 1-2, and >2 km, respectively. When stratifying by distance to the leak site, HIV prevalence at transportation-related locations was similar to neighborhoods within 2 km of the leak site (17.5% vs. 15.8%) and to neighborhoods and parks beyond 2 km (10.5% vs. 9.4% and 11.0%, respectively) (Figure 2).

In addition to elevated HIV and HIV viremia, 71.0% of participants meeting within a kilometer of the site reported exchanging sex for goods, compared to 73.7% of those meeting within 1-2 km and 64.4% of those only meeting more than 2 km away (Figure 3). Fewer people reported exchanging goods for sex, but the probability also decreased the further the meeting location was from the leak site (Figure 3). Those meeting within 2 km of the leak site also reported more casual partners (57.9%, 57.0%, 33.5% reporting 10+ partners in <1, 1-2, and >2 km distance groups, respectively), as well as elevated proportions of sleeping outside in the last 12 months (15.0%, 19.6%, 4.5%) (Supplemental Table 2). Unweighted comparisons between those meeting within 2 km and outside 2 km are available in the supplement (Supplemental Table 3).

Sexual Stigma and Sexual Partner Meeting Locations

Anticipated stigma was more common than enacted or internalized stigma, regardless of source, but enacted and internalized stigma showed greater differences by HIV status (Table 1).

All stigma measures, regardless of source, were significantly associated with meeting within 2 km of the leak site (Figure 4). Most notably, a one-point increase in internalized stigma was associated with 5.1 (3.2-8.5) times the odds of meeting near the site. A one-point increase in experiencing enacted stigma in a healthcare setting (4.2, 2.6-7.2), from family members (3.8, 2.4-6.1), and from other members of society (3.8, 2.3-6.4) was also strongly associated.

Anticipated stigma, whether in a healthcare setting (1.8, 1.2-2.7), from family members (1.6, 1.3-2.1), and from other members of society (1.4, 1.0-1.9), had a weaker, but still significant, association. In our sensitivity analysis restricting to only participants living outside the 2 km radius, the associations remained statistically significant and consistent in direction, though with point estimates further from the null (Odds Ratio = 1) and wider confidence intervals (Figure 4). Adjustment for potential confounders generally resulted in point estimates closer to the null, with a smaller impact on the main analyses compared to sensitivity analyses. Adjusted confidence intervals newly crossed the null for anticipated stigma from family and society sources in both main and sensitivity analyses, but remained statistically significant for all other stigma types and sources (Figure 4).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study of MSM in Bhopal, men who met male sexual partners near the original leak site of the 1984 gas disaster were more likely to be living with HIV, to be viremic, to have many casual partners, and to engage in transactional sex. We also observed that sexual stigma was a key correlate of meeting a partner near the original leak site. Finally, we observed higher HIV prevalence and viremia among men meeting partners at transportation-related venues, like train stations and bus stops, with the most reported spaces being 1-2 km from the leak site. Taken together, our findings have two major implications: that the areas around the original 1984 gas leak site could be targeted for venue-based HIV control interventions in Bhopal; and that downstream consequences of the 1984 Bhopal gas disaster may continue to impact the spatial distributions of sexual behaviors and HIV burden in MSM four decades later.

One mechanism that could link the 1984 gas tragedy to the modern sexual behaviors of MSM in Bhopal is environmental stigma. Geographic areas which experience industrial contamination are often subject to enduring stigma, with magnitudes depending on distance to the contamination site, the level of poverty of the area, and the speed and adequacy of government response (57) (58) (59) (60). The combination of environmental stigma, poverty, and neglect can give rise to or amplify "marginal spaces," which are subject to social exclusion, reduced visibility, and lower normative control, and can become hotspots for homelessness, sex work, and drug use (61). MSM in India face stigma and social consequences for being identified as MSM and may seek "marginal spaces" to avoid detection when meeting partners (36).

The observed link between sexual stigma, engagement with sex work, and meeting a partner near the original leak site, may be because more marginalized MSM choose these locations to avoid detection.

Sexual stigma is linked to HIV and associated risk behaviors through a variety of mechanisms (62). First, sexual stigma in the healthcare setting can reduce the likelihood of HIV testing and viral suppression (63, 64), leading to increased potential for onward transmission. Furthermore, stigma can reduce engagement with prevention interventions, such as pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) (64, 65). Sexual stigma from familial or societal sources, as well as internalized stigma, may contribute to desires to remain hidden and thus might influence sexual risk-taking and one's choice of sexual partner or the venue in which they meet them (38, 40). A similar effect is seen in people with HIV, who also experience stigma related to their HIV status, and they may also seek the same hidden or stigmatized places to meet sexual partners (66), potentially pairing stigmatized MSM without HIV and MSM with HIV together. The combination of increased willingness to engage in risk behaviors, together with increased exposure to partners living with HIV, creates an environment where the risk of HIV acquisition is heightened. Taken together, the links between HIV, risk behaviors, stigma, and marginal spaces provide a potential mechanism for the heightened burden of HIV among MSM meeting near the 1984 gas leak site.

The idea that HIV transmission is associated with mobility and transportation is well documented in the literature. The HIV epidemic has spread along trucking routes in India and elsewhere (67-69) and even modern analyses identify clusters of new infections and sex work venues near major highways (70, 71) and public transport nodes (72). Despite elevated HIV prevalence and viremia, major transportation sites were over 1 km from the leak site, outside the region identified with the highest burden of HIV and prevalence of associated risk behaviors. This suggests that the presence of these transportation-related sites alone does not explain the relationship between HIV and distance to the 1984 site.

Venue-based outreach for HIV-related interventions is a common strategy for populations where no sampling frame exists (73, 74). Our results suggest that venues near the original leak site can be targeted, potentially beginning with major transportation hubs and with additional locations chosen after consultation with community informants. Interventions such as condoms and event-driven PrEP are sensible choices for venue-based interventions but even testing and linkage to care interventions in these settings could reach underserved MSM who would not be reached through clinic-based interventions (40, 75). The literature on environmental stigma suggests that quick decontamination responses alongside strong governmental support after the next "big event" could curtail the development of environmental stigma and its persistent impact (58) (59) (60). In addition to decontamination, economic interventions which reduce entrance into sex work could be considered. Given that the next industrial disaster or similar destabilizing event may have consequences for the burden of HIV among vulnerable populations, the HIV prevention community could be proactive in advocating for early institutional intervention.

This study had a few key strengths. To our knowledge, this is among the first studies to integrate HIV testing, identification of sexual partner meeting locations, and measurement of sexual stigma in a community-based sample of MSM. Sexual partner meeting locations are rarely collected and to our knowledge this is the first study to obtain such meeting locations alongside systematic HIV testing in India. Geospatial studies of HIV burden and risk have predominantly concentrated on the residential location of participants, which may not be appropriate to inform all interventions. Further, our "big events" framework provides the first potential link between an industrial disaster and the spatial distribution of HIV prevalence.

This study also has limitations. We examined distance to the original gas leak site rather than a measure of gas exposure over space, which was not readily available. The gas did not expand equally in all directions, spreading relatively further to the southeast. Distance is therefore an imperfect proxy for gas exposure, though this has also been used in previous studies (19, 20).

However, because our proposed mechanism linking the 1984 gas disaster and HIV is not direct gas exposure, but rather the creation or exacerbation of marginal spaces facilitated by environmental stigma, it is possible that proximity to the leak site, not just level of gas exposure, could shape perceptions of the surrounding area. Participants could report only up to four areas where they most often met partners, so those classified as not meeting near the site may still have met partners there less frequently. Participants reporting more locations were more likely to report a meeting near the original gas leak site and were more sexually active, and thus the link between meeting near the leak site and HIV could have been due to those associations.

However, the spatial gradient of HIV prevalence remained when restricting to participants reporting four locations. Our study was cross-sectional, and thus we cannot assume the direction of causation from our data alone. This has implications for individual-level outcomes, like whether meeting a partner near the leak site was preceded or followed by an HIV infection.

At the ecological level, the Union Carbide pesticide plant was placed near a slum and perhaps that area was already stigmatized and a meeting center for MSM engaged in sex work before the gas leak. However, other slums exist in Bhopal, many of which are outside the 2 km radius of the gas leak, so this likely would not fully explain our results. The leak site also lies near Bhopal's main railway and bus stations, and we cannot fully distinguish proximity to the leak site from proximity to these transport hubs. However, HIV prevalence was highest within 1 km of the leak site, closer than the major transportation hubs, and was elevated near the leak site across venue types. Future analyses could examine similar marginal spaces created or exacerbated by industrial and natural disasters to see if similar links to HIV and stigma are identified.

In conclusion, we identified an area in Bhopal, India where modern MSM meeting male sexual partners faced greater stigma and had a higher prevalence of HIV and associated sexual risk behaviors. We hypothesize that this spatial distribution is linked to the 1984 Gas Disaster, echoing a historical pattern of "big events" exacerbating infectious disease risk in marginalized populations. More disasters and "big events" will occur. If left unaddressed, these events could exacerbate the risk of HIV and other infectious diseases in vulnerable populations for decades to come.
