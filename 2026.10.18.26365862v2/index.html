research_questions Schizophrenia and depression have been linked with anhedonia which may present a transdiagnostic deficits that cuts across traditional diagnostic entities. Schizophrenia is a severe and highly complex mental disorder that is characterized by heterogenous symptoms ranging from psychotic, negative and cognitive symptoms. Depression one the other hand, is a debilitating mental disorder that affects millions of people causing disabilities and morbidities every year. It is characterized by depressed mood and anhedonia (Bedwell et al., 2014; Husain and Roiser, 2018). While these two disorders are quite distinct they share dysfunctions in the domains of reward and motivational processing, in particular both disorders are characterized by persistent anhedonia. Voxel-Based Morphometry (VBM) is a powerful method of analyzing neuroimaging data. Previous meta-analyses have identified gray matter deficits in the frontal lobe, temporal lobe, insular and thalamus in schizophrenic patients compared to healthy controls (Torres et al., 2016; Wei et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2019). VBM studies have reported that patients with depression experience widespread local abnormalities in several brain regions such as the frontal gyrus, anterior cingulate gyrus and hippocampus (Tang et al., 2007; Wagner et al., 2008). Against this background, we aimed to (1) Determine common brain volumetric abnormalities in patients with schizophrenia and depression, (2) Identify disorder-specific brain volume abnormalities in patients with schizophrenia compared with healthy controls (3) Identify disorder-specific brain volume abnormalities in patients with depression compared with healthy controls (4) Explore the identified brain regions on the network, behavioral, and molecular-genetic levels. How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). Abstract

Anhedonia, social withdrawal and executive deficits represent transdiagnostic symptoms in depression, schizophrenia and addiction, yet transdiagnostic brain volumetric alterations and their contribution to these symptom domains have not been systematically characterized. Here, we capitalized on a pre-registered (https://osf.io/s57uq) coordinate-based meta-analysis across 147 VBM studies (n=13,041) to determine common and disorder-specific gray matter deficits in patients with schizophrenia (90 studies), major depression disorder (44 studies) and alcohol use disorder (13 studies). Original case-control articles comparing patients with healthy controls, published in English and reporting whole-brain results were included. We found shared GMV alterations in the ventromedial prefrontal cortex, ventral anterior cingulate cortex, insula, superior temporal gyrus. On the network level, we found robust functional interactions of the identified shared regions with the cortical midline structures as well as social and theory of mind-related behavioral functions. Subsequent characterization analyses highlighted the involvement of dopaminergic and serotonergic neurotransmitters as well as associated genetic annotations. Overall, the present metaanalysis found common as well as separate GMV abnormalities in schizophrenia, major depressive disorder and alcohol use disorder suggesting a common neurostructural substrate and treatment target for social withdrawal and anhedonia across these disorders.

Introduction

Anhedonia -the diminished capacity to experience pleasure or interest in previously rewarding activities -represents a key transdiagnostic symptom across major depressive disorder (MDD), schizophrenia (SZ), and alcohol use disorder (AUD) [1, 2].

Together with transdiagnostic deficits in executive [3, 4] and social functioning [4, 5], these impairments contribute substantially to the chronic course, long-term functional disability and poor treatment response in these conditions. These transdiagnostic symptoms are not responsive to the established interventions [6, 7] and determining the underlying neural basis could help to determine targeted novel interventions for the transdiagnostic dysfunctions.

Schizophrenia is a chronic mental disorder associated with negative symptoms including anhedonia and asociality. With a lifetime prevalence of approximately 1%, it continues to contribute to the global burden of disease [8]. The first positive symptoms typically emerge after the development of cognitive and negative symptoms [9]. Previous neuroimaging meta-analyses have suggested volumetric decrease in patients with schizophrenia in several networks encompassing the temporolimbic structures, insula and striatal regions [10]. Major depressive disorder is often characterized by depressed mood and anhedonia (prolonged loss of interest in almost all daily activities). Some neurobiological models have been proposed to account for MDD pathogenesis including the role of dysfunctional cortico-limbic networks in MDD [11, 12]. Previous VBM metaanalyses have found gray matter decreases in patients with MDD in regions . encompassing the anterior cingulate cortex, frontal gyrus, hippocampus and insula [13] [14] [15].

Alcohol use disorder (AUD) is highly prevalent substance use disorder that carries a huge global health burden and causes more than 2 million deaths each year [16, 17].

Individuals with AUD often suffer from compulsive use of alcohol despite the negative social and cognitive consequences that arise [18]. Numerous studies have documented gray matter reductions among individuals with AUD compared to healthy controls. Furthermore, previous VBM meta-analyses on AUD suggested significantly reduced gray matter in extensive regions of the prefrontal cortex, striatal and insular regions, anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) as well as parts of the posterior cingulate cortex (PCC) [19] [20] [21].

While MDD, SZ and AUD present unique symptoms and clinical phenotypes, accumulating evidence indicates shared neurobiological substrates. The transdiagnostic symptom deficits in anhedonia, cognition and social domains face substantial treatment challenges [22, 23]. Anhedonia in these disorders has been closely linked to dysregulation of mesolimbic reward circuitry, particularly dopaminergic projections from the midbrain to the ventral striatum and ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) [24, 25]. Converging evidence from disorder-specific studies indicates dysregulations in dopaminergic functioning and reward-related functioning of the striatum and vmPFC in MDD, SZ and AUD [26] [27] [28] [29].

Executive functioning deficits including working memory and inhibition deficits have been identified as major endophenotype candidates across the disorders and patients with these disorders have consistently demonstrated dysfunctional prefrontal cortex engagement during executive tasks [30] [31] [32] [33]. Social dysfunctions including empathy and theory of mind (ToM) have been hypothesized to represent a trans-diagnostic symptomatology domain [34, 35] alterations in these domains have been linked to functional and structural alterations in the vmPFC in depression, schizophrenia and AUD [5, [35] [36] [37]. However, the neural basis of the transdiagnostic deficits and their associations with the behavioral domains have not been systematically determined.

Neuroimaging meta-analyses have been increasingly adopted as the gold standard for solving heterogeneity and generalizability problems in neuroimaging research [38, 39] as well as determine transdiagnostic brain structural signatures [13, 40, 41]. The current meta-analysis aimed to determine common and separate gray matter volume alterations in schizophrenia, depression and alcohol use disorder patients compared to healthy controls to determine transdiagnostic alterations that underly anhedonia, executive deficits and social dysfunctions. Specifically, we aimed to (a) Determine disorder-specific GMV deficits between patients and healthy controls within disorders, (b) Determine the common gray matter deficits in schizophrenia, major depression disorder and alcohol use disorder (c) Employ advanced network, behavioral, and molecular-genetic analyses to dissect the contribution of these alterations to the three transdiagnostic symptom clusters. In line with previous literature, we hypothesized that all three disorders would exhibit decreased brain volumes in prefrontal regions involved in executive control and value processing. We further hypothesized that schizophrenia patients would show gray matter decreases in the thalamus, amygdala and . hippocampus, depression patients would exhibit GMV decreases in prefrontal brain regions while alcohol use disorder would show GMV alterations in frontostriatal brain regions.

Methods

The present meta-analysis followed standard procedures of coordinate-based metaanalyses and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [42, 43]. To enhance credibility and interpretability of the results, the meta-analysis was pre-registered on the Open Science Framework repository prior to data analysis (https://osf.io/s57uq). Of note, we initially did not pre-register the inclusion of AUD group given that the idea only developed when screening the literature for MDD and SZ.

Data Extraction

Important demographic, clinical and statistical information was extracted from eligible studies. The data extraction process including authors' names, titles of articles, year of publication, mean age, number of participants, medication information, etc., was conducted extensively prior to data analysis.

Statistical Analysis

The meta-analysis was performed using SDM-PSI toolbox version 6.2.1 (https://www.sdmproject.com). The steps are summarized as follows: (a) Peak coordinates of between-group differences (patients versus healthy subjects) and their corresponding effect sizes (t/z values) were extracted. Z-values were converted to tvalues using the standard statistical converter (https://www.sdmproject.com/utilities/?show=Coordinates). (b) Preprocessing was performed to estimate the lower and upper bounds (Hedges' g) of the most probable effect size images [38]. (c) The mean was calculated using maximum-likelihood estimation (MLE) and meta-analysis of non-statistically significant unreported effects (MetaNSUE) algorithms. This step estimates the most likely effect sizes generating several imputations according to estimates within the bounds [44]. (d) Next, imputed study images were recreated. (e) Finally, the permutation test evaluated the combined meta-analysis images for statistical significance [38].

Results of the meta-analysis were visualized by the MRIcroGL (NITRC: MRIcroGL: Tool/Resource Info). The analyses were considered significant at p < .0025 uncorrected, with a voxel-level threshold of k=10 voxels. This threshold balances Type I and Type II errors and has been employed in previous meta-analyses [13, 27, 45].

Peak coordinates of the identified clusters were used to determine whether these clusters are part of common or distinct functional large-scale networks. Sphere-based 6mm radius centered seed regions at the identified shared coordinates were used for the voxel-wise functional connectivity analyses. A radius of 6mm was selected because it provides statistically robust network analyses according to previous studies [46]. Next, behavioral level characterization was employed using the Neurosynth database (https://www.neurosynth.org/). This analysis highlighted the specific behavioral . functions associated with the common regions. Thirdly, genetic underpinnings of identified regions were identified using the Brain annotation (BAT) toolbox. For this analysis, we extracted the top ten genes in order of their regional expression values and plotted the results. These genetic annotations enabled the identification of risk factors associated with psychiatric disorders.

Finally, we explored the roles of monoamine neurotransmitters i.e., dopamine transporter (DAT) and serotonin (5HT1b) which possess both functional and clinical importance in the pathogenesis of common psychiatric disorders as well as a significant role in motivation and reward processing disruptions in these disorders. Brain structure has been associated with neurotransmitter transporters/receptors. DAT was selected because it plays a vital role in adjusting dopamine availability and subsequent dopamine-mediated behaviors while 5HT1b was selected on the basis of its neurological roles of mood, aggression, memory, stress sensibility and anxiety. Receptor-level analysis was therefore conducted to compare the mean densities of dopamine and serotonin receptors in the common regions of gray matter deficits in the three disorders. Receptor characterization followed these steps: First, Positron Emission Tomography (PET) atlases of dopamine and serotonin were acquired (https://xtra.nru.dk/FS5ht-atlas/, https://www.nitrc.org/projects/spmtemplates). Next, reference regions (occipital lobe and cerebellum) were removed by WFU Pickatlas. 6mm sphere-shaped ROIs were created on MarsBaR region of interest toolbox (http://marsbar.sourceforge.net). The corresponding mean values of the two neurotransmitters in the common brain regions of the three disorders were extracted using the Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience Lab (CANlab) Core toolbox (https://canlabweb.colorado.edu/).

Heterogeneity, sensitivity and publication bias

Tests for heterogeneity were performed on the SDM-PSI platform and examined by the I² index. Jackknife sensitivity analysis was conducted on Anisotropic Effect Size-Signed Differential Mapping (AES-SDM) to measure the robustness of the meta-analysis findings. Publication bias was evaluated statistically and interpreted by Egger's tests and funnel plots.

Exploratory analyses -meta-regression and linear model analysis

We conducted meta-regression analyses to evaluate the potential confounding effects of gender (number of females) and age across the three disorders. A linear model analysis was performed to assess medication effects in SZ and MDD group, but not in AUD due to limited data. Here, medication and non-medication studies were coded as 1 and 0 respectively. A further sub-group meta-analysis of non-medication studies in the two disorders was performed to validate the original meta-analytic findings.

Results of the VBM meta-analysis of SZ, MDD and AUD

The present meta-analysis revealed robustly decreased GMV across meta-analyses. We initially determined alterations within each disorder and found that compared to the healthy reference groups: a) MDD patients demonstrated reduced GMV in the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC), mid temporal gyrus and left insula, b) Patients with SZ showed decreased GMV in widely distributed network encompassing the bilateral insula, vmPFC and thalamus. c) Individuals with AUD exhibited reduced GMV largely in the vmPFC, bilateral insula and cerebellum. There were no GMV increases in any of the three disorders (Fig. 2a-c). Next, we determined shared alterations by a quantitative comparison analysis of the meta-analytic findings and found four regions exhibiting commonly decreased gray matter across the disorders, i.e., vmPFC extending to the ventral anterior cingulate cortex (vACC), insula and superior temporal gyrus (STG) (Fig. 2d). Main meta-analytic findings are displayed in Table 1 and Supplementary Table 5 for the disorder-specific clusters.

Characterization of VBM alterations on the network, behavioral, genetic and receptor level

We characterized the identified common regions on the network, behavioral, genetic and receptor levels. Network analyses demonstrate the voxel-wise functional connectivity patterns of the common core regions of GMV decreases. Network patterns of the vmPFC revealed strong functional interactions of this region with cortical midline regions such as the frontal pole and posterior cingulate regions. The identified insula region exhibited a strong functional interaction with the salience network (including the bilateral insula and the dorsal ACC, dACC) while the vACC coupled strongly with core regions of the default mode network (DMN), including the medial prefrontal cortex, posterior cingulate and the bilateral angular gyrus while the STG showed strong functional interaction with the bilateral temporal regions (Fig. 3a-d).

Behavioral-level analyses revealed that the vmPFC was associated with social and theory of mind related functions. The insula strongly associated with pain, motor and primary functions (coordination and regulation of body functions) while the STG showed stronger associations with theory of mind, social and autobiographical functions. The vACC coupled more with the default network, autobiographical and theory of mind related functions (Fig. 4a-b).

Genetic-level characterization showed high gene expressions of the DMRT3 gene in the vmPFC, insula and STG. DMRT3 gene is mostly responsible for nerve impulse transmissions. However, the vACC revealed high expression of GABRQ gene which is responsible for mediating responses in the central nervous system (Fig. 4c). Receptor-level analysis revealed overall higher dopamine (DAT) and serotonin (5HT1b) mean values in the STG and insula respectively. Specifically, mean densities of the vmPFC were 0.0398 and 0.1365 for DAT and 5HT1b respectively (t=-11.95, df=1028, SD=0.1298). The insula had mean densities of 0.1309 and 0.1730 for DAT and 5HT1b respectively (t=-4.45, df=1028, SD=0.1519). The STG had mean densities of 0.1348 and 0.1319 for DAT and 5HT1b respectively (t=0.44, df=1028, SD=0.10). The vACC had mean densities of 0.1129 and 0.1087 for DAT and 5HT1b respectively (t=0.57, df=1028, SD=0.11). More details are presented in Supplementary Fig. 5a.

Neurostructural assessment of transdiagnostic GMV abnormalities across disorders

Capitalizing on data from over 13,000 individuals allowed us to robustly determine transdiagnostic reductions in GMV in the vmPFC extending to the vACC, insula and STG.

The vmPFC has been identified as a key region linked with the psychopathology of several psychiatric disorders across several meta-analyses [194]. Dense projections of the vmPFC with the hypothalamus, amygdala and ventral striatum mediate its critical role in emotion regulation and fear as well as reward and motivational processes [195, 196]. Animal and human studies have consistently demonstrated a role of the vmPFC in representation and updating of reward values and outcomes as well as social cognitive functions [197] [198] [199].

Previous studies suggested a link between vmPFC dysfunction and value and decisionmaking deficits in AUD, with SZ and MDD showing shared alterations in value, decisionmaking and social functions related to the vmPFC [200]. Severity of depressive symptoms and associated cognitive and social impairments were associated with variations in the vmPFC in depression [194, 201]. Previous meta-analyses have found significantly reduced GMV in medial prefrontal regions across mood but also anxiety disorders [13].

With respect to the further functional network characterization of this region we observed strong coupling with cortical midline regions of the core DMN and metaanalytic associations with social domains, including theory of mind, autobiographic memory or mentalizing, suggesting that alterations in this region may contribute to transdiagnostic social deficits [194]. The observed alterations in the vmpFC may thus contribute to social impairments across disorders including altered empathy and processing of social values [35, 202]. Further analyses revealed that monoamine neurotransmitters involved in reward and social processes, specifically dopamine and serotonin exhibited a high expression in this region. The literature has extensively unraveled the core roles of dopaminergic and serotonergic signaling and how implications of these signals underlie neuropsychiatric disorders. Impaired serotonergic signaling in the vmPFC have been described in animal models and this has specifically implicated vmPFC stimulation [203]. Taken together, the results from GMV loss, functional networks, behavioral and molecular characterizations may suggest several possibilities suggesting that vmPFC deficits reflect impaired a) regulation of negative emotions, b) reward and value representation, or c) social and autobiographical functioning in patients with SZ, MDD and AUD.

Alterations of the insula (both anterior and posterior) have been identified as a common psychopathological substrate in several neuropsychiatric disorders [41, 204]. On the anatomical and functional level, recent models have proposed that insula alterations may be implicated in depressive, schizophrenic and substance use disorders [205] [206] [207].

Anatomical alterations of the insula have been strongly associated with negative symptoms in SZ, with significant GMV reductions observed in several studies [208] suggesting associations with social and cognitive deficits. In MDD, SZ and AUD, neuroanatomical abnormalities of the insula have been frequently reported [10, 89, 209]. Insula alterations have been involved in several domains, including alterations in . incentive motivation, interoception, social functioning and pain [210] [211] [212]. Our behavioral characterization revealed that the identified insula region was associated with pain, in line with evidence from the literature indicating its crucial role in altered pain and pain empathic processing especially in MDD [213] and SZ [214]. Of the four common regions identified in the three disorders, the insula recorded the highest mean value of serotonin (5HT1B) neurotransmitter. The serotonin system is associated with mood and social interactions in humans, which may link it to anhedonia and social deficits across psychiatric disorders [215]. On the network level, the insula coupled strongly with regions encompassing the salience network which help integrate sensory information and also help in assessing the salience of stimuli, therefore, structural decreases in the region may cause challenges in identifying important social stimuli and responding to emotional states.

The ventral ACC (vACC) is associated with several cognitive and emotional processes and is often described as an interconnection of these two processes [216, 217]. GMV decreases in the vACC has been associated with the negative symptoms including anhedonia and emotional dysregulations in psychiatric disorders, with a recent metaanalysis also suggesting robust GMV ACC decreases in AUD and post traumatic disorders [19]. Mirroring the characterization of the adjacent vmPFC this region exhibited strong connections with the core DMN and a behavioral characterization including social and autobiographical domains. Together with the vACC's role in emotional conflict and regulation, this may indicate that dysfunctions in this region may underlie impaired integration of affective regulation with social and autobiographical processes. Dysfunctions in social and autobiographical domains are further supported by our observation of robust decreases in the STG GMV across the disorders. This region plays a crucial role in social cognition and integration of affective and mnemonic processes strongly involved in the pathophysiology of several psychiatric disorders. While previous case control studies and meta-analyses on morphometric alterations in this region in these disorders remained inconsistent [21, 218], a growing number of studies has implicated dysfunctions in this region with deficits in social functioning [219].

Strong network connections of the identified STG region with the extended DMN including anterior temporal regions and the behavioral characterization further confirmed an involvement in social and autobiographical processing. Impairments in the STG may mediate deficits in these domains which have been consistently been identified as factors contributing to the development and maintenance of psychiatric disorders [220].

While MDD, SZ, and AUD exhibit overlapping deficits in the domains of reward processing/anhedonia, executive functions, and social processes, a neurobiological characterization of the regions showing decreased GMV integrity across these disorders supports a neurobiologically informed framework indicating transdiagnostic dysfunctions in the domains of autobiographical and social processing.

Limitations

There are some limitations to be considered in the present meta-analysis. First, SZ and MDD studies included in the main analyses had partly different medication with the . literature suggesting that patients exposed to medication may show progressive structural changes in GMV [221, 222]. However, our sub-group meta-analyses focusing on studies with unmediated samples confirmed the main findings (Supplementary Fig. 3). Secondly, information on duration of duration of illness or alcohol use was not extensively provided across the three disorders. This important variable may affect the findings if the differences are extreme across studies and this should be further explored in future meta-analyses. Thirdly, we performed a coordinate-based meta-analysis which are more generalized and may often be less accurate than image-based meta-analyses.

Sharing of individual level data in future studies may help to overcome this limitation. Fourth, some studies did not provide information regarding age of onset of illness. It is recommended that original studies should provide detailed clinical information of all included patients to ensure more comprehensive analyses in the future.

Conclusion

Taken together, we found specific GMV abnormalities across several cortical and subcortical brain regions supporting a neurobiologically-informed model of transdiagnostic dysregulations in social and autobiographical domains. Given the robust transdiagnostic deficits in GMV alterations in the vmPFC, insula, ventral ACC and superior temporal gyrus, transdiagnostic interventions may target these regions and/or social and autobiographical deficits.

Meta-analytic characterization of SZ, MDD and AUD revealed: a) shared GMV decreases in the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) extending to the ventral anterior cingulate cortex (vACC), insula and superior temporal gyrus (STG) reflecting transdiagnostic deficits, b) robust GMV decreases within each disorder, with comparably focused decreases in MDD, while SZ and AUD exhibited more widespread decreases, c) regions exhibiting shared GMV decreases were characterized by functional interactions with cortical midline structures and default mode core regions (vmPFC, vACC), the salience network (INS), and bilateral temporal regions (STG) and behavioral domains centered around social cognitive and autobiographical functions. Additional analyses highlighted the involvement of dopaminergic and serotonergic neurotransmitters as well as genetic annotations associated with architecture and function of the nervous system. Exploratory meta-regression suggested no significant associations between age and gender (number of females) across the main meta-analytic findings. The identified regions may mediate shared symptomatic deficits in reward/hedonic, executive and social functioning, with further meta-analytic characterization supporting a neurobiologically-informed account of transdiagnostic social and autobiographical deficits.
