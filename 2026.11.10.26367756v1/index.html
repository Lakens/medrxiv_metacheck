Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Case line lists, which record dates of onset, recovery, and hospitalisation, are among the most granular data in infectious disease epidemiology and can provide insights into transmission dynamics. Dynamic survival analysis (DSA) fits a deterministic, differential-equationbased model to line lists to estimate parameters such as infectivity and the infectious period. Infectious periods are usually not exponential, and incorporating this into mechanistic models for line-list data often requires partial differential equation (PDE) schemes that can be difficult to implement and must be customised for each model, which may limit their use.

Here, we propose Discrete Dynamic Survival Analysis (DDSA), a discrete-time DSA for case line lists based on difference equations. It matches calendar-day data directly without interpolation, and its log-likelihood is differentiable via forward-mode automatic differentiation, enabling off-the-shelf gradient-based samplers without model-specific numerical infrastructure. Non-exponential infectious periods are accommodated through a boxcar model or a discrete analogue of the "device of stages". We apply the DDSA method to three historical outbreaks of influenza A(H1N1), Ebola, and measles. DDSA estimates of transmission parameters were close to published analyses where these were available, an agreement that partly reflects shared modelling choices, and the method handles incomplete line lists with missing onset or recovery dates. Synthetic experiments show that recovery intervals identify the infectious period, while infection times and final size constrain R 0 when the population at risk is known. Discrete DSA provides a simple, extensible framework for simulation and inference of infectious disease dynamics.

1 Introduction

Mathematical models can offer insights into the transmission of infectious disease, allowing us to estimate key epidemiological quantities such as the reproductive number, forecast the number of new cases [1], and explore counterfactual scenarios, such as the impact of school closures or vaccination campaigns [2, 3]. Many models use aggregated case data i.e., counts of new cases per day or week, to infer model parameters. However, deeper insights can be gained through the use of case line lists, which record individual-level event times such as symptom onset, hospitalisation, recovery, or death [4, 5]. While aggregate counts reveal general trends, line lists provide detailed tracking of individual events, allowing the estimation of key time dynamics, such as the infectious period.

In current practice, line-list data are analysed in several ways: direct survival analysis with parametric (e.g., exponential, lognormal or Weibull) natural-history interval models, which lack a mechanistic link to population-level transmission dynamics [6, 7]. Individual-based simulation models, which can capture fine-scale contact structure but are computationally intensive and difficult to calibrate via gradient-based methods [8, 9], and aggregation of the line list to daily counts, discarding individual-level timing information [10, 11]. Each method presents trade-offs in realism, computational feasibility, and data accuracy.

KhudaBukhsh et al. [12] proposed a modelling paradigm, originally introduced as Survival Dynamical Systems (SDS) but now known as Dynamic Survival Analysis (DSA) [13] [14] [15], to fit dynamical models of disease transmission to line lists using concepts borrowed from survival analysis. By assuming a large population size, DSA simplifies the analysis of line lists by using a mean-field model described by differential equations to compute the hazard of infection over time, and applying standard survival analysis techniques to estimate natural history parameters of infection. This method has subsequently been extended to consider non-exponential distributions of key quantities such as the infectious period [13] and the impact of repeat testing in discrete time [16]. Variants of DSA have been used in multiple contexts, including Influenza A(H1N1) on a college campus [12], foot and mouth disease in the UK [13], Ebola in DRC [14, 17], and COVID-19 at a national level [13], at a state level [18, 19], in an individual prison [20], and on a college campus [16].

For simple models, such as the standard SIR, DSA involves solving a system of ordinary differential equations (ODEs) to compute the hazard of infection, which can be obtained using standard numerical solvers [12]. The assumption of exponentially distributed infectious periods in these models is, however, often unrealistic. Incorporating non-exponential distributions into DSA requires reformulating the transmission model using partial differential equations (PDEs) [13], which in turn typically require custom discretisation schemes. Together, these requirements may limit the adoption of DSA in practice.

Most studies using DSA to date have employed a continuous time, differential equation based model to compute the infection hazard. However, data such as symptom onset times and recovery times are normally recorded in the form of dates, thus continuous-time likelihoods must either interpolate the model's solution or treat observed dates as approximations to continuous event times. However, data such as symptom onset and recovery times are typically recorded as dates. Thus, continuous-time likelihoods must either interpolate the model's solution or treat observed dates as approximations of continuous event times. Wascher et al. [16] avoided this in their interval DSA, which uses a discrete-time Reed-Frost-type SEIR model to estimate prevalence and time-varying transmission from repeated SARS-CoV-2 testing in a closed population. Their model assumes a known population size and fixed sojourn-time distributions, and is fitted with a bespoke Metropolis-Hastings-within-Gibbs sampler that imputes individual infection and sojourn times. More recently, Roy et al. [15] addressed this challenge for temporally aggregated count data by deriving a closed-form marginal likelihood that treats daily or weekly incidence as interval-censored observations in continuous time. Here, we propose Discrete Dynamic Survival Analysis (DDSA), a discrete-time DSA for case line lists in which the population at risk can be either known or estimated, and extend it to non-geometric infectious period distributions and multiple groups, such as different age groups. The primary aim of this study is to make DSA easier to apply to line lists recorded by date. In discrete time, the likelihood is evaluated on the calendar days of the data, and such infectious periods are captured without potentially complex, model-specific numerical schemes, so that every model can be fitted with standard tools for Bayesian inference. We implement the framework in Julia using Turing.jl [21], and demonstrate inference on simulated and real-world line list data.

In DDSA, a recurrence map replaces the ODE and advances the epidemic state one step at a time, on the same time grid as the dated records. No solver is needed, and the map is compatible with forward-mode automatic differentiation, allowing the application of Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithms such as the No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS) [22] directly. In discrete time, infectious periods that are not exponential can be modelled using discrete phase-type (DPH) distributions, which are specified by finite sub-stochastic matrices [23]. They include the negative binomial and distributions with finite support, and can approximate others. Their probability mass functions reduce to repeated matrix-vector products and are likewise differentiable with respect to the DPH parameters. Time-varying parameters, for example a contact rate that changes after an intervention, can enter the map as one value per step (β k ), without changing how the likelihood is evaluated. The result is a complete inference pipeline for line list data, including flexible dwell-time distributions and multi-group population structure, that can be expressed concisely in a standard probabilistic programming language without a custom numerical framework.

To meet this aim, we (i) derive the discrete-time DSA log-likelihood and show that it converges to the continuous-time formulation as ∆t → 0; (ii) extend it to non-geometric infectious periods through two representations, a boxcar (infection-age) model and a discrete analogue of the 'device of stages'; (iii) extend it to populations with multiple epidemiological groups; (iv) implement the full framework in Julia by fitting every model variant using the NUTS sampler in Turing.jl; and (v) assess what each part of the likelihood identifies, and how DDSA compares with continuous-time DSA, on synthetic data and on simulated outbreaks with known parameters. We demonstrate DDSA through applications to line list data from three real outbreaks: influenza A(H1N1) at a university campus [12], Ebola in the Democratic Republic of the Congo [14], and measles in Hagelloch, Germany, 1861 [24].

Discrete-time generalized linear chain trick (GLCT map).

The Linear Chain Trick (LCT) replaces the single infectious compartment I with k exponential sub-stages, each governed by the same rate γ, resulting in an Erlang-distributed infectious period within an ODE system. Hurtado and Kirosingh [30] extended this to the Generalized Linear Chain Trick (GLCT), which allows for varying rates per stage, thereby enabling the representation of any distribution in the phase-type family (e.g., exponential, Erlang, Coxian, or hyperexponential). The theoretical foundation is the "device of stages" by Cox and Miller [31], which builds non-exponential waiting times from memoryless stages in series or parallel. A DPH with r stages and mean m > r steps has CV2 ≥ 1/r -1/m, attained by the negative binomial [23, 32]. The device-of-stages formulation may require fewer compartments than the boxcar model and avoids the need to truncate the infection-age distribution. However, it requires committing to a specific parametric family: the number of stages r and their associated progression probabilities must be specified or inferred, rather than being determined directly from the data. 2(c)(i) Synthetic data

Synthetic line lists were generated from the discrete GLCT map with four geometric stages (r = 4) at ∆t = 1 d, each discretising an exponential stage with rate λ = 1 d -1 (continuoustime mean 4 d), with β = 0.5 day -1 and N = 99. The values to recover were R0 = 3.16 and a mean infectious period of W = 6.33 d. This mean exceeds the continuous-time mean because a geometric stage lasts longer on average than the exponential stage it discretises, and the two agree as ∆t → 0 (Supplementary Section S1.4). The difference matters when comparing discrete-time and continuous-time quantities. For example, fixing a continuous-time removal rate at 1/4 d -1 biases R 0 (Section 3(b)).

2(c)(ii) Historical case studies

We used three historical outbreak line lists, each chosen to illustrate a different aspect of the DDSA framework (Table 3). All analyses assumed that the recorded cases were a random sample of infections in the population at risk, so whether a case was recorded did not depend on its infection date or infectious period [12, 13]. Unrecorded cases then lower the estimated population size N , which is why N is read as an effective size, but leave the infection-time and recovery terms unchanged.

H1N1 WSU, 2009. The source dataset contains 2,276 rows. KhudaBukhsh et al. reconstructed individual infection times from daily counts. Of the sampled recovery times, 13 fell after the end of the observation window (day 104) and were treated as right-censored.

Ebola DRC, 2018. 2(d) Implementation

All analyses were implemented in the Julia programming language [37] using the probabilistic programming framework Turing.jl [21] for Bayesian inference. The code and notebooks for each analysis are archived on Zenodo [38]. DDSA is also available as a Julia package DiscreteDy-namicSurvivalAnalysis.jl [39], whose vignettes reproduce these analyses.

The analyses were implemented by evaluating the discrete recurrences from Section 2(b) directly within the likelihood, with continuous transition rates converted to per-step event probabilities via expressions of the form p = 1 -e -λ ∆t . As a result, the log-posterior remains differentiable in the continuous parameters, allowing the use of gradient-based inference in Turing.jl.

Priors and bounds for every analysis are listed in Supplementary Section S1. Integer-valued parameters had discrete uniform priors and were summed out of the likelihood.

Uniform priors were adopted in settings where epidemiologically motivated bounds could be specified from the data context. For transmission and removal rates, upper bounds were chosen to exceed plausible values for the pathogen and population, while lower bounds preclude zero or near-zero rates that are inconsistent with an observed outbreak. For initial infected fractions, a Beta prior concentrated near zero reflects the expectation of a small founding seed. These uniform priors assign equal mass within their support and do not penalise values near the bounds, which can affect posterior shape when data are sparse. Sensitivity to the prior bounds is examined in Supplementary Section S2.3. However, log-normal or half-normal alternatives for rate parameters are a direction for future work.

All DDSA analyses used the same sampler settings: NUTS with four chains of 2,000 postwarm-up draws after 1,000 warm-up iterations, with 4,000 draws for the NegBin DPH and Boxcar fits of Scenario B. Chains were started from jittered data-based values, as chains started from prior draws occasionally became trapped in low-density regions in replicated simulations. The convergence targets were R < 1.01 and ESS bulk ≥ 100 per parameter [40]. The count-based comparators of Section 3(b) were sampled with longer warm-up and started from the prior. Full sampler settings and starting values are given in Supplementary Section S5.1. Gradients were computed with ForwardDiff [41] via the default Turing.jl automatic differentiation backend.

To handle non-exponential infectious periods, we implemented a self-contained phase-type module in the accompanying code, which integrates continuous phase-type (CPH) and discrete phase-type (DPH) families within the standard Distributions.jl interface [42]. 3(a) Data availability: inference from partial line lists

The contribution of each likelihood term was quantified by fitting DDSA to the synthetic dataset of Section 2(c)(i) under three data scenarios: (A) the full line list (ℓ I +ℓ R +ℓ constr ); (B) infection times only (ℓ I + ℓ constr ); and (C) recovery intervals only (ℓ R ). In Scenarios A and B, GLCT and Boxcar models with a NegBin DPH and a geometric model were fitted. In Scenario C, the GLCT and Boxcar models are identical, so one NegBin DPH model and a geometric model were fitted. The phase count r was summed out of the likelihood (Supplementary Section S1.4).

Results are summarised in Table 4 and Figure 1. Recovery intervals alone (Scenario C) recovered the mean infectious period, with r at its cap of 4, which equalled the true value here, while β was not identified. A geometric fit to the same intervals produced a similar mean with a wider range, but its predicted interval distribution decreased consistently. It missed the observed peak at 6-7 d and placed about 37 of the 92 intervals at 1-3 d, where none were observed (Figure 2, bottom row). With infection times only (Scenario B), R 0 was constrained, with intervals that included the true values for the GLCT and Boxcar fits. The full line list (Scenario A) returned narrow posteriors for all parameters, with the GLCT and Boxcar posteriors overlapping closely.

In the model using the geometric distribution (r ≡ 1), the misspecification did not show up in the infection-time posterior predictive checks (Figure 2). However, it biased the estimates depending on which data were available. With the full line list, the recovery intervals held the mean infectious period near its true value, so the wrong shape was absorbed by β and R0 was inflated by 14%. In the case of infection times only, R 0 was held by the final size and stayed close to the value implied by the observed attack rate of 0.92 under the final-size relation (R0 ≈ 2.74), with an interval that contained the true value. 3(b) DDSA vs count-based compartmental inference

This analysis explores the benefits of recording individual recovery times in addition to incidence. DDSA was compared with a continuous-time compartmental ODE fitted to aggregated daily incidence counts under Poisson or Negative-Binomial observation models [43], on the synthetic outbreak of Section 3(a). The count model was fitted using four different configurations: an exponential infectious period with a Poisson likelihood, an exponential infectious period with a Negative-Binomial likelihood, a staged Erlang-4 infectious period with a Negative-Binomial likelihood, and an exponential infectious period with the removal rate fixed at 1/µ (where µ = 4 d is the mean of the continuous-time Erlang-4 infectious period from which the synthetic DPH is built). The count models relied only on daily incidence counts. The first three models estimated the removal rate jointly with the transmission rate. In contrast, DDSA used the complete line list information, which included the infection day and recovery interval for each case, along with the final count of cases out of the 99 individuals at risk. All summaries were compared on the calendar-day scale.

With the removal rate estimated, the three count models gave posterior medians for R 0 of 4.6-7.5 (truth 3.16), with 95% credible intervals within [1.76, 20.16], and mean infectious periods with 95% credible intervals within [1.7, 47.2] d. This ambiguity persisted regardless of whether the shape of the infectious period was correctly staged (Figure 3). Fixing the mean infectious period at 4 d narrowed the R 0 posterior but biased it below the truth (R0 = 2.75 [2.36, 3.15]), because the assumed rate overestimated removal relative to the effective discrete infectious period of 6.33 d.

DDSA, which also uses the recovery intervals and final size, recovered β, the mean infectious period and R 0 (Table 4, Scenario A, GLCT). Every draw used r = 4, the true value and the largest allowed by the shortest recorded interval of 4 d, so this concentration reflects both the cap and the data (Supplementary Section S2.3). All five models reproduced the observed incidence curve (Figure 3A), so they differed in what they identified.

Adjusting one factor at a time (see Supplementary Section S2) showed that the loss of information on R 0 in the count models during this outbreak with a high attack rate (91 out of 99 infected individuals; the loss was smaller at moderate attack rates) can be attributed to a single likelihood term. Specifically, the Poisson and Negative Binomial count models do not include the never-infected term (N -K) log(1 -τ ) found in the Binomial final size equation. A count model with a never-infected cell recovered the DDSA estimate of R 0 from infection times alone (Scenario B), with either the discrete map or an ODE. The incidence curve fixes the early growth rate, along which R 0 and the mean infectious period trade off [26, 44], and the recovery intervals, which counts do not contain, fix the position along this ridge. In one simulated outbreak at each size up to 10,000, the count models moved towards the truth as the population grew, with the discrete map and an ODE giving similar R 0 . However, the never-infected count gave DDSA much narrower credible intervals across all population sizes (as detailed in Supplementary Section S2).

DDSA was applied to the line list from the 2009 H1N1 outbreak at Washington State University, the dataset analysed by KhudaBukhsh et al. Recovery intervals, recorded in whole days (Section 2(c)(ii)), entered the likelihood through the geometric PMF of Eq. The observation window begins mid-epidemic, implying a substantial initial infected fraction ρ, and the population size N at risk is unknown. N enters only through the final-size constraint, where it is confounded with the attack rate τ (β, γ, ρ). Priors were therefore placed on γ (centred on a mean infectious period of 5.5 d) and on N (bounded by the WSU student body [34]; Supplementary Table S1), and all four parameters (N , β, γ, ρ) were estimated jointly. [12] conditioned their likelihood on n (initially susceptible population) and imputed it at each iteration of a hybrid Gibbs sampler as n ∼ NegBinom(K, τ ), with τ given by the deterministic final-size equation. 3(e) Measles, Hagelloch, Germany, 1861

The 1861 measles outbreak in Hagelloch, Germany, comprised K = 188 cases over 86 days [24]. Infectious-period durations were computed as δ i = (rash dateprodrome date) + 4 days, reflecting the assumption of Neal and Roberts [36] that children were infectious from one day before the prodrome until three days after the rash appeared. These durations had a mean of 7.95 days and a coefficient of variation of 0.22, below that of a geometric distribution with the same mean (0.94), so the infectious period was modelled with a Negative Binomial DPH. Since every duration is at least δ min = 4 days, an offset Negative Binomial DPH was used. This model consisted of δ min -r deterministic one-day stages followed by r geometric stages with progression probability p. Its support starts at δ min , its mean is δ = δ min -r + r/p, and it requires that r ≤ δ min . The model sampled δ and set p = r/(δ -δ min + r), so that δ keeps its meaning across values of r. It was assumed that a single index case from Class 2 initiated the outbreak, so the initial infectious fraction was fixed at ρ = 1/N 2 for Class 2 and zero for Classes 0 and 1. The line list partitions the population into three classes: pre-school children (Class 0, K0 = 90), younger school students (Class 1, K1 = 30), and older school students (Class 2, K2 = 68, including the index case). Between-group transmission was captured using a 3×3 who-acquiresinfection-from-whom (WAIFW) matrix B, where B ij is the per-day transmission rate to group i from group j:

The per-step force of infection on group i is then 1 -exp(-Λ i ), where Λ i = ∑︁ j B ij I j and I j denotes the infectious fraction of group j. Within-class transmission is governed by β C1 (Class 1) and β C2 (Class 2), a global term β G acts across all groups, and a school-topreschool term β H captures transmission from infectious school students to pre-school children. This structure is a mean-field, group-level approximation of the individual-level contact model of Neal and Roberts [36] in its homogeneous-mixing form (their M θ model without distance decay). The rates β C1 , β C2 and β G correspond to their classroom and global routes, and β H acts as a proxy for their same-household term. In their model, the household term is applied between any two members of the same household. However, as the compartmental model does not represent this, a single household rate was restricted to the school-to-preschool direction. This assumption was based on the pre-school group forming a secondary wave in the line list, in which most cases were attributed to school-age children (53 of 90), 41 of them within the same family [24]. Preschool-to-school and within-preschool household transmission therefore enter through β G.

The GLCT map was the primary analysis, as it represents the same Negative Binomial family with a smaller state vector than the Boxcar map, which was fitted as an implementation cross-check. The integer-valued parameters (r, N 0 , N 1 , N 2 ) were summed out of the likelihood. The class sizes were inferred through Binomial final-size constraints, with prior ranges bounded by the 197 children under 14 recorded by Pfeilsticker [24] (Supplementary Table S3). Neal and Roberts [36] instead fixed the population sizes to match the case count (188 individuals, all infected), which assumes a 100% attack rate.

The phase count reached its upper limit, with r = min i δ i = 4, in nearly every draw, indicating that the cap was binding. At r = 4, the recovery-interval likelihood was still increasing, and the estimated infectious period showed more variability than the recorded durations (Supplementary Section S4.3). The Boxcar fit used the same law and has the same cap, so the agreement between the two fits checks the implementation rather than the law. A binding cap can bias R 0 upward (Supplementary Section S4). The fit gave β C1 > β C2 with P (β C1 > β C2 ) = 0.996, in qualitative agreement with Neal and Roberts [36], who found strong evidence of transmission within classroom 1 and weaker evidence of transmission within classroom 2. For Class 1, the model-implied attack rate closely matched the observed one (Table 8), whereas attack rates for Classes 0 and 2 were underpredicted by about 15%. The fitted epidemics also peaked closer together in time than observed. The model peaked on day 26 in Class 1 and on day 28 in Classes 0 and 2, whereas the largest daily counts fell on days 22, 35 and 32 in Classes 1, 0 and 2 (Figure 7). These differences were consistent with the mean-field approximation, which averages over children with heterogeneous, localised household exposures. Finally, the posteriors for N 0 and N 2 concentrated near their upper bounds, and the total across the three classes, about 210, exceeded the number of children who could have been infected. This may reflect the smaller final size of the mean-field model rather than a larger population at risk. Fixing the class sizes at the case counts gave a similar R 0 (2.17) and the same ordering β C1 > β C2.

4 Discussion

DDSA offers a method to estimate key epidemiological parameters, including the reproduction number, the distribution of the infectious period, and the effective size of the population at risk, directly from line lists. It reformulates the survival likelihood of dynamic survival analysis [12, 13] in discrete time, so that individual infection times, infectious-period durations and final size are evaluated on the calendar-day grid of the line list. DDSA has three practical advantages. (i) No continuous solution has to be interpolated onto observed dates. (ii) The infection-time PMF and the discrete phase-type recovery distribution are computed exactly by iterating the recurrence map. As a result, a non-geometric infectious period requires neither a numerical PDE solve nor a numerical convolution. (iii) The likelihood can therefore be written directly in a standard probabilistic programming language and sampled with off-the-shelf NUTS, without model-specific numerical code. Continuous-time DSA can also be sampled with NUTS, as our re-implementations were (Supplementary Section S3), but only with the addition of ODE and PDE solvers, which the discrete maps replace. DDSA's estimates are comparable to those of continuous-time DSA. On simulated outbreaks, the two methods agreed except when the daily step was coarse relative to epidemic growth (Supplementary Section S3; Limitations). The continuous-time re-implementations used for this comparison were checked against the published codes [12, 13]. Computational speed was not assessed in this study, and a comparison of sampling efficiency (ESS per second) with continuous-time DSA is reserved for future work. Synthetic experiments based on one outbreak of 99 people, with checks at larger population sizes (Supplementary Section S2), suggest that the infection-time and recovery components of the likelihood play different inferential roles (Table 5). Infection times determine the early growth rate, along which R 0 and the mean infectious period trade off [26, 44]. When the at-risk population is known, the number of never-infected individuals bounds R 0 . Recovery intervals identify the infectious period, which, together with the growth rate, fixes R 0 . Its shape is identified within the one-stage-per-day NegBin DPH used here, whose number of stages cannot exceed the shortest recorded interval (Limitations). These roles also determine where a misspecified infectious period becomes visible. Under a geometric infectious period, the incidence curves remained visually similar to those of the correctly specified DPH models. Nevertheless, the estimates were biased in a direction set by the available data. With the full line list, the recovery intervals held the mean infectious period near its true value, and R 0 was inflated by 14%.

With infection times only, the final size held R 0 near the value implied by the observed attack rate (posterior median 2.70, against 2.74 from the final-size relation; truth 3.16, inside the 95% CrI), while β was inflated by 60% and the mean infectious period was roughly halved. Only the recovery intervals revealed the misspecification. The geometric fit missed their peak at 6-7 d and placed about 37 of the 92 intervals at 1-3 d, where none were observed (Figure 2, bottom row). In this example, therefore, checks of the incidence curve did not detect a misspecified infectious-period shape, whereas a check of the recovery intervals did.

Comparing DDSA with count models separates what is lost by aggregating the data from what is lost by choosing a count likelihood. In this high-attack-rate outbreak, Poisson and Negative-Binomial count models did not identify R 0 because they omit the individuals who were never infected, not because the data were aggregated. With N known, a count model with a never-infected cell recovered the DDSA estimate of R 0 from infection times alone, without recovery intervals and without fixing the removal rate. At lower attack rates, a Poisson count model also constrained R 0 , although less precisely (Supplementary Section S2), so this loss should not be generalised to all incidence-only analyses. Without the never-infected count, R 0 was constrained closely only once the removal rate was fixed, consistent with KhudaBukhsh et al. [12] and Putter et al. [45], who also fixed the recovery-time distribution because only incidence was observed. The estimate was then only as accurate as the fixed rate. Here, an assumed mean of 4 d biased R 0 below the truth. Aggregation does matter for the infectious period. Without recovery intervals, its shape is not identified, and its mean is identified only weakly, through R 0 and the growth rate. This follows from data resolution rather than from the likelihood formulation. Roy et al. [15] developed a marginal likelihood for count data that treats summarised counts as interval-censored survival observations in continuous time. When the population at risk is known, our count model with a never-infected cell is equivalent to their likelihood (Supplementary Section S2). The two approaches are complementary. Countbased DSA needs only incidence data, which are more widely available, whereas line lists add individual recovery intervals, which identify the infectious period directly. Where data collection can be planned, these results suggest recording, in addition to onset dates, the date each case stops being infectious (or a removal proxy such as hospitalisation) and the size of the population at risk.

Surveillance line lists are rarely complete. In the analyses considered here, the DDSA likelihood served as the core observation model. It was supplemented with survivor terms for right-censored recoveries in the H1N1 case study, and with a convolution over latent onset times for hospitalisation-only Ebola records. Both are discrete counterparts of terms in the continuoustime DSA likelihood [12, 13]. Records without a recovery or removal date contributed only to the infection-time and final-size terms, as in Scenario B and for the Ebola onset-only records. This allows incomplete line lists to be handled within the same general framework, without ad hoc imputation.

The case studies benchmark the DDSA inference framework against published continuoustime DSA analyses of the same data. They do not test whether an SIR model describes these outbreaks accurately. For H1N1 at WSU, fitting the line list analysed by KhudaBukhsh et al. [12] reproduced the mean recorded recovery interval and gave a transmission rate similar to theirs, with a slightly lower reproduction number and a larger effective population size. How the population size was treated explains much of these differences (Section 3(c); Supplementary Table S11). Across the three Ebola waves, DDSA reproduced the trends of the continuous-time DSA fit of Vossler et al. [14]. The removal rate γ rose from Wave 1 to Wave 3, a shortening of onset-to-hospitalisation, while β did not fall. Posterior medians suggest R 0 was highest in Wave 2, but this ordering depends on the removal definition and on credible intervals that likely understate uncertainty. These agreements partly reflect shared modelling choices. Both analyses use a mean-field SIR model with the same removal definition, and for H1N1 the recovery times were themselves generated by KhudaBukhsh et al. from the exponential infectious period (mean 5.5 d) assumed in their analysis.

The Hagelloch case study was used to stress-test DDSA on a heterogeneous, group-structured outbreak, where school-centred and within-household transmission depart from homogeneous mixing. The three-group model needed only a 3 × 3 transmission matrix in the same recurrence map. It reproduced the qualitative finding of the individual-level analysis of Neal and Roberts [36], with more transmission within classroom 1 than within classroom 2 (P (β C1 > β C2 ) = 0.996). The GLCT and Boxcar implementations gave closely matching summaries (R0 = 2.17), an internal check that the two discrete representations agree when the dwell-time family is shared. The stress test also exposed the limits of the mean-field approximation in this setting. The model underpredicted the attack rates of Classes 0 and 2, brought the class epidemics closer together in time than observed, and placed N 0 and N 2 near their upper bounds. The stage cap was also binding (Section 3(e)).

More generally, other compartmental structures can be accommodated within DDSA provided the mean-field basis of the survival likelihood is preserved. Additional compartments and further stratification into demographic groups or spatial patches, for example, all follow from the same difference-equation construction, as the Hagelloch model illustrates. These structures assume a closed population in which each individual is infected at most once. As a result, the susceptible population S k only decreases, and the final-size term applies. Extending DDSA to endemic transmission, with births, waning immunity or reinfection, would require changes to the map and to the likelihood. The map would need inflows to the susceptible class and initial conditions near the endemic state rather than a small seed. Additionally, since S k would no longer only decrease, the infection-time distribution would be constructed based on the per-step incidence S k h k rather than relying on the differences in S k . In this context, individual survival would be defined by the hazard h k from the moment a person becomes susceptible. Furthermore, the final-size term would be replaced by a term representing the number of cases within the observation window, reflecting the person-time at risk. The only term that may remain unchanged would be the recovery-interval term. These extensions were not implemented here and are left for future work.

5 Limitations

The case studies highlight limitations of data fidelity and observational proxies. Not all event times used in the case studies were independently observed. In the H1N1 dataset, recovery times were generated using an exponential infectious period with a mean of 5.5 d, so that case study is best interpreted as a demonstration of the discrete inference framework under inherited assumptions, and the estimate of γ as a consistency check. It was retained as it provides a direct comparison with the survival-analytic approach of KhudaBukhsh et al. [12]. Additionally, in the Ebola analyses, hospitalisation was used as a proxy for removal. The inferred infectious period should therefore be read as a model-based removal interval tied to the observation process, and the R 0 values are conditional on that definition and on the observation window. For the same reason, the population sizes N , N w and N 0 -N 2 are best read as effective at-risk sizes within the chosen model and window, not as total population sizes. The recording of a case is part of the same observation process. Cases missed at a constant rate are absorbed into these effective sizes (Section 2(c)(ii)), although in Hagelloch, where the class sizes were bounded, missed cases would also have lowered the estimated R 0 . However, if recording practices changed during an outbreak, for example as case finding improved, or if they depended on the duration or severity of a person's illness, this would affect the infection-time curve or the recorded infectious periods. Such changes were not modelled here, and would be hard to separate from changes in transmission using the line list alone.

The transmission models rely on simplifying assumptions that DDSA shares with continuoustime DSA. Mean-field forces of infection average over individual-level heterogeneity in contacts, which can be a poor match when transmission is concentrated in households or classrooms. In Hagelloch, for example, the group-level model underpredicted the attack rates of Classes 0 and 2 by about 15% and brought the class epidemics closer together in time than observed. The analyses also assume a closed population over the observation window, which suits single outbreaks but not endemic transmission. The same assumptions affect how uncertainty is represented. The predictive bands reflect only parameter uncertainty, so they do not include day-to-day variation, which a chain-binomial version of the GLCT or Boxcar map would add. In addition, the infection-time likelihood treats infections as independent draws from the meanfield curve. In simulated epidemics, intervals for the infectious period, informed by individual recovery intervals, were generally close to nominal. Credible intervals for R 0 were narrower than nominal even with the correct model. Nominal 95% intervals covered the true R 0 in 17-80% of outbreaks, least often near R0 = 1, whereas coverage was close to nominal on data drawn independently from the mean-field curve (Supplementary Section S4). The R 0 intervals of the Ebola waves, whose R 0 is close to 1, may therefore understate the uncertainty. Methods that may correct the R 0 intervals are outside the scope of this study.

The discrete-time formulation also carries a predictable step-size dependence. At ∆t = 1 d the DPH means exceed the corresponding continuous-time Erlang means, so estimates must be converted before they are compared with continuous-time ones. In simulations, the daily step also affected estimates in two ways (Supplementary Section S4.2). When continuous-time infectious periods were recorded by day, same-day removals became one-day intervals, which biased the mean infectious period upward by about 2%. When the epidemic grew quickly, R 0 was biased upward. At R0 = 4, DDSA estimates exceeded those of continuous-time DSA by 7-9% by the end of the outbreak. A finer time step removed the first bias and reduced the second.

The GLCT map, built stage by stage, allows at most one stage transition per day, so r cannot exceed the shortest recorded interval, and a single short interval can set this limit. In simulations, this cap overstated the variability of the infectious period and biased R 0 upward (Supplementary Section S4.3). The Boxcar map accepts a law without this minimum and so has no such cap, although its R 0 intervals remained narrower than nominal at the daily step. Coxian or branched DPHs (Section 2(b)(ii)), in which an early exit or a branch with few stages can take the short intervals, may relax the cap and allow a less variable infectious period, but fitting them was left for future work.

Each analysis uses a single ∆t, so data recorded at different reporting intervals would need to be aggregated or entered as interval-censored terms. As the present case studies use uniform daily cadences, neither adjustment was necessary.

Finally, on the computational side, all DDSA analyses in the main text used one set of sampler settings (Section 2(d)), which met the convergence targets here but has not been tested more widely. Summing integer-valued parameters out of the likelihood is exact, but its cost grows with the summed support whenever the parameter changes the recurrence map. For instance, the Hagelloch model evaluates the map for each of the 40 combinations of r and N 2 at every gradient, which makes it the most computationally demanding analysis. Other automatic-differentiation backends may perform differently, but these were not tested.

6 Conclusions

We have presented Discrete Dynamic Survival Analysis (DDSA), a discrete-time reformulation of dynamic survival analysis for calendar-day epidemic line lists. The DDSA likelihood has the same three components as continuous-time DSA (infection times, recovery intervals and final size), but each is a discrete analogue computed from a difference-equation map that replaces the continuous-time ODE. Infection times enter through a Multinomial distribution over calendar days, recovery intervals through discrete phase-type distributions, and the final size through a Binomial term. These components converge to their continuous-time counterparts as ∆t → 0, and each is computed exactly by iterating the map, without solver-based interpolation or custom numerical schemes for non-geometric infectious periods. They are differentiable in the continuous parameters, so gradient-based inference runs in standard probabilistic programming frameworks without model-specific numerical code.

Applied to three historical outbreaks of influenza, Ebola, and measles, under both homogeneous and group-structured compartmental models, DDSA accommodated incomplete line lists through composable likelihood terms for right-censored recoveries and missing onset times. Where published continuous-time DSA analyses were available (influenza and Ebola), its estimates were similar, except for the effective population size and reproduction number for H1N1. This agreement partly reflects shared modelling choices. Synthetic experiments showed that each type of data plays a different inferential role. Infection times determine the growth rate, the number never infected bounds R 0 when the population at risk is known, and recovery intervals identify the infectious period. As a result, a misspecified infectious-period shape biased the estimates while leaving the incidence fit visibly unchanged. Only the recovery intervals revealed it. When recovery intervals were observed, the misspecification inflated the estimated reproduction number. Without them, it inflated the transmission rate and shortened the infectious period.

By keeping discrete-time inference aligned with the calendar-day structure of line-list data while remaining expressible in standard probabilistic programming tools, DDSA offers a transparent and extensible route to fitting mean-field epidemic models of single outbreaks to individual-level records.
