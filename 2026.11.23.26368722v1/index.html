Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Abstract

Sickle cell disease (SCD) is associated with widespread multi-organ comorbidity, but these outcomes have never been quantified comprehensively against a population without hemoglobinopathies or hemolytic anemias. That gap limits clinical appreciation of the breadth of SCDassociated illness and, because burden-of-disease frameworks require exposure-outcome effect sizes, it also leaves SCD systematically under-counted in global health estimates. Using longitudinal utilization and demographic data from MarketScan for 2016 to 2019, we fitted Bayesian meta-regressions of comparative rates of 30 acute and chronic diseases among individuals with and without an SCD diagnosis, by age and sex. Individuals with SCD had elevated risk of nearly every outcome, with the highest relative risk (RR) and incidence rate ratio (IRR; per person-year) generally in the youngest age groups. This included well-established problems such as chronic pain (RR=12.0, 95% UI 11.0-13.0), but also conditions in which impaired perfusion or persistent vascular injury may be causal: preeclampsia (IRR=9.7, 7.8-12.1), postpartum hemorrhage (IRR=13.6, 11.2-16.1), pulmonary hypertension (RR=96.3, 85.9-108.1), acute myocardial infarction (IRR=28.7, 24.5-34.3), ischemic stroke (RR=16.7, 15.0-18.6), kidney disease (RR=11.9, 10.9-13.0), asthma (RR=3.21, 2.98-3.44) and lower respiratory infections (IRR=2.85, 2.69-3.02). These results supply the inputs needed to quantify indirect SCD burden in comparative risk assessment frameworks. These results establish SCD as a whole-body risk factor rather than a single-organ disease, which should inform treatment guidelines and population-level health programs in settings where SCD is endemic.

Introduction

Sickle cell disease (SCD) refers to an autosomal recessive disorder of hemoglobin that affects millions of people worldwide. [1] [2] [3] Depending on genetic, socioeconomic, and environmental factors, people born with SCD experience subsequent health complications, ranging from more frequent acute events to chronic complications that can lead to multi-organ failure and early death. [2, 4, 5] Over 1,000 babies are born with SCD in the US annually, where increasing life expectancy of those with SCD has led to steady increases in the size of the SCD population between 2000 and 2021. [6] Life expectancy of people with SCD has increased substantially in recent decades due to advances in medical knowledge and technology, alongside novel therapies. In the US, life expectancy of individuals with SCD increased from 20 years of age in 1970 to 52 years between 2008 and 2016. [7, 8] Nevertheless, mortality among pregnant women with SCD remains 10-26 times greater than in pregnant women without SCD, a statistic which has not improved, even when compared to decades-old data. [9] [10] [11] Hydroxyurea remains the most widely available and primary treatment for SCD. [12] [13] [14] Transformative gene therapy treatments are available, though likely out of reach for the vast majority of patients-with US list prices of approximately $2 million per treatment. [15] Consequently, there is a heightened prevalence of chronic comorbidities within this population, underscoring the need to understand the magnitude of increased disease risk.

Sickle cell hemoglobin (HbS) polymerization causes malformed red blood cells and is a defining feature of SCD. The sickling of red blood cells causes obstructed blood flow, hemolysis, and other cellular abnormalities that reduce oxygen delivery to tissues, leading to ischemia, chronic organ damage, and eventual tissue death and organ failure. Several narrative reviews have detailed the array of complications associated with SCD, including but not limited to neurological, cardiovascular, developmental, obstetric, ocular, bone, dermatological, renal, and infectious complications. [16] [17] [18] [19] Additionally, socioeconomic factors such as access to and utilization of affordable health care, and environmental factors like climate and air quality have been associated with the frequency and severity of SCD complications. [2, [20] [21] [22] The lack of a common understanding of SCD complications negatively affects patients' medical care, well-being, and survival. In particular, it is important to understand how the risk of complications varies over the lifespan, as patients may be especially vulnerable to lapses in treatment or preventative care in the transition from pediatric to adult care. [23] [24] [25] Using health-care utilization data among commercially insured individuals in the USA from 2016 to 2019, this study aimed to estimate the prevalence and incidence (per person-year) as well as the relative risks or incidence rate ratios for chronic and acute disease complications among individuals with SCD compared to those without SCD, overall and stratified by age and sex. We frame these estimates deliberately as exposure-outcome effect sizes rather than as a US descriptive profile because such effect sizes are the quantity that burden-ofdisease frameworks require in order to attribute non-hematologic disease to SCD. The meta-regression design allows for future data additions from other sources that can measure comparative risk from parts of the world with the highest prevalence of SCD.

Methods

Data sources

This study was completed as part of Global Burden of Diseases, Injuries, and Risk Factors Study which was reviewed and approved for waiver as non-human subjects research by the University of Washington Institutional Review Board (study number 9060). The requirement for informed consent was waived because the data were fully de-identified. Longitudinal health-care utilization and demographic information for years 2016 through 2019 were obtained from Truven MarketScan, a national health insurance claims database with de-identified state-level data for over 50 million beneficiaries with employer-sponsored health insurance. [26] Specifically, this included: (1) commercial claims and encounters database for privately insured and employer-insured persons and their dependents in the USA (by state) and (2) the Medicare supplemental and coordination of benefits database for Medicare-eligible retirees with employer-sponsored supplemental plans (also separate by state). This represents a convenience sample of the population of employed or formerly-employed individuals and their dependents in the USA. Non-physician encounters provided in the laboratory or pharmacy setting were excluded from outpatient services. For each facility (outpatient versus inpatient) and reporting year, we deduplicated data rows with identical patient identifier, service date, diagnosis position, and ICD-10 code.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All records with at least six months of continuous insurance enrollment across calendar years 2016 through 2019 were included. A minimum six-month follow-up was chosen to ensure sufficient data availability for each observation. There were no exclusion criteria related to pharmaceutical interventions. Person-time accrued while a beneficiary resided in a US territory was excluded, but individuals could still contribute person-time from calendar years in which they resided in a US state.

Identifying SCD cases and controls

Our primary exposure was SCD diagnosis. An SCD diagnosis was defined as having at least one inpatient visit or two outpatient visits with any derivatives of ICD-10 code D57, excluding D57.3 (sickle cell trait), an approach that aligns with previous studies of SCD using insurance claims data. [27, 28] An SCD diagnosis at any point during the study window qualified a patient as an SCD cohort member. To maximize the utilization of Marketscan, we also assigned each individual to a specific genotype -HbSS, HbSC, HbSβ+/HbSβ, and HbOS (all others) -with an algorithm adjudicating diagnosis disagreements. [6] Of note, however, because genotype assignment based on ICD-10 codes has not been validated these results are only shown in Supporting Information.

Patients in the control group (i.e., without SCD) were excluded in all analyses if they had chronic conditions similar to SCD by the mechanism of anemia (hemolysis). ICD-10 codes for exposure and control definitions are defined in S1 Appendix.

In addition to dichotomous assignment to the SCD or control group, we grouped all records by age strata (<20, 20-29, 30-39, 40-49, 50-59, and 60+ years), sex (male, female), and state of residence at enrollment into insurance program. The MarketScan database did not contain information on socioeconomic status, race, or ethnicity.

Outcome definitions

A total of 30 candidate outcomes were evaluated for association with SCD, including known direct, disease-defining sequelae of SCD (e.g., vaso-occlusive crises, stroke) and other conditions where SCD is hypothesized or has been reported to be a risk factor (e.g., kidney disorders, respiratory diseases, pregnancy complications). The MarketScan database did not contain mortality information and there were not enough data on certain complications of SCD (e.g., leg ulcer, acute chest syndrome) to perform analyses. Because anemia is a key feature of SCD and can improve after a transfusion, which we were unable to measure with our database, we did not include this outcome in our analysis.

Outcome-specific ICD-10 codes were informed by case-finding algorithms in published literature and are defined in S1 Appendix. For chronic disease outcomes, a diagnosis was defined as the presence of relevant ICD-10 codes in at least one inpatient visit or two outpatient visits on different service dates at any point during the follow-up. Following the initial diagnosis, the chronic disease was assumed present through the end of follow-up. For acute disease outcomes, a diagnosis was defined as the presence of relevant ICD-10 codes in at least one inpatient or one outpatient visit within a defined duration window (i.e., the duration of time following the first incidence of the acute diagnosis after which another diagnosis may reflect a new incident case for the same individual rather than a subsequent visit for the same disease episode). Multiple diagnosed cases could occur across the follow-up period. Diseases of pregnancy were measured among all women of reproductive age (i.e., aged 10-54 years), though estimates for females under age 20 and age 50 or older were censored due to small counts. Use of disease-modifying treatments, such as hydroxyurea, transfusions, or dialysis, was not measured as part of this study.

Data preparation and statistical modelling

Rate (prevalence or incidence) and ratio (relative risk or incidence rate ratio) models were fit independently. We calculated prevalence and incidence, respectively, as the number of diagnosed cases over the total number of individuals or person-years, where the presence of at least one diagnosis in a year was counted as one person-year, in the exposed or unexposed groups. A number of processing steps were completed prior to meta-regression. First, to avoid bias introduced by small sample sizes and zero counts in log-and logit-transformed data, we applied an offset to incidence and prevalence data, replacing zero prevalence or incidence rates with 0.5 times the lowest non-zero value, and replacing prevalence of one with one minus 0.5 times the highest non-zero value. We calculated the standard error of prevalence using the Wilson method and of incidence using the Poisson distribution method, considering the number of reported cases. [29] [30] [31] [32] The input data for RR and IRR models were calculated from the prevalence or incidence observations with SCD cases as numerator and controls as denominator. Certain outcomes were excluded from reporting if the number of all-age, both-sex cases for that group totaled fewer than 50 or, for ratios, if the all-age, both-sex rate in the exposed or unexposed group was less than 5 per 10,000.

The observations of age-, sex-, and state-specific prevalence and RRs (for chronic outcomes) and incidence rates and IRRs (for acute outcomes) were inputs to outcome-specific meta-regression with Bayesian priors, regularization, and trimming (MR-BRT) models, whose methodology has been detailed elsewhere. [33] For each SCD complication, we conducted three MR-BRT models with random slopes and intercepts for different states: (1) without any additional covariates (i.e., a "conventional" metaanalysis), (2) with sex as a categorical covariate, and (3) with sex and age as categorical covariates (i.e., allowing for non-linear age patterns). In the meta-regression models, each effect size was weighted by the inverse of its variance, leading to estimates with smaller uncertainty having more weight in the models. Each MR-BRT model yielded 500 random samples (assuming a normal distribution) using the point estimate and the uncertainty from both fixed and random effects.

Model evaluation and uncertainty

One of the characteristic strengths of MR-BRT is its ability to quantify uncertainty both using conventional uncertainty intervals as well as by incorporating the additional quantitative uncertainty arising from residual uncertainty of the input data and model fit. Incorporation of residual heterogeneity into assessing model fits serves as a powerful mechanism to evaluate overall strength of evidence supporting associations between exposures and outcomes. All uncertainty intervals shown in our results include residual heterogeneity.

The delta method was used to transform standard errors of a logit-transformed prevalence or log-transformed rate back to the linear scale. [34] The 2.5th, 50th, and 97.5th percentiles were calculated to represent the median and 95% uncertainty intervals (UI). All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.2.2. Results

Of all insurance beneficiaries in the MarketScan database, 52,709,530 had at least six months of continuous enrollment, resided in the USA, and did not have other hemoglobinopathies. There were a total of 15,462 individuals with SCD and 52,694,068 individuals without SCD or other hemoglobinopathies. Both groups had more females than males, with 60% and 52% of the SCD and control cohorts being female. Our genotype assignment algorithm identified (74%) of the SCD cohort as having the HbSS genotype (Table 1, Note: all additional genotype-specific results are shown only in Supporting Information).

In intercept-only meta-analyses, SCD had a significantly elevated RR or IRR for all included health outcomes (Fig 1, Table 2). The most elevated RRs and IRRs were in chronic and acute outcomes, respectively, that are very rare in the general population but are known complications of SCD, such as pulmonary hypertension (RR=96.3, 95% UI 85.9-108.1) and avascular necrosis (IRR=328, 294-369) This analysis also found that there were significantly elevated risks for other acute and chronic cardiovascular conditions, such as acute myocardial infarction (IRR=28.7, 24.5-34.3), and venous thromboembolisms (IRR=20.6, 18.8-22.2). Additionally, individuals with SCD had significantly elevated risks of kidney disease (RR=11.9, 10.9-13.0), preeclampsia (IRR=9.7, 7.8-12.1), and cirrhosis (RR=15.1, 14.2-16.2). The lowest estimated IRR via the intercept-only models was for upper respiratory infections but still had a 26% higher risk in individuals with SCD versus without SCD (IRR=1.26, 95% UI 1.21-1.32).

Sex-and age-specific results revealed important pattens. (Table 2, Fig 2 , Fig 3, eFigure 1-3). In the SCD cohort, vaso-occlusive crises and hypertension had the highest incidence and prevalence, respectively, amongst males (124 events per 100 person-years [95% UI 118-139] and 41.1% [39.3%-43.1%]) and females (126 events per 100 person-years [120-131] and 39.4% [37.7%-41.1%]) (Table 3).

The age pattern of prevalence and incidence of most conditions revealed monotonic increases in both SCD and non-SCD groups, but the age trend was much more stark amongst those without SCD or other hemoglobinopathies.

The comparatively "flat" age trends in patients with SCD, reflecting early onset of many conditions, meant that the highest mean RRs or IRRs among both males and females for 14 of 24 measured conditions were in the under-20 age group. Under-20 females and males with SCD, respectively, had 24.6 (19.6-30.2) and 41.0 (33.4-50.1) times the risk of chronic pain and 167 (135-207) and 192 (154-238) times the risk of ischemic stroke compared to patients without SCD in the same age group (Table 2). The IRRs and RRs of otitis media, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), and upper respiratory infections (URIs) were highest in individuals 60 years of age and older among both males and females. Male patients ages 60 years and older with SCD had 6.27 (95% UI 3.77-11.0) times the incidence rate of otitis media, 5.16 (4.00-6.55) times the risk of IBD, and 1.62 (1.38-1.92) times the rate of URIs compared to male patients aged 60 years and older without SCD or other hemoglobinopathies (Table 2). Female patients aged 60 years and older with SCD had 6.16 (3.80-10.7) times the incidence rate of otitis media, 4.96 (3.90-6.10) times the risk of IBD, and 1.49 (1.27-1.76) times the rate of upper respiratory infections compared to female patients aged 60 years and older without SCD or other hemoglobinopathies (Table 2).

Rates and ratios differed significantly by sex for half of the outcomes measured. Pregnant females with SCD had incidence rates 5.6 to 13.6 times higher for health complications compared to pregnant females without SCD or other hemoglobinopathies (Fig 1). For most maternal outcomes, incidence rates were highest in individuals with or without SCD aged 30 to 39 years (Fig 3, Table 3). The rates of asthma and atrial fibrillation were higher in males with SCD compared to females with SCD (26.4 [24.4-28.6] versus 22.1 [20.3-24.0] and 18.8 [16.1-21.8] versus 13.0 [10.9-15.2]). The RRs were statistically significantly more elevated in females compared to males for only two conditions: atrial fibrillation (12.8 [11.0-14.7] for females versus 6.81 [6.01-7.88] for males) and sleep apnea (5.25 [4.56-5.99] for females versus 3.68 [3.20-4.22] for males).

Discussion

We identified significantly elevated rates of numerous adverse health outcomes in a commercially insured cohort of patients with SCD living in the USA, including not only established SCD complications, but also other common illnesses where SCD is a biologically plausible risk factor. SCD can affect nearly every organ system, and males and females with SCD are at a significantly elevated risk of health complications, including potentially fatal cardiac and vascular events, at all ages. Though risk of cardiac and vascular events increases with age in patients with and without SCD, the mean relative risks and rate ratios of complications in those younger than 20 years were extremely elevated in part due to the rarity of such diseases in children and young adults without SCD or other hemoglobinopathies and may indicate the need for increased pediatric surveillance strategies. The evidence presented in this study, alongside factors associated with increased mortality such as socioeconomic status, household composition and disability, minority status and primary language, housing type, and access to transportation, should be considered in patient case management and education. [36] Our findings are consistent with evidence in the literature showing that the overall disease frequency among patients with SCD is notably higher than in those without SCD. [22, 37, 38] Studies indicate that the increased occurrence of complications in SCD is due to the multi-system impact of the disease, driven by vaso-occlusive crises (VOCs), chronic hemolysis, and resulting end-organ damage, emphasizing the need for an expert, interdisciplinary care team from an early age. [39] [40] [41] Few studies report the RR or IRR between patients with versus without SCD. In a retrospective cohort study using a multisystem database with over 100 million patients across 30 countries, patients with SCD had higher risk of many cardiovascular outcomes including ischemic heart disease, atrial fibrillation and flutter, heart failure, and pulmonary hypertension, even after adjusting for demographic and cardiovascular risk factors. [42] In a meta-analysis, Oteng-Ntim and colleagues found that pregnant women with SCD have high risks of adverse maternal and perinatal outcomes, which is consistent with our findings. [43] A meta-analysis of three studies by Noubiap and colleagues also reports increased odds of venous thromboembolism in non-pregnant adults with SCD (OR = 4.43 [2.62-7.48]), though at a lower magnitude than the all-age, both-sex IRR estimated in our study (IRR=20.6 [95% UI 18.8-22.2]), likely due to the different study populations and sample sizes. [44] Consistent with our findings, a study by Manwani and colleagues investigating the complications associated with SCD using US administrative claims data from the Optum Research Database, ICD-coded diagnoses, and employing similar inclusion criteria for identifying cases, found that SCD patients experience a high burden of complications, including acute kidney injury, chronic kidney disease, and stroke, which increase with age. [45] There are limited and mixed reports on myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) in patients with SCD, but the characteristic chronic hemolysis and subsequent inflammation in SCD provides a potential mechanism of oncogenesis worth monitoring in this population. [46] Another potential explanation for elevated rates of MDS in patients with SCD could be miscoding ICD-coded diagnoses as patients with SCD generally have more hematology encounters than an average control patient.

This study is not without limitations. First, while the use of ICD-coded data is a gold standard of population health measurement and usage of administrative data allows for efficient matching to control patients, ICD-coded data are not perfect. [47] In the USA, provision and documentation of medical care is typically siloed from coding and billing which, coupled with complexities of fragmented documentation platforms, incomplete provider knowledge about SCD, and presence of multiple comorbidities, can lead to omission of codes and/or misassignment of SCD status, especially for less severe complications or when care is provided by non-specialists. [27, 48] We aimed to mitigate by prioritizing care setting and provider specialty reports for SCD status and by requiring at least one inpatient and two outpatient encounters for chronic disease and duration windows for acute conditions.

Second, while MarketScan data has advantages in terms of sample size, it's reliance on commercial insurance claims has potential for selection bias and less exact matching. These could lead to underestimation of effect sizes (uninsured persons with severe diseases like SCD that are highly represented in Medicaid would be expected to have higher complication rates than those with insurance) and overestimation of uncertainty (deidentification of Marketscan data limits the number of variables that are available for matching). [49] Efforts to replicate the findings of this study would benefit from utilizing data from a setting with universal health care and/or integrating data from both commercial claims and Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) beneficiaries. Third, our analysis is not able to account for differences in healthcare utilization overall or account for the impact of interventions such as hydroxyurea or newborn screening. [50] The meta-regression approach that we employed will accommodate future integration and synthesis of multiple data sources, which may continue to strengthen the comprehensiveness of evidence of SCD complications. Fourth, while we were able to partially compensate for small case counts for some outcomes with careful data processing and modelling approaches, there are a number of outcomes with plausible associations to SCD that we could not study because they are not routinely treated within the US health-care system and/or not documented using ICD codes, such as mental health comorbidities, [51, 52] oral disorders, anthropometry and metabolic risk factors, and infectious diseases not commonly seen in the USA. [53] [54] [55] Our analysis of pregnancy outcomes further could not account for the potential differential fertility in people with SCD, which may have biased assessments of IRR for pregnancy complications. The implications of the results extend well beyond the geographic setting that produced them.

Namely, our study -the first to comprehensively quantify the health impacts of SCD as a risk factor by age and sex -highlights the massive gap in population health assessments on the impact of conditions like SCD. Global benchmarking frameworks such as the GBD Study consider "causes" and "risk factors" separately. For causal analyses of diseases and injuries, indirect burden is ignored and instead, burden is only counted when death and disability are directly and uniquely assigned to a given condition. This "one death one cause" paradigm can be a useful heuristic for accounting purposes when only limited data exist, but ends up fundamentally misrepresenting the impacts of severe systemic diseases like SCD where direct burden is only a small proportion of the total, as we have previously demonstrated [6].

While the indirect burden due to risk factors can be quantified via comparative-risk assessment (CRA) frameworks, GBD and similar efforts have historically restricted CRA to only a subset of risk factors historically considered 'modifiable' via population-level policy interventions. [56] In demonstrating the measurable, large, and significant excess risk of outcomes associated with exposure to SCD, our study provides a key building block to allow for full quantification of indirect burden and address the massive underestimation of SCD burden globally. We expect that full enumeration of SCD burden clearly demonstrates how a comprehensive SCD strategy is needed to achieve national and global public health targets such as those espoused in the Sustainable Development Goals. SCD is a lifelong, multiorgan disease with numerous complications to screen starting at birth and continuing throughout the lifespan. Multidisciplinary approaches are required to ensure universal screening for SCD, close follow-up and evaluation for complications, and provision of appropriate preventive care and treatment.
