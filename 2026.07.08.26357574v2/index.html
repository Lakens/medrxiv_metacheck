Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Effect Sizes in t-tests and F-tests works

The Effect Size module checks if effect sizes are correctly reported in t-tests and F-tests.

The Effect Size check searches for regular expressions that match typical ways in which effect sizes are reported. It subsequently checks different ways in which Cohen’s d, g, ηp2, and ωp2 can be computed against the reported value. If effects are missing, or might be incorrect, you the module provides a warning. The module was validated on APA reported statistical tests, and might miss effect sizes that were reported in other reporting styles. It was validated by the Metacheck team on papers published in Psychological Science.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Abstract

Hospitals now use AI models to support clinical decisions. Increasingly, these models read patient data from electronic health records through FHIR, the standard format for exchanging health data. Before the data reach the model, they are converted: codes are mapped, units are normalised and records become model inputs. This conversion is rebuilt at every hospital, and when it fails, for example when a lab value arrives in an unexpected unit, the model still accepts the input and returns a score, and the error can pass unnoticed. 1 Introduction

A validation result for a clinical prediction model is a statement about two things: the model, and the inputs on which it was measured. Published work usually treats the second as fixed, since the inputs are simply what the development dataset contained. For a system connected to an electronic health record through FHIR this assumption no longer holds. Its inputs are produced at run time, by retrieving resources from a server and translating them into features through code systems and mapping rules, and this translation is rebuilt at every site where the system is deployed. The validation result is therefore conditional on a process that validation on a fixed dataset does not observe. When that process changes, as it does when a laboratory value arrives in an unexpected unit or under a local code the mapping does not recognise, the model continues to receive well-formed input and to return a score. The evidence offered for it, however, now refers to a system that is no longer the one running.

This situation is not exceptional. FHIR has become the common interface through which clinical AI systems obtain their inputs and return their outputs [1, 16], and SMART on FHIR allows external applications to run inside EHR workflows under scoped authorisation [14]. These standards ensure that a resource can be read from any conforming server. They do not ensure that it means the same thing at every site, nor that it reaches the model in the form its developers assumed. Models validated at one institution are known to lose performance at another [8, 48]. For systems connected through FHIR, the exchange layer is one place where such shifts are introduced, and unlike most sources of shift it can be inspected directly.

Two bodies of work bear on this problem. Frameworks for evaluating clinical AI specify in detail how a model should be validated and governed after deployment [5, 26, 32, 43], and the most recent of them ask that its inputs follow interoperability standards and be quality-controlled [26]. They state these requirements as principles, however, and leave open what a developer must show about the data path. Research on interoperability and data quality characterises how health data are exchanged and where they fail [1, 24], but it does not follow those failures into the behaviour of a model. Published FHIR-integrated AI systems inherit this division: the integration is reported as infrastructure, and the model is evaluated as a separate object [2]. There is, as a result, no instrument that asks of a single system whether its data path and its model have both been shown to work.

The present work addresses this gap with a benchmark. We do not attempt to certify that a system is safe, which would require access to the system itself. We ask a narrower question that can be answered for any published system: what its publication demonstrates about the data path and the model taken together. Figure 1 places the model inside the FHIR architecture and marks where conventional assurance begins. FHIRTrustBench extends the assessment to the stages on either side of the model, and proposes a gate on the data layer, a proposed minimum level of reporting on the data path.

The contributions of this paper are the following: • A benchmark instrument.

FHIRTrustBench defines 46 sub-criteria in five dimensions (Figure 2). Each is a question answerable from a publication, with explicit rules for what counts as met or partially met, and each is traced to the work it draws on. Of these, 35 adapt requirements already stated in existing frameworks; the remaining 11 ($) take requirements those frameworks state only as principles and make them checkable at the level of FHIR resources.

• A non-compensatory scoring rule. A system is reported as a profile of dimension scores rather than a single total, so that strength in one dimension cannot conceal weakness in another. FHIR specificity is additionally evaluated against a provisional pre-model gate (Section 3.3).

• An application to 16 published systems. Two coders applied the benchmark independently (weighted 𝜅 = 0.80).

No system passes the gate, and no publication fully reports either conformance of its inputs to FHIR profiles or monitoring for interoperability drift after deployment.

• Released artifacts. The codebook and scoring code are released with the quotation that supports each rating, so that any score can be checked against its source and the benchmark applied to new systems.

Section 2 reviews FHIR and existing evaluation guidance, Section 3 describes the benchmark and its application, Section 4 reports the results, and Section 5 discusses their implications and limitations.

2 Related Works

FHIR represents clinical data as typed resources exchanged through a common interface [16]. A resource that parses is not thereby fit for a model. Conformance to a profile adds constraints on cardinality and terminology that a valid resource may still violate [15], and the Must Support obligation of US Core requires an element only when the data exist [18]. Two conformant servers can thus return the same patient with different elements present, under different codes. Whether conformance and plausibility [24] have been checked before data reach a model is a property of each implementation, not of the standard.

The same standard offers several routes between model and record. Bulk Data export supplies training extracts, while SMART on FHIR and CDS Hooks deliver patient-level resources at the point of care [12] [13] [14]. A model is therefore often trained on data assembled by one route and served by another. Outputs return through the same layer, yet a stored RiskAssessment carries no mark that a model produced it; tagging of this kind is only now being standardised [17], even as HTI-1 begins to require disclosure of source attributes for predictive decision support [34].

Evaluation guidance, meanwhile, takes the model as its unit of analysis. TRIPOD+AI asks that predictors be defined, including how and when they were measured, and that data preparation, including preprocessing and quality checks, be described [5], and the FDA's principles ask for robustness to real-world variation [43]. FUTURE-AI comes closest to the data path: it recommends building on interoperability standards such as FHIR, and continuous quality control of inputs for missing or out-of-range values, inconsistent units and incorrect preprocessing [26]. These requirements apply to any data source and are stated at the level of variables and preprocessing steps. None specifies the FHIR-level evidence a reader would need to check them when a model's inputs are assembled at run time from a FHIR server: the profiles inputs were validated against, the terminology bindings and unit conversions applied, and the mapping from resource elements to features. Studies of dataset shift name updated EHR software and changed coding practice among its causes [8, 48], but identify them after performance has fallen rather than before deployment.

International standards address AI assurance at the level of the organisation and its processes: ISO/IEC 42001 specifies requirements for an AI management system, ISO/IEC 5338 defines processes for the AI system life cycle, and IEEE 7000 sets out a process for addressing ethical concerns during system design [19] [20] [21]. They define how an organisation should manage AI, not what should be verified about the data a system receives, and none specifies checks at the level of FHIR resources.

FHIR-integrated systems reflect this division. A review of 39 such systems found many lacking EHR interoperability in practice and few with externally validated evidence [2]. Systems that do run on FHIR, from multi-site deep learning [35] and live risk prediction [39] to agents acting inside FHIR environments [22], report the data path as method rather than as something evaluated.

3 Benchmark Instrument Design and Application 3.1 Instrument Design

FHIRTrustBench assesses reported evidence in five dimensions relevant to deploying FHIR-integrated clinical AI. Each dimension corresponds to a distinct upstream failure risk identified in the clinical AI literature: a system can score well on one dimension while being critically exposed on another, and that profile of scores is itself a safety-relevant finding. Each dimension is made assessable through a set of sub-criteria, 46 in total (Figure 2). Each subcriterion is a question answerable from a study's text, with explicit rules for what counts as met or partially met. Every sub-criterion is traceable to at least one published framework, standard or empirical study. We classify each by its precedent in existing AI evaluation frameworks. Of the 46, 35 are adapted from requirements those frameworks already state. The remaining 11 are specialised ($): the frameworks state the requirement as a principle, or for AI systems in general, and FHIRTrustBench turns it into a check answerable from a publication at the FHIR level. The five dimensions are: • FHIR specificity (FS, 10 sub-criteria): whether the data the AI system consumes through FHIR (and the outputs it writes back) are specified, conformance-checked, semantically coded, complete, plausible and traceable, such that the model receives what its developers think it receives [15, 18, 24].

This dimension carries most of the benchmark's FHIR-specific content. Six sub-criteria are specialised: conformance of inputs to named profiles or implementation guides (FS2) and unit normalisation (FS4), which make existing calls for standards and input quality control checkable, together with temporal integrity (FS6), a reproducible FHIR-to-feature mapping (FS8),representation of AI outputs written back to the record (FS9) and portability across independent FHIR implementations (FS10).

• AI validation (AV, 9 sub-criteria): whether the model's performance on its intended task has been measured on data it was not developed on, with appropriate metrics, uncertainty, comparators and settings [5, 26, 43]. Most subcriteria are adapted from established reporting guidance. AV8 specialises general robustness requirements to the input variation that FHIR exchange actually produces, such as missing elements, alternative codings and vendor differences. AV9 specialises task-level validation to generative and agentic systems acting on the patient's FHIR record.

• Workflow integration (WI, 8 sub-criteria): whether the system is specified and evaluated as part of a real clinical workflow: who uses it, when it is triggered, how outputs are presented and acted on, how humans oversee and override it, and how the human-AI team performs [28, 43, 44]. WI2 specialises the general requirement to describe integration into the specific EHR pathway used (SMART on FHIR launch, CDS Hooks service or embedded component) [12, 14].

• Trustworthiness evaluation (TE, 11 sub-criteria): whether the system has been assessed for fairness, privacy and security, safety, and transparency to its users, using named methods rather than statements of intent [4, 5, 33]. One is specialised: least-privilege authorisation of the system's FHIR access (TE-P1), which existing frameworks address only as generic access control.

• Governance readiness (GR, 8 sub-criteria): whether mechanisms exist to keep the system safe after deployment: performance monitoring, controlled updates, local validation, logging, incident handling, assigned accountability and decommissioning [26, 32, 43]. GR2 specialises input monitoring to interoperability drift: FHIR and implementationguide upgrades, terminology releases and changes in unmapped code rates [8].

Full definitions, scoring rules and source mappings for all 46 sub-criteria are provided in the released codebook.

3.2 Rating Rules

Each sub-criterion is rated from the full text of the publication, including supplementary material where available, using one of five codes: • M (met): the publication reports every element the subcriterion requires.

A verbatim quote and its location are recorded.

• P (partial): the publication addresses the sub-criterion but omits at least one required element (e.g., the FHIR resources used are named but not the FHIR version). The quote and the missing element are recorded.

• X (explicitly not done): the publication states that the sub-criterion was not done, not possible, or out of scope. A quote is recorded.

• NR (not reported): the publication is silent, or mentions the sub-criterion only as future work. • NA (not applicable): the sub-criterion's applicability condition is not met (e.g., calibration for a system that does not output probabilities; governance items for a system neither deployed nor planned for deployment). The reason is recorded.

Coders check applicability first, then assign the highest code the text supports. When torn between two codes, they assign the higher one and flag the decision. Using FHIR does not by itself satisfy any FS sub-criterion; each requires its own evidence. Separating X from NR distinguishes a reported absence from silence. The two receive the same score, but the distinction is retained in the evidence record. Item-specific rules for M, P and NA are given in the codebook. For example, FS8 asks whether the mapping from FHIR resources to model features is documented. It is Met when each feature is defined by its FHIR element, code and time window, such as 'last creatinine' as an Observation with LOINC 2160-0 in the 24 hours before prediction. Wang et al. [45] report inputs "such as visit history, medication history, laboratory values, and vital signs" and link these groups to FHIR resource categories, but give no element, code or window for any feature. FS8 is therefore Partial; reporting those three elements for each feature would make it Met.

3.3 Dimension Scores and the Pre-Model Gate

Codes are converted to points (M = 2, P = 1, X = NR = 0); NA items are excluded. For a study 𝑠 and dimension 𝑑 with 𝐴 𝑠,𝑑 applicable sub-criteria, the dimension score is the share of attainable evidence the publication provides.

If fewer than three sub-criteria in a dimension are applicable, 𝑆 𝑠,𝑑 is reported as NA rather than as a percentage of one or two items. Scores are not aggregated across dimensions. Each study is reported as a profile of five dimension scores, so strength in one dimension cannot compensate for weakness in another. A validation result is conditional on the data path that produced its inputs (Section 1); a well-validated model fed by an unverified data path therefore remains unverified, however strong its other scores. Interoperability assurance is additionally evaluated against a provisional pre-model gate. A study passes the gate if FS1 (exchange pathway) and FS3 (terminology binding) are at least partially met and 𝑆 𝑠,FS ≥ 50%. FS1 is mandatory because every other FHIRspecificity sub-criterion presupposes a known route from record to model, and FS3 because terminology binding is the part of interoperability that FHIR's structure does not enforce: a resource can be valid while carrying a code the model does not recognise. The other eight sub-criteria enter only through the score, so within FHIR specificity strong reporting on some can still offset silence on others, including profile conformance (FS2), unit normalisation (FS4) and the FHIR-to-feature mapping (FS8). The gate thus sets a floor on the two preconditions rather than a complete guarantee; making further sub-criteria mandatory would require evidence on their relative importance that is not yet available. The 50% threshold asks that a publication document at least half of the attainable evidence on its data path. It is a proposed reporting threshold, not a validated decision boundary, and its sensitivity, including a stricter gate, is reported in Section 4. Gate status is reported alongside the profile, never combined with it.

FS3 and FS8 concern the data a model reads. Two systems read no FHIR data and use FHIR only to write outputs: Li et al. [27] generates resources from clinical text, and Wen et al. [46] reports measurements derived from CT images. For these, the two items were coded against model inputs as the item rules require, and output representation is assessed by FS9. Treating FS8 for the first and FS3 and FS8 for the second as not applicable raises their FHIRspecificity scores to 31% and 25%, and the gate result is unchanged; excluding both leaves no system passing among the remaining 14.

3.4 Benchmark Subject Pool

Benchmark subjects are published FHIR-integrated clinical AI systems. A study was eligible if it (IC1) describes a clinical AI system (machine learning, large language model or agent); (IC2) obtains or returns data through FHIR, including SMART on FHIR, CDS Hooks or Bulk Data; (IC3) describes the system's architecture or data pipeline; (IC4) is a primary study; (IC5) is peer-reviewed or a substantive preprint in English; and (IC6) was published in 2016 or later. Standards, guidelines, reviews, editorials, abstract-only publications and pipelines without an AI component were excluded.

The 16 benchmark subjects were assembled through an initial targeted search on 11 October 2026, seeded with the 39 studies included in the scoping review of Balch et al. [2] and extended with web searches combining FHIR, SMART on FHIR, CDS Hooks and Bulk FHIR with machine-learning, language-model and agent terms. Of 29 candidates assessed, 16 met all criteria (Appendix A); six could not be confirmed as eligible and seven were excluded (Appendix B).

To estimate how far this set covers the literature, we then searched PubMed and arXiv on 31 October 2026 (search strings in the released artifact). The searches returned 226 records, 222 after removing duplicates. Title and abstract screening against IC1-IC6 identified 58 further studies as likely eligible and 42 whose eligibility requires full-text review; 13 of the 16 benchmark subjects were among the records retrieved. The 16 coded systems are therefore an application set rather than a census of FHIR-integrated clinical AI, and coding the remaining eligible studies is left to future work.

3.5 Coding, Adjudication and Agreement

Two coders rated all 16 studies independently from full text. After a first round, the FHIR-specificity rules were clarified where coders had read them differently, and the second coder recoded that dimension blind to the first coder's codes. Two studies first coded from their abstracts were recoded from full text once it became available. Agreement is computed on the codes from this final round, before adjudication, as quadratic weighted Cohen's 𝜅 on the points scale over cells both coders rated applicable, with applicability agreement reported separately. Cells on which the coders differed were resolved by discussion against the item rules; a final code could differ from both coders' codes, and rule clarifications reached during adjudication were applied to every study. Of the 162 disputed cells, the final code matched the first coder's in 85, the second coder's in 73, and neither in 4. Agreement statistics and scores were computed with Python scripts released with the artifact.

4 Results

4.1 Agreement between coders

Two coders applied FHIRTrustBench independently to the 16 systems, which produced 736 coded cells. On the 630 cells that both coders judged applicable, they assigned the same points in 82.2% of cases, giving a quadratic weighted 𝜅 of 0.80 (linear 𝜅 = 0.74). Prevalence-adjusted agreement on the three-point scale was 0.73. Agreement was substantial or higher in every dimension, ranging from 𝜅 = 0.65 for governance readiness to 0.85 for AI validation, and the coders also agreed on whether a sub-criterion applied to a system at all in 94.8% of cells (𝜅 = 0.75).

The structure of the remaining disagreement is as informative as its size. Of the 112 applicable cells on which the two coders assigned different points, 110 differed by a single step of the scale. Only twice did one coder read a publication as fully meeting a criterion where the other found nothing to credit. The sub-criteria are therefore specific enough that two readers, working without contact, arrive at the same judgement or at neighbouring ones. 4.2 No system passes the FHIR-specificity gate

None of the 16 systems passes the gate (Figure 3). Nine satisfy its first two conditions, a specified FHIR exchange pathway (FS1) and at least partial terminology binding (FS3), and every one of these nine then falls short of the required 50% on FHIR specificity as a whole. The highest scores, 44%, belong to Tarumi et al. [40] and Wang et al. [45]; the median is 32% and the lowest 17%. The remaining seven systems do not reach the score threshold either, and they fail earlier, at terminology: six do not report how codes in their FHIR inputs were bound or mapped, and one reports an approach that does not meet the criterion. One of the seven, Toro Beltran et al. [41], also leaves the exchange pathway itself unspecified. The result does not rest on the adjudication. Scored with either coder's codes alone, no system passes. Treating non-applicable subcriteria as unreported, which removes any advantage a system could gain from a narrow scope, leaves the count at zero, and so does lowering the threshold to 45%. The first systems to pass appear only at a threshold of 40%, where the two highest-scoring systems clear it and the other 14 still do not. A stricter gate that also makes FS2, FS4 and FS8 mandatory leaves the count at zero before the score is consulted: no system reports all five at least partially, and only three report profile conformance at all.

4.3 The data path is named but not specified

Table 1 places FHIR specificity against the other four dimensions, and the comparison shows what kind of shortfall the gate is detecting. FHIR specificity is the dimension in which publications most often say something about a sub-criterion: 57.1% of its applicable cells were coded at least Partial, more than in any other dimension. It is also among the dimensions in which they least often say enough. Only 4.8% of its applicable cells were coded Met, against 17.6% for AI validation and 19.5% for workflow integration. Partial is the most frequent code in FHIR specificity, and six of its 10 subcriteria are fully met by no system in the sample. Part of this gap reflects the strictness of the specialised items, which are Met in 1.2% of applicable cells against 9.4% for the adapted FS items. The gap does not disappear when only adapted items are compared: these are Met in 9.4% of cells in FHIR specificity, against 17.3% in AI validation and 15.2% in workflow integration.

The item-level codes locate the shortfall precisely. Fifteen of the 16 publications identify the FHIR exchange pathway at least partially (FS1), and 15 at least partially document where their inputs came from (FS7). The sub-criteria that describe what happens to the data between the record and the model are a different matter. No system fully reports validating its inputs against a FHIR profile or implementation guide (FS2), and only three report any such check.

No system fully reports how the codes in its inputs were bound or mapped to terminologies (FS3). The same holds for the normalisation of units and values (FS4) and for the handling of temporal ordering and duplicate resources (FS6). The mapping from FHIR resources to model features (FS8) is the clearest case: 13 publications describe it in part, and none describes it completely. Of the three systems for which output write-back applies, none specifies how its outputs are represented and returned to the record in full (FS9). Testing whether a pipeline carries across FHIR implementations (FS10) is fully reported by a single system, McMurry et al. [29].

Read together, these codes describe a consistent reporting practice. A reader of these publications can usually tell which record a system draws on and that the data arrive as FHIR. What the reader generally cannot reconstruct is the sequence of transformations that turns those resources into the inputs on which the model was trained and evaluated, and it is precisely in that sequence that a coding error or a unit mismatch would enter the model as valid input.

4.4 Validation stops short of the interface

Model evaluation is reported in considerably more detail than the data path. Seven systems fully report a comparator or benchmark (AV7), and the share of applicable AI-validation cells coded Met is more than three times that of FHIR specificity. This attention does not extend to the point where the model meets the FHIR data. No system fully reports testing its robustness to input variation arising from interoperability (AV8); two address it in part and 14 do not report it. Only two systems fully report external validation (AV5).

4.5 Workflow, trustworthiness and governance

Workflow integration shows the same asymmetry on the clinical side of the model. Eight systems fully specify their intended users and setting (WI1), and eight fully specify how the system is integrated with the EHR (WI2). How clinicians work with the system is far less often reported: no publication fully reports a usability or human-factors evaluation (WI6) or an assessment of human and AI performance in combination (WI7), and one mentions information or training for users (WI8).

Trustworthiness evaluation has the highest share of unreported cells among the dimensions defined for all 16 studies, at 65.7%, and no publication reports testing AI-specific security threats (TE-P3). Governance readiness applies only to the eight systems that describe deployment or a concrete plan for it, and among these 76.6% of cells are unreported, with a single cell coded Met. None of the eight reports monitoring for drift in its inputs or in the interoperability layer (GR2), and none describes how the system would be withdrawn or rolled back (GR8). The absence of drift monitoring is the governance counterpart of the gaps in FHIR specificity: a system whose input transformations are not specified offers no reference against which a later change in those inputs could be detected.

Across the five dimensions, complete reporting is concentrated in AI validation and workflow integration, the two dimensions closest to the model and its immediate use. It is scarce in the dimensions that describe how data reach the model and how the system is maintained once deployed.

5 Discussion

A gate that no system passes. That none of the 16 systems passes the gate is not a statement about the threshold; it is a statement about what these publications allow a reader to verify. The gate asks one question: does a publication document its data path well enough for its validation results to be reproduced and assessed? For every system in the sample, including those whose models are evaluated most carefully, the answer is no. Whether meeting the gate predicts that a result can in fact be reproduced has not been tested; the gate summarises reporting, not reproducibility. This does not show that the systems are unsafe. It shows that their safety cannot be judged from their published record. A reported AUROC describes a model applied to a particular input distribution, and when the transformations that produce that distribution are unspecified, the estimate cannot be reproduced or transferred to another implementation. Experience with deployed models shows why this matters. The externally measured performance of a widely deployed proprietary sepsis model fell well below that reported by its developer [48], changes in coding practice and clinical IT systems are documented sources of dataset shift [8], and calibration drifts as the incoming data change [6]. None of these studies concerns FHIR, and we do not attribute their failures to it. They motivate the gate because they show that performance depends on how inputs are produced, and that a change in that process may surface only after deployment. The gate asks publications to document that process so that it can be checked before.

Why the gate is drawn where it is. The rationale for the gate's conditions is given in Section 3.3. Its second condition does most of the work: seven of the 16 systems fail at terminology binding, the point at which an error raises no exception and enters the model as data. The 50% threshold then requires a publication to document at least half of the attainable evidence on its data path. It is a moderate and provisional bar, fixed in the codebook before any study was scored, and the conclusion does not hinge on its position: no system passes at 45%, and at 40% only the two highest-scoring systems do (Section 4).

Lowering the threshold until some systems pass would fit the instrument to the sample it is meant to assess, and a benchmark's validity rests on its criteria being set independently of the results they produce [3, 36]. We therefore keep the threshold as a provisional reference point and report its sensitivity. The released scoring code sets the threshold as a single parameter, so a health system with a different tolerance for risk can apply a stricter or more lenient gate; comparisons across systems remain meaningful as long as they use the same one.

The interface between record and model.

The results describe a specific gap. Publications name their FHIR source and record where their inputs come from, but rarely specify the transformations that turn resources into features. Existing reporting guidelines explain why this gap persists. TRIPOD+AI and PROBAST+AI ask authors to define predictors and to describe data sources, preprocessing and quality checks [5, 31]. They do so in terms that apply to any data source, so a publication can satisfy them without stating which FHIR elements, codes and time windows produced each variable. FHIRTrustBench adds no new requirement here; it specifies the FHIR-level evidence that would show these existing requirements were met. FUTURE-AI's call for input quality control describes what developers should do; FHIRTrustBench asks whether a publication shows that it was done. Documentation practices from the fairness literature have the same boundary. Model cards and datasheets describe the model and the dataset [9, 30], and model facts labels bring this information to clinicians [38], yet the interface between record and model is documented by none of them. FHIRTrustBench makes that interface reportable at the level of FHIR resources, extending data-quality notions such as conformance and plausibility [24] to the path a clinical AI system actually depends on.

Closing the gap requires little new machinery. FHIR already provides profiles, terminology bindings and a reference validator [15, 18], so specifying the data path is largely a matter of reporting what was done: the profiles inputs were checked against, the value sets they were bound to, and the mapping from resources to features. Standards and regulation are moving in the same direction. Certification rules now require developers of predictive decision support to disclose source attributes [34], and HL7 has drafted a FHIR implementation guide for AI transparency [17]. FHIRTrust-Bench offers a way to check, from the published record, whether that transparency extends to the data path. The governance findings follow from the same gap: a reader cannot tell whether drift in inputs is monitored when the reference it would be measured against is not reported, and predetermined change control plans [42] presuppose that the expected inputs are known.

Limitations.

FHIRTrustBench assesses what a publication reports, not what a system does. A system may validate its inputs without saying so, and an NR code records only that the evidence is absent from the record. Linked code repositories were not coded. For open-source systems, mappings and terminology handling documented only in code are therefore scored as not reported, which may understate FHIR specificity for those systems. We regard the published record as the appropriate unit, since it is the evidence on which health systems and regulators must rely when deciding whether to adopt a system; separating X from NR keeps "not done" distinct from "not reported". The 16 systems come from an initial targeted search; a later systematic search suggests a larger eligible literature, and whether the gate result holds across it remains to be tested. The sample also spans different system types, from deployed decision-support models to a benchmark environment for LLM agents. Because non-applicable sub-criteria are excluded, each system is scored against its own applicable criteria, so scores describe a system's reporting rather than rank systems of different types against one another. Agreement was estimated for one pair of coders from the same group, one of whom contributed to the instrument's design, and two studies were recoded after their first coding round. The reported 𝜅 therefore shows that the codebook is applied reproducibly within one team; whether it transfers to coders who did not help build it requires independent coding with the frozen codebook. The gate's threshold and its two mandatory sub-criteria rest on the reasoning in Section 3.3, not on empirical calibration; validating them, for example against an expert panel and against well-documented reference implementations, is future work. Finally, the scores have not yet been compared with how systems perform after deployment, so whether better-reported systems are also safer in practice is an open question.

6 Conclusion

A validation result for a FHIR-integrated clinical AI system holds only for the inputs it was measured on, and those inputs are produced by a data path that validation on a fixed dataset does not observe. FHIRTrustBench brings that path into the assessment. It does so with sub-criteria answerable from a publication, a profile of dimension scores that does not let strength in one dimension offset weakness in another, and a provisional gate on FHIR specificity that sets a minimum of data-path reporting for assessing model validation. Applied by two independent coders to 16 published systems, the benchmark showed that no system passes the gate. The shortfall has a consistent form: these publications name the exchange pathway and the source of their inputs, but not the transformations that turn FHIR resources into model features. The model is evaluated carefully, while the interface on which its evaluation depends is not documented.

Because the data path is built from standard and inspectable components, much of this gap can be closed by reporting what was done, though some systems may first need to add the checks. Coding the wider eligible literature, testing whether benchmark scores track behaviour after deployment, and aligning the sub-criteria with emerging FHIR transparency standards are the natural next steps. The released codebook, scoring code and evidence record are intended to make each of them possible.

Ethics and Privacy Statement

This study analyses published articles only. It involved no human participants and no patient data, so institutional review was not required; the released evidence record contains short quotations from the coded publications and no personal information.

The main risk lies in how the scores are read. FHIRTrustBench rates what a publication reports, not how a system behaves, and a low score for a named study is a statement about its documentation rather than evidence that the system is unsafe or that its developers acted carelessly. We state this distinction wherever scores are reported and release the quotation behind every rating, so that any score can be checked and contested against its source. A second risk is that a reporting benchmark invites reporting to the benchmark: authors could satisfy sub-criteria in wording without changing practice. The item rules guard against this partly by requiring named methods and results rather than statements of intent, but no reporting instrument removes the risk entirely.

The intended benefit is that health systems and regulators, who must decide on adoption from published evidence, can see which parts of a FHIR-integrated system that evidence covers. Failures in the data path are not distributed evenly: sites with fewer informatics resources are less able to detect them locally, which makes their documentation in the published record a question of fairness as well as safety.
