Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Introduction

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) threaten health gains, productivity and household budgets worldwide. India's burden is large and rising: an estimated 101 million adults have diabetes, 315 million have hypertension and 254 million have generalised obesity [1]. Unhealthy diets, physical inactivity, tobacco use and excess body weight are the principal modifiable drivers [1] [2] [3]. In the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021, a substantial share of disability-adjusted life-years was attributable to these behavioural and metabolic risks, so a corresponding share of the burden is potentially avoidable through prevention [3]. Because these exposures are widespread and often coexist, prevention offers large health and economic returns.

The need is not confined to low-and middle-income countries. In the United States, chronic diseases are the leading causes of illness, disability and death and account for about 90% of the nation's $5.3 trillion in annual health care expenditure; three in four adults have at least one chronic condition and more than half have two or more [4]. More than 40 million Americans have diabetes, a further 115 million adults have prediabetes, and obesity affects about 40% of adults [5]. Because the same modifiable behaviours -poor nutrition, physical inactivity, tobacco use and harmful alcohol use -drive this burden [4, 5], lifestyle intervention is needed both to prevent disease and as an essential component of therapy alongside pharmacotherapy, in high-income as well as Indian settings [2, 3].

Lifestyle interventions can prevent or improve NCDs. In Asian Indians with impaired glucose tolerance, lifestyle modification reduced incident diabetes by 28.5% relative to standard advice in the Indian Diabetes Prevention Programme [6]. Plant-based dietary patterns [7], Yoga for hypertension [8] and Yoga-based programmes for glycaemic control [9] have supportive evidence, and a 2026 meta-analysis of Indian adults with hypertension found that lifestyle interventions lowered systolic and diastolic blood pressure [10]. A multicentre Indian trial also found that a structured Yoga and lifestyle programme was . feasible, with promising recruitment, follow-up and adherence in people at high risk of diabetes [11]. Much of this evidence, however, comes from efficacy trials or single-disease programmes. Less is known about whether a comprehensive, comprehensible lifestyle package that promotes self-determination can be delivered routinely alongside a busy family-medicine clinic, especially for multimorbid industrial workers [12, 13]. Implementation is constrained by limited time, staffing, tools and financing among providers [14], and patients face barriers to sustained adherence [15].

ESIC Medical College & Hospital, Faridabad, India, provides free care at the point of use to industrial workers and their families covered by the Employees' State Insurance Scheme (ESI Scheme). Within this tertiary-care setting, patients are, however, free to move between hospital departments. The Integrative Health Clinic (IHC) is a family-medicine-style outpatient clinic open to patients of all ages with chronic conditions. Care is organised around the patient's context, beliefs and goals rather than a single diagnosis, and it explores and addresses the root causes of illness to support overall health and recovery. All patients receive the lifestyle intervention as an add-on to standard of care, aimed at integrative body-mind, whole-person care, with a focus on lifestyle change.

For this, it follows the 21-item Health Building Lifestyle Guideline, with a tracked 0-21 lifestyle score [2, 16, 17], and delivers care through a 7-step protocol based on the COM-B model [16]. The programme combines a plant-based diet, daily Yoga asanas and breath control (pranayama), and a home-prepared four-spice infusion [18] [19] [20] [21] [22] [23]. All patients are followed up through calls, during which they are asked about their regularity with the three advised practices and any relief of presenting symptoms.

The clinic is led by a physician trained in public health and therapeutic Yoga, who demonstrates Yoga in the clinic with two on-site medical interns.

Prior single-disease signals from this clinic -an irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) remission case report combining diet, lifestyle and Yoga [19], a randomised trial of pranayama in exposed healthcare professionals [24] and public descriptions of the approach [25] motivated a larger evaluation. We therefore examined a 20-month real-world cohort of 1,737 patients to assess feasibility, compliance with the three lifestyle components and patientreported relief, and to determine whether the programme could be delivered within routine family-medicine care. We explicitly address the single-visit constraint through return-visits to the clinic and call-based feedback.

Methods

Design and aims.

This was a retrospective single-arm cohort reported in accordance with the STROBE statement [26]. The study aimed to assess compliance with the three lifestyle components separately -spice infusion, diet and Yoga -and patient-reported relief. The analysis coded each outcome once per patient using the most recent definitive answer rule.

Setting and dates.

The observation window comprised the 20-month incident period from 1 February 2025 to 30 September 2026, with follow-up records through 4 November 2026. Data collection began in January 2025, but the January records were lost in a hospital system upgrade and are unavailable for this analysis. The clinic began in September 2024 as a pilot, initially functioning on Saturdays and was converted into a regular, six days a week (9 AM-1 PM) unit since 1 December 2024 under a Dean's office directive. Hospital departments were advised to refer patients with chronic conditions, and patients also came by word of mouth. Our incident cohort was defined by the cut-off rule: a first visit on or before 30 September 2026. Records from October 2026 contributed follow-up data only, and patients whose first visit fell after 30 September 2026 (98 patients, 116 records) were excluded. The analysed denominator comprised patients with a usable telephone contact who were called; patients without a usable telephone contact were excluded from all outcome analyses; they are retained in the cohort flow and compared with the called group in Table 5.

Medication posture.

Prescribing at the IHC stayed within safe, guideline-concordant categories, with a conservative use of drugs; for example, topical non-steroidal antiinflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) were preferred to oral tablets for musculoskeletal pain; antitussives were discouraged while steam inhalation was advised for cough; antacids were discouraged in favour of a plant-based diet for dyspepsia; specific drugs for constipation were avoided in favour of a whole-food, plant-based diet, adequate hydration and movement. Given the widespread prevalence of vitamin-mineral deficiency in India, and particularly among the lower socioeconomic groups, to which most the clinic's target population belonged, vitamin and mineral supplements were liberally prescribed. No medication-related adverse effects were recorded during the observation period.

Participants. and diagnosis coding. Diagnoses were grouped into 14 systems and 323 canonical conditions for burden counting after mapping 699 raw diagnosis labels. Although patients could have conditions in multiple systems, each patient was analysed as a single unit, with outcomes counted once per patient, not per episode or visit. Insurance numbers, where present, were used solely for the family-linkage analysis and do not appear in any patient-level output.

Intervention (replicable).

Within this care model, advice on three lifestyle components was added to standard pharmacotherapy. The first was a home-prepared morning infusion of four whole culinary spices: 10 g each of coriander (Coriandrum sativum) seed, fenugreek (Trigonella foenum-graecum) seed, cumin (Cuminum cyminum) seed and cinnamon (Cinnamomum spp.) bark, soaked overnight in 1 litre of water. A glassful was taken lukewarm first thing in the morning before Yoga; the water was replenished for up to three days, after which a fresh infusion was prepared. The infusion was advised as one component of the clinic's three-component lifestyle programme, alongside dietary modification and daily Yoga, and was never evaluated separately. Patients were advised to continue prescribed pharmacotherapy. The second was a plant-based diet and diurnal routine based on the 21-item Health Building Lifestyle Guideline [16], including red/green/yellow items, motivational interviewing and team reinforcement. The third was daily practice of 20 Yoga asanas and pranayama: the physician demonstrates the sequence in the clinic, patients practise under supervision, and a video-series link is shared to support home practice [18]. The guideline was synthesised from WHO and ICMR-NIN guidance and behaviour-change principles (COM-B, Health Belief Model and self-efficacy) [2,16,17] and delivered through its published 7-step protocol (detailed sequence in Appendix A): lifestyle assessment; traffic-light education; self-scored baseline; barriers and goal-setting; physician reinforcement; physician-led demonstration; and follow-up calls [16]. Group medical attendance, and whole family care, where feasible, were encouraged.

Clinic workflow and time per patient.

The clinic was organised around a zero-waiting workflow in a single room with Yoga mats. Before their consultation, patients read the bilingual lifestyle guideline [27] and glanced through Yoga and lifestyle web content shared on WhatsApp on their own mobile. They also observed repeat-visit patients by their side practising Yoga and pranayama and, intermittently, heard them recounting their . experiences. A trainee doctor or nurse then saw patients one at a time for vital signs, anthropometry and history as a preliminary assessment. Thereafter, the clinic doctor examined each patient to establish the diagnosis. Standard care, including clinically indicated investigations, was provided for these diagnoses. Patients returned to the mat to practise Yoga and pranayama while prescriptions and the investigation list were prepared. Before leaving, lifestyle advice was repeated and recall done; patients were asked to followup in 2-3 weeks with test reports. At the repeat visits, compliance was reviewed and patients' experiences heard; weight was rechecked as a marker of compliance; goals were revisited and advice was reinforced in person, by telephone or by WhatsApp. Each patient spent about 30 minutes per visit over sessions that covered knowledge, attitude and supervised practice of the asanas. Such an elaborate protocol aimed to operationalised the three COM elements -Capability, Opportunity and Motivation -so that patients leave the clinic with a concrete inventory of lifestyle changes to be followed and, with the clinic's experience, return with a sense of motivation and empowerment to take responsibility for their own health.

Outcomes. The primary outcome was the patient-level coded classification of reported relief (YES/NO/no definitive answer). Secondary outcomes were the patient-level compliance of spice, diet and Yoga practices. Records were ordered by call date, and the most recent definitive answer (YES/NO) was taken as the patient's code; if no definitive answer was recorded ("no response" or "not interested"), the patient was classified as having no definitive answer. "Not interested" was treated as an intentional non-answer, not a definitive NO.

Relief measurement. Relief was deliberately elicited with a single generic question. The term "Relief" is open to individual interpretations and may reflect the patient's most pressing problems rather than a standardised, disease-specific construct. Validated diseasespecific questionnaires would have been preferable, but they were impractical in a large, diagnostically diverse cohort. A single item of :"relief" was chosen for simplicity, comprehensibility, flexibility across conditions and acceptability to patients. For future rounds, we propose adding a percentage-relief question -"compared with your first visit, what percentage relief have you had?" -where 0 represents no relief, to improve discrimination.

Dose levels.

The five-level compliance with the three lifestyle practices was defined as follows:0 = no components,1 = any one component and2 = any two components of the three (spice, diet and Yoga);3 = all three components compliant; and4 = all three components compliant with at least one revisit. A revisit alone never qualified as a dose. Dose-response is reported as two estimates: a responder estimate (YES among patients with a definitive answer, YES/NO) and a conservative estimate (YES among all analysed patients, including patients with no definitive answer in the denominator).

Response attribution.

All patients with a usable telephone contact were contacted. "Contacted" means that a call date was recorded at any point (800 of 1,035 single-visit and 666 of 702 revisit patients). Because diet, Yoga, the infusion and medicines were delivered jointly, attribution to any single component was not possible.

Screening definitions.

Screening operated at two levels. Clinical screening meant identification of behavioural risks and other conditions from history and examinationfor example, tobacco abuse -and their addition to the coded diagnosis; all the 114 tobaccoabuse diagnoses (114 patients; tobacco abuse is a single canonical condition, so pairs = patients) were identified in this way. Objective-evidence screening was applied to valid records for conditions that could be unobserved or silent: obesity-suggestive (BMI >28 kg/m² without an obesity code on the same record), diabetes-suggestive (HbA1c ≥6.5% without a diabetes code), sustained unlabelled hypertension (≥2 readings with systolic ≥140 mmHg or diastolic ≥90 mmHg on records without a hypertension label) and anaemiasuggestive (haemoglobin <10 g/dL without an anaemia code). A flagged patient was added to the main counts only if they had never been coded with that condition anywhere in the record; patients whose code appeared at another visit were excluded. Thresholds were pragmatic single-cut choices. Because the hypertension rule requires two elevated readings on separate visits, all 39 never-coded hypertension patients had at least one revisit; the obesity, diabetes and anaemia screens, which require only a single measurement, also identified single-visit patients. Alternative cut-off checks increased the never-coded counts without changing the overall pattern: obesity 161 → 198 at BMI ≥27.5 and 415 at BMI ≥25; anaemia 44 → 264 with WHO adult haemoglobin thresholds (<13 g/dL in men, <12 g/dL in women). The 246 objective-evidence additions are reported in the Table 2 footnote and Table S3; clinically screened diagnoses such as tobacco abuse are included in the main counts but are not part of the 246 objective-evidence additions.

Ascertainment policy. The main burden tables include all patient-condition pairs from contacted patients. Clinically screened diagnoses were added to the coded record. Table 2 and Table S3 report the 246 objective additions and their provenance; this avoids undercounting objective disease while retaining the coded diagnosis as the clinical record.

Harms.

No harms were reported in this retrospective extraction. Concurrent medicines were continued throughout. As this was a record review without prospective safety monitoring, the absence of recorded harms refers to documented events only.

Statistics.

Analyses were descriptive, with explicit denominators and column percentages. Classification was once per patient by the most recent definitive answer, and correlations used analysed-only denominators. The primary relief estimate used the 1,016 analysed patients with a definitive answer. The conservative lower bound used all 1,466 analysed patients as the denominator and counted every non-YES (NO and no definitive answer) as no relief. We did not assume relief (or lack of it) for non-responders and therefore did not calculate a best (or worst) case estimate. Disease-specific paired analyses with effect sizes, confidence intervals and sensitivity analyses are deferred to follow-up papers, one disease at a time (for example, hypertension, diabetes, obesity, arthritis or acid-peptic disease).

Ethics and approval.

The retrospective, de-identified case-series analysis was notified to the Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC), ESIC Medical College & Hospital, Faridabad (EC Reg. No. EC/NEW/INST/2023/HR/0307), on 2 March 2026. The Committee in its letter of 18 March 2026 raised no objection to publication, provided that confidentiality and anonymity were strictly maintained and no identifiable information was disclosed. On 22 October 2026, the Committee extended its no-objection to the proposed analysis and to matching family members by insurance number for analytical purposes, subject to the same conditions of strict confidentiality and anonymity, non-disclosure of identifiable information and compliance with applicable institutional policies. The no-objection determinations cover the de-identified data reported here, including follow-up records through 4 November 2026. Patients younger than 18 years were dependants of insured workers and were included as part of the same de-identified record review.

Results

Flow.

The incident-window rule excluded 98 patients (116 records) whose first visit fell after 30 September 2026, and the validity filter then excluded 183 records without a coded diagnosis entry (fully undiagnosed patients and stray blank rows), leaving 4,047 valid records from 1,737 unique patients (1,035 single-visit and 702 revisit patients). Sex and age were available for all 1,737 patients; outcome data only for the 1,466 called. Per-dose denominators are given in Table 4, and monthly valid-diagnosis records ranged from 91 to 269 (all cleaned records in Figure 1 ranged from 93 to 280). Cohort flow and baseline characteristics are summarised in Table 1, and the two contact groups are compared in Table 5.

Baseline characteristics and burden. The cohort of 1,737 comprised 904 female patients (52.0%; mean age 40.3 ± 14.6 years) and 833 male patients (48.0%; mean age 44.8 ± 15.1 years). The analysis was restricted to the 1,466 patients with a usable telephone contact who were called. System-level sex, age and revisit distributions for the analysed patients are given in Table S1. Among them, the most frequent patient-condition pairs were hypertension (511), obesity (397), arthritis (268), diabetes mellitus (246), gastritis (163), tobacco abuse (114), hypothyroidism (103) and anaemia (99); complete system-level caseloads are shown in Table 2. Tobacco abuse was the most frequent behavioural-risk code: 114 patients (114 patient-condition pairs; 7.8% of the analysed cohort; 16.2% of the 703 men), all male. All tobacco-abuse cases were identified through clinical screening (history and examination) and added to the diagnosis. Objective-evidence screening contributed 246 patient-condition pairs in 233 patients, with 13 patients meeting criteria for two conditions (obesity 161, hypertension 39, anaemia 44, diabetes 2); these pairs are included in the Table 2 counts and detailed in the Table 2 footnote and Table S3. In the full 1,737-patient cohort, coded-diagnosis prevalence was hypertension 29.8% (518), arthritis 18.0% (313), diabetes mellitus 15.6% (271), obesity 15.0% (260) and gastritis 11.1% (193); these are coded diagnoses in the full cohort and differ from the patient-condition pair counts in Table 2, which include screening-detected cases among the 1,466 called patients. Patients without a usable telephone contact (n = 271) were similar in sex and age but were more often singlevisit (86.7% vs 54.6%) and had a lighter cardiometabolic case-mix (hypertension 17.0% vs 32.2%, obesity 8.9% vs 16.1%, diabetes 10.0% vs 16.6%; Table 5). Because these patients had fewer and milder recorded conditions, their exclusion would, if anything, tend to understate rather than inflate the benefit observed; this remains an inference, as their outcomes were not measured.

Clinic uptake and continuity. Clinic footfall grew over the observation window, from 139 recorded visits in February 2025 to 280 in September 2026. The net growth came from returning patients: first visits fell from 130 in February 2025 to 76 in September 2026 (59 in August), while return visits rose from 9 to 204 (188 in August). The return-visit share of monthly visits therefore climbed from 6.5% to 72.9% (72-76% in July-September 2026; Figure 1); part of this rise is mechanical, because the cohort started empty and accumulated revisits over time. Participation also spread within families. Grouping patients by shared insurance number (the insured worker and registered dependants share one number) yielded 1,412 families, of which 274 (19.4%) were represented by more than one member. In 138 of these families (50.4%), at least one member first attended after the family's first member (180 patients, 10.4% of the cohort; median interval 64.0 days, interquartile range (IQR) 18.5-195.5); the remaining 136 multi-member families attended on the same day. Patients spanned all ages, from infancy to 87 years; 101 patients (5.8%) were under 18, including 40 children among the 180 who attended after a family member. Together with . the return-visit pattern, this family clustering is consistent with gradual intra-family diffusion of the programme (a clinic observation; referral pathways were not audited).

Provider referrals. Conventional-medicine providers regularly referred chronic, resistent `cases to the clinic, notably from Obstetrics & Gynaecology, Internal Medicine, Gastroenterology and Psychiatry. The nature of referral patterns were a clinic observation and were not systematically audited; they may contribute to a case mix weighted toward chronic, previously managed conditions.

Contact, outcomes and adherence. Of the 1,466 analysed patients, 1,016 answered the practice items and gave a definitive relief answer. Of the 1,466 analysed patients, 850 (83.7%; 95% CI 81.3-85.8) reported relief (YES) and 166 reported no relief (NO); the remaining 450 (850 + 166 +450 = 1,466) gave no definitive answer. Treating all 450 nonresponders as no relief, the conservative lower bound of reported relief was 58.0% (850/1,466). The 26 patients recorded as "not interested" were retained as having no definitive answer, not NO, and were not counted as relief. All 1,016 patients with a definitive relief answer also answered the three practice items on the same call. Compliance was YES for the spice infusion in 783 patients (77.1%), for diet in 846 (83.3%) and for Yoga in 825 (81.2%) (Table 3). Counting all non-responders as non-compliant gave lower bounds of 53.4%, 57.7% and 56.3% (n = 1,466). Among the 86 patients with tobacco abuse and a codable relief vote, 77 (89.5%) reported relief. Non-responders were not assumed to have relief (or lack of it), so no best (or worst) case estimate is reported.

Full-trio practitioners.

A total of 697 patients (47.5% of the 1,466 analysed) practised all three components (spice infusion, diet and Yoga): of these, 365 had a single visit and 332 had at least one revisit. Reported relief was YES in 679 (97.4%; Table 4, Figure 2). Full-trio patients accounted for 679 of the 850 relief reports in the analysed cohort (79.9%). Relief was reported by 165 of 204 patients practising one or two components (80.9%) and by 6 of 115 respondents practising none (5.2%; respondents only). Relief was found to be dosegradient dependent (Table 4): 326/332 (98.2%) at dose 4, 353/365 (96.7%) at dose 3, 142/159 (89.3%) at dose 2, 23/45 (51.1%) at dose 1 and 6/115 (5.2%) at dose 0 among respondents, with the conservative estimate identical at doses 1-4 and 1.1% (6/565) at dose 0 (Figure 2). Among analysed patients (n = 1,466), the correlation between full-trio compliance and relief was 0.55 (phi); full-trio compliance was recorded in 45.6% of single-visit and 49.8% of revisit patients.

Disease-system adherence and relief.

Adherence and relief varied modestly across the 14 disease systems (Figures 3 and 4). Among analysed patients who answered, spice-infusion compliance ranged from 62.5% (eye/ENT) to 88.9% (skin), diet advice from 72.4% (neurological) to 95.0% (infections), and Yoga practice from 75.0% (eye/ENT) to 85.7% (neoplastic/cystic). Relief ranged from 75.0% (eye/ENT) to 89.7% (infections, 35/39). Several extremes arose in the smallest systems, and patients with conditions in more than one system are counted in each (Table 2), so these comparisons remain descriptive.

Discussion

A culturally adapted, team-delivered traffic-light lifestyle intervention was feasible in a busy teaching-hospital clinic, with sustained uptake and high responder-reported benefit. This extends the acceptability and clinic-experience findings reported in [16, 28] by adding quantified outcomes. The final extract yielded a monotone dose gradient from 5.2% at dose 0 to 98.2% at dose 4 (responder estimate). Among the patients practising all three of the lifestyle advice, revisit patients reported relief more often than single-visit patients (87.2%.

[395/453] vs 80.8% [455/563]; difference 6.4 percentage points, 95% CI 1.9-10.9). This revisit advantage, like the dose gradient, is consistent with a genuine behaviour-benefit link, but also with selection effects and with more complete ascertainment among patients who return. Proposed common mechanisms (gut motility and barrier function, the braingut axis and microbiome, anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects, metabolic effects, autonomic and neuroendocrine effects, and dietary fibre) remain hypotheses [29, 30]. Our intervention components have supportive randomised evidence elsewhere (plant-based diets [7], Yoga for hypertension [8] and diabetes [9], and structured diabetes prevention in Asian Indians [6]); family-adapted programmes improve engagement [31], and self-efficacy mediates adherence [15, 32]. These findings suggest that the combined lifestyle programme may help relieve symptoms in patients with chronic diseases, although the single-arm design cannot establish causality.

Context. Dietary diversity, rather than supplementation alone, is the sustainable route to micronutrient adequacy in India [33]. Score-based, person-centred counselling of this type is typically cost-neutral or cost-saving [34]. The multimorbidity burden and the broadly similar relief pathways observed here are consistent with India's multimorbidity literature [12]. In public-sector health delivery, operationalising dietary interventions requires adapting international Food as Medicine (FAM) constructs to local socio-economic realities. The clinic's 21-item traffic-light guideline functions as an accessible, low-cost implementation of a whole-food, plant-predominant dietary pattern. Rather than relying on commercial meal delivery, it guides industrial worker families toward affordable, locally available whole plant foods, fresh salads, and culinary spices while eliminating ultraprocessed red-category items.

Comparability with worker populations. This clinic cohort is largely self-selected. For context, cohort prevalence estimates were of similar magnitude to independent regional estimates for industrial workers. A survey of 323 Faridabad factory workers found hypertension in 48.6%, diabetes in 10.5% and obesity in 31.8% [35]. Camp-based screening of 2,588 Faridabad workers found hypertension in 30.9% [36]. The state-wide Haryana STEPS survey found elevated blood pressure in 26.2%, raised blood glucose in 15.5% and overweight or obesity in 35.2%, with tobacco use in 38.9% of men [37]. Corresponding cohort figures, including screening-detected cases, were hypertension 34.9%, diabetes 16.8% and obesity 27.1%. Tobacco abuse (7.8% of the analysed cohort; 16.2% of the 703 men) was below the 41% reported among male industrial workers [38] and the 38.9% among Haryana men [37]. The disease profile in the study cohort is consistent with under-recording of behavioural risk factors in routine care rather than a lower habit burden. Tobacco abuse was nonetheless the most frequent behavioural-risk code in the cohort, and tobacco users who engaged with the programme appeared to respond well: 77 of the 86 tobacco-abuse patients with a codable relief vote (89.5%) reported relief (symptom relief; cessation was not verified).

Choice environment.

Patients visited the IHC exercising free choice among competing options in a tertiary, multi-speciality hospital where consultations, investigations and medicines are free at the point of use, and where dedicated Traditional and Complementary Medicine (AYUSH: Ayurveda, Yoga and naturopathy, unani, siddha and homeopathy) services are also available. The clinic's main message to prospective patients was explicit: "Come if you want to restore your health, and roll back disease from root causes through self-effort and lifestyle choices of diet, Yoga and spice infusion." Participation therefore reflects self-selection toward lifestyle care -a consideration relevant to interpreting the compliance and relief patterns reported here.

The practice of lifestyle medicine Lifestyle medicine is not a pill, and it cannot be evaluated or delivered through a one-size-fits-all protocol. It requires a deep inquiry into the patient's way of life to understand not only the root cause and underlying pathology but also one of the harder questions in medicine: why this patient became ill at this particular time. The art of practice of lifestyle medicine includes understanding the patient's mental attitude and physical infirmities, using the best available evidence and offering a different perspective: a choice between broad-based health and relief from many of the conditions troubling them, on the one hand, and continued deterioration and the development of related conditions if the offending lifestyle continues, on the other. Patients who make this choice were offered a protocol customised to their condition within the 21-item lifestyle guideline and the three core interventions of spice infusion, diet and Yoga. In the IHC clinic Yoga protocol, some asanas were emphasised for particular conditions (for example, Sarvangasana for haemorrhoids). The clinic also emphasised adequate salad intake in the morning and a light, vegetable-based boiled meal in the early evening, sufficiently before sleep. The advice consistently includes complete avoidance of the seven red-category items (foods and addictions), movement matched to the patient's readiness, and a daily rhythm aligned with the sun's diurnal cycle. The skills of patient interviewing, non-judgemental listening, root-cause formulation, motivation and continuity across every call and visit are learned competencies, not incidental by-products. We propose to codify this approach and offer it to interested physicians so that it can be practised more widely; the website lifequality.org.in is one attempt by the authors to make this knowledge openly available and encourage wider uptake [39] [40] [41]. While delivered within a public-sector tertiary teaching hospital framework serving an industrial workforce, the Integrative Health Clinic (IHC) protocol operationalises the standards described by the American College of Lifestyle Medicine (ACLM) and the broader lifestyle-medicine community [42]. By structuring care around the core pillars of lifestyle medicine-specifically integrating whole-food plantpredominant nutrition, structured daily Yoga and pranayama, and circadian alignmentthe clinic moves beyond traditional patient education into active therapeutic lifestyle change (ITLC -structured, clinician-guided modification of daily health behaviours as a prescribed, tracked part of care). Furthermore, the integration of family-centred attendance, group peer demonstrations, and a 0-21 tracked lifestyle score illustrates the feasibility of delivering comprehensive lifestyle medicine within a routine outpatient workflow.

Respect for the patient. The cardinal principle of the IHC clinic has been to keep the patient foremost. This is reflected chiefly in respect for patients' faith, beliefs, need for attention and dignity, and in treating them as partners in their wellness journey rather than passive recipients of dispensed medicines. This approach helped build trust and mutual connection and improved compliance with lifestyle advice and practices.

Patient questions and group attendance. During visits and call, the patients often asked many questions about lifestyle and, especially, about the three interventions. The clinic team encouraged questions and took time to answer them, often collectively. Group consultation is now an accepted part of lifestyle-medicine practice. We combined the repeat-visit with new patients, which allowed knowledge, attitudes and practices to spread across successive patient groups and enabled the team to explain lifestyle practices to the group at once.

Provider time and training. Individualised lifestyle attention is desirable for every patient with a chronic, lifestyle-related condition, but routine clinic time may not permit it. Training providers in the essentials of lifestyle medicine -including its practical nuances, . patient engagement, motivation and workflow management to reduce waiting and keep patients engaged -can therefore help make individualised care feasible at scale.

Access, literacy and motivation.

The beneficiary population -ESIC industrial workers and their families -faces steep barriers to clinic follow-up and telephone contact, which should frame the revisit and response figures. Most workers do 12-hour shifts six days a week, and workplace rules generally bar personal calls during shifts and non-emergency hospital visits. Many workers and family members have limited schooling and, where smartphones are available, limited familiarity with their use. Health-seeking behaviour is largely reactive, and the dominant motive for hospital attendance is access to free medicines, rather than lifestyle counselling. It was correspondingly difficult to persuade newly attending patients, in particular, to value and practise lifestyle change alongside medication. These circumstances partly explain the observed participation: only 702 of the 1,737 patients (40.4%) returned for at least one revisit, and 1,466 (84.4%) had a usable telephone contact and were called; 450 analysed patients never gave a definitive answer (30.7%). The revisit and telephone-response rates are therefore best interpreted in the light of access, literacy and motivational constraints rather than as indicators of how patients valued the programme. The constraints described in this paragraph are clinic observations and were not systematically audited.

Implications. The traffic-light, COM-B and Yoga components should be retained. The next step is prospective, controlled, disease-specific cohorts with predefined primary endpoints (for example, HbA1c, blood pressure or validated symptom scores), defined intervals, capture of harms, intention-to-treat analyses including non-responders, effect sizes and confidence intervals. No claims of cure, pan-disease efficacy or remission without predefined criteria are being made, and patients were advised to continue prescribed treatment. The four-spice culinary infusion remains under study and is not a proven treatment. A further way forward is population-level access to anonymised records: we propose that anonymised data from ESIC's Dhanwantri portal be made available in CSV form to interested planners, administrators and researchers, to assess the health of worker beneficiaries, to study prevalent conditions (anaemia, occupational disorders, hypertension and diabetes) and to enable data-driven preventive and promotive planning.

Strengths of the study. The intervention was delivered uniformly through a published 7step, COM-B-based protocol with a tracked 0-21 lifestyle score, provider-lead, in-clinic demonstration of Yoga and the four-spice infusion, and structured follow-up calls; the protocol and replication resources are publicly available [16, 18, [39] [40] [41]. High compliance with the three practices was largely a product of this delivery and follow-up protocol and plausibly contributed to the reported relief. Medication use was conservative, yet within guideline-concordant categories, with liberal correction of widespread micronutrient deficiency, keeping lifestyle change as the principal addition to standard care. The analysis reports the full 1,737-patient cohort alongside the 1,466 called patients, explicitly compares the two groups, and uses conservative denominators throughout, so responder estimates are not inflated by assumption.

Limitations. The main limitation is the absence of an untreated or alternative-treatment comparator; natural variation, regression to the mean, concurrent drug changes, placebo effects and selective follow-up may all have contributed to the observed changes. Because . the components were delivered jointly, their effects cannot be attributed separately to diet, Yoga, the infusion or medication. Additional limitations include self-reported binary relief (subject to recall and social-desirability bias and open to individual interpretation because the question was generic), classification based on the most recent definitive answer rather than at a fixed time point, 450 analysed patients without a definitive answer (30.7%), a single centre, contamination from free-text diagnoses and the failure to separate contact mode (telephone, WhatsApp or clinic). No validated disease-specific endpoints or effect sizes are presented, and all proportions carry only simple binomial uncertainty. Burden counts include screening-detected cases identified by pragmatic single-cut thresholds rather than clinical adjudication (thresholds and alternative cut-off checks are described in Methods and Table S3). No efficacy or disease-modifying claim is made for the four-spice infusion or for any individual spice. A three-state check of the underlying records (YES / NO / not recorded) showed that for 450 of the 1,466 analysed patients both the practice fields and the relief answer were not recorded; these are the same patients, and no patient had one recorded without the other. The missingness is therefore not at random, no imputation was attempted, and these patients are reported as a separate stratum rather than as confirmed non-adopters (see the note to Supplementary Table S2).

Future research. The four-spice infusion described here is a precisely described, low-cost, self-administered culinary preparation delivered to all patients as one component of a multi-component lifestyle programme. Because diet, Yoga, the infusion and pharmacotherapy were delivered jointly, and because relief was a single generic patientreported item, this study cannot attribute any outcome to the infusion, and no efficacy or disease-modifying claim is made for it. Its value at this stage is as a reproducible, culturally familiar exposure that can now be investigated directly. Prior work on the constituent spices provides component-level, predominantly metabolic and preclinical evidence only: meta-analyses of cinnamon and fenugreek for glycaemic control [20, 21], a placebocontrolled trial of cumin essential oil on glycaemic and inflammatory indices [22], and a preclinical systematic review of coriander and inflammatory mediators [23]. A randomised crossover trial of a polyphenol-rich seven-spice curry (6 g and 12 g) in healthy adults reported acute changes in gut microflora composition [43]. These studies used isolated spices, defined supplemental doses or a cooked curry matrix, and single or acute exposuresnot this home-prepared aqueous infusion; no study has evaluated this combination, this preparation or chronic daily use. We therefore propose a staged research agenda: (i) chemical and microbiological characterisation of the infusion, including batch variability, changes in composition across the three-day replenishment cycle, and the safety of that protocol; (ii) a prospectively registered, controlled, component-isolating study with predefined validated endpoints (bowel habit, pain, glycaemic and inflammatory markers) and systematic harms capture, including tolerability and interactions with concomitant medication; and (iii) only thereafter, evaluation of any role in chronic disease management.Furthermore, to overcome the limitation of joint delivery and strengthen causal inference without compromising routine care, prospective series of N-of-1 (singlepatient randomised crossover) trials offer a highly rigorous methodological next step. By alternating randomised blocks of specific lifestyle components-such as comparing periods of plant-based diet and Yoga with versus without the four-spice infusion-N-of-1 designs can isolate component-specific effects, control for regression to the mean, and generate aggregated Bayesian population-level evidence. Blinding to a home-prepared infusion is not feasible, so expectancy effects would remain, and an appropriate washout between blocks cannot be specified until the preparation is chemically characterised. This approach directly aligns with the personalised, root-cause paradigm of Lifestyle Medicine as championed by ACLM and the International Board of Lifestyle Medicine (IBLM).

Behaviour-change trajectory.

The practice of Lifestyle change is difficult, for patients and providers alike. Providers must convince patients of the need to change, while avoiding exaggeration of the consequences of not doing so. The trajectory of behaviour change is uncertain, with pitfalls that include lassitude, doubt, peer influence and misinterpretation or misuse of readily available medical information. To counter these threats, the clinic emphasised regular visits (at 2-3-week intervals) and follow-up by telephone, WhatsApp messages and polls. Yet many patients described the challenges of making lifestyle changes and the delicate social choices involved, and some relapsed after initial success. Encouragingly, patients who experience the benefits of change and sustain it for a reasonable period can follow the desired path with less difficulty thereafter.

Key conclusions

1. The process of behaviour change in routine care (KAP/COM-B): This report describes how knowledge, attitude and practice were built in a busy routine setting by a multidisciplinary team trained in all elements. The 21-item guideline clinicbased Yoga demonstration with a video-series link for home practice, and in-clinic demonstration of the four-spice infusion (with a preparation video) supported compliance. The published 7-step protocol [16], delivered by the multidisciplinary team (sequence in Appendix A), addressed all three COM components -capability, opportunity and motivation [17] -and supported high understanding and compliance [15, 16, 32]. The team observed peer demonstration in action [32]: repeatvisit patients performed Yoga and described their experience before new cases, doctor-led Yoga sessions drew participants, and patients practising in the clinic visibly gained confidence. This is a plausible engagement mechanism rather than a measured effect. 2. Whole-body pattern: Reported relief appeared across a range of chronic conditions spanning many systems, pointing to shared mechanisms such as inflammation, gut health, cardiovascular health, neuro-endocrine balance and mental health [29, 30]. 3. Joint delivery: Though diet, exercise and lifestyle changes were delivered together, making their individual contributions unmeasurable, yet their combine effect might have been the principal factor in contributing to well being and relief. In fact, combined diet-and-exercise approaches have the strongest trial support [6, 8, 9]. Yet, whether the components acted together to produce the observed difference remains a hypothesis. 4. Family effect: Patients were encouraged to bring family members and practise the lifestyle changes together. In total, 274 of 1,412 families (19.4%) were represented by more than one member, and 180 patients (10.4%) first attended after another family member [31]. Joint home practice itself was not measured. 5. Interrelated systems: Chronic conditions co-occurred (mean 2.24 patientcondition pairs per patient; 63.9% had conditions in two or more systems), and adherence and reported relief were broadly similar across systems. This is consistent with, but does not establish, a whole-body benefit [12].

Conclusion

This report provides exploratory, hypothesis-generating evidence of feasibility and responder-reported benefit. Prospective controlled evaluation is required before any claim of effect can be made.
