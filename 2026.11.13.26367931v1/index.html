To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 2 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Parkinson's disease (PD) is associated with sleep and motor function impairment, even in the early stages. Here, we investigated whether passive wearable-based sleep and activity tracking can detect PD and its associated pathological hallmarks, dopaminergic deficit and α-synuclein pathology. A combination with the MDS score modestly improved detection. Findings support further evaluation of wearable monitoring for PD screening and biomarker enrichment in larger populations.

Introduction

Parkinson's disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder defined by two primary, connected pathological hallmarks: the loss of dopaminergic neurons in the brain's nigrostriatal pathway and the accumulation of misfolded α-synuclein proteins in the central and peripheral nervous systems [1]. The disease manifests with cardinal motor symptoms such as resting tremor, bradykinesia, and rigidity, as well as early non-motor signs including, inter alia, sleep disturbances, olfactory loss, and depression. It is estimated that by the time of clinical diagnosis, more than 50% of dopaminergic neurons have been lost [2]. The prodromal phase of PD may therefore begin years before the onset of the key motor features [3]. This window presents an opportunity for effective intervention and testing of neuroprotective and diseasemodifying therapies, rendering early detection crucial.

Dopamine transporter (DAT) single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) scanning, also referred to as DAT scan, has shown high diagnostic precision for identifying nigrostriatal dopaminergic neurodegeneration and is frequently employed as a surrogate endpoint for PD progression in prodromal research [4]. More recently, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)-based α-synuclein seed amplification assays (SAA) have been shown to provide a more direct etiologic confirmation of the disease's underlying synucleinopathy through the detection of misfolded α-synuclein aggregates [5, 6]. Although reliable, both tests require specialized clinical resources that limit their suitability for cost-efficient large-scale screening. Research criteria based on risk factors and the emergence of non-motor and early motor symptoms have also been proposed for diagnosing prodromal PD [7, 8]. While useful, their application requires neurological evaluations and niche ancillary testing, potentially limiting the identification of individuals with early, subclinical disease [9]. Therefore, an accessible, unobtrusive, and scalable method remains essential for identifying individuals at risk of PD who warrant specialist assessment and confirmatory biomarker testing.

With their growing adoption, mobile and wearable technologies offer promising opportunities. Recent work has demonstrated the potential of fit-for-purpose smartphone-based tasks to predict DAT scan results [10], yet, such approaches require active engagement, which may limit adherence [11], and may be susceptible to behavioral changes due to monitoring awareness (the Hawthorne effect). In contrast, sleep and activity profiles, which are affected by PD even in its early stages owing to established prodromal conditions such as REM sleep behavior disorder (RBD) and sub-threshold parkinsonism [8, 12], can be passively tracked via wearable data [13, 14]. Latest studies demonstrate that relevant features derived from wrist wearables could identify PD years before clinical diagnosis [14] and are associated with dopaminergic deficit and α-synuclein pathology in individuals at risk of the disease [15]. Nevertheless, recent work showed that cross-cohort generalization of wearable metrics-based machine learning models remains a challenge [16]. For clinical translation, however, wearable-derived measures must produce consistent results across device hardware and populations, demonstrate temporal reliability, and rely on reproducible and interpretable algorithms [17] [18] [19].

This study investigates whether sleep and activity metrics (SAM) derived from passively collected free-living wrist-wearable data can identify persons with PD (PwPD), dopaminergic deficit, or α-synuclein pathology. Leveraging wrist accelerometry data from international cohorts, we developed the machine learning-based SAM-PD score and evaluated its performance in differentiating: (1) individuals with PD from healthy controls (HCs); (2) at-risk individuals with normal or abnormal DAT scan results; and (3) atrisk individuals with positive or negative CSF α-synuclein SAA results. We further compared the SAM-PD score against the Movement Disorder Society (MDS) prodromal PD risk score and investigated whether their combination enhances detection beyond what either approach achieves alone across several pathological hallmark scenarios. To ensure trustworthiness of the SAM-PD score, we assessed explainability, test-retest reliability, performance bias across key sensitive variables, and generalizability across different device hardware and geographic regions. With SAM-PD, we aim to introduce a digital tool that could complement established clinical measures and pave the way for unobtrusive, scalable, and costeffective screening of individuals at risk of PD.

Results

Cohort characteristics

We analyzed free-living wrist accelerometry data from 323 participants in the U.S.-based Verily Study Watch cohort of the Parkinson's Progression Markers Initiative (PPMI) study (NCT01141023) and 81 participants in the European AI-PROGNOSIS Digital Biomarkers Development (dBM-DEV) study (NCT06444789). Participants in the Verily Study Watch cohort were monitored for 484 ± 202 days (mean ± standard deviation) using the Verily Study Watch device (Verily Life Sciences LLC, Dallas, TX, USA), whereas participants in the dBM-DEV study were monitored for 40 ± 29 days using the Garmin Vivoactive 5 device (Garmin Ltd., Schaffhausen, Switzerland). After applying inclusion criteria requiring at least one 14-day monitoring window including seven days and seven nights of adequate wearable data ('Datasets and cohorts', Methods), 283 participants from the Verily Study Watch cohort (133 PwPD, 26 HCs, and 124 individuals at risk of PD) and 61 participants from the dBM-DEV study (20 PwPD, 20 HCs, and 21 individuals at risk of PD) qualified for further analysis. The demographic and clinical characteristics of included participants are summarized in Table 1. No statistical differences in sex, age, or body mass index (BMI) were observed between PwPD and HCs in either cohort (p ≥ 0.197). Within the Verily Study Watch cohort, at-risk participants differed in age from both HCs (p < 0.05) and PwPD (p < 0.001) and included a higher proportion of females than PwPD (61.3% vs. 40.6%, p < 0.001). A similar age difference was observed in the dBM-DEV cohort between at-risk participants and both HCs (p < 0.05) and PwPD (p < 0.001). Between cohorts, significant differences were observed in age among both PwPD (p < 0.001) and at-risk participants (p < 0.001), and in sex for the at-risk group only (p = 0.001). Detailed results of statistical comparisons are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

For the Verily Study Watch cohort, DAT scan and CSF α-synuclein SAA data were available. DAT scan imaging and CSF collection were performed before wearable data collection began, whereas SAA testing was conducted on the stored CSF samples after completion of wearable data collection. For analyses involving DAT scan results, we excluded PwPD and at-risk individuals whose DAT scans were acquired more than three years before the start of wearable data collection, while retaining all HCs regardless of the DAT scan acquisition date. A sleep and activity metrics-based PD score

We computed 32 sleep and activity metrics from raw acceleration signals resampled to 25 Hz (Supplementary Table 2). Temporally aggregating these metrics over observation windows using five statistical measures resulted in 160 candidate features. We then used the computed feature sets from the Verily Study Watch PwPD (n = 133) and HC (n = 26) groups to develop a machine-learning model that distinguishes PwPD from HC. We refer to this model as the SAM-PD model and to its output probability as the SAM-PD score. We first trained, calibrated, and internally evaluated the model under a nested Leave-One-Subject-Out (LOSO) cross-validation scheme. Feature selection within the outer training set of each LOSO fold, based on temporal reliability and collinearity, reduced the feature set to 15 aggregated sleep and activity metrics ('Machine learning', Methods). These were combined with age, sex, weight, and height for subsequent model fitting. Of the 160 candidate features, 104 exhibited good test-retest reliability between each participant's first qualifying fortnight and the subsequent fortnight (ICC(2,1) 95% confidence interval lower bound ≥ 0.75) across folds. Clustering highly correlated features (Spearman's correlation coefficient |ρ| ≥ 0.90) and retaining the most temporally reliable feature from each cluster produced the final set of 15 features per fold. The consensus feature set across all LOSO folds comprised four sleep, four upper-limb movements and seven ambulatory activity metrics, providing a compact representation of wearable-based sleep and activity digital phenotypes. Among the classification algorithms evaluated, balanced random forest and ridge logistic regression achieved identical area under the ROC curve (AUROC) (Supplementary Table 3). The former was however selected for SAM-PD because of its lower expected calibration error (ECE). All results reported hereafter correspond to the balanced random forest-based model. Table 3 summarizes the performance of the LOSO-evaluated calibrated and uncalibrated SAM-PD models, with sensitivity and specificity reported at the Youden-optimal operating point [21]. The calibrated model achieved AUROC of 0.93 (95% CI: 0.88-0.97) and an AUPRC of 0.98 (95% CI: 0.97-1.00). Probability calibration reduced the ECE from 0.17 (95% CI: 0.14-0.22) to 0.04 (95% CI: 0.03-0.09). Detailed performance metrics are provided in Supplementary Table 4 and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) and precision-recall curves are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. In Supplementary Table 4, we also report performance metrics at an operating point that maximizes the F1 score and at an operating point that maximizes sensitivity while maintaining specificity ≥ 0.50, representing a plausible configuration for a screening system. Calibration curves are shown in Supplementary Fig. 2. Hereafter, SAM-PD scores correspond to the calibrated model output probabilities.

SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP)-based feature contributions to the SAM-PD model are shown in Fig. 2. Estimated REM sleep duration was the strongest individual driver of the PD probability, followed by short-term acceleration entropy and peak acceleration. Walking cadence, the variability of short-term acceleration entropy, and estimated REM sleep latency were also among the most predictive features. Among the demographic variables, younger age was associated with a higher PD probability, whereas sex, weight, and height contributed negligibly. This counter-intuitive age association may reflect conditional relationships among predictors or cohort composition and is discussed further in the Discussion section.

We further performed an exploratory assessment of model fairness for the PwPD versus HC classification task by evaluating performance across three attributes, age, sex, and BMI, and calculating disparity metrics, including the Demographic Parity Difference (DPD), Demographic Parity Ratio (DPR), and Equalized Odds Difference (EOD) (Supplementary Tables 5 and 6). Classification performance metrics were computed only for subgroups with at least five participants in each outcome class. AUROC point estimates remained high across evaluable age ranges (50-69 years: AUROC = 0.96, AUPRC = 0.99; ≥ 70 years: AUROC = 0.92, AUPRC = 0.98), as well as between females (AUROC = 0.96) and males (AUROC = 0.90). For BMI subgroups, performance was higher in the overweight subgroup (AUROC = 0.98, AUPRC = 1.00) compared to the normal weight subgroup (AUROC = 0.85, AUPRC = 0.97). Regarding disparity metrics, DPR ranged from 0.86 (age) to 1.00 (BMI), DPD from 0.00 (BMI) to 0.12 (age), and EOD from 0.13 (sex) to 0.33 (BMI). The small number of HCs in certain subgroups limits the precision of these estimates. The cohort was predominantly White (n = 153) and of unspecified ethnicity (n = 110), whereas all other racial and ethnic subgroups were underrepresented, precluding meaningful fairness evaluation for these attributes.

Association with dopaminergic deficit and α-synuclein pathology

Having established that SAM-PD effectively discriminates PwPD from HCs, we next assessed whether the model captures the presence of dopaminergic deficit and α-synuclein pathology in the Verily Study Watch at-risk group. To this end, we retrained and calibrated the SAM-PD model using the complete Verily Study Watch PwPD (n = 133) and HC (n = 26) dataset, with the consensus feature set and hyperparameters derived from the LOSO cross-validation, and then applied it to the at-risk individuals without any additional tuning. We further compared the performance of SAM-PD with two versions of the MDS prodromal PD risk score: a version incorporating all available risk and prodromal markers, and a mini version incorporating only markers that do not require specialized testing (see Methods, 'MDS prodromal PD risk score and combination strategies'). Figure 3 illustrates the relationship between biomarkers of dopaminergic deficit (DAT scan-derived minimum putaminal specific binding ratio [SBR]) and α-synuclein pathology (CSF α-synuclein SAA mean maximum fluorescence [Fmax]) and the SAM-PD and MDS scores. In the combined PwPD and HC cohort (Fig. 3a), higher SAM-PD scores were associated with lower SBR (Pearson r = -0.60, p < 0.05, n = 112) and higher SAA mean Fmax (r = +0.48, p < 0.05, n = 128); 20 of 148 participants were excluded from the Fmax correlation analysis, because their SAA results were obtained using a different assay protocol. In the at-risk group (Fig. 3b), the SAM-PD score showed weak-to-moderate, statistically significant correlations with both biomarkers (SBR: r = -0.44, p < 0.05, n = 121; mean Fmax: r = +0.40, p < 0.05, n = 98) in the expected direction. The MDS score showed similarly significant correlations (SBR: r = -0.33, p < 0.05, n = 121; mean Fmax: r = +0.31, p < 0.05, n = 98), whereas the mini-MDS risk score correlated significantly, albeit weakly, only with SBR (r = -0.18, p < 0.05, n = 121).

Table 4 summarizes the performance of the SAM-PD and MDS scores in detecting the presence of dopaminergic deficit and α-synuclein pathology in the at-risk group. ROC and precision-recall curves are shown in Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4, respectively, and additional performance metrics are included in Supplementary Table 7. For dopaminergic deficit, the SAM-PD score showed higher AUROC but comparable AUPRC to the MDS risk score in distinguishing individuals with normal versus abnormal DAT scans (AUROC = 0.86, 95% CI: 0.75-0.94; AUPRC = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.25-0.73 vs. AUROC = 0.80, 95% CI: 0.66-0.91; AUPRC = 0.46, 95% CI: 0.21-0.68). For α-synuclein pathology, the SAM-PD model showed comparable AUROC and higher AUPRC than the MDS risk score in distinguishing individuals with negative versus positive SAA (AUROC = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.62-0.90; AUPRC = 0.50, 95% CI: 0.30-0.75 vs. AUROC = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.65-0.87; AUPRC = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.25-0.63). SAM-PD detected the presence of at least one pathological feature with an AUROC of 0.79 (95% CI: 0.67-0.90) and AUPRC of 0.59 (95% CI: 0.40-0.79), demonstrating higher AUPRC and comparable AUROC to the MDS risk score (AUROC = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.65-0.85; AUPRC = 0.50, 95% CI: 0.35-0.68). In identifying individuals with both pathological features, SAM-PD achieved an AUROC of 0.94 (95% CI: 0.86-1.00) and an AUPRC of 0.57 (95% CI: 0.30-0.96), performing comparably to the MDS risk score (AUROC = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.86-0.98; AUPRC = 0.56, 95% CI: 0.23-0.85). Across all four pathological hallmark scenarios, AUROC and AUPRC were consistently higher for SAM-PD than the mini-MDS risk score.

For the SAM-PD score, the decision threshold was set at the Youden-optimal operating point (0.78) derived from the LOSO-based model training and evaluation for PwPD versus HC classification. For the MDS score, the recommended threshold for prodromal PD (0.80) was used [8]. SAM-PD demonstrated the highest sensitivity across all pathological hallmark scenarios while maintaining a specificity consistently ≥ 0.85, comparable to that of the MDS score. The mini-MDS score yielded the highest specificity across all four scenarios, but had very low sensitivity, indicating limited case detection at the recommended 0.80 threshold. Supplementary Fig. 5 shows the distributions of the SAM-PD, MDS, and mini-MDS scores across pathological hallmark scenarios in Verily Study Watch at-risk individuals. Pairwise comparisons using a two-sided Mann-Whitney U test yielded statistically significant differences between the positive and negative groups across all scenarios for all three scores.

External validation in independent cohort

To assess its generalizability, we applied the SAM-PD model, trained using the PwPD and HC cohorts of the Verily Study Watch dataset, to the independent dBM-DEV cohort (n = 61) without additional tuning. The same decision threshold (0.78) as in the at-risk analyses was used. Despite differences in hardware and geographic location, SAM-PD demonstrated similar performance in distinguishing PwPD (n = 20) from HCs (n = 20), achieving an AUROC of 0.95 (95% CI: 0.87-0.99) and an AUPRC of 0.95 (95% CI: 0.89-0.99), with a sensitivity of 0.85 (95% CI: 0.62-0.97) and a specificity of 0.90 (95% CI: 0.68-0.99). ROC and precision-recall curves are shown in Supplementary Fig. 6, whereas detailed performance metrics, including those at alternative decision thresholds, are provided in Supplementary Table 8.

Figure 4 shows the distributions of the SAM-PD score and its constituent sleep and activity metrics across the PwPD, at-risk, and HC groups in the Verily Study Watch and dBM-DEV cohorts. Statistical comparisons were performed using rank-based analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) for sleep and activity metrics, with age and sex adjustment, and the two-sided Mann-Whitney U test for the SAM-PD score. In both cases, Bonferroni correction was applied. In the Verily Study Watch cohort, the SAM-PD score and input feature values of at-risk individuals generally fell between those of PwPD and HCs. Pairwise comparisons revealed statistically significant differences between the PwPD group and each of the other two groups for all measures except non-REM (NREM) sleep duration and walking-bout acceleration. No significant differences were observed between HCs and at-risk individuals, except for walking-bout acceleration and hourly acceleration. Similar patterns were observed for the SAM-PD score and most input features in the dBM-DEV cohort. Nevertheless, for nearly half of the metrics, the differences between PwPD and each of the other two groups were not statistically significant. Notably, in this cohort, at-risk individuals, all of whom had PSG-confirmed RBD, also exhibited significantly shorter REM sleep duration, longer REM sleep latency, and longer NREM sleep duration than HCs, whereas these sleep metrics were comparable to those of PwPD. The detailed adjusted and unadjusted comparisons are provided in Supplementary Table 9.

Test-retest reliability

We next evaluated the temporal reliability of the SAM-PD score and its constituent sleep and activity metrics by conducting a test-retest analysis in both the Verily Study Watch (n = 119) and dBM-DEV (n = 50) cohorts. To characterize how reliability changes with longer monitoring, we computed the absoluteagreement, single-measurement intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC(2,1)) over consecutive, nonoverlapping windows of 3, 7, 10, and 14 days spanning each participant's first qualifying fortnight and the immediately subsequent fortnight, assuming that participants' health status remained stable throughout this interval. Figure 5 shows the evolution of ICC(2,1) across observation windows for the SAM-PD score and its input features in the Verily Study Watch and dBM-DEV cohorts. In both cohorts, reliability improved with longer aggregation windows, with sleep and hourly step features showing the greatest improvement. The selected Verily Study Watch features were required to meet the good-to-excellent reliability criterion (ICC(2,1) 95% CI lower bound ≥ 0.75) at the 14-day window, and all also showed good-to-excellent reliability in the dBM-DEV cohort (ICC(2,1) ≥ 0.75). The SAM-PD score achieved excellent absolute agreement in both cohorts when computed from features aggregated over 14 days (Verily Study Watch: ICC(2,1) = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.94-0.97; dBM-DEV: ICC(2,1) = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.93-0.98). Supplementary Table 10 reports ICC(2,1) values for all input features and the SAM-PD score in both cohorts, together with the relative importance of each feature in the model.

Complementarity with the MDS prodromal PD risk score

To explore the complementarity of the SAM-PD and MDS scores, we first examined their correlation in the at-risk group of the Verily Study Watch cohort. The SAM-PD score showed a moderate positive correlation with the MDS risk score (r = 0.43, p < 0.05) and a weak, non-significant positive correlation with the mini-MDS risk score (r = 0.17, p = 0.06). Together with their comparative performance in detecting pathological features, these findings suggest that the SAM-PD and MDS scores may capture complementary aspects of individuals' health status.

We then investigated whether combining the SAM-PD and the MDS scores could improve the detection of pathological hallmarks. We evaluated several score-combination strategies ('MDS prodromal PD risk score and combination strategies', Methods), including fusion into an Ensemble PD score using a meta-learner, maximum-score screening, and sequential screening. Table 5 summarizes the performance for the Ensemble PD score, which consistently yielded higher performance metrics than the other two approaches, whereas performance metrics for all combination strategies are reported in Supplementary Table 7. ROC and precision-recall curves are shown in Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4, respectively. For dopaminergic deficit, the Ensemble PD score achieved an AUROC of 0.89 (95% CI: 0.80-0.96) and an AUPRC of 0.61 (95% CI: 0.35-0.83), whereas for α-synuclein pathology, it achieved an AUROC of 0.77 (95% CI: 0.64-0.91) and an AUPRC of 0.56 (95% CI: 0.36-0.79). The Ensemble PD score also achieved an AUROC of 0.81 (95% CI: 0.70-0.91; AUPRC = 0.67, 95% CI: 0.50-0.83) for detecting the presence of either pathological hallmark and an AUROC of 0.97 (95% CI: 0.90-1.00; AUPRC = 0.81, 95% CI: 0.50-1.00) for detecting the concurrent presence of both disease hallmarks. Compared with the standalone scores (Table 4), the Ensemble PD score had a higher or equal AUROC in all four pathological hallmark scenarios and the highest AUPRC in each scenario; however, the corresponding 95% confidence intervals overlapped with those of the standalone scores. The mini-Ensemble PD score, obtained by fusing the SAM-PD and mini-MDS scores, achieved AUROC estimates comparable to those of SAM-PD alone and at least as high as those of both MDS score versions, yet consistently lower AUPRC estimates than the Ensemble PD score. The fitted meta-learner coefficients are reported in Supplementary Table 11. In both meta-learners, the SAM-PD score received greater weight than the MDS or mini-MDS risk scores.

The Ensemble PD score achieved higher sensitivity than the standalone MDS risk score across all pathological hallmark scenarios (0.52-0.86 vs. 0.37-0.71), while maintaining comparable specificity (0.89-0.92 vs. 0.86-0.88). For detecting the concurrent presence of both disease hallmarks, both ensemble scores reached a sensitivity of 0.86; however, this result should be interpreted cautiously given the small number of positive cases (n = 7). The mini-Ensemble PD score matched or exceeded the Ensemble PD score in sensitivity in all four scenarios, while showing lower specificity. In Supplementary Table 12, we also report performance metrics at an operating point that maximizes the F1 score and at an operating point that maximizes sensitivity while maintaining specificity ≥ 0.50. Supplementary Fig. 5 shows the distributions of both ensemble scores across pathological hallmark scenarios in Verily Study Watch at-risk individuals. Pairwise comparisons using a two-sided Mann-Whitney U test yielded statistically significant differences between the positive and negative groups across all scenarios for both scores.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that passive sleep and activity tracking using wrist accelerometry can effectively discriminate PwPD from HCs and identify at-risk individuals with dopaminergic deficit or α-synuclein pathology. Key strengths include the generalizability of the SAM-PD score to an independent cohort, the good-to-excellent temporal reliability of both the score and its constituent sleep and activity metrics, and its complementarity with the MDS prodromal PD risk score. All sleep and activity metrics used in this study were derived from raw accelerometer signals using open-source algorithms, further supporting reproducibility and independent validation across new cohorts and wearable hardware platforms. We framed model development as a PwPD-versus-HC classification task because our primary aim was to develop a PD screening system rather than models for detecting the specific pathological hallmarks.

We then evaluated the SAM-PD model in at-risk individuals, considering the presence of dopaminergic deficit or α-synuclein pathology as biologically relevant markers of future phenoconversion [22] [23] [24]. Importantly, the vast majority of PwPD in the training set had objective evidence of nigrostriatal degeneration and α-synuclein pathology: 98.8% (85/86) exhibited a dopaminergic deficit on DAT imaging and 86.1% (105/122) had a positive SAA test (Fig. 1). In contrast, none of the 26 HCs had an abnormal DAT scan result or a positive SAA test. These findings support the biological relevance of the clinical labels used for model development and motivate evaluation of the SAM-PD score against biomarker status in atrisk individuals.

Sleep and activity metrics were selected to build the core SAM-PD model for distinguishing PwPD from HCs because both domains are affected by the disease and encompass early, subclinical manifestations that may go unnoticed. Indeed, sleep disturbances [25] and subtle upper-limb motor changes [26] are among the earliest signs of the disorder that can be captured unobtrusively using wrist accelerometry. In this study, the most predictive features comprised a combination of sleep, upper-limb movement, and ambulatory metrics that showed biologically plausible contributions to the SAM-PD score. Shorter REM sleep duration was the strongest predictor and, together with reduced sleep efficiency, reduced estimated NREM duration and longer estimated REM sleep latency, increased the score, consistent with sleep disturbances commonly observed in PD. REM sleep time was also identified as a significant predictor in a similar study by Schalkamp et al. (2025) [15], which used precomputed sleep features derived from a different sleep-staging algorithm. Moreover, the contribution of salient upper-limb movement and ambulatory metrics to the SAM-PD output is consistent with the motor impairment associated with PD. Higher short-term acceleration entropy, indicating a more irregular movement intensity during brief hand-movement bouts, increased the SAM-PD score. This may reflect the increased variability of upper-limb movements described in PD, which has been linked to bradykinesia and is most pronounced for fast, brief movements [27]. Features reflecting reduced peak acceleration and lower hourly acceleration, as well as lower variability in walking-bout acceleration, further increased the score. These findings underscore the importance of reduced movement vigor, overall activity volume, and constrained movement patterns, respectively. In addition, reduced and more variable walking cadence, indicative of slower and less rhythmic stepping, together with reduced and less variable hourly step counts, increased the SAM-PD score. This pattern is consistent with impaired gait and decreased, more stereotyped daily activity that is common in PD [28].

While advanced age is an established risk factor for PD [29], our SHAP-based analysis indicated that younger age was associated with an increased predicted probability of PD within the SAM-PD model. This counter-intuitive association may reflect conditional relationships among predictors or cohort composition. The SAM metrics used in our model may vary with age independently of disease status. In this context, demographic variables may serve to contextualize these metrics rather than act as isolated predictors. For instance, older age is naturally associated with reduced activity and altered sleep patterns, regardless of disease status. Therefore, a lower activity profile in a younger individual may represent a greater deviation from the expected age-specific baseline compared with a similar profile in an older individual. Alternatively, this finding may partly reflect the underrepresentation of HCs in younger age subgroups of the development cohort. Further investigation in larger and more age-balanced populations is needed to determine the reproducibility and underlying drivers of this age association.

For detecting PD, the SAM-PD score achieved an AUROC of 0.93 (AUPRC = 0.98) in the U.S.based Verily Study Watch cohort. Similar high discrimination was also observed in the European dBM-DEV study cohort (AUROC = 0.95; AUPRC = 0.95), which was monitored using a different, consumergrade smartwatch. The findings support the geographic and cross-device generalizability of SAM-PD.

Performance in the Verily Study Watch cohort was slightly higher than that reported by Schalkamp et al. (2025) [15], who used a logistic regression model trained on sleep, physical activity, and vital sign data (AUPRC = 0.96). However, differences in participant selection and model evaluation between the studies preclude direct performance comparison. The SAM-PD score and input feature values of at-risk individuals generally fell between those of PwPD and HCs. Pairwise comparisons in the Verily Study Watch cohort showed statistically significant differences between the PwPD group and the two other groups in almost all cases. Such differences were largely absent between HCs and at-risk individuals, which is to be expected, as not all at-risk individuals had confirmed neuropathological features of interest or other pathologies that could affect sleep or activity. This was not the case for the dBM-DEV cohort, where all at-risk individuals had PSG-confirmed RBD. Although similar patterns to those of the Verily Study Watch cohort were observed in most upper-limb movement and ambulatory metrics of the three groups, at-risk individuals in the dBM-DEV cohort exhibited significantly shorter estimated REM sleep duration, longer estimated REM sleep latency, and longer estimated NREM sleep duration than HCs, whereas these sleep metrics were comparable to those of PwPD.

Temporal reliability analyses further showed that two weeks of data were sufficient to produce reliable (ICC(2,1) > 0.75) sleep and activity input metrics, as well as SAM-PD scores, in both cohorts. This supports the repeatability of measurements over short monitoring periods, a prerequisite for applications such as participant selection for clinical trials. However, the utility of the score for trial enrichment remains to be established prospectively. Exploratory fairness analyses in the Verily Study Watch cohort showed high discriminative performance for the PD versus HC classification task across evaluable age and sex subgroups, with minor to no disparities. The largest disparities, both in error rates (EOD = 0.33) and in discrimination, were observed between BMI subgroups, with a drop in AUROC for the normal weight subgroup compared to the overweight subgroup (|ΔAUROC| = 0.13); a modest error-rate disparity was also observed for age subgroups (EOD = 0.20). However, due to the small number of HCs in certain subgroups, these findings necessitate further investigation in larger cohorts. Evaluation by race and ethnicity was not possible due to the limited diversity in the cohort.

Turning to pathological hallmark detection, the SAM-PD model effectively detected the presence of dopaminergic deficit and α-synuclein pathology in the Verily Study Watch at-risk group and demonstrated weak-to-moderate but significant and plausible correlations with the corresponding biological markers. The score performed comparably to the MDS prodromal PD risk score across pathological hallmark detection scenarios, despite the latter requiring specialized evaluation and, for certain markers, resource-intensive confirmatory testing, such as PSG. Overall, SAM-PD was consistently more sensitive than the MDS score across scenarios, at matched or slightly higher specificity. These findings align with those reported in the study by Schalkamp et al. (2025) [15]. However, across corresponding pathological hallmark detection scenarios, SAM-PD achieved higher recall and precision than the digital score reported in that study at the decision threshold maximizing the F1 score (Supplementary Tables 7 and 12). Although direct comparison is limited by differences in the study cohorts, cross-validation scheme, and potential variations in abnormal DAT scan classification, the passively captured SAM-PD score alone performed comparably in detecting dopaminergic deficit (AUROC = 0.86) to a recently reported model trained solely on smartphone motor test-derived features to directly predict DAT scan results (AUROC = 0.82; sexadjusted AUROC = 0.80) [10].

Regarding its alignment with the established research criteria, the SAM-PD score correlated moderately with the comprehensive MDS prodromal PD risk score, and showed a weak, non-significant correlation with the mini-MDS risk score. Combining the SAM-PD and comprehensive MDS scores into an Ensemble PD score improved AUROC and AUPRC across pathological hallmark scenarios. At operating thresholds balancing sensitivity and specificity, the ensemble score achieved higher sensitivity relative to the comprehensive MDS score alone while maintaining a specificity of ≥ 0.80. These improvements in discrimination, together with the modest correlation between the scores, suggest that SAM-PD captures distinct aspects of nocturnal sleep and daily activity dynamics that may provide complementary information beyond that captured by current MDS criteria. Notably, combining the SAM-PD score with the mini-MDS score, which comprises only markers that do not require specialized testing, achieved discrimination comparable to the SAM-PD and the comprehensive MDS scores alone. Combining wearable-based passive sleep and activity tracking with a questionnaire assessing risk markers that can be self-reported with relative confidence (e.g., via a mobile application) may create a transferable, tiered screening strategy that could help triage individuals for specialized clinical assessment. Such a tiered approach is essential as questionnaire-based MDS criteria alone often lack sufficient predictive value for general population screening [9]. Moreover, unlike active digital assessments, passive monitoring enables continuous data collection with minimal user engagement. This is particularly relevant for long-term monitoring of individuals who may not yet experience symptoms and therefore have little motivation to engage with task-based protocols over extended periods to detect subtle changes [11].

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Regarding the representativeness of the training sample, the class imbalance between PwPD and HC (approximately 84% positive) does not reflect population prevalence. Because probability calibration was performed using a stratified subset of this prevalence-enriched cohort, recalibration on population-representative samples will be required to better align probabilities with true disease likelihoods. In addition, Platt scaling was chosen over non-parametric calibration partly because of the small size of the held-out calibration set, which limits the precision of the calibration estimates. The small number of HCs also limits the precision of specificity estimates. Moreover, model fairness cannot be reliably assessed across underrepresented demographic groups, necessitating evaluation in broader, more diverse cohorts.

There are also certain methodological constraints that should be noted. For the PwPD and at-risk groups, DAT imaging was performed up to three years before wearable data collection. Although serial imaging shows that nigrostriatal dopaminergic decline during the prodromal phase progresses gradually over the years [30], the three-year interval may introduce disagreement between measured biomarker status and biological status during wearable monitoring. For HCs, baseline scans were retained regardless of acquisition date, assuming that the gradual age-related decline in striatal DAT binding seen in healthy individuals would not change their normal scan classification. Moreover, the gait detector used in the upperlimb movement feature extraction pipeline was trained on data from healthy individuals. Although it was optimized for high sensitivity in detecting gait bouts, further validation in populations with impaired gait patterns is warranted. Lastly, another limitation is the lack of information about the wrist on which participants wore the wearable device in the Verily Study Watch cohort, which may have obscured lateralized motor symptoms.

Overall, this study represents a meaningful advance toward integrating passive wearable-based monitoring into PD screening pathways. Further validation in larger, population-representative cohorts, such as the UK Biobank [31] and the All of Us Research Program [32] is now needed to address the limitations of the study and further establish whether the SAM-PD score and its associated sleep and activity metrics can predict conversion from at-risk status to clinically manifest PD. Such validation would extend the present cross-sectional findings to longitudinal clinical outcomes. If validated across diverse populations, combining passive wearable monitoring with established clinical risk frameworks could enable scalable identification of individuals who warrant specialist assessment and further confirmatory testing for PD.

Methods

Datasets and cohorts

We utilized data from the Parkinson's Progression Markers Initiative (PPMI) Verily Study Watch sub-study (NCT01141023) and the AI-PROGNOSIS Digital Biomarkers Development (dBM-DEV) study (NCT06444789). Participants in the Verily Study Watch cohort belonged to one of three groups: (a) clinically diagnosed PwPD, including participants from the PPMI de novo PD and genetic PD cohorts, (b) HCs with no neurological conditions, or (c) individuals at risk of PD, belonging to the PPMI Prodromal cohort, with one or more of the following characteristics: one or more PD-associated genetic variants (in this cohort GBA and/or LRRK2), hyposmia according to the PPMI Consensus Cohort Committee, or PSGconfirmed RBD [33, 34]. The Verily Study Watch substudy was conducted in the U.S. from 2018 to 2020, and participants were monitored using the Verily Study Watch (Verily Life Sciences LLC, Dallas, TX, USA). The device is equipped with multiple sensors, including a triaxial accelerometer and gyroscope, photoplethysmography, ambient temperature, pressure, electrodermal activity, and humidity sensors. Participants in the dBM-DEV study cohort likewise belonged to one of three groups: (a) clinically diagnosed PwPD, (b) HCs with no neurological conditions or parasomnias, or (c) individuals at risk of PD based on PSG-confirmed RBD. The dBM-DEV study was conducted in Germany, France, and Spain between October 2024 and January 2026, and participants were monitored using the Garmin Vivoactive 5 smartwatch (Garmin Ltd., Schaffhausen, Switzerland). The device is equipped with a triaxial accelerometer and photoplethysmography.

In both cohorts, we applied wrist-wearable wear-time inclusion criteria requiring at least one 14day monitoring window with a minimum of seven valid days and seven valid nights. A day was considered valid if the participant wore the device for at least 10 hours during the waking period [35], defined in this study as 06:00-23:00. A night was considered valid if the sleep detection algorithm ('Sleep and activity metrics extraction', Methods) identified a main sleep block of at least four hours. After applying the weartime inclusion criteria, 283 participants from the Verily Study Watch cohort (133 PwPD, 26 HCs, 124 atrisk individuals) and 61 participants from the dBM-DEV study cohort (20 PwPD, 20 HCs, 21 at-risk individuals) qualified for further analysis. For those participants, all non-overlapping 14-day windows meeting the minimum requirement of seven valid days and seven valid nights during the observation period were included in the analysis.

The PPMI study and Verily Study Watch substudy were approved by the relevant institutional review boards or ethics committees at participating sites, and all participants provided written informed consent. The PPMI study is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT01141023). This study involved secondary analysis of de-identified data obtained from the PPMI database (https://www.ppmi-info.org) and additionally used DAT scan and CSF α-synuclein SAA results from the at-risk group, obtained also from the PPMI database after approval by the PPMI Data Access Committee. The PPMI clinico-demographic data were accessed on 12 March 2024, the genetic data on 16 April 2024, the Verily Study Watch sensor data on 22 November 2023, and the sequestered biomarker data (DAT scan and CSF α-synuclein SAA) on 19 November 2024. The AI-PROGNOSIS dBM-DEV study was approved by the relevant ethics committees at participating sites (Germany: Ethikkommission an der TU Dresden -BO-EK-2012024; France: Comité de protection des personnes Ile de France III -2023-A02136-59; Spain: Comité de Ética de la Investigación con medicamentos del Hospital Ruber Internacional de Madrid -Sa-18773 / 15 -EC: 535), and all participants provided written informed consent. The AI-PROGNOSIS dBM-DEV study is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06444789). This study involved secondary analysis of de-identified data obtained from the AI-PROGNOSIS dBM-DEV study database on 2 July 2026.

Missing data handling

Height and weight were missing for five dBM-DEV participants (three at-risk, one PwPD, one HC). Values were imputed using sex-and age-specific national reference means [36] for four participants and sexspecific dBM-DEV cohort means for the remaining one. Of the five participants, one at-risk and one HC were among the 61 participants included in the analysis.

Sleep and activity metrics extraction

For this study, we focused exclusively on accelerometer data, as it is the most ubiquitous sensor in wrist wearables. Raw tri-axial accelerometer signals were retrieved, and segments affected by sensor malfunctions, battery depletion, or other acquisition errors were excluded. Data acquired by both the Verily Study Watch, at a nominal sampling frequency of 100 Hz, and the Garmin Vivoactive 5, at a nominal sampling frequency of 25 Hz, were expressed in g and resampled to a uniform frequency of 25 Hz. Linear interpolation was used in the sleep and ambulatory metric extraction pipelines, whereas polyphase antialiasing resampling was used in the upper-limb movement metric extraction pipeline. Using accelerometer data sampled at 25 Hz facilitates reproducibility and consistent downstream analyses, as this is a typical sampling frequency for consumer-grade wrist wearables.

From the preprocessed signals, we estimated sleep and activity metrics. Activity metrics comprised upper-limb movement and ambulatory metrics. These metrics were selected to quantify aspects of motor function and sleep that may be affected by PD, even in its early stages. Sleep metrics were computed from the preprocessed acceleration signals using the open-source sleep detection and sleep staging pipeline 'asleep', developed by the Oxford Wearables group [37]. Its core human activity recognition model has been validated against video recordings and diary logs [38, 39], and its sleep staging model against polysomnography [37]. The pipeline identifies sleep windows and computes within-sleep metrics, including total sleep time, sleep stage duration, and sleep fragmentation. We considered only nighttime sleep periods and all sleep metrics were computed at a daily resolution. Ambulatory metrics, including number of steps and cadence, were derived using the open-source 'stepcount' pipeline, also developed by the Oxford Wearables group [40], which detects ambulatory activity and steps from wrist acceleration [40]. Its core step detection model has been validated against video recordings [40]. Metrics were computed at minute-level or hourly-level resolution.

Upper-limb movement metrics included hourly acceleration, computed as the Euclidean norm minus one (ENMO) of the triaxial acceleration signal [41], and kinematic features derived from a previously-validated pipeline for assessing movement slowness [42]. Briefly, this pipeline identifies fivesecond intervals of hand movement from triaxial acceleration data, excludes bouts classified as gait, and computes kinematic features from the acceleration vector magnitude within the remaining bouts, which are then statistically aggregated over 10-minute periods. For the gait detector, we trained a LightGBM classifier using time-and frequency-domain features extracted from the acceleration vector magnitude in the CAPTURE-24 dataset (n = 151 participants) [43]. The classifier achieved an AUROC of 0.78 (95% CI: 0.76-0.79), evaluated under LOSO cross-validation. We set the operating threshold to 0.20 to prioritize gait detection sensitivity, thereby minimizing the risk of undetected gait periods contaminating the kinematic features of interest. This flexibility in tuning the operating threshold, combined with the five-second granularity of hand-movement segmentation, necessitated a custom gait detector rather than the 'stepcount' algorithm used for extracting the ambulatory metrics.

All computed sleep and activity metrics are summarized in Supplementary Table 2. Finally, all sleep and activity metrics were temporally aggregated per participant by computing summary statistics. For each metric, we calculated the mean, median, standard deviation (SD), 25th percentile (Q25), and 75th percentile (Q75). Unless otherwise stated, temporal aggregation was performed by pooling the valid days and nights of each participant's eligible 14-day monitoring windows over the entire observation period and computing each statistic once on the pooled data.

Machine learning

We developed the SAM-PD model by training a classifier to differentiate between PwPD and HCs using the temporally-aggregated sleep and activity metrics from the Verily Study Watch cohort. Model development was carried out in two stages. First, we employed a nested leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) cross-validation to perform feature selection, optimize hyperparameters, calibrate predicted probabilities, and evaluate candidate classifiers. Second, we trained and calibrated the final SAM-PD model using the consensus feature set, best-performing classifier, and hyperparameter configuration derived across the LOSO folds for subsequent application to the Verily Study Watch at-risk group and the dBM-DEV cohort.

During the first stage, we applied a two-step feature selection procedure within the outer training set of each LOSO fold to retain metrics that were both temporally reliable and not highly correlated. In the first step, we assessed the test-retest reliability of each candidate metric aggregated over 14 days, using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) (Test-retest reliability analysis, Methods). Metrics with an ICC(2,1) 95% CI lower bound below 0.75 were excluded to minimize the influence of noisy measurements on downstream model performance. Second, to address multicollinearity, we aggregated the remaining sleep and activity metrics over the entire observation period, identified clusters of highly correlated metrics (Spearman |ρ| ≥ 0.90), and retained the most temporally reliable metric from each cluster.

Following feature selection, the participants of the outer training set were group-stratified and split into an 80% training subset for model fitting and hyperparameter optimization and a 20% calibration subset for probability calibration. Fitting and calibration were performed using the participants' selected sleep and activity metrics, aggregated over the entire observation period. Three classification algorithms were evaluated for the SAM-PD model: ridge logistic regression, balanced random forest, and gradient-boosted trees (LightGBM). Hyperparameters were optimized within the training subset using an exhaustive grid search over predefined configurations (Supplementary Table 13). Each configuration was evaluated using stratified group-based 5-fold cross-validation, repeated five times, with AUROC as the optimization metric. Predicted probabilities were calibrated on the calibration subset using Platt scaling, which was selected because its low complexity is better suited to the small calibration-set size. Class imbalance was handled intrinsically by the balanced random forest, whereas class weighting was applied to the other classifiers. The classifiers were evaluated on the held-out participants to obtain subject-level performance estimates for the PwPD-versus-HC classification task.

In the second stage, we trained and calibrated the classifier that achieved the highest AUROC and lower ECE after calibration in the first stage, using data from all PwPD and HC participants and following the same training-calibration split strategy. We used the consensus feature set and hyperparameter configuration derived across the LOSO folds. The consensus feature set comprised metrics selected in more than half of the LOSO folds. For numerical and categorical hyperparameters, the consensus value was the median and mode across folds, respectively. The resulting SAM-PD model was then evaluated, without any retraining or recalibration, on the Verily Study Watch at-risk group and the dBM-DEV study cohort. Unless otherwise stated, SAM-PD scores for participants in these two groups correspond to the probabilities generated by the final trained and calibrated SAM-PD model from their temporally-aggregated sleep and activity metrics. In contrast, SAM-PD scores for PwPD and HCs in the Verily Study Watch cohort correspond to the test-subject probabilities obtained during the LOSO cross-validation of the bestperforming calibrated classifier.

Evaluation against pathological hallmarks

We assessed the ability of the SAM-PD, MDS prodromal PD risk, and Ensemble PD scores to detect dopaminergic deficit and α-synuclein pathology. Specifically, we first evaluated the correlation of each score with continuous biomarker measurements derived from DAT scan imaging and CSF α-synuclein SAA. We then assessed each score's ability to distinguish individuals with normal and abnormal test results. Analyses were performed in the Verily Study Watch cohort.

Dopaminergic deficit.

For the participants in the Verily Study Watch cohort, dopaminergic deficit was assessed using DAT scan imaging before the wearable data collection period. To account for disease progression between imaging and wearable monitoring, we excluded DAT scans acquired more than three years before the wearable data collection in the PwPD and at-risk cohorts [44]. For HCs, we retained all DAT scans regardless of their acquisition date, as no meaningful progression of dopaminergic deficiency is expected in this cohort. For each participant, we quantified dopaminergic deficit using the minimum putaminal Specific Binding Ratio (SBR) from the DAT scan closest to wearable data collection. The putaminal SBR of the most affected hemisphere is the most discriminative single DAT-derived measure, as nigrostriatal degeneration preferentially begins in the posterior putamen during the premotor stage [45]. For the classification analysis, participants were categorized as having normal dopaminergic function or dopaminergic deficit based on their DAT scan result. DAT scan results were classified as normal or abnormal according to the PPMI visual interpretation and consensus process. Each scan was independently rated by two reviewers, with a third reviewer adjudicating discordant ratings; the consensus classification was determined by agreement between two reviewers. When no visual interpretation was available, scans were classified as abnormal if the minimum putaminal SBR was below 1.037, the PPMI-derived threshold for distinguishing PwPD from HCs, and as normal otherwise [46]. Scans were classified by visual interpretation in all 26 HCs, 28 of 86 PwPD, and 56 of 121 at-risk participants, whereas the remaining scans were classified by the SBR threshold. Because DAT scan imaging preceded wearable data collection, the dopaminergic-deficit analysis is cross-sectional with respect to the SAM-PD and MDS scores and should be interpreted as assessing the association between the score and pre-existing dopaminergic status rather than the prediction of a future outcome.

α-Synuclein pathology. α-synuclein SAA data were available for participants who underwent lumbar puncture for cerebrospinal fluid collection [47]. CSF was collected before wearable data collection, and SAA assays were performed on the stored samples afterward. The assay was performed using two protocols, with fluorescence read at 24 or 150 hours. Because maximum fluorescence is protocoldependent, correlation analyses were restricted to the 150-hour readout, which was the only protocol applied across the entire at-risk cohort. For these analyses, we used the mean maximum fluorescence (Fmax) of three replicates. For the classification analysis, participants were categorized as having αsynuclein pathology based on a positive SAA result or not having α-synuclein pathology based on a negative SAA result, regardless of assay protocol. We used the SAA result from the CSF sample collected closest to, and before, the start of wearable data collection. If the sample was assayed more than once, the earliest assay result was used.

Presence of either or both pathological hallmarks. We further evaluated the ability of SAM-PD and MDS scores to identify individuals exhibiting at least one or both pathological hallmarks. For the atleast-one-hallmark analysis, participants with an abnormal result on either test were considered positive, even if the result of the other test was unavailable. In the both-hallmark analysis, participants were considered positive if they had an abnormal DAT scan and a positive SAA result. Participants with neither pathological feature, defined as having both DAT scan and SAA results available and negative, served as the comparison group in both analyses.

MDS prodromal PD risk score estimation and combination strategies

We first compared the SAM-PD score with the MDS prodromal PD risk score and then investigated their complementary ability to detect dopaminergic deficit and α-synuclein pathology in the Verily Study Watch cohort. The MDS prodromal PD risk score is the output of a Bayesian classifier that estimates the probability of prodromal PD based on established risk markers and prodromal features [7]. It first assigns an age-based pretest probability, which is then updated using Bayesian likelihood ratios for each risk marker or prodromal feature present. Likelihood ratios have been derived from epidemiological studies [8].

We computed two versions of the MDS prodromal PD risk score: a version utilizing all available risk and prodromal marker information and a version relying only on markers that do not require specialized ancillary testing and can be self-reported with relative confidence, referred to as the mini-MDS risk score. The former operationalized the risk and prodromal markers of the MDS research criteria for prodromal PD that were available in the PPMI dataset, comprising 23 variables. These included, but were not limited to, the polygenic risk score (PRS) or presence of genetic mutations, such as those in the GBA or LRRK2 genes, olfactory loss confirmed by the University of Pennsylvania Smell Identification Test (UPSIT), subthreshold parkinsonism assessed using MDS-UPDRS Part III, and PSG-confirmed RBD. The omitted markers were the DAT scan result, which served as one of the ground truths of this study; substantia nigra hyperechogenicity, occupational solvent exposure and plasma urate, as these data were not uniformly collected in PPMI; and physical inactivity, for which no relevant assessment or measure was available. In addition to age, the mini-MDS risk score was calculated using the following variables, adopting an approach similar to De Klerk et al. [9]: biological sex, smoking status (never, current, former), caffeine consumption, having a first-degree relative with PD, RBD screening questionnaire result, and the presence of type II diabetes mellitus, constipation, daytime somnolence, urinary dysfunction, depression, and cognitive impairment. A complete list of all markers used in both versions of the MDS risk score is provided in Supplementary Table 14. All markers were extracted from assessments conducted before or at the beginning of the Verily Study Watch data collection. Former smoking status, caffeine consumption, and occupational exposure to pesticides reflect historical exposure.

To investigate their complementary ability to detect pathological hallmarks, we evaluated three approaches for combining the SAM-PD and MDS scores: maximum score-based screening, sequential screening, and score fusion using a meta-learner. In the maximum score approach, participants were classified as positive (having one, either, or both pathologies) if either score exceeded its respective decision threshold. In the sequential screening approach, participants were classified as positive only if both scores exceeded their respective decision thresholds. For the meta-learner approach, we trained a logistic regression model to distinguish PwPD from HCs using the SAM-PD and MDS scores as predictors. Logistic regression was selected as the meta-learner because of its simplicity and interpretable weighting of the input predictions. The meta-learner was trained on the out-of-fold SAM-PD probabilities and MDS scores of the Verily Study Watch PwPD and HC participants, and its performance and operating thresholds were estimated with nested LOSO cross-validation. In each outer fold, the SAM-PD probabilities used to train the meta-learner were regenerated with the held-out participant excluded from SAM-PD training, ensuring that the participant's data were not used to train either model before the final prediction was generated. Similar to the SAM-PD model, hyperparameters for the logistic regression model were optimized using an exhaustive grid search over predefined configurations within this scheme. Each configuration was evaluated using stratified group 5-fold cross-validation, repeated three times, with AUROC as the optimization metric. The final logistic regression model was then trained on the complete PwPD and HC dataset using the hyperparameter configuration derived across the LOSO folds. This final logistic regression metalearner, together with the MDS risk score and the SAM-PD model trained and calibrated on the entire PwPD and HC dataset, formed the final system. This system was applied without further tuning to the Verily Study Watch at-risk group to generate Ensemble PD scores. All combination strategies were evaluated using both the MDS and mini-MDS risk scores; for the meta-learner approach, the latter yielded the mini-Ensemble PD score.

Polygenic risk score calculation

PRSs were calculated for each participant in the Verily Study Watch cohort. The PRS calculation was based on the combined set of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with PD identified in the metaanalyses by Nalls et al. [48] and Leonard et al. [49]. Ambiguous, multiallelic, and variants located in the LRRK2 gene were systematically excluded from the analysis. For inclusion in the MDS prodromal PD risk score, participants were subsequently classified into high-or low-PRS categories using quartile thresholds derived from the PRS distribution in the unified Accelerating Medicines Partnership Parkinson's disease (AMP-PD) cohorts [51]. Participants with PRS values within the interquartile range were considered unclassified, and the PRS-associated marker was not included in the calculation of the MDS score.

Classification performance evaluation

Classification performance was evaluated using AUROC, AUPRC, sensitivity, specificity, precision, balanced accuracy, and F1 score. Because the classification tasks investigated in this study exhibited varying degrees of class imbalance, we used both AUROC and AUPRC as primary evaluation metrics. For the SAM-PD and Ensemble PD scores, operating point-dependent metrics were estimated at the Youdenoptimal threshold, derived from the corresponding PwPD-versus-HC classification tasks in the Verily Study Watch cohort. During LOSO cross-validation, fold-specific thresholds were used, the median of these thresholds was applied to the at-risk group and the dBM-DEV cohort. The Verily Study Watch at-risk group and dBM-DEV cohort were not used for threshold selection. Performance was also evaluated at two alternative operating points representing plausible screening configurations: the threshold that maximized the F1 score and the threshold that maximized sensitivity while maintaining specificity ≥ 0.50. For all versions of the MDS risk score, the recommended threshold for prodromal PD (0.80) was used [8].

Fairness analysis

To assess potential demographic disparities in SAM-PD model performance, we stratified the PwPD and HC predictions of the Verily Study Watch cohort by age, sex, BMI, ethnicity, and race. Within each subgroup, classification performance was evaluated using AUROC and AUPRC, calculated only for subgroups with at least five participants in each outcome class. Race and ethnicity subgroups were defined according to PPMI. For age subgrouping, we followed the ranges defining young-onset (< 50 years), middle-onset (50-69 years), and late-onset (≥70 years) PD [52]. For BMI subgrouping, we followed the World Health Organization categories [53]: underweight (BMI < 18.5), normal weight (25.0 > BMI ≥ 18.5), overweight (30.0 > BMI ≥ 25.0), and obesity (BMI ≥ 30.0). We also assessed disparities using three metrics: the Demographic Parity Difference (DPD), Demographic Parity Ratio (DPR), and Equalized Odds Difference (EOD).

Test-retest reliability analysis

We performed a test-retest reliability analysis to determine the minimum temporal aggregation interval to obtain reliable SAM-PD scores and constituent sleep and activity metrics. We followed an approach similar to [54]. The analysis covered each participant's first qualifying fortnight and the immediately subsequent fortnight, assuming each participant's health status remained fairly stable throughout this interval. Sleep and activity metrics were aggregated over non-overlapping windows of 3, 7, 10, and 14 days and used to derive the corresponding SAM-PD scores. A calendar day was considered valid if the participant wore the wearable device for at least 10 hours during the 06:00-23:00 waking period and a main sleep block of at least four hours was detected for the corresponding night. For each aggregation interval, the aggregated metrics and resulting SAM-PD scores from the first and second windows were treated as test and retest measurements, respectively. For each aggregation interval, we calculated the two-way random effects, absolute agreement, single-measurement intraclass correlation coefficient [ICC(2,1)] with 95% confidence intervals [20] for all metrics and the SAM-PD score. Only participants with at least one valid test-retest pair at a given aggregation interval were included in the corresponding ICC analysis.

Model interpretability analysis

To characterize how each input contributed to the SAM-PD score, we computed SHAP values [55] on heldout data within the LOSO cross-validation. In each LOSO fold, SHAP values for the held-out participant were computed with the same fitted model that generated their prediction. SHAP values were estimated with the permutation explainer applied to the calibrated SAM-PD probability. Individual feature importance was quantified as the mean absolute SHAP value across participants.

Statistical analyses

Demographic characteristics were compared between groups within each cohort and between cohorts within each group using two-sided Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous variables (age and BMI) and Pearson's chi-squared tests without continuity correction for sex. No multiple-testing correction was applied to these baseline comparisons because they were intended as descriptive comparisons. Pairwise comparisons of the age-and sex-accounting SAM-PD, MDS, and Ensemble PD scores were performed using two-sided Mann-Whitney U tests, whereas comparisons of individual sleep and activity metrics were performed using rank-based analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) adjusted for age and sex [56]. Statistical significance was assessed at a nominal threshold of α = 0.05. Bonferroni correction was applied for multiple comparisons, where indicated. Unless otherwise stated, continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as n (%). Pearson's correlation coefficients were calculated to assess linear relationships between continuous variables. Spearman rank correlation was used for feature selection to account for potential non-linear associations. For ICC analyses, CIs were derived using the Fdistribution. To quantify uncertainty in classification performance, 95% CIs for sensitivity, specificity and precision were estimated using the Clopper-Pearson method [57], whereas 95% CIs for all other performance metrics were calculated using 1,000 bootstrap iterations with replacement. Analyses were conducted in Python 3.10.9. Raw accelerometry was processed with actipy 3.8.0, sleep and step metrics were derived with asleep 0.4.16 and stepcount 3.9.1 (self-supervised model, weights ssl-20230208), respectively, and temporal aggregation statistics were computed with tsfresh 0.20.2. Machine learning models were implemented using scikit-learn 1.1.1, imbalanced-learn 0.9.1, and LightGBM 3.3.0, and SHAP analysis was performed using shap 0.42.1. A fixed random seed of 42 was used for all stochastic procedures. Statistical testing was performed using scipy 1.10.1, pingouin 0.5.4, and statsmodels 0.14.2. Visualizations were generated using seaborn 0.12.2 and matplotlib 3.7.5. PRS calculations were performed in R 4.3.2. All analyses, except PRS calculations, were performed on a workstation equipped with a 24core CPU (3.8 GHz), 128 GB of RAM, and 48 GB of VRAM.
