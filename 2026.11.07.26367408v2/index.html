Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Atherosclerotic plaque vulnerability drives ischemic cardiovascular events, yet the cellular and molecular signatures distinguishing symptomatic from asymptomatic human plaques remain poorly defined. Here, we compared 53 symptomatic and asymptomatic human carotid plaques using single-nucleus (snRNA-seq; 17 samples, 19,100 nuclei; AtherOMICS biobank) and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq; 36 samples, 68,983 cells; four public datasets), analysed independently and combined by meta-analysis. Across sequencing approaches, symptomatic plaques showed cell-state shifts toward later or terminal states in osteochondrogenic smooth muscle cells (SMCs) and CD4⁺ T cells, as well as transcriptional changes toward inflammatory and epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in endothelial cells (ECs), T cells, and fibroblasts. Nuclear profiling uncovered a depletion of fibroblasts in symptomatic plaques, marked by downregulation of RGS5. Integrating these transcriptomic signatures with genome-wide association data for coronary, cerebrovascular, and peripheral atherosclerotic disease, we found that genetic risk was enriched primarily in vascular wall cells (SMCs, fibroblasts, ECs), rather than immune cell types. The analyses further nominated higher TWIST1 expression in ECs (colocalized with risk across four atherosclerotic phenotypes), lower GUCY1A1 expression in fibroblasts and macrophages, and higher MRAS expression in T cells as candidate driver mechanisms linking genetic risk to symptomatic plaque transition. These findings provide novel insights into cellular and molecular phenotypes associated with plaque vulnerability and identify potential driver mechanisms of symptomatic plaque transformation that could inform therapeutic development.

INTRODUCTION

Atherosclerosis is the principal pathology underlying ischemic cardiovascular diseases, which remain the leading causes of death and disability worldwide. 1, 2 Atherosclerotic plaques arise within the subendothelial space and exhibit marked heterogeneity in clinical behavior. 3 Plaque vulnerability is thought to be a major driver of this heterogeneity, 4 yet the mechanisms governing plaque fate remain incompletely understood and are not directly targeted by current therapies. Histological hallmarks of plaque vulnerability do not fully explain divergent clinical trajectories, motivating the search for cellular and microenvironmental determinants that may drive plaque destabilization. 5 Single-cell transcriptomic studies have provided novel insights into the cellular and molecular architecture of human atherosclerosis, revealing greater cellular and functional diversity than is captured by histology or bulk profiling. [6] [7] [8] For example, they have revealed that smooth muscle cells (SMCs) undergo phenotypic transition toward fibromyocyte and osteochondrogenic states. 9, 10 Macrophages resolve into inflammatory, lipid-associated foamy, heme-handling, and remodeling populations with distinct functional programs, 11 whereas endothelial cells (ECs) stratify into quiescent, proangiogenic, and endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndoMT)-associated states linked to vascular remodeling and neovascularization. 6, 7 Despite these advances, existing studies have largely been limited by sample size and have not systematically associated cellular and molecular features with clinical trajectories. Consequently, the cell states and microenvironmental programs associated with plaque progression and destabilization remain incompletely defined.

Here, we compared symptomatic and asymptomatic human carotid plaques from 53 samples using single-nucleus (snRNA-seq; n = 17; AtherOMICS biobank 12 ) and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq; n = 36; four public datasets), analysing each modality separately and meta-analysing comparable estimates. We systematically compared cell-type composition, transcriptional programs, pathway activity, and lineage trajectories across modalities, clinical phenotypes, and established histological features of plaque vulnerability. By integrating our results with data from genome-wide association studies (GWAS), we further detected cellspecific differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in symptomatic plaques that might drive genetic risk for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.

In contrast, mast cells (0.619) and plasma cells (0.763) exhibited lower crossmodal concordance, with within-to-cross AUROC reductions of 0.30 and 0.19, respectively, suggesting greater modality sensitivity among these less abundant immune populations.

Symptomatic plaques are characterized by fibroblast depletion and trajectory shifts towards CD4⁺ T-cell, osteochondrogenic SMC and EndoMT states

We next compared cellular compositions between symptomatic and asymptomatic plaques across the scRNA-seq and snRNA-seq datasets (Fig. 4a). Fibroblasts were less abundant in symptomatic plaques in snRNA-seq (OR = 0.73, 95% CI = 0.56-0.94), and in the metaanalysis with scRNA-seq data (OR = 0.75, 95% CI = 0.59-0.96, P = 0.020, heterogeneity index (I²) = 0%). This association did not parallel the baseline modality-recovery pattern, as fibroblasts were not preferentially recovered by snRNA-seq, and the cross-modality estimate showed no heterogeneity. No other broad or fine cell populations showed significant differences in abundance between symptomatic and asymptomatic plaques.

To determine whether symptomatic status was associated with altered cellular state progression, we compared lineage-specific pseudotime distributions using condiments (Supplementary Fig. 3, Supplementary Table 3), followed by sample-level analyses based on weighted median pseudotime and terminal-state enrichment (Fig. s 4b, c). CD4⁺ T cells showed the most consistent evidence of altered state progression in symptomatic plaques.

Further, CD4⁺ T cells showed significantly later median pseudotime in snRNA-seq (difference = 0.10, 95% CI = 0.03-0.15) and in the cross-modality meta-analysis (difference = 0.07, 95% CI = 0.02-0.13). Terminal-state occupancy was greater in snRNA-seq (OR = 3.62, 95% CI = 1.68-7.84) and in the meta-analysis (OR = 3.12, 95% CI = 1.65-5.88). Notably, this association was strongest in snRNA-seq despite substantially greater T-cell recovery and finer terminal-state resolution in scRNA-seq, arguing against preferential T-cell recovery as an explanation for the disease association. Osteochondrogenic SMCs showed significantly later weighted median pseudotime in scRNA-seq (difference = 0.13, 95% CI = 0.01-0.21) and in the cross-modality meta-analysis (difference = 0.03, 95% CI = 0.00-0.06), with a concordant shift in snRNA-seq. Terminal-state occupancy was likewise greater in scRNA-seq (OR = 3.47, 95% CI = 1.01-11.98), with a concordant direction of effect in the meta-analysis (OR = 2.53, 95% CI = 0.89-7.21).

EndoMT ECs likewise showed greater terminal-state occupancy in symptomatic plaques in scRNA-seq (OR = 4.82, 95% CI = 1.53-15.13) and in the cross-modality meta-analysis (OR = 3.34, 95% CI = 1.25-8.96). EndoMT ECs were more abundant in snRNA-seq at baseline, whereas the symptom-associated terminal-state shift was strongest in scRNA-seq, again making differential state recovery alone an unlikely explanation. By contrast, CD8⁺ T-cell terminal-state enrichment was restricted to scRNA-seq (OR = 12.37, 95% CI = 1.45-105.5) and was not supported in snRNA-seq (OR = 0.71), resulting in substantial cross-modality heterogeneity (I² = 84%).

Symptomatic plaque-associated transcriptional changes are modality-dependent and point to inflammatory and EMT-related programmes

To characterize symptom-associated transcriptional remodeling, we performed cell-typespecific pseudobulk differential expression analyses on scRNA-seq and snRNA-seq datasets.

Consistent with the substantial gene-level differences between scRNA-seq and snRNA-seq defined above, symptom-associated differential expression was strongly modality-dependent, with 361 significant DEGs identified in scRNA-seq compared with only 17 in snRNA-seq (Fig. 5a). ECs and T cells accounted for the majority of scRNA-seq DEGs (196 and 102 genes, respectively), with ADAMTS2 (log₂FC = 7.00, FDR = 2.4 × 10⁻³) leading in ECs.

Fibroblasts represented the most transcriptionally altered cell population in snRNA-seq (8 genes) (Fig. 5a), led by a substantially lower expression of RGS5 among symptomatic plaques (log₂FC = -4.41, FDR = 1.8 × 10⁻⁴) (Fig. 5b), the largest-effect-size gene in the snRNA-seq dataset. RGS5 expression was also positively associated with fibrous cap thickness, a marker of plaque stability (ρ = 0.716, FDR = 0.016) (Fig. 5c,d). Fibroblasts also showed lower GUCY1A1 (log₂FC = -1.93, FDR = 0.017) and higher MMP2 (log₂FC = 2.27, FDR = 0.017) and TIMP3 (log₂FC = 2.68, FDR = 0.007) expression in symptomatic plaques, and SMCs showed higher COL11A1 (log₂FC = 2.29, FDR = 0.017) in snRNA-seq. Importantly, no genes reached FDR statistical significance in the same cell type across both modalities (Supplementary Table 5). Baseline differences in cell-type recovery between modalities did not account for these gene-level associations (Fig. 2c).

Genetic risk for atherosclerotic disease could be driven by genes differentially expressed in symptomatic plaques

Finally, we integrated the scRNA-seq and snRNA-seq datasets with genome-wide association study (GWAS) data for coronary artery disease (CAD), large-artery atherosclerotic stroke (LAS), peripheral artery disease (PAD), and carotid plaque presence (CaP) toTo identify plaque cell types and states implicated by genetic risk and to determine whether genetically prioritized genes were differentially expressed between symptomatic and asymptomatic plaques, we integrated the scRNA-seq and snRNA-seq datasets with GWAS data for coronary artery disease (CAD), large artery stroke (LAS), peripheral artery disease (PAD), and carotid plaque presence (CaP).

We first used stratified linkage disequilibrium score regression (S-LDSC) to test whether disease heritability was enriched in genomic annotations derived from specific plaque cell types and states. 19 S-LDSC revealed strong enrichment of genetic risk in vascular wall cell types. Fibromyocyte, contractile, and osteochondrogenic SMC states, together with fibroblasts, were significantly enriched for CAD and PAD heritability in both scRNA-seq and snRNA-seq datasets (FDR < 0.05; Fig. S4a), with CaP showing a similar pattern in snRNAseq. Furthermore, there was enrichment of quiescent ECs for CAD in both modalities, whereas proangiogenic and EndoMT EC states were enriched in scRNA-seq for CAD, PAD, and LAS. Alternative approaches prioritizing risk genes from GWAS signals based on either colocalization with expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs) in arterial tissue or the inclusive locus-to-gene (L2G) model by Open Targets confirmed similar enrichment in vascular wall cells, mainly SMC states (Supplementary Fig. 4b, Supplementary Tables 8 9 10).

Next, we investigated whether individual genes implicated in the genetic risk of atherosclerotic disease were differentially expressed between symptomatic and asymptomatic plaques (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Fig. 4c; Supplementary Table 11). Focusing on genes supported by colocalization between arterial tissue expression and atherosclerotic disease risk, we identified 12 genes that were differentially expressed in specific cell types between symptomatic and asymptomatic individuals. Among them, TWIST1 (log₂FC = 3.04), TMEM59L (log₂FC = 4.25), BMP1 (log₂FC = 1.10), and PCDHGB5 (log₂FC = 1.19) were upregulated in ECs of symptomatic plaques, whereas ATP2B1-AS1 was downregulated (log₂FC = -1.05) in ECs of symptomatic plaques. TMEM59L, MRAS and HLA-DQB2 were additionally upregulated in T cells, while GUCY1A1 was downregulated in MPs in scRNAseq and in fibroblasts in snRNA-seq. Notably, TWIST1 showed strong colocalization of aortic expression with all four atherosclerotic phenotypes (CAD posterior probability hypothesis 4 [PP.H4] = 0.994, LAS = 0.994, PAD = 0.985, CaP = 0.873; Fig. 6b), whereas, MRAS showed colocalization of arterial expression with CAD risk across all three tested tissues (aorta PP.H4 = 0.996, coronary PP.H4 = 0.997, tibial PP.H4 = 0.996), but not with LAS, PAD, or CaP (Fig. 6c). Using an alternative prioritization strategy based on L2G scores, we observed partially overlapping associations, including BMP1 and GUCY1A1, while identifying additional DEGs mainly in ECs, including CELSR2 (log₂FC = 6.60), NGF (log₂FC = 5.54), EDNRA (log₂FC = 4.81), ITIH4 (log₂FC = 4.04), SFRP2 (log₂FC = 3.59), and FST (log₂FC = 3.47) (Fig. S4c).

To further evaluate the genetic support and directionality of these associations, we performed cis-Mendelian randomization (MR) analyses (Supplementary Table 12). These analyses showed concordant effects for genetically proxied expression of several genes. Higher genetically proxied TWIST1 expression in aorta was associated with higher LAS risk (β = 0.612, q = 1.4 × 10⁻⁹), CAD risk (β = 0.303, q = 2.5 × 10⁻¹¹), and PAD risk (β = 0.262, q = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵), while higher genetically proxied MRAS expression in aorta, tibial artery, and coronary artery was associated with increased CAD risk (β = 0.061, q = 1.5 × 10⁻²¹ in aorta; β = 0.131, q = 9.5 × 10⁻⁹ in tibial artery; and β = 0.156, q = 2.7 × 10⁻¹⁰ in coronary artery) (Fig. 6d).

Higher genetically proxied HLA-DQB2 expression in tibial artery was associated with increased PAD risk (β = 0.047, q = 1.3 × 10⁻⁷), whereas lower genetically proxied GUCY1A1 expression in tibial artery was associated with increased CAD risk (β = -0.419, q = 6.6 × 10⁻¹³). DISCUSSION In this study, we compared cellular and molecular features between symptomatic and asymptomatic human atherosclerotic plaques using snRNA-seq and scRNA-seq data across 53 patients. We found that symptomatic plaques exhibit a depletion of fibroblasts characterized by reduced RGS5 expression, cellular shifts toward osteochondrogenic SMC, CD4⁺ T-cell, and EndoMT states, and enrichment of inflammatory and EMT-related transcriptional programs in ECs, T cells, and fibroblasts. Integration of cell-specific transcriptional signatures of symptomatic plaques with genomic risk data for atherosclerotic disease nominated local TWIST1, GUCY1A1, and MRAS expression as candidate drivers of symptomatic plaque transformation.

Fibroblasts showed both compositional and transcriptional remodeling in symptomatic plaques. SnRNA-seq profiling revealed significant fibroblast depletion, an association that remained significant in the cross-modality meta-analysis. Previous single-cell and experimental studies have identified distinct plaque fibroblast states and shown that fibroblastlike cells can accumulate within the fibrous cap and influence cap thickness. [20] [21] [22] In particular, IL-1β inhibition in models of clonal hematopoiesis increased fibroblast-like cell accumulation and fibrous cap thickness, whereas depletion of these cells reduced cap thickness. 22 The remaining fibroblasts in symptomatic plaques showed pronounced transcriptional remodeling, including lower RGS5 and GUCY1A1 and higher MMP2 and TIMP3 expression. These changes were preferentially detected in snRNA-seq. Notably, experimental studies have also shown that RGS5 loss perturbs the vascular niche through complementary mechanisms: reduced RGS5 promotes endothelial NF-κB signalling and dysfunction in human endothelial cells, 23 whereas pericyte-specific Rgs5 loss in mice induces TGFβ2-dependent paracrine fibroblast activation. 24 A broader role in plaque remodeling is supported by apolipoprotein Edeficient mice, in which Rgs5 deficiency increased lesion and necrotic-core burden while reducing SMC and collagen content. 25 In our cohort, higher fibroblast RGS5 expression was associated with greater fibrous cap thickness. Together, these findings place the observed RGS5 downregulation in the context of experimental studies linking RGS5 deficiency to NF-κB-mediated inflammatory signalling, fibroblast activation and reduced plaque stability.

Fibroblasts in symptomatic plaques were further characterized by reduced GUCY1A1 expression, which converged with genomic data, pointing to arterial downregulation of GUCY1A1 expression as a driver of CAD risk. GUCY1A1 encodes the alpha-1 subunit of soluble guanylate cyclase (sGC), which is the primary receptor driving the vasoprotective actions associated with nitric oxide signaling. 26 Experimental studies indicate that the consequences of altered sGC signalling in atherosclerosis are strongly cell-context dependent.

Functional characterization of the GUCY1A3/GUCY1A1 CAD locus implicated altered sGC signalling in vascular SMC migration, 27 whereas platelet-specific loss of sGC increased plaque formation and vascular inflammation and pharmacological sGC stimulation reduced leukocyte recruitment and atherosclerosis. 28 These divergent effects argue against a uniform vascular consequence of sGC perturbation. The concomitant increase in MMP2 and TIMP3 further suggests active extracellular-matrix turnover; experimental studies showing reduced lesion development with MMP2 deficiency and aggravated atherosclerosis with TIMP3 loss support functional relevance of this protease-inhibitor axis, although increased TIMP3 in symptomatic fibroblasts may represent a compensatory response. 29, 30 We also found a shift toward an osteochondrogenic SMC state in symptomatic plaques. In scRNA-seq, osteochondrogenic SMCs from symptomatic plaques were further along the pseudotime trajectory and showed greater representation of terminal states; snRNA-seq showed the same directional shift. Consistent with this finding, COL11A1, a cartilageassociated collagen, was increased in the broad SMC compartment. Lineage-tracing and single-cell studies have linked fibromyocyte states to fibrous cap formation and lower CAD risk, whereas osteochondrogenic states are consistently associated with cartilage-like matrix remodeling and calcification. 9, 10 Consistent with our findings, osteogenic and extracellularmatrix programs have been reported in advanced symptomatic human plaques, 31 and CADassociated regulatory activity has been localized to SMC transition states. 32 Another significant difference was the higher representation of terminal EndoMT-associated EC states in symptomatic plaques. ECs also showed enrichment of inflammatory and EMTassociated pathways, including TNF-NFκB, IL6-JAK-STAT3, complement activation, hypoxia, and glycolysis. EndoMT has been described as a feature of advanced and unstable atherosclerotic plaques, 33 but its effects appear to depend on the specific molecular program involved. 34 Recent experimental work has identified TWIST1 as a driver of endothelial phenotypic modulation and EndoMT in atherosclerosis, with endothelial Twist1 deletion reducing lesion size and promoting a more stable plaque phenotype. 35 Notably, TWIST1 was also upregulated in ECs from symptomatic plaques in our study. This finding was further supported by genetic analyses, which linked variants associated with increased TWIST1 expression in human arterial tissue to atherosclerotic phenotypes across vascular beds, including CAD, LAS, PAD, and CaP. Together, these findings identify endothelial TWIST1 as a potential mediator of EMT-associated changes in symptomatic plaques.

In line with the phenotypic differences between symptomatic and asymptomatic plaques, which were most prominent in vascular wall cells, genetic analyses also highlighted these cell types. S-LDSC, L2G, and arterial eQTL-based analyses, genetic risk for CAD, LAS, PAD, and CaP was particularly enriched in contractile, fibromyocyte, and osteochondrogenic SMC states and in fibroblasts, with additional endothelial associations depending on the phenotype and modality. These findings were consistent across complementary approaches to cell-state enrichment and gene prioritization and were observed across both scRNA-seq and snRNA-seq datasets. They are also consistent with human single-nucleus chromatin studies that have localized CAD-associated regulatory activity to phenotypically modulated SMC states, including fibromyocytes. 32 The most reproducible immune-state difference involved CD4⁺ T cells. In snRNA-seq, CD4⁺ cells occupied later pseudotime positions and showed greater terminal-state commitment than in asymptomatic plaques. This is consistent with previous human carotid studies reporting greater CD4⁺ T-cell activation and differentiation in symptomatic lesions and plaque-specific clonal expansion with evidence of recent T-cell receptor engagement among effector CD4⁺ cells, 8, 15 supporting local antigen-experienced differentiation. MRAS, which was upregulated in symptomatic T cells here and strongly supported by CAD colocalization and cis-MR, provides a potential genetic link to this phenotype. MRAS forms a SHOC2-PP1C complex that promotes RAF-MAPK signaling, 36 although CAD-associated regulatory effects at this locus have previously been localized predominantly to SMC transition states, including fibromyocytes. 32 Together, these findings extend earlier descriptions of plaque T-cell activation by identifying a sample-level CD4⁺ differentiation shift associated with symptomatic disease and nominate MRAS as a potential link between genetic risk and this immune-state transition.

Previous studies of human atherosclerosis have used single-cell technologies to define plaquecell heterogeneity and disease-associated states, 37 but direct comparisons of how whole-cell and nuclear RNA profiling capture these states have been limited. 38, 39 In this study, mean normalized expression showed only modest concordance between scRNA-seq and snRNAseq. These differences likely reflect differences in the cellular compartments captured by the two assays, as well as differences in cell recovery and transcriptional responses to tissue dissociation. Comparisons in other tissues have shown similar differences between scRNA-seq and snRNA-seq in pre-mRNA representation, cell-type recovery, and responses to tissue processing. 38, 39 Modality-specific findings remain informative when the corresponding modality has a clear measurement advantage for the relevant biological compartment.

Concordance between the two modalities was greater for broad cell identities than for individual genes or pathways. At the disease-association level, no cell-specific DEGs reached significance in both modalities, and EC pathway effects differed in direction. Cross-modality meta-analysis supported fibroblast depletion and shifts toward terminal CD4⁺ T-cell and later osteochondrogenic SMC states in symptomatic plaques, although individually each of these effects reached significance in only one modality. The cross-modality analyses therefore serve as an interpretive framework for the disease associations rather than a separate benchmarking endpoint, and modality-specific associations should be interpreted cautiously.

Several limitations should be considered. First, although samples from 53 patients were included in the overall analyses, many comparisons remained underpowered, limiting our ability to detect anything but relatively large cellular and molecular differences. This was particularly relevant for cell-abundance comparisons, where fibroblast depletion was the only significant difference identified, and for correlations with histology, which were restricted to in-house snRNA-seq data from only 15-16 patients per endpoint. Larger datasets, ideally integrated across centers, will be important to enable better-powered comparisons. Second, we observed substantial differences in both cellular composition and transcriptional profiles between scRNA-seq and snRNA-seq, reflecting inherent differences in the strengths and limitations of the two technologies, including differential recovery of cytoplasmic and nuclear transcripts, dissociation-induced stress signatures in scRNA-seq, and cell-type recovery biases. These differences introduce technical variability that may have obscured biological differences between symptomatic and asymptomatic plaques. Accordingly, differences in the number of differentially expressed genes between modalities should not be interpreted as differences in sensitivity. Third, the scRNA-seq cohort comprised four independently generated public datasets, whereas the snRNA-seq cohort was generated independently within AtherOMICS, and the two modalities were obtained from different samples. Thus, modality effects cannot be fully separated from cohort-and study-specific effects, limiting direct comparisons between the two datasets. Fourth, the symptomatic-asymptomatic comparisons were necessarily cross-sectional and therefore cannot establish whether the identified cellular states precede clinical events, contribute to plaque destabilization, arise as consequences of advanced disease, or represent compensatory responses. Similarly, pseudotime analyses infer transcriptional ordering from static measurements and cannot establish temporal progression or lineage fate in vivo. 40 In conclusion, this work provides a comprehensive atlas of cellular and molecular differences between symptomatic and asymptomatic human atherosclerosis. Our findings highlight fibroblast depletion, cellular shifts toward osteochondrogenic SMC, EndoMT, and CD4⁺ Tcell states, and molecular changes across cell types pointing to inflammatory and EMTassociated transcriptional programmes as signatures of symptomatic plaques. Integration with genetic risk data nominated locally DEGs, including TWIST1, GUCY1A1, and MRAS, as candidate causal drivers of symptomatic plaque transformation. These findings identify specific cellular programs that distinguish symptomatic from asymptomatic human carotid plaques and provide cell-specific prioritized targets for mechanistic studies of plaque destabilization.

METHODS

Study cohort for single-nucleus RNA-seq data

AtherOMICS is a prospective biobank enrolling patients undergoing carotid or femoral endarterectomy at the Vascular Surgery and Neurology Departments, LMU University Hospital Munich. Eligible participants were consecutively identified by screening of admission and surgery records; inclusion required age ≥18 years, endarterectomy scheduled within 7 days, and provision of written informed consent. Exclusion criteria were inability to consent, restenosis after prior endarterectomy or stenting, and stenosis attributable to prior local radiation. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität Munich (protocol numbers 121-09, 22-0135, 23-0772), conformed to the 2008 Declaration of Helsinki, and all participants provided written informed consent.

Carotid stenosis was classified as symptomatic or asymptomatic based on a qualifying ipsilateral cerebrovascular event including ischemic stroke, transient ischemic attack, amaurosis fugax, or central retinal artery occlusion. Plaque tissue was collected intraoperatively and dissected into 5 mm segments centered on the region of greatest atherosclerotic burden, defined by plaque thickness; surrounding segments were flash-frozen and stored at -80 °C for omics profiling. Segment location (proximal versus distal) was retained as a covariate in downstream analyses.

Single-cell and single-nucleus RNA-seq data processing and integration

Publicly available scRNA-seq datasets of human carotid atherosclerotic plaques were identified by searches of PubMed and the Gene Expression Omnibus from database inception to 9 June 2025. Searches combined terms relating to human carotid atherosclerotic plaques and carotid endarterectomy, scRNA-seq, and symptomatic status. Candidate records were screened for the following exclusion criteria: datasets not comprising human carotid plaque tissue, datasets without individual-level symptomatic or asymptomatic clinical annotation, and datasets without reprocessable carotid-plaque expression data. Of 16 records identified, 11 represented unique studies or datasets and four met the inclusion criteria, comprising 36 samples (16 symptomatic and 20 asymptomatic; Supplementary Table 13). 8, 10, 20, 31 Mocci et al. provided Smart-seq2 gene-by-cell read-count matrices, whereas the remaining datasets provided UMI-based count matrices. All datasets were processed using a uniform Seurat-based workflow (v5.0.3). 41 Cells were retained if they contained >300 detected genes, >1,000 counts, and <20% mitochondrial reads. Each dataset was normalized using SCTransform with mitochondrial read fraction regressed out, followed by principal component analysis (PCA). Doublets were identified within each dataset using DoubletFinder, 42 with an expected doublet rate of 5%, and only singlets were retained. Highly variable genes (3,000 per dataset) were selected using the vst method, and integration features were selected across datasets using SelectIntegrationFeatures. Dataset integration and batch correction were performed using Harmony (v1.2.3) 43 on the PCA embedding, accounting for sample identity and sequencing technology. The Harmony-corrected embedding (dimensions 1-10) was used to construct a shared nearest-neighbor graph (k = 10), and clustering was performed using the Louvain algorithm at a resolution of 1.3. Sample collection, nuclei isolation, library preparation, sequencing, read alignment, and UMI quantification for human carotid plaque snRNA-seq have been described previously. 12 For the present study, raw UMI count matrices and corresponding gene-barcode annotation files were imported into R and processed using Seurat. Nuclei were retained if they had >150 detected genes, >150 total UMIs, and <20% of UMIs mapping to mitochondrial genes. After quality control, doublets were identified and removed on a per-sample basis using DoubletFinder (v2.0.4), assuming an expected doublet rate of approximately 4% per library. Gene expression was normalized and variance-stabilized using SCTransform, with the mitochondrial fraction regressed out to reduce technical variation. Highly variable features (1,500 genes) were selected across samples, integration anchors were identified using FindIntegrationAnchors, and a shared integrated assay was generated with IntegrateData. PCA was performed on the integrated assay, and the first 15 principal components were used to construct a shared nearest-neighbor graph (k = 40). Unsupervised clustering was performed using the Louvain algorithm with a resolution of 1.4.

Cell type annotation

Broad lineage identities were assigned using canonical marker genes obtained from published single-cell studies and atlases of human atherosclerotic and vascular tissues. 9, 11, [13] [14] [15] [16] [17] [18] Clusterlevel expression profiles from SCTransform-normalized data were evaluated using three complementary approaches: marker-based scoring, reference-based correlation using clustifyr (v1.18.0), and gene set enrichment analysis using GSVA/ssGSEA (v2.0.7). Final lineage labels were determined based on concordance across these approaches, together with manual inspection of established marker gene expression. Finer transcriptional subtypes were resolved using a hierarchical annotation strategy. Clusters assigned to each broad lineage were subset and re-analyzed independently to identify within-lineage states, which were subsequently propagated back to the full dataset.

Cross-modality cell-type discriminability analysis

To assess whether broad cell-type annotations were transcriptionally comparable across scRNA-seq and snRNA-seq, we quantified within-and cross-modality cell-type discriminability using MetaNeighbor. 44 Expression matrices were restricted to genes shared by at least 10 cells or nuclei in either modality. To limit differences in cell-type abundance, each broad cell type was downsampled to a maximum of 2,000 cells or nuclei per modality. Counts were library-size normalized and log-transformed, and highly variable genes were identified using MetaNeighbor::variableGenes with modality specified as the study variable. Cell-type discriminability was quantified using MetaNeighborUS, with modality as the study identifier and harmonized broad cell type as the cell-type label. Hierarchical clustering of AUROC profiles was performed using Euclidean distances and complete linkage.

Compositional analysis

Cell-type proportions were compared between scRNA-seq and snRNA-seq using propeller (speckle v1.6.0, logit transformation). 45 For each cell type, sample-level proportions were logit-transformed and modeled by modality, yielding empirical Bayes-moderated t-statistics and modality-specific mean proportions. Analyses were performed at both broad and fine annotation levels; P values were adjusted separately within each level using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method.

Associations between cell-type composition and symptomatic status were evaluated separately for each modality using beta-binomial regression with a logit link, with cell-type counts modeled relative to total cells recovered per sample (asymptomatic as reference). In the snRNA-seq dataset, generalized linear models included clinical group, plaque segment location, centered and scaled age, and sex as fixed effects, implemented in glmmTMB (v1.1.11). In the integrated scRNA-seq dataset, generalized linear mixed models included clinical group and sex as fixed effects and a random intercept for dataset of origin. Finer Slingshot curve weight, and terminal occupancy was defined as the fraction of assigned cells or nuclei with normalized pseudotime >0.7, computed at the sample-branch level for samples that contributed at least 10 cells or nuclei to that branch. Branches were tested when they contained at least three samples per clinical group. Differences between symptomatic and asymptomatic plaques were evaluated using beta-binomial models with the number of terminal and non-terminal cells or nuclei as the response variable; snRNA-seq models included clinical group and sex as fixed effects, and scRNA-seq models included clinical group and sex as fixed effects, with dataset of origin as a random intercept. Cross-modality effect sizes were obtained via a fixed-effects meta-analysis of logit-scale estimates from both modalities using metafor, restricted to branches tested in both modalities. P values were adjusted using the BH method within each modality.

Cross-modality gene expression concordance analysis

To compare gene expression between scRNA-seq and snRNA-seq at both the global and celltype-specific levels, pseudobulk count matrices were generated within each modality by aggregating raw counts across cells or nuclei within each sample (global) or within each sample and annotated broad cell type (cell-type-specific). Analyses were restricted to genes shared between modalities. Lowly expressed genes were filtered by retaining genes with mean counts per million (CPM) ≥ 0.5 and detectable expression (CPM > 0) in at least 10% of pseudobulk profiles; within each analysis (global or cell-type-specific), genes were then further filtered using edgeR::filterByExpr given the corresponding design matrix. 48 Library sizes were subsequently normalized using Trimmed Mean of M-values (TMM) normalization, and normalized expression values were obtained in voom space. Mean logCPM was calculated separately for scRNA-seq and snRNA-seq by averaging across pseudobulk profiles within each modality. Gene-wise mean expression levels in scRNA-seq and snRNA-seq were then compared using global and cell-type-specific scatter plots, and concordance between modalities was summarized using the squared Pearson correlation coefficient (R²).

Differential expression analysis

Cell-type-specific differential expression was assessed using a pseudobulk framework. Raw counts were aggregated per sample and cell type to generate sample-level expression profiles.

Genes with insufficient expression were excluded by retaining genes with mean CPM ≥ 0.5 and detectable expression (CPM > 0) in at least 10% of pseudobulk profiles, and additional expression filtering was performed within each cell type using edgeR::filterByExpr given the corresponding design matrix. Pseudobulk profiles with low library sizes were removed within each cell type before testing.

In the snRNA-seq dataset, cell-type-specific differential expression between symptomatic and asymptomatic plaques was assessed using edgeR negative binomial generalized linear model including clinical group as the primary predictor and plaque segment location, centered age, and sex as covariates. Analyses were performed only for cell types with at least three pseudobulk profiles per clinical group and with ≥50 genes remaining after expression filtering. Quasi-likelihood F-tests were used as the primary inference procedure; for cell types in which no genes reached FDR < 0.05, the analysis automatically reverted to likelihood-ratio testing. Multiple testing correction was performed using the BH method, with FDR < 0.05 considered significant.

In the integrated scRNA-seq dataset, differential expression was assessed using edgeR quasilikelihood F-tests only. For each cell type, design matrices were constructed adaptively to include clinical group and to include sex and/or dataset-of-origin covariates only when these varied within that cell type; models were fitted only when both clinical groups were represented by at least two pseudobulk profiles. P values were adjusted using the BH method, with FDR < 0.05 considered significant.

Pathway enrichment analysis

To quantify systematic modality-associated shifts in pathway activity between scRNA-seq and snRNA-seq, Hallmark gene sets (MSigDB collection H) were obtained using the msigdbr package (v25.1.1). 49 For each broad cell type, gene-level modality effects were estimated from joint voom models that included modality, clinical group, sex, and their interaction terms, and extracted as the log fold change for scRNA-seq relative to snRNA-seq. These gene-level effects were aggregated within each Hallmark pathway by computing the mean log-fold change across all retained genes after expression filtering and cross-modality gene intersection, restricting analyses to pathways with at least 20 genes represented.

To assess symptom-associated pathway enrichment within each modality, gene set enrichment analysis was performed separately for each broad cell type using Hallmark gene sets. For each cell type, genes from the symptomatic versus asymptomatic pseudobulk differential expression analysis were ranked by log fold change, and enrichment was tested using the fgsea (v1.32.4) 50 multilevel algorithm. Gene set sizes were restricted to 3-1000 genes.

Normalized enrichment scores and adjusted P values were reported, with statistical significance defined at FDR < 0.05 for both scRNA-seq and snRNA-seq.

Subcellular localization enrichment analysis

To determine whether modality-associated transcriptional differences reflected differential capture of nuclear versus cytoplasmic transcript pools, genes were classified using Gene Ontology cellular-component (GO:CC) annotations retrieved via org.Hs.eg.db. Nuclear identity was defined by annotation to spliceosome-, transcription factor complex-, or nucleolus/nuclear-body-related terms (eight GO:CC terms); cytosolic identity was defined by annotation to ribosomal and cytosolic ribonucleoprotein complex terms (five GO:CC terms).

Genes annotated to both categories were excluded, and analyses were restricted to genes with an unambiguous nuclear or cytosolic annotation.

Modality-associated differential expression was derived from the joint voom model described above (modality × clinical group + sex), using the modality coefficient (scRNA-seq relative to snRNA-seq) from each cell-type-specific fit. Genes with P values < 0.05 and positive log fold change were classified as scRNA-seq-upregulated; genes with P values < 0.05 and negative log-fold change were classified as snRNA-seq-upregulated. For each broad cell type, enrichment of scRNA-seq-upregulated genes among cytosolic annotations and of snRNA-sequpregulated genes among nuclear annotations was tested using two-sided Fisher's exact tests.

ORs and 95% CIs were calculated with a Haldane-Anscombe correction (addition of 0.5 to all cell counts) when any contingency table cell was zero. P values were adjusted across cell types and tests using the BH method.

Dissociation stress analysis

To evaluate dissociation stress-related transcriptional differences between scRNA-seq and snRNA-seq, pseudobulk expression profiles were generated for each modality using final broad cell-type annotations. Raw counts were aggregated per sample and cell type, restricted to genes shared between modalities, and filtered to retain genes meeting expression-based criteria and passing filterByExpr with the corresponding design matrix. Pseudobulk counts were normalized using TMM values, transformed using the voom procedure, and analyzed using linear models implemented in the limma framework (v3.62.2). 51 For each broad cell type, scRNA-seq and snRNA-seq pseudobulk profiles were jointly modeled using a design that included modality, clinical group (symptomatic versus asymptomatic), sex, and the modality-by-symptom interaction term, followed by empirical Bayes moderation.

A predefined dissociation/stress gene set comprising immediate early and stress-response genes was evaluated in two complementary ways. First, modality-associated shifts in the stress program were summarized descriptively within each cell type by extracting the modality coefficient (scRNA-seq relative to snRNA-seq) from the joint model and computing the mean log-fold change (scRNA-seq -snRNA-seq) across genes in the stress gene set.

Standard errors and 95% confidence intervals were derived from the dispersion of gene-level modality coefficients within the gene set. Second, to formally test whether stress-related differences between symptomatic and asymptomatic plaques were modality-dependent, the modality-by-symptom interaction term was assessed using limma's competitive gene-set test (camera) applied to the interaction contrast. 52 This test was performed within each broad cell type when sufficient stress-module genes were represented. P values from the interaction gene-set tests were adjusted across cell types using the BH procedure, with FDR-adjusted values reported.

Stratified LD score regression

Cell-type marker genes were identified separately for each broad and fine cell type in scRNAseq and snRNA-seq data. Expression specificity was calculated from cell-type-averaged, lognormalized expression as the proportion of each gene's summed expression attributable to that cell type, and the top 10% of genes ranked by this specificity score were retained as the cell type's marker set. Gene sets were converted to genomic annotations by extending gene coordinates by ±100 kb, and LD scores were computed for chromosomes 1-22 using LDSC (v1.0.1) with European reference data from the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3. GWAS summary statistics were munged for LDSC and restricted to HapMap3 variants outside the major histocompatibility complex. Partitioned heritability was estimated for each combination of trait (CAD, LAS, PAD, CaP), modality (scRNA-seq, snRNA-seq), and cell type by jointly modeling the focal annotation with the 97 baseline annotations. P values were adjusted using the BH method.

Gene-level prioritization using locus-to-gene mapping and colocalization

Candidate genes were prioritized using L2G scores from the Open Targets Platform GraphQL API v4. 59 Disease-level queries were performed for CAD (MONDO_0005010), LAS (MONDO_0005490), and PAD (MONDO_0005386). Genes with L2G scores ≥0.5 were retained, and duplicate trait-gene associations were collapsed by retaining the maximum L2G score. Colocalization between GWAS summary statistics and GTEx arterial eQTLs (aorta, coronary artery, and tibial artery) was performed using coloc.abf. 60 Variants shared between each GWAS and eQTL dataset were tested separately for each of the 12 trait-artery combinations using prior probabilities of p₁ = 10⁻⁴, p₂ = 10⁻⁴, and p₁₂ = 10⁻⁵. Genes with posterior probability for a shared causal variant (PP.H4) >0.8 were considered colocalized.

Marker gene enrichment and disease-state expression of L2G and colocalized genes

L2G-prioritized genes and colocalized genes were tested separately for enrichment among cell type-specific marker genes defined for each broad and fine annotation. Background gene sets were restricted to genes expressed in the corresponding modality and broad cell type.

Enrichment was assessed using one-sided Fisher's exact tests, with odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals estimated from 2 × 2 contingency tables. P values were adjusted using the BH method within each trait for L2G analyses and within each trait-artery combination for colocalization analyses. Cell-type-specific differential expression between symptomatic and asymptomatic plaques was assessed as described above. P values were adjusted using BH method across pooled L2G-prioritized or colocalized genes, separately for each evidence class, cell type and modality.

Mendelian randomization

Colocalized genes were further tested using two-sample cis-MR. Instruments were selected from GTEx arterial eQTLs at P<1×10⁻⁵ and LD-clumped using PLINK with the European 1000 Genomes reference panel at r²<0.1 within a 10,000 kb window. Exposure and outcome data were harmonized using TwoSampleMR::harmonise_data(). 61 The corresponding trait GWAS (CAD, LAS, PAD, CaP) served as outcome. Effect estimates were derived using the Wald ratio (single-instrument genes) or inverse-variance weighting (multi-instrument genes). P values were adjusted separately within each tissue-trait analysis using the BH method.
