How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Soil-transmitted helminth control relies on mass anthelmintic treatment, yet helminthanthelmintic-microbiome interactions and their role on treatment response remain poorly understood. We characterised the microbiome, resistome, and functional profiles of 858 paired stool metagenomes from 429 children treated with albendazole or a fixed-dose albendazole-ivermectin co-formulation in the ALIVE clinical trial in Ethiopia and Mozambique. Baseline microbiome composition differed more by country than infection status and was not associated with albendazole efficacy or adverse events. Co-formulation treatment, particularly three consecutive daily doses, was associated with depletion of Enterobacteriaceae, predominantly Escherichia coli, in Ethiopia, with reductions in E. coliassociated resistance genes and metabolic pathways. Genome-resolved analyses of over 22,000 high-quality metagenome-assembled genomes confirmed this depletion across multiple E. coli strain-level populations, linking taxonomic, resistome, and functional signals to one ecological event. These findings reveal an off-target effect of combination anthelmintic treatment relevant to large-scale deworming programmes and expand genomic resources for African gut microbiota.

[div-01]

Soil-transmitted helminths (STHs) are a group of intestinal nematodes comprising the whipworm Trichuris trichiura, the roundworm Ascaris lumbricoides, the threadworm Strongyloides stercoralis, and the two major human hookworms, Necator americanus and Ancylostoma duodenale 1,2 . Collectively, they infect nearly one-quarter of the world's population and cause tremendous morbidity, mainly through chronic malnutrition, malabsorption, anaemia and, in severe cases, intestinal obstruction, resulting in impaired growth and developmental delay 3,4 . To mitigate this burden, World Health Organization (WHO)-promoted control programmes rely primarily on the mass drug administration (MDA) of anthelmintics to at-risk populations in endemic countries, particularly preschooland school-aged children and women of reproductive age 5 . However, anthelmintics available to treat and control STH infections, mainly albendazole (ALB) and, more recently, in combination with ivermectin (IVM) 6 , show substantial variability in treatment response 7.

Efficacy varies between STH species, being generally lower against T. trichiura, intermediate against hookworms and higher against A. lumbricoides 8 , but can also differ substantially between and within geographical locations for the same species, particularly for T. trichiura and hookworms 9-13 . Among other reasons, the gut microbiome has been proposed to influence treatment response of STH to anthelmintics, as observed for other drugs 14,15.

As intestinal residents, STH interact closely with the gut microbiome. STH infection has been associated with differences in microbial diversity and composition, although the direction and magnitude of this association vary substantially across helminth species, infection intensity, host age and lifestyle, and geographical setting [16] [17] [18] [19] [20] [21] [22] . STH infection has also been linked to shifts in microbial metabolic potential, plausibly reflecting the parasite's modulation of the intestinal immune and nutritional environment and creating conditions that support mucosal colonisation or persistence within the gut ecosystem 16,20,23,24. Considerably less attention has been paid to the direct interplay between anthelmintic treatment, parasite clearance and the gut microbiome. Studies investigating whether the pretreatment microbiome influences drug efficacy have found little evidence of an association with ALB efficacy [25] [26] [27] , whereas an exploratory study of ALB-IVM combination treatment reported associations between cohort-specific enterotypes and treatment outcome 26 . Conversely, longitudinal studies have shown that anthelmintic treatment can shape the gut bacterial composition, although findings have varied across STH species, populations and treatment regimens 27-30. In vitro evidence further suggests that IVM and related compounds can directly inhibit the growth of selected gut bacteria due to antibioticlike activity 30 . However, these studies have included limited numbers of participants in single cohorts, the reported associations have not been independently validated across geographical settings, and microbiome characterisation has relied predominantly on 16S rRNA gene sequencing or shallow metagenomics, limiting characterisation mainly to taxonomic composition and leaving the mechanisms driving microbiome-worm-treatment interactions largely unknown. In addition, these studies have generally not assessed treatment safety, despite the frequent gastrointestinal disorders associated with anthelmintic drugs and their potential association with the gut microbiome 9,12.

To address these gaps, we characterised the gut microbiome before and after treatment in the ALIVE clinical trial 12 , which evaluated a new fixed-dose co-formulation (FDC) of ALB and IVM against STH infections, using higher IVM doses than the standard weight-based regimen (200 μg/kg). In this work, we combined read-based analyses with metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) to comprehensively characterise the taxonomic composition, resistome, and metabolic and functional profile of the microbiome, defining (i) the baseline characteristics of microbiomes and their association with sociodemographic and infectionrelated factors, such as STH species and infection intensity, (ii) the interaction between microbiome and treatment efficacy and adverse effects, and (iii) how the different treatment regimens can shape the gut microbiome.

Results

Study population and STH infection within the ALIVE clinical trial

The phase 3 of the ALIVE clinical trial evaluated the safety and efficacy of an FDC of ALB and IVM versus ALB monotherapy against T. trichiura and hookworms. The FDC was administered either as a single dose (FDCx1) or on three consecutive days (FDCx3). S. stercoralis, an initial trial target, was evaluated only as an exploratory outcome due to recruitment difficulties, and A. lumbricoides was present only as coinfections. We included participants recruited in Mozambique and Ethiopia with paired pre-and post-treatment stool samples available for microbiome analysis.

After sequencing and quality control (Supplementary Fig. 1A,B), the final dataset comprised 858 samples from 429 participants, including paired pre-and post-treatment samples from 156 participants recruited in Manhiça District, Mozambique, and 273 participants recruited in Bahir Dar, Ethiopia (Fig. 1A). Characteristics of the study population are in Table 1. Among the 156 participants from Mozambique, 107 were infected with T. trichiura alone, two with hookworm alone, one had T. trichiura-hookworm coinfection, and 46 had A. lumbricoides-T. trichiura coinfection (Fig. 1B). In Ethiopia, 230 participants were infected with hookworm alone, 17 with S. stercoralis alone, one with T. trichiura alone, and 25 had hookworm-S. stercoralis coinfection (Fig. 1B). Notably, the participants allocated to FDC arms received higher IVM doses than the standard regimen, with a median IVM dose of 333 μg/kg ranging from 202 μg/kg to 600 μg/kg.

Baseline microbiome characterisation reveals differences between geographic locations and coinfected participants Overall taxonomic microbiome structure was most strongly associated with the country, although age and all infection-related variables were also significant in the full cohort (Fig. 1C,D). However, STH species were highly unevenly distributed between study sites, and participant age also differed between countries (Fig. 1B and Table 1); we therefore stratified subsequent analyses by country. After stratification, only S. stercoralis infection in Ethiopia, particularly among participants coinfected with hookworm, remained significantly associated with taxonomic variation (Fig. 1C,D; Supplementary Tables S1). Shannon-index alpha diversity did not differ significantly according to any of the baseline variables evaluated (Supplementary Tables S1). We next examined which bacterial taxa differed between countries, and between monoand coinfected participants only in Ethiopia. Using MaAsLin3 31 and ANCOM- BC2 32 , 24 taxonomic features differed significantly between countries by both differential-abundance (DA) testing methods (Fig. 1E, and Extended Data Figs. 1 and 2). By contrast, no taxonomic features were associated with coinfection status in Ethiopia using both methods, although MaAsLin3 alone identified five (Extended Data Fig. 1), including depletion of Bacteroidota, Bacteroidaceae, and Prevotella in coinfected individuals.

Baseline antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) and functional read-based compositional profiles showed patterns similar differential patterns to taxonomy (Extended Data Fig. 3A-D): in the full cohort, age and all evaluated infection-related variables were significantly associated with both profiles, with the country of origin again showing the strongest association. After stratification, hookworm-S. stercoralis coinfection in Ethiopia and T.

trichiura-A. lumbricoides coinfection in Mozambique remained significantly associated with resistome and functional composition (Extended Data Fig. 3A,B). Resistome alpha diversity differed significantly between countries (Extended Data Fig. 3E), being higher in Mozambique for both set of data, and, after stratification, between mono-and coinfected participants in Ethiopia only (Extended Data Fig. 3F).

DA analyses identified 33 ARGs and 170 metabolic pathways that differed significantly between countries, including depletion of the Bacteroidales-associated ARGs in Ethiopia and marked enrichment of aerobic-respiration pathways in Mozambique (Extended Data Fig. 4).

In contrast, features associated with coinfection status within countries were identified only by MaAsLin3 (Extended Data Figs. 1). These findings demonstrate pronounced differences in the taxonomic, resistome and functional profiles of the gut microbiome between Mozambican and Ethiopian participants, with substantially weaker within-country coinfection associations. Subsequent analyses were therefore stratified by country.

Complete statistical results are in Supplementary Tables S1-S3 (diversity) and S4-S6 (differential abundance).

Pretreatment gut microbiomes are not associated with drug efficacy or safety

We next assessed whether pretreatment microbiome composition was associated with treatment efficacy, initially defined as cure using microcopy. As previously reported 12 , FDCx1 and FDCx3 achieved high efficacy, particularly against T. trichiura; in our microbiome cohort, both regimens achieved cure rates of 100% in Mozambique and at least 80% in Ethiopia (Fig. 2A).

Few participants remained infected after treatment, precluding adequately powered cured-vs-non-cured comparisons in those arms, so we restricted the primary efficacy analyses to participants receiving ALB alone -those infected with T. trichiura in Mozambique and hookworm in Ethiopia.

Baseline alpha/beta diversity and differential abundance (by either MaAsLin3 or ANCOM-BC2) showed no significant association with treatment outcome (Supplementary Table S7).

Secondary efficacy endpoints, individual egg reduction rate (ERRi) 33 (Fig. 2B) and qPCR- defined cure only for T. trichiura 34 (Fig 2A), likewise showed no significant microbiome associations (Supplementary Table S7). Because IVM may provide limited additional efficacy over ALB against hookworm 10 , implying FDCx1 and ALB could be equivalent, we also compared baseline microbiome composition between cured and non-cured participants in the merged ALB-FDCx1 arms in Ethiopia; this again identified no significant associations (Supplementary Table S7). Overall, these analyses provided no evidence that baseline taxonomic composition, resistome or functional potential was associated with ALB efficacy.

We also assessed whether the pretreatment microbiome was associated with treatment safety, as measured by adverse event (AE) occurrence. AEs were most frequent in the FDCx3 arm, followed by FDCx1 and ALB (Fig. 2C,D), and occurred most often among Mozambican participants (Fig. 2C); gastrointestinal disorders (GIDs) -particularly abdominal pain and headache -were the most frequently reported (Fig. 2D). Within each treatment arm, we first compared baseline taxonomic composition and functional profile of participants with and without any AE, and separately for treatment-related GIDs given their potential link to the intestinal microbiome; neither analysis identified significant associations in any arm (Supplementary Table S7). Pooling FDCx1 and FDCx3 participants to increase power likewise identified no significant associations for overall AEs or GIDs (Supplementary Table S7).

Collectively, these analyses provided no evidence that pretreatment microbiome characteristics were associated with AE occurrence.

ALB-IVM FDC treatment shapes the gut microbiome composition, resistome and functional profile

We assessed the impact of treatment on gut microbiome taxonomic composition, resistome, and functional profile, comparing samples before and after treatment, stratified by country and treatment arm. At the taxonomic level in Mozambique, we observed no significant differences in alpha or beta diversity after treatment (Supplementary Table S8) and, although MaAsLin3 identified several differentially abundant taxa and metabolic pathways, ANCOM-BC2 confirmed none (Extended Data Fig. 5).

By contrast, FDC treatment in Ethiopia was associated with a decrease in Enterobacteriaceae in both FDCx1 and FDCx3, and Escherichia and E. coli in FDCx3 (Fig. 3A).

Reductions were also observed in Mozambique but were not significant by either method (Fig. 3A). No correlation was observed between individual ALB or IVM doses and these reductions (Supplementary Fig. 2). Repeating the analysis among only ALB-arm participants who achieved cure -to test whether parasite clearance itself, rather than non-cured participants masking an effect, drove the Enterobacteriaceae decrease -identified significant taxa only by MaAsLin3, with Enterobacteriaceae and its members not significantly reduced (Supplementary Table S9). In the Ethiopian FDCx3 arm, the alpha diversity of both resistance genes and metabolic pathways (Shannon index) decreased significantly after treatment (Extended Data Fig. 6A). Overall composition of the resistome and pathway profiles (PERMANOVA) also shifted after both FDCx1 and FDCx3 (Extended Data Fig. 6B; Supplementary Table S8), whereas taxonomic diversity and composition did not show significant changes change.

DA analyses likewise identified features consistently detected by both methods in the FDCx1 arm and, more prominently, the FDCx3 arm (Extended Data Fig. 5). Complete results are in Supplementary Tables S9-S11 (taxa, ARGs, and pathways, respectively). For the resistome, both methods identified 15 ARGs conferring resistance to various antibiotic families as significantly less abundant after FDCx3 (Fig. 3B, Extended Data Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table S10,11). Pathway DA showed more changes after FDCx3 than after FDCx1: 69 pathways were significantly lower after FDCx3 (by both methods) versus 2 after FDCx1 treatment (Fig. 3C and Extended Data Fig. 5). Species-stratified HUMAnN profiles showed that E. coli was the predominant contributor to most of these pathways (Extended Data Fig. 7), with pathway-specific contributions substantially lower after treatment (Fig. 3D, four most abundant pathways), indicating this reduction was largely driven by E. coli depletion.

Genome-resolved analyses confirm broad, strain-nonspecific depletion of E. coli and its carried ARGs

To further characterise these changes in the microbiome, we reconstructed MAGs from all pre-and post-treatment samples. Across the 858 stool metagenomes, we recovered 60,738

MAGs and retained 22,032 high-quality MAGs for downstream analyses after quality filtering. Dereplication at 95% average nucleotide identity (ANI) grouped these MAGs into 1,372 representative species-level genomes, comprising 1,366 bacterial (Extended Data Fig. 8A) and six archaeal genomes (Extended Data Fig. 8B). Of the bacterial representatives, 516 were recovered in both countries, while 528 and 324 were detected exclusively in Ethiopia and Mozambique, respectively (Extended Data Fig. 8C-E). Among the 22,032 high-quality MAGs, 518 could not be assigned to a species by GTDB-Tk and were grouped into 161 representative genomes. Of these, 158 could be taxonomically assigned at the genus level, and three only at the family level (Extended Data Fig. 9), likely representing poorly characterised or novel species-level populations.

We next investigated whether the treatment-associated reduction in Enterobacteriaceae, particularly E. coli, reflected the depletion of specific phylogenetic lineages or strain-level populations. Comparing read-based abundance with the recovery and distribution of Enterobacteriaceae MAGs (Fig. 4A) showed that composition differed markedly between countries: E. coli dominated in Ethiopia, whereas Mozambique contained a prominent, treatment-stable Klebsiella pneumoniae population alongside an additional E. coli population and minority taxa uncommon in Ethiopia (Fig. 4A). Dereplicating Enterobacteriaceae MAGs at 99% ANI yielded 50 strain-level clusters (Supplementary Fig. 3A), 31 of which were E. coli (Fig. 4B). Two E. coli clusters were substantially more prevalent, with no evidence of country-specific populations (Supplementary Fig. 3B); major strains showed similar treatment responses within each country, with the clearest reductions following FDC in Ethiopia (Fig. 4C), indicating the depletion was not driven by loss of one dominant strain but reflected a broader reduction in E. coli.

We also characterised the resistome of reconstructed MAGs using RGI/CARD, enabling direct comparison with the read-based resistome analyses. Overall, 14,719 high-quality

MAGs harboured at least one ARG (mean 3.85 ARGs/MAG). The 15 ARGs significantly reduced after FDCx3 in the read-based analysis were carried exclusively by Enterobacteriaceae MAGs, almost all specifically by E. coli (except OmpA), with no significant variation after FDCx3 treatment (Fig. 4D). Overall, FDC treatment, particularly three consecutive doses, was associated with a broad, strain-nonspecific depletion of E. coli in Ethiopia, accompanied by depletion of E. coli-carried ARGs and metabolic pathways.

Metabolic pathway changes converge across countries and regimens despite limited taxonomic and resistome overlap

Because requiring concordant MaAsLin3-ANCOM-BC2 significance might be conservative, we also examined features significant by MaAsLin3 alone, intersected between countries within each arm, and between FDCx1 and FDCx3 within each country. No features were shared between countries in the ALB arm, and taxonomic/resistome responses to the FDC regimens also showed limited overlap between countries (Fig. 5A,B). Catenibacterium decrease was the only effect associated with FDCx1 treatment in both Ethiopia and Mozambique, though with small effect estimates and no ANCOM-BC2 support (Supplementary Table S8). No taxa or ARGs were shared between FDCx1 and FDCx3 in Mozambique (Fig. 5A,B). Within Ethiopia, Enterobacteriaceae, Escherichia and E. coli were the only taxonomic features associated with both FDC regimens (Fig. 5A), reinforcing the treatment-associated reduction in this bacterial lineage in this country. For the resistome, all six ARGs associated with FDCx1 were also associated with FDCx3; four -tet(W), tet(40), tet(O/32/O) and tet(O/M/O) -showed concordant increases after both regimens (Fig. 5C, Supplementary Table S10), distributed across a broad range of bacterial taxonomic groups when mapped to high-quality MAGs (Fig. 5D).

In contrast, metabolic pathways showed substantially greater cross-country overlap: 28 pathways were detected in all four country-by-treatment comparisons, of which 20 increased concordantly across both countries and FDC regimens (Fig. 5E). These comprised pathways spanning amino acid metabolism (n=8), nucleotide/translation-related metabolism (n=5), cofactor/quinone/redox metabolism (n=4), cell-wall metabolism (n=2) and central carbon metabolism (n=1). Species-stratified HUMAnN profiles indicated this increase was distributed across multiple bacterial contributors rather than one species (Extended Data Fig. 10), consistent with functional redundancy such that a community-wide shift in growth state or niche occupancy can register functionally without a corresponding taxon-level signal. Together, these findings suggest that, beyond the pronounced depletion of E. coli in Ethiopia, FDC treatment may induce a weaker but more broadly distributed ecological response, reflected in relative increases in tetracycline-resistance genes across diverse taxa in Ethiopia and in metabolic pathways shared across regimens in both countries.

Discussion

In this study, we provide a comprehensive assessment of interactions among STH infection, anthelmintic treatment, and the gut microbiome in a large multicentre clinical trial. Three major findings emerged. First, geographical origin was the strongest determinant of microbiome variation, substantially outweighing associations with infection status or other baseline variables. Second, we found no evidence that baseline microbiome composition, resistome, or functional profile was related to albendazole efficacy or to the occurrence of adverse events. Third, combination treatment with ALB and high doses of IVM was associated with changes in the gut microbiome, particularly after three consecutive doses, that in Ethiopia was characterised by depletion of Enterobacteriaceae and E. coli and accompanied by reductions in E. coli-associated metabolic pathways and ARGs.

The influence of helminth coinfection and microbiome characteristics, as we observed in our baseline analysis, has been described previously [16] [17] [18] 22 , although the specific features suggest more than a generic consequence of harbouring multiple helminth species. In Ethiopia, we found a weak coinfection signal plausibly attributable to S. stercoralis, which has been specifically linked to mucin-degrader enrichment and altered short-chain fatty acid metabolism during chronic infection 35, 36 . Similar country-of-origin effects have been reported in other microbiome clinical studies, highlighting one of the major challenges of identifying microbiome biomarkers in geographically diverse populations Although direct antibacterial activity of IVM has been demonstrated in vitro 30 , our findings do not establish such an effect in vivo, and recent evidence suggests this may be specifically unlikely for E. coli: in a panel of gut isolates repeatedly exposed to IVM and moxidectin, E.

coli was the only species showing no clear phenotypic or genomic shift, attributed to limited drug access across its Gram-negative envelope and consistent with reported E. coli insensitivity to macrolide antibiotics 30 . This aligns with our own strain-level data, where depletion was broadly distributed across 31 E. coli clusters with no evidence of a selective sweep (Fig. 4B,C), more consistent with indirect, ecological depletion than direct selection.

Notably, the same study found downregulated multidrug-efflux gene expression (mdtI, mdtJ, ydhC) upon exposure, raising the possibility that some ARG depletion reflects altered expression rather than abundance alone. Alternative mechanisms are also plausible: of the samples (Supplementary Fig 1C), per-sample comparison showed that GlobDB left less information unclassified when compared to GTDB (Supplementary Fig 1D). All downstream analyses used the post-decontamination MAGs and their corresponding quality metrics. MAGs were classified as high quality when they had at least 90% estimated completeness, no more than 5% estimated contamination and passed the GUNC chimerism assessment. These criteria retained 22,032 high-quality MAGs from the 60,738 MAGs initially reconstructed.

MAG dereplication and taxonomic classification

High-quality MAGs were dereplicated at 95% average nucleotide identity (ANI) using dRep v3.5.0 with fastANI, multiround primary clustering, greedy secondary clustering and tertiary clustering 85 . This produced 1,372 representative species-level genomes. Taxonomic assignments were obtained from GTDB-Tk classifications. Representative genomes that could not be assigned to a named species were retained as poorly characterized or potentially novel species-level populations.

For higher-resolution analysis of treatment-associated changes in Enterobacteriaceae, all high-quality MAGs assigned to this family were dereplicated separately at 99% ANI to define strain-level genomic clusters. Cluster prevalence was calculated as the proportion of samples within each country, treatment arm and time point from which a MAG belonging to the corresponding cluster was recovered.

Phylogenetic analysis of representative MAGs

Representative bacterial and archaeal MAGs were analysed using GTDB-Tk v2.7.1.

Conserved bacterial bac120 and archaeal ar53 marker proteins were identified and aligned using the GTDB-Tk identify and align workflows. Approximate maximum-likelihood phylogenies were inferred using FastTree v2.1. P-values from diversity analyses were adjusted using the Bonferroni method within each microbiome profile and analysis family.

Differential-abundance analyses Differential abundance was evaluated for bacterial taxa, ARGs and metabolic pathways using MaAsLin3 31 as the primary method and ANCOM-BC2 32 as a sensitivity analysis.

MaAsLin3 jointly modelled feature prevalence and abundance among samples in which the feature was detected. Taxonomic relative abundances were analysed using total-sum scaling and log transformation, whereas ARG RPKM and pathway CPM profiles were analysed without further normalisation or transformation. Continuous covariates were standardised by MaAsLin3. For ANCOM-BC2 analyses of taxonomic composition, relative abundances were converted to sequencing-depth-scaled pseudo-counts by multiplying each relative abundance by the number of host-depleted reads in the corresponding sample and rounding to the nearest integer. ANCOM-BC2 was run with structural-zero detection disabled and a pseudo-count sensitivity score of zero. For longitudinal analyses, participant identifiers were included as a random effect.

Before differential-abundance testing, taxa were required to have a relative abundance of at least 0.5% and to occur in at least 5% of samples in the corresponding analysis. ARGs and metabolic pathways were required to occur in at least 5% of samples. Features were considered significant in MaAsLin3 at a false-discovery-rate-adjusted joint qV<V0.01 and in ANCOM-BC2 at a Bonferroni-adjusted PV<V0.05. Features meeting the respective significance thresholds in both methods were considered robustly associated.
