To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

No two brains are wired alike, and much of a person's cognitive and clinical state is written in this individual variation, not in the group average. Structural connectomes, however, are typically read through group atlases that summarize connectivity into regional averages, leaving this individual signal untapped. We build a vertex-wise structural connectome, in which every cortical surface vertex is a node, and apply it across the Alzheimer's continuum. Using an independent dataset for methodological development and a multisite cohort of 317 participants from ADNI-4, we show that connection distance and organizational scale form a nested hierarchy, from a long-range backbone to fine, short-range structure. The individual fingerprint is strongest in medium-and short-range connections and becomes less distinctive as connectivity is pooled into atlas parcels. This individual structure can be recovered across spatial scales, although its fidelity depends on diffusion acquisition quality, with multishell data providing a more informative reference than single-shell data. In the aging brain, an individually referenced balance between short-and long-range connectivity is associated with episodic memory, cognitive reserve, depressive symptoms, and shared molecular pathology. While cognitive associations largely reflect diagnostic differences, associations with depressive symptoms and pathology burden remain detectable after adjustment for diagnosis. These findings show that individual structural connectomes retain clinically and biologically relevant variation that is not fully captured by diagnostic classification or group-level summaries.

Introduction

Human brain organization varies substantially across individuals, and this variability is increasingly recognized not as noise around a canonical brain but as a biologically and clinically meaningful signal in its own right (Mueller et al. 2013; Mansour L et al. 2021). Most neuroimaging, however, characterizes the brain by comparing group means within predefined atlas parcels, an approach that describes the average brain while discarding the subject-specific features that may be the ones of clinical interest.

A subject-specific framework has been proposed for functional MRI to recover this lost information, characterizing each brain densely enough to map its own organization rather than pooling participants (Laumann et al. 2015; Braga and Buckner 2017; Gordon et al. 2017 Gordon et al. , 2026 https://paperpile.com/c/JCOSwmTo/UiCg+KmC5+W9H1+iZE5. An equivalent framework has not been established for structural connectivity, which is still analyzed almost entirely through group atlases and regional averages. Yet the raw material for one is clearly present. Whole-brain white-matter architecture is distinctive enough to identify a person from diffusion MRI with near-perfect accuracy (Yeh et al. 2016) https://paperpile.com/c/JCOSwmTo/m9zS an identifiability that can be quantified as a fingerprint score (Amico and Goñi 2018) https://paperpile.com/c/JCOSwmTo/2Bmv that strengthens with spatial resolution, and that concentrates in heteromodal association cortex (Mansour L et al. 2021; Mueller et al. 2013).

In parallel, the connectome is organized as a multiscale, hierarchically nested community structure (Betzel and Bassett 2017) whose form is strongly shaped by the physical cost of connection distance, to the point of self-similarity across scales (Zheng et al. 2020). These two literatures have largely developed apart, and at coarse spatial grain. Identifiability has been measured mostly at the voxel (Yeh et al. 2016) or atlas-parcel (https://paperpile.com/c/JCOSwmTo/2Bmv) level, while multiscale community structure has been characterized on regional graphs rather than at the resolution of the cortical surface itself. As a result, three questions remain open: whether connection distance and community scale are distinct organizational axes or a single fused one; whether the individual fingerprint is carried preferentially by connections of a particular length; and whether the structure that distinguishes individuals is preserved under atlas parcellation, and at which spatial scale it is most informative.

Alzheimer's disease (AD), the leading cause of dementia in later life, offers a natural test of whether this individual structure matters. AD is defined biologically by amyloid and tau pathology (Jack et al. 2018 https://paperpile.com/c/JCOSwmTo/nbbU yet that pathology is not distributed uniformly across patients. Tau deposition follows distinct spatiotemporal trajectories in different individuals, and these trajectories map onto the clinical variants of the disease: alongside the common amnestic form are presentations that begin with visual, language, executive, or behavioral failure, each tracking the regional distribution of tau and neurodegeneration more closely than the more diffuse burden of amyloid (Vogel et al. 2021; Jones et al. 2024 https://paperpile.com/c/PnwIrF/sC3f+JQvF. This heterogeneity is not independent of the brain's wiring: models in which pathology spreads along brain networks reproduce the spatiotemporal evolution of tau across the cortex (Vogel et al. 2021; Raj et al. 2025). If the relationship between pathology and connectivity is spatially specific and differs between individuals, an individually resolved, vertex-wise structural connectome should capture disease-relevant information that regional and group-level representations discard.

The same heterogeneity extends to how individuals carry the disease. Two axes are central here: individuals with similar levels of pathology differ markedly in the cognition they retainhttps://paperpile.com/c/JCOSwmTo/XlLE+1Sus a capacity termed cognitive reserve (Stern et al. 2020; Pappalettera et al. 2024) , and in their affective burden, with depression bidirectionally entwined with the disease as risk factor, prodrome, and symptom (Huang et al. 2024). Whether an individually resolved structural connectome carries information about these dimensions beyond what diagnostic classification already captures is an important clinical question.

Here we develop a vertex-wise structural connectome, in which every cortical surface vertex is a node, and show that it retains individual-specific structural information that atlas parcellation discards. To make this approach accessible and reproducible, we provide an open, interactive toolbox that guides users through the construction and analysis of vertex-wise structural connectomes and generates scripts for application to their own data. Where vertex-level connectivity representations have been recently used to improve cross-subject correspondence (Zhou et al. 2026) https://paperpile.com/c/JCOSwmTo/S8v4 we characterize each connectome individually, without aligning its organization to a group representation. We find that connection distance and organizational scale are coupled in a nested hierarchy that is reproducible within individuals and distinctive between them. This subject-specific pattern is carried most distinctively by medium-and short-range connections. Although individuals remain identifiable across spatial scales, vertex-wise representation retains more individual-specific information than atlas representations. Finally, we relate an individually referenced short-to-long connectivity balance to clinical and pathological dimensions across the Alzheimer's continuum. Although its associations with episodic memory and cognitive reserve largely reflect diagnostic differences, associations with depressive symptoms and shared molecular pathology remain detectable after controlling for diagnosis, highlighting individual variation that is not fully captured by diagnostic classification.

We establish these findings in two independent cohorts: a dataset used for methodological development and acquisition-related analyses (Aerts et al. 2018) https://paperpile.com/c/JCOSwmTo/E1zg and a multisite ADNI cohort comprising 317 participants across the Alzheimer's continuum (Jack et al. 2008). The individual structural connectome thus emerges not as a refinement of the group average but as a distinct level of description, one that complements group-level analyses, that carries clinically meaningful information about the person, and that requires individual data of sufficient quality to recover.

Results

Demographics

We developed the method on the BTC dataset (OpenNeuro ds001226; Aerts et al. 2022 Aerts et al. https://paperpile.com/c/JCOSwmTo/rfn8+E1zg 2018)) , a multi-shell diffusion-MRI dataset, using 10 healthy control subjects (4 women; aged 48-75 years; mean 59.6, SD = 10.3; one further control was excluded at quality control, Supplementary Materials S1) to assess the conditions under which subject-specific structure can be recovered. We then applied the validated method to 322 ADNI-4 participants spanning the Alzheimer's continuum (Weiner et al. 2023 https://paperpile.com/c/JCOSwmTo/yxTv 218 cognitively normal (CN), 80 with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), 23 with AD, and a single non-AD dementia case. Subject-specific structure requires multishell acquisition

To determine the acquisition conditions under which subject-specific connectivity can be recovered, we degraded the BTC data of the 10 controls along two axes: streamline count and shell sampling. We measured identifiability, IDIFF, defined as the similarity between each participant's degraded and full-data connectivity fingerprints minus the mean similarity to other participants' fingerprints (Amico and Goñi 2018). Fingerprints were defined as the SIFT2-weighted connectivity of each vertex to vertices within 14.5mm, averaged within 360 parcels for the comparison between participants (Fig. 2).

Streamline count had a modest effect: subsampling the 5-million-streamline tractograms to 100,000 streamlines reduced IDIFF from .63 (95% CI .60 to .66), the ceiling at which each reference is compared with itself, to .54 (95% CI .51 to .58). All participants remained identifiable from every subsample, and two independent subsamples of the same tractogram identified each other at every count (IDIFF .46 at 100,000 to .62 at 2 million streamlines).

Shell sampling had a stronger effect. With each degraded acquisition reprocessed from the diffusion signal onward, IDIFF was .62 (95% CI .59 to .66) with three shells, .50 (95% CI .45 to .54) with two, and .28 (95% CI .19 to .37) with one, and .27 (95% CI .18 to .37) when the angular directions were halved; the three-shell value represents the metric's ceiling because each full-data fingerprint was compared with itself. Removing each shell reduced IDIFF in every participant (paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p = .002 for each removal); p = .32 for halving the directions. Single-shell acquisition degraded, but did not erase, individual identifiability: similarity to each participant's own reference decreased from .86 with two shells to .56 with one, while similarity to other participants decreased from .36 to .28. This loss coincides with the transition at which a single shell no longer supports three-tissue constrained spherical deconvolution, so that tissue compartments can no longer be separated when fiber orientations are estimated. It therefore reflects a joint limitation of the acquisition and the model, rather than the complete loss of individual distinctiveness. The reduction in identifiability was also observed when short-range connectivity was defined using alternative distance thresholds (Supplementary Materials S2).

The ADNI-4 protocol (three shells, b = 500/1000/2000s•mm -2 , 114 diffusion directions, 2.0mm isotropic) lies on the multishell side of this transition: it matches the BTC reference in shell count and exceeds it in angular sampling and spatial resolution, although its maximum b-value is lower. A connection-distance hierarchy organizes the vertex-wise structural connectome Cortical connections span a broad range of lengths, from short links between neighboring regions to long-range tracts, and we first asked how this range is distributed, deriving the band boundaries from the data rather than imposing a priori thresholds. In the 217 CN participants who passed registration quality control, we randomly sampled 3 million connections per participant, measured the Euclidean distance between endpoint vertices on the white matter (a lower bound on fiber path length), and weighted each connection by its SIFT2 strength. The weighted length distribution was unimodal, peaking at 11-12mm with a heavy tail beyond 50mm. Its shape was highly consistent across participants: the lower and upper weighted tertiles fell at 14.3 ± .6mm and 27.9 ± 2.1mm (mean ± SD), respectively, and were unrelated to cortical coverage. In contrast, the proportion of connectivity weight in the long-range band varied substantially between individuals (ranging from .18 to .46), providing the basis for the individual-level comparisons in the clinical analyses below.

Partitioning the cohort-average weighted distribution into tertiles produced three bands, short (< 14.5 mm), medium (14.5-27.5 mm), and long (> 27.5 mm), carrying .34, .32, and .34 of the total connectivity weight. A Gaussian mixture analysis of long connection lengths did not identify a preferred number of components, suggesting that the three bands provide a practical partition of the distribution rather than distinct, naturally occurring classes. The boundaries also have an anatomical interpretation.

Because endpoint separation underestimates fiber path length, connections in the long band have a path length of at least 27.5 mm, whereas the short band falls within the conventional 3-30mm range attributed to short association (U-)fibers (https://paperpile.com/c/JCOSwmTo/pLti+dlDa Attar et al. 2020; Shastin et al. 2022). Distance and scale are coupled in a nested community hierarchy Cortical communities can be described at many scales, from a few large modules to many small ones. Whether this organizational scale is coupled to connection distance remains unclear. We decomposed each CN connectome (n = 217) into a multi-resolution community hierarchy by Leiden modularity-based detection applied to the strongest 10% of connections across a resolution sweep (γ = .05-5), yielding a coarse-to-fine cascade (cohort mean 2 to 55 modules). Detection used connectivity weight alone and was blind to distance, allowing us to assess whether a relationship between connection distance and organizational scale emerged from the resulting partition.

The hierarchy was strongly nested (mean containment .93). At the lowest resolution, the partition was the hemispheric split in most participants: two modules were detected in 174 of the 217 participants, and both modules were hemisphere-pure in 140. Each finer level was nested within its predecessor (mean containment .92 to .93 across successive transitions beyond the hemispheric step). As scale became finer, modules became progressively shorter-range and more compact in every participant: within-module connection length fell from 25.0 (95% CI 24.8 to 25.2) to 16.4 (95% CI 16.3 to 16.5)mm, radius of gyration fell from 46.5 to 17.7 mm, and the within-module connectivity fraction from .86 to .34 (sign test p < .001 for all three measures). The trends in connection length and compactness were unrelated to cortical coverage (|ρ| ≤ .17 at every level). A distance-blind partition thus yields progressively shorter-range and geometrically more local fine modules, consistent with a progressive decrease in average within-module connection length from larger-scale to finer-scale modules (Fig. 4).

The hierarchy is an individual fingerprint, strongest in medium-and short-range connections

To establish whether the hierarchy identifies individuals, we compared the reproducibility of each person's partition across two split halves of their connectome with the similarity of partitions between participants, using the adjusted mutual information (AMI). Split-half reproducibility was high at every level (ranging from .82 to .89), reaching its lowest value at γ = .2 and its highest at the coarsest level, whereas similarity between participants was lower throughout (.57-.66 beyond the coarsest level, which corresponded to the hemispheric split in most participants). The gap, ranging from .18 to .25, was present at every level, with bootstrap 95% CIs excluding zero, and defines an individual fingerprint. Individual identification was achieved in 99.5-100% of participants from γ=.5 onward, and in 75-90% of participants at the three coarse levels. Because the two halves share the same acquisition, registration and cortical surface, split-half reproducibility represents a ceiling for test-retest reliability rather than a direct estimate of it.

Restricting connectivity to each distance band and comparing matched halves of the vertex-wise band-strength maps showed that every participant was identified from every band. In this sense, mediumand short-range connectivity was more individually distinctive than long-range connectivity at every spatial scale., except for the short band at the largest smoothing kernel (kNN 400; .34 versus .36). For example, IDIFF on Glasser-360 parcel means was .65 for the medium band, .60 for the short band and .47 for the long band, with bootstrap 95% CIs for the between-band differences excluding zero. This pattern reflected greater between-person similarity in long-range strength maps (between-subject r = .53, compared with .35 for the medium band and .40 for the short band). Identifiability was also higher in participants with lower cortical coverage (ρ = -.52 to .-.60); coverage-adjusted values are therefore reported alongside the unadjusted results (Supplementary Materials S4). The cortical reliability of individual differences in band strength was high and near-uniform across the cortex (vertex-wise ICC .97-.99), although it was slightly lower in paralimbic and medial temporal cortex, where endpoint coverage was the lowest. Reliability did not differ between primary sensory and heteromodal association cortex. The individual fingerprint therefore is strongest in medium-and short-range connections (Fig. 5).

Regional atlases summarize connectivity by averaging it within predefined parcels. To see whether this averaging discards individual structure, we compared the vertex-wise band-fraction maps with the same quantities averaged within Desikan-Killiany (68) and Glasser (360) parcels: band composition varied smoothly within individual parcels, so averaging replaced the graded vertex maps with a single regional value and erased the fine, short-range structure shown above to carry identity.

Consistently, connectome identifiability rose monotonically with resolution, from IDIFF = .10 (DK-68; (Desikan et al. 2006) to .19 (Glasser et al. 2016) to ~.46 at the vertex scale. That identifiability keeps rising to the vertex scale rather than saturating or degrading is not guaranteed: finer resolution reveals more individual structure only if the acquisition can characterize it reliably, so the monotonic increase demonstrates that diffusion data of this quality resolve individual structure to the vertex scale, not merely that finer parcellations create more apparent distinctions.

The same dependence governed cortical routing: the mean number of intermediate nodes between distant anchor regions grew from 3.3 (DK) to 6.6 (Glasser) to 9.7 at the vertex scale, revealing increasingly detailed multi-step routes at higher spatial resolution. These routes were themselves individual, with within-subject routing similarity (.65) far exceeding between-subject (.29), and a group-average connectome reproduced no individual's routing (.40).

Individual-referenced short-to-long balance is associated with cognitive and affective variation across the Alzheimer's continuum Having established that vertex-wise structural connectivity preserves reliable individual differences, we next examined whether these differences capture clinically meaningful differences in cognitive and affective functioning. Because individuals within the same diagnostic category can differ substantially in cognitive performance and depressive symptoms, we assessed these associations beyond group-average patterns. We therefore referenced every participant against a cognitively normal model rather than against the patient group. We focused on the short-to-long balance, which captures the relative contribution of short-and long-distance connections to each participant's structural connectome. At each vertex, connectivity was reduced to the short-to-long balance, defined as the relative streamline strength of the shortest and longest distance tertiles (short band < 14.5 mm, within the range of short association U-fibers; long band > 27.5 mm, including longer association and projection pathways), and smoothed over a surface area of approximately 917mm 2 . Each participant's short-to-long balance was compared with the balance expected from the normative model fitted to the 208 cognitively normal participants. The difference was expressed as a deviation z-score controlling for age, sex, and cortical coverage (Fig. 6).

The resulting deviation maps were highly reproducible and participant-specific. Correlations between independently estimated maps from the same participant were .91 before smoothing and .99 after smoothing. All 301 participants could be identified from either estimate. Despite this strong individual-level reproducibility, the deviations showed little consistent spatial pattern when averaged across the cohort. Nevertheless, they were associated with diagnostic status after accounting for the site.

Patients showed higher short-range share than CN participants, with five significant clusters across the precuneus, superior frontal, and lateral temporoparietal cortex (largest p FWE = .0004; cortex-wide partial r = .18, 95% CI .04-.31). The AD-versus-CN contrast also identified three significant clusters (largest p FWE = .001). Neither contrast was significant without site adjustment.

We next examined whether these individual differences were associated with cognitive performance and depressive symptoms. In pooled analyses, short-to-long balance was associated with episodic memory (six clusters; largest p FWE = .005), memory reserve (five clusters; largest p FWE = .008), and CDR-SB (four clusters; largest p FWE = .005). Higher short-range share was associated with poorer episodic memory and lower memory reserve, as well as greater CDR-SB scores. However, these associations were no longer significant after adjusting for the diagnostic group, and none was significant when analyses were restricted to cognitively normal participants or patients, except for CDR-SB within cognitively normal participants (one cluster, p FWE = .043). This pattern suggests that the cognitive associations largely reflected differences along the diagnostic continuum rather than associations detectable within diagnostic groups.

The association with depressive symptoms showed a different pattern. Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS) scores were associated with the short-to-long balance across two clusters (largest p FWE = .0008).

Importantly, this association remained significant after adjusting for diagnostic group, with one cluster in the left superior parietal/sensorimotor cortex (p FWE = .003; Holm-corrected p = .022; partial r = .15, 95% CI .06 to .26). The association was not significant in analyses restricted to either cognitively normal participants or patients, and there was no evidence of an interaction between GDS scores and the diagnostic group (p = .90). This suggests that the association with depressive symptoms was not fully accounted for by diagnostic classification, although it was not demonstrable within either diagnostic subgroup. In contrast, perceived stress showed no significant association (largest p FWE = .18). The individually referenced balance tracks Alzheimer's pathology burden beyond diagnosis

Having established that the individually referenced short-to-long balance captures differences in clinical functioning, we next tested whether it also tracked the underlying molecular pathology of Alzheimer's disease. We related each participant's vertex-wise short-range deviation to five markers spanning the Alzheimer's pathological cascade: amyloid burden (PET centiloids), plasma p-tau217, tau-PET, neurofilament light (NfL), and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP). To capture their shared contribution, we also derived a pathology-burden composite from the first principal component of these five markers in the 168 participants with complete data (116 CN, 42 MCI, and 10 AD; 58% of variance explained; loadings .40 to .53). All associations were tested using vertex-wise cluster-mass permutation, controlling for age, sex, education, site, and cortical coverage.

The short-to-long balance was associated with the composite pathology burden across the cohort.

Higher pathology burden was associated with a higher short-range share across six clusters, with the largest over the right inferior parietal and superior temporal cortex (largest p FWE = .001; cortex-wide partial r = .28, 95% CI .11 to .44). Importantly, this association was not explained entirely by diagnostic differences: it remained significant after adjusting for diagnostic group, although it was spatially more restricted, with one cluster over the right inferior parietal and middle temporal cortex (p FWE = .021; partial r = .21, 95% CI .01 to .38). The association was also detectable within patients (three clusters, p FWE = .004; n = 52), but not within cognitively normal participants (n = 116; partial r = .06).

The association was not driven by one particular pathology marker. When examined individually in the same participants, the five markers showed similar cortex-wide partial correlations with the short-to-long balance, ranging from .19 to .23. None of the pairwise differences was significant (all Steiger p ≥ .59). Thus, the vertex-wise structural connectome was associated with their shared pathological burden, rather than showing a preferential relationship with any one marker. These results do not support a specific ordering of the associations along the pathological cascade. The strength of this association depended on accounting for site differences. Without site adjustment, the cortex-wide association was negligible (partial r = -.02), whereas adjustment for site alone yielded a positive association (r = .31).

The association was nevertheless consistent across the cohort: it remained significant when each of the 19 sites was excluded in turn, with partial correlations ranging from .22 to .33 and confidence intervals excluding zero. It also remained detectable when site effects were incorporated into the normative model (p FWE = .041 after adjustment for diagnostic group).

Finally, we assessed whether the time between plasma collection and imaging influenced the result. In 38% of participants contributing to the composite, archived plasma had been collected more than two years before the imaging visit. Discussion

We built a vertex-wise structural connectome to test whether the organization of an individual cortex carries information that group averaging cannot recover. Our results reveal four complementary properties of this representation. First, individual identification depends on the quality of the diffusion data, with multishell acquisition providing the most informative reference. Second, connection distance and community scale are coupled in a nested hierarchy. Third, individual fingerprints are detectable across the spatial scales tested, but their distinctiveness is greatest at the vertex level and is carried preferentially by medium-and short-range connections. Finally, an individually referenced measure of connection-length balance is associated with diagnostic status and Alzheimer's pathology, while its relationships with cognition, cognitive reserve and depressive symptoms differ in their sensitivity to diagnostic adjustment. Together, these findings establish vertex-wise structural connectomics as a framework for characterizing individual differences in cortical organization. Rather than treating interindividual variation as noise to be averaged away, the framework provides a way to measure that variation and investigate its relationships with clinical and biological characteristics.

Connection distance and community scale are coupled within a nested hierarchy. Coarse, long-range modules form a backbone that nests progressively shorter-range, spatially compact communities, consistent with the multiscale, hierarchically nested community structure described for the connectome (Betzel and Bassett 2017https://paperpile.com/c/JCOSwmTo/Pky2 and with its geometric self-similarity across scales (Zheng et al. 2020 ). Our contribution is to show that this distance-and-scale fusion holds at the resolution of the cortical surface itself and within single individuals, rather than only in group-level regional graphs. Importantly, this organization was recovered using a partitioning procedure that did not explicitly incorporate connection distance, suggesting that spatial embedding is an important organizing feature of the structural connectome. The hierarchy should consequently be interpreted as a description of the observed organization, rather than evidence that distance and community scale are coupled independently of spatial geometry. This geometric interpretation also provides context for the fingerprinting results: when we applied the functional MRI-derived canonical functional precision-imaging partition (Gordon et al. 2017 (Gordon et al. , 2026;; Gratton et al. 2020) to the structural connectome, the resulting modules were largely explained by cortical geometry, suggesting that individual structural information may be expressed not only through differences in community membership but also through the connections linking conserved endpoints.

The individual fingerprint was carried preferentially by short-and medium-range connections and became less distinctive as connectivity was summarized within atlas parcels. This extends structural fingerprintinghttps://paperpile.com/c/JCOSwmTo/4BNv previously demonstrated at the voxel (Yeh et al. 2016) and atlas-parcel (https://paperpile.com/c/JCOSwmTo/2Bmv) levels, and shown to strengthen with increasing resolution (Mansour L et al. 2021) , to the cortical surface. Importantly, identification and individual distinctiveness are not interchangeable: participants remained identifiable across the community scales tested, but their connectivity patterns were most distinctive at the vertex level. This loss of distinctiveness with parcellation partly reflects the removal of short-range connections whose endpoints fall within the same parcel. These connections accounted for approximately a third of connectivity weight with the Desikan-Killiany atlas and an eighth with the Glasser atlas. Consistent with this, the same atlas provided a more distinctive fingerprint when applied to vertex-derived maps than when used to construct a parcel-level connectome. Thus, vertex-wise construction preserves individual information that can subsequently be summarized at coarser scales, whereas direct parcel-level construction discards some of that information from the outset. This finer representation, however, comes with greater sensitivity to anatomical alignment. A displacement of just two millimetres substantially degraded the unsmoothed vertex fingerprint, highlighting how small registration errors can affect individual-level comparisons. This is the structural counterpart of the misalignment problem addressed by hyperalignment in functional neuroimaging (Haxby et al. 2011 (Haxby et al. https://paperpile.com/c/JCOSwmTo/Cjvq+woTx+fkwD 2020;; Feilong et al. 2018) , and connectivity-guided surface registration (Zhou et al. 2026) may push the usable scale finer. The vertex-wise connectome is best used as the measurement, and comparison between people is best made at kernel areas of a few hundred square millimetres or on parcel means of the vertex-derived maps. The fingerprint also survived the removal of each participant's cortical geometry, supporting the interpretation that it reflects individual connectivity patterns rather than simply differences in cortical folding.

The clinical analyses make the consequences of individual referencing explicit. Although the individually referenced short-to-long balance provides a reliable characterization of individual connectivity organization, its clinical associations require a qualified interpretation. Matched group contrasts using the same inference framework distinguished participants with Alzheimer's disease from cognitively normal participants after accounting for acquisition site. Associations with episodic memory, dementia severity and the memory-reserve index were spatially similar to the diagnostic contrast, but none remained significant after adjustment for diagnostic group or was detectable within either subgroup.

These associations therefore appear to reflect differences related to diagnostic status rather than variation among individuals within the same diagnostic group. The association with depressive symptoms showed a different pattern: it remained detectable after adjustment for diagnosis, although it was not significant within either subgroup and did not differ between groups. Thus, this association was not fully accounted for by diagnostic classification, although it does not establish a depression-specific effect within either group. Perceived stress, in contrast, was not associated with the measure. The dependence of these clinical effects on acquisition-site adjustment is also important. Diagnostic composition differed across ADNI sites, and site effects were sufficiently strong that no clinical associations were detectable without accounting for site. Acquisition sites should therefore be considered when applying normative models to multisite clinical datasets.

The pathology analysis extends the same logic from behaviour to biology. A composite of amyloid, p-tau217, tau-PET, neurofilament light and GFAP was associated with the individually referenced connectivity measure across the Alzheimer's continuum, and this association remained detectable after adjustment for the diagnostic group. The association was detectable within the patient group, but not within cognitively normal participants, and was robust to leaving out individual acquisition sites. These findings suggest that individual variation in connection-length organization is related to shared variation across several markers of Alzheimer's pathology, beyond differences between diagnostic groups. The five markers contributed similarly to the composite, so we cannot establish whether the connectome is preferentially sensitive to any particular component of the biomarker cascade or infer a temporal sequence. The association was weaker in the subset with plasma samples collected within two years of imaging, although uncertainty in the timing of sample collection limits the interpretation of this sensitivity analysis. Accordingly, we cannot conclude that the measure preferentially reflects downstream, structure-damaging pathology. Rather, it appears to capture individual connectivity variation associated with a broader burden of Alzheimer's pathology.

A practical implication follows from the degradation analysis. The acquisition experiments show that the quality of the individual fingerprint depends on the diffusion information available, with the transition from multishell to single-shell data producing a substantial reduction in similarity to the three-shell reference. Nevertheless, individual identification remained possible under single-shell acquisition, indicating that reduced diffusion information degrades individual-level characterization rather than necessarily eliminating identifiability. ADNI-4 itself uses a two-tier acquisition scheme, reserving the multishell Advanced protocol for multiband-capable scanners and applying a single-shell Basic protocol elsewhere. Consequently, only a subset of ADNI-4 participants acquired with the Advanced protocol supports the multishell analyses presented here, illustrating how acquisition choices can constrain the application of individual-level structural connectomics even within large imaging studies.

The position of ADNI-4 on our acquisition ladder is inferred from its protocol rather than directly measured in this cohort. These findings therefore do not establish a universal minimum acquisition protocol, but quantify the effects of reducing diffusion information under the acquisition and processing conditions tested here. Where individual-level inference is the goal, multishell sampling should be considered at the protocol-design stage, while future methods that recover tissue separability from sparser sampling could extend the approach to a wider range of datasets. Tractography settings also matter for the distance bands. The minimum streamline length alone shifted the reference cohort's tertile cut-offs from 9.5 and 20 mm to the values used in ADNI, indicating that distance thresholds should be re-derived for each dataset and processing pipeline rather than assumed to transfer directly between studies.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, vertex-wise connectomes require accurate cortical surface reconstruction and interindividual alignment, as small correspondence errors can disproportionately affect high-resolution connectivity representations. Second, the connectome remains an indirect representation of white-matter anatomy: streamline endpoints and Euclidean distances approximate cortical connectivity and connection length, while tractography is affected by gyral bias and incomplete cortical coverage. The distance bands should therefore be understood as operational categories rather than direct anatomical classifications, and may require re-estimation for different acquisition and processing pipelines. Finally, the clinical analyses are cross-sectional and rely on a limited set of cognitive and affective measures, restricting conclusions about temporal relationships and the clinical meaning of the observed associations. The framework was also evaluated using high-quality diffusion data, and its performance across datasets with different acquisition protocols and image quality remains to be established.

In conclusion, vertex-wise structural connectomics preserves individual differences in cortical organization that are lost through group averaging and atlas parcellation. Our findings demonstrate its potential for characterizing individual connectivity patterns across spatial scales and relating them to clinical and biological variation, while highlighting the importance of diffusion-data quality and accurate anatomical correspondence. By treating interindividual variability as a signal rather than noise, this framework opens new opportunities to investigate how individual differences in structural brain organization relate to cognition, affect and health.

Methods

Study design and cohorts

We aim to recover the structure that distinguishes one brain from another and to relate it to a person's clinical and affective state. Standard connectomics averages connectivity into atlas parcels and compares group means, which removes the very individually-shifted features that carry this information.

We therefore adapt the principle of precision imaginghttps://paperpile.com/c/JCOSwmTo/iZE5+W9H1 established for functional connectivity (Gordon et al. 2026 (Gordon et al. , 2017)) , to the structural connectome: every cortical surface vertex is treated as a node, and each brain is characterized on its own terms rather than pooled. The study uses two independent cohorts under a frozen-template design, so that the method is fixed before it is applied and the application cannot be tuned to its result. On the reference cohort, the BTC (Aerts et al. 2022), a high-quality multi-shell acquisition, we develop the method and establish the data quality it requires: by progressively degrading these acquisitions and reprocessing each end to end, we determine the minimum quality at which the subject-specific measures remain reliable, which lets us decide in advance whether a given clinical dataset qualifies. We then apply the fixed method to the clinical cohort, ADNI-4 (Jack et al. 2008), which meets this requirement, to recover subject-specific structure across the Alzheimer's continuum. To make this structure individual rather than relative to the patient group, we reference each brain against a normative model built from cognitively-normal participants: at every vertex we express connectivity as the balance between short-range (U-fiber-range) and long-range connections and score how far a person departs from the value expected for their age and sex, so that individual deviations from this healthy reference, not differences between group means, become the unit of analysis.

Datasets

Data used in the preparation of this article were obtained from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) database (adni.loni.usc.edu). The ADNI was launched in 2003 as a public-private partnership, led by Principal Investigator Michael W. Weiner, MD. The original goal of ADNI was to test whether serial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), positron emission tomography (PET), other biological markers, and clinical and neuropsychological assessment can be combined to measure the progression of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and early Alzheimer's disease (AD). The current goals include validating biomarkers for clinical trials, improving the generalizability of ADNI data by increasing diversity in the participant cohort, and providing data concerning the diagnosis and progression of Alzheimer's disease to the scientific community. For up-to-date information, see adni.loni.usc.edu. The present analyses used data from the fourth phase of the study (ADNI-4), downloaded in February 2026.

The Brain Tumor Connectomics pre-operative dataset (BTC) is an openly available multimodal 3 T MRI dataset on OpenNeuro that pairs high-quality, multi-shell diffusion imaging with structural and functional acquisitions in pre-operative brain-tumour patients and healthy control subjects (Aerts et al. 2022 (Aerts et al. , 2018)). The group structural template, the connectivity-gradient alignment basis, and the reliability (acquisition-degradation) analyses were derived from the 10 healthy controls. The clinical cohort was drawn from the ADNI, a multi-site study of older adults that spans cognitively normal, mild cognitive impairment, and AD.

Image acquisition and preprocessing

For both cohorts, the raw inputs were T1-weighted anatomical and diffusion-weighted (DWI) acquisitions; detailed information about both MRI data acquisition sequences can be found in the supplementary materials S6. The T1-weighted image was processed with FreeSurfer 8.2.0 (Fischl 2012) to reconstruct the cortical surfaces, which served as the structural reference onto which connectivity was later projected. The DWI data was preprocessed using the MRtrix3 (Tournier et al. 2019) toolbox. Fiber orientation distributions were estimated by multi-shell multi-tissue constrained spherical deconvolution (MSMT-CSD; (Jeurissen et al. 2014) https://paperpile.com/c/JCOSwmTo/VgqB and anatomically-constrained tractography (ACT; (Smith et al. 2012) was performed with second-order integration over fiber orientation distributions (iFOD2). The five-tissue-type segmentation required by ACT was generated from the FreeSurfer 8.2.0 reconstruction.

For each subject, a whole-brain tractogram was generated and weighted by SIFT2 (Smith et al. 2015) In ADNI-4, 10 million streamlines were generated using dynamic seeding, with backtracking and cropping at the grey-white matter interface, and a minimum streamline length of 10 mm. In BTC, 5 million streamlines were generated by seeding from the grey-white matter interface, with a minimum streamline length of 5 mm. Both tractograms were weighted using SIFT2, allowing streamline contributions to be quantitatively weighted when constructing the connectome.

Vertex-wise structural connectome

For each subject, a vertex-wise structural connectome was built in three stages: (i) registration, (ii) surface projection, and (iii) endpoint-to-vertex aggregation.

First, for each participant, the T1-weighted volume was registered to the subject's DWI space using a hybrid procedure. A boundary-based registration (BBR) was computed using FSL (Jenkinson et al. 2012) https://paperpile.com/c/JCOSwmTo/Bz7e and mutual-information FLIRT registration was computed as an independent candidate. Then, we selected the transform that minimized the median distance between streamline endpoints and the cortical surface, thereby choosing the registration that best aligned tractography with anatomy on a per-subject basis.

Second, the FreeSurfer white-matter surfaces of both hemispheres were projected into DWI space using the selected transform. Vertex-wise parcel labels were taken from the surface from the Desikan-Killiany atlas (Desikan et al. 2006) https://paperpile.com/c/JCOSwmTo/UidU which is registration-independent. The resulting surfaces comprised approximately 244k to 291k vertices per subject. The vertex count varies across subjects because these are each subject's native FreeSurfer tessellation rather than a resampled common mesh; cross-subject correspondence is not assumed at this stage but is established downstream by projecting each subject onto the shared fsaverage template in the next steps.

Network Hierarchy

The connection-distance bands were derived from the cognitively normal cohort. For each participant, we sampled 3 million connectome edges with replacement and computed the Euclidean distance between their endpoints on the native white-matter surface, using this distance as a lower bound on fibre length (Roberts et al. 2016; Betzel et al. 2016). Each distance was weighted by the corresponding SIFT2 streamline strength. The resulting weighted distance distributions were binned at 1-mm intervals, normalized to unit sum for each participant, and averaged across participants with equal weight. The cohort-average distribution was then partitioned into weighted tertiles to define short-, medium-, and long-range connection bands.

To characterize the scale of cortical organization, each subject's connectome was thresholded to its top 10 % of edges by weight and partitioned with the Leiden algorithm under a modularity objective (igraph implementation; (Traag et al. 2019) across a sweep of resolution parameters (γ = .05, .1, .2, .5, 1, 2 and 5; two optimization iterations; modules smaller than 100 vertices removed). Ordering the resulting partitions from coarse to fine produced a nested cascade, the nesting of which was quantified as containment, the mean over finer modules of the largest fraction of a module falling within a single coarser module.

Each level was then summarized with SIFT2-weighted measures computed over a sample of (10 6 ) edges: the mean within-module connection length, the module spatial compactness (the size-weighted mean radius of gyration of each module's vertex coordinates on the inflated surface), the segregation (the fraction of total connectivity contained within modules), and the endpoint-mean fractional anisotropy and cortical thickness along within-module connections. We also quantified the fraction of sampled connections falling within modules. A subject was flagged as a metric outlier when its mean within-module length had an absolute cohort-level z-score greater than 3.5.

Subject-specific measures

Before applying the method to ADNI, we established the acquisition requirements for recovering subject-specific structure by degrading the high-quality BTC acquisitions from 10 healthy controls along two axes and reprocessing each degraded dataset end-to-end, from response-function estimation through constrained spherical deconvolution, tractography and SIFT2. One axis reduced the streamline count, using random subsamples ranging from 100,000 to 2 million streamlines from each 5-million-streamline tractogram (three random subsamples per condition). The other reduced diffusion quality by dropping shells and angular directions, from the full multishell acquisition to two shells or a single shell with either 30 or 15 directions. For each degraded dataset, we computed differential identifiability, defined as the difference between the mean similarity of each participant's degraded fingerprint to their own full-data reference and the mean similarity to the full-data references of other participants. Fingerprints were based on short-range (< 14.5 mm) connectivity strength aggregated to the Glasser-360 parcellation (Glasser et al., 2016). We also assessed identification accuracy and estimated 95% confidence intervals using 10,000 participant-level bootstrap samples.

The reliability of the hierarchy within a subject was measured by splitting each connectome into two disjoint edge sets, partitioning each independently across the resolution sweep, and computing the adjusted mutual information (AMI) between the two partitions at every level. Between-subject similarity was measured by computing the AMI between partitions from different subjects after projection to the fsaverage5 surface. All 23,436 unique between-subject pairs were evaluated in both comparison orders.

Identifiability was quantified as the difference between within-and between-subject AMI, and identification accuracy was assessed by determining whether each partition was most similar to the other partition from the same subject.

A vertex-wise individual-dominance map, defined as one minus group typicality, was computed at γ = .5 to localise where subjects diverged most from the group. We further quantified band-restricted identifiability by building a vertex-wise connectivity-strength map for each band, splitting the edges into two disjoint halves, and projecting each half to fsaverage5. Fingerprints were compared at multiple spatial scales: at the native vertex level, after k-nearest-neighbour smoothing (k = 6, 20, 50, 150 and 400), and after aggregation to the Glasser-360 and Desikan-Killiany parcellations. For each scale, identifiability was calculated as the difference between within-subject and between-subject fingerprint correlations. The analyses were also repeated after regressing cortical coverage from each feature.

Vertex-level reliability was additionally assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC(1)) between the two split-half connectivity-strength maps across participants. The spatial distribution of reliability was compared across Mesulam cortical classes using spin tests (1,000 rotations per hemisphere; (Alexander-Bloch et al. 2018).

Individual routing was assessed by tracing shortest paths between predefined anchor parcels on each subject's connectome and counting the intermediate nodes on each route at three spatial resolutions ; routing signatures were then compared within subjects, between subjects, and against a group-average connectome. To test whether the standard precision-imaging partition reflects individual connectivity or cortical geometry, we compared each subject's Infomap consensus parcellation (Rosvall and Bergstrom 2008) with a geometry-only k-means parcellation of the cortical surface coordinates with the same number of modules, quantifying their agreement by AMI together with the spatial compactness of the Infomap modules.

For the clinical analyses, connectivity at each vertex was expressed as the fraction of its total SIFT2 strength carried by each band, smoothed on the fsaverage5 surface by k-nearest-neighbour averaging (k = 150). A cognitively normal normative model, in which the short-band fraction was modeled as a function of cortical coverage, age and sex, was fitted at each vertex using cognitively normal participants. Each participant's departure from the expected value was expressed as a deviation z-score, with leave-one-out scoring used for the cognitively normal participants. This individually referenced, fraction-based deviation was the measure used throughout the clinical analyses. The log-ratio of short-to long-range connectivity was examined as a sensitivity measure.

Group-specific measures Group-level organization was assessed in two ways. To test whether the disease produces a shared spatial pattern, we computed the signed group-mean deviation map and the case-control contrasts (AD versus cognitively normal, and all patients versus cognitively normal), together with a vertex-wise cancellation index defined as the ratio of the signed group mean to the mean absolute deviation, where zero indicates a purely individual and one a fully shared pattern. To relate the individual deviations to phenotype, we fitted vertex-wise models across subjects testing the association between each participant's deviation and episodic memory, a memory-reserve index, dementia severity (CDR-Sum of Boxes), depression (Geriatric Depression Scale), and perceived stress (PSS-10). These associations were examined using pooled models, models additionally adjusting for diagnostic group, and separate models within the patient and cognitively normal groups. For depression, we additionally tested the interaction between GDS scores and diagnostic group. The reciprocity of the redistribution was assessed as the spatial correlation between the short-and long-band association maps, with the medium band as a null comparator.
