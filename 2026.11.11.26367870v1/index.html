How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

How Effect Sizes in t-tests and F-tests works

The Effect Size module checks if effect sizes are correctly reported in t-tests and F-tests.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Cervical cancer is the leading cause of cancer death in women in 26 countries, including in sub-Saharan African countries. Cervical and other cancer risks can be greatly reduced through timely HPV vaccination and early screening. But it continues to pose a significant public health challenge in LMICs such as

Nigeria. This study reports on the implementation and evaluation of a pair of social media behavior change campaigns to increase HPV vaccination amongst girls ages 9-14 and reduce vaccine hesitancy amongst girls and their parents in Bauchi state, Nigeria.

The primary objectives are to conduct a quasiexperimental evaluation of the social media campaign; to examine the effect of higher versus lower levels of campaign exposure on HPV vaccination and related outcomes; to determine whether the social media campaign followed its planned theory of change; and to identify which social media messages were most effective in promoting HPV vaccination.

We implemented an HPV vaccination social media promotion campaign, using videos designed to encourage parents to vaccinate their daughters, at scale in Bauchi state on the Meta and TikTok social media platforms. We used a quasi-experimental design, a heavy-up approach, that experimentally manipulated the level of social media content delivered in Bauchi. We used the Virtual Lab platform, an open-source platform that uses social media to recruit participants using advertising and creates panels of respondents for data collection through an online survey, and also collected social media metrics including impressions and engagement. The campaign was effective in increasing HPV vaccination rates from 69% at baseline to 86% at endline over 2 campaign phases that spanned approximately 3 months.

Based on GEE modeling, we found that higher levels of engagement with the campaign were associated with higher vaccination rates at midline and endline. Pro-vaccine attitudes and reduced vaccine hesitancy also predicted higher vaccination rates. Also, the campaign produced additional HPV vaccinations at low cost: Adjusted for campaign exposure cost of $0.81 per additional vaccination in campaign phase 1, $2.10 per vax in phase 2, and $2.40 for the total campaign period.

INTRODUCTION

The World Health Organization (WHO) reports that cervical cancer is the fifth most commonly diagnosed cancer in women, with 604,000 new cases estimated in 2024, and that it is the leading cause of cancer death in women in 26 countries, including in sub-Saharan Africa. 1 Lack of systemic Human Papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination and screening are a significant contributor to the burden of cancer among women in low and middle income countries (LMIC) generally, and sub-Saharan Africa in particular, 2 unlike in many developed countries. A report by the WHO ranks Nigeria fifth globally in cervical cancer deaths, with estimates of 12,000 new cases and 8,000 deaths annually. 3 Cervical and other cancer risks can be greatly reduced through timely HPV vaccination and early screening. But it continues to pose a significant public health challenge in LMICs such as Nigeria. 4 Risk factors include lack of knowledge about the benefits of HPV vaccination, concerns about safety and efficacy of the vaccine, and mis-and disinformation about health risks. 5 Interventions to promote acceptance among parents and adolescent girls and young women, and to increase motivation, opportunity, and ability to receive the HPV vaccine are needed. 6, 7 Social media has been studied as a potentially promising approach to promote HPV vaccination and reduce vaccine hesitancy. 8 Social media content can potentially promote the benefits of vaccination, attempt to reduce barriers (such as myths and misinformation about health risks), and increase acceptance and intentions to vaccinate. 9 Social media content can use strategies such as influential community members, 10 entertainment education, 11 and raise awareness about opportunities and ability to vaccinate. 12 However, there have been relatively few studies that have rigorously evaluated the efficacy of social media to promote HPV vaccination. 13 More research using experimental and quasiexperimental methods are needed in this area.

Nigeria has increased efforts in recent years to increase multiple forms of community health promotion and healthcare, including vaccination, in recent years. 14 These include promotion efforts such as through Maternal, Newborn, and Child Health Week (MNCH) efforts led by the Ministry of Health (MoH). 15 One potential opportunity to increase HPV vaccination is to promote MNCH week participation, and to encourage parents to bring their adolescent girls to receive the HPV vaccine at these events. Social media may be an effective strategy to achieve this goal.

Social Media Campaign to Promote HPV Vaccination in Bauchi State Nigeria

This study reports on the implementation and evaluation of a pair of social media behavior change campaigns to increase HPV vaccination amongst girls ages 9-14 and reduce vaccine hesitancy amongst girls and their parents in Bauchi state, Nigeria. Bauchi is the fifth largest state in the country, located in the northern and central part of Nigeria, with a population of over 8.3 million in 2025. 16 This evaluation study, funded by the Gates Foundation, has the overall objective of identifying which campaigns and strategies are effective in increasing HPV vaccination rates and reducing vaccine hesitancy, and at what level of cost-effectiveness.

The current study analyzed a theory of change (ToC) co-developed with the campaign implementation team in order to understand the processes and effects of social media in influencing HPV vaccination and vaccine hesitancy. 17 The ToC posited that social media could influence drivers of vaccine hesitancy, according to the 5 Cs approach, by promoting motivation, opportunity, and ability to vaccinate, and by promoting pro-vaccination social norms. 18, 19, 20 The Gates Foundation's primary goal for the HPV vaccine is to achieve the WHO target of eliminating cervical cancer by ensuring 90% of girls worldwide are vaccinated against HPV by age 15. 21 The foundation supports this by funding initiatives with multi-lateral organizations and major nongovernmental organizations (NGOs) to increase vaccine access, support research into single-dose vaccines and new HPV vaccines, and partner with governments and organizations to scale up vaccination programs in low-and middle-income countries. There is a specific focus on increasing HPV vaccination rates, and reducing vaccine hesitancy, among 9-14 year-old girls in priority states in Nigeria, including Bauchi state. As part of this initiative, our research team sought to evaluate the efficacy of social media to increase HPV vaccination rates and reduce vaccine hesitancy in Bauchi state.

Specific objectives of the study

The primary objectives to be evaluated in this study are: 1) To conduct a quasi-experimental evaluation of a social media campaign to encourage parents to attend the MNCH week community events in Bauchi state and have their 9-14 year old girls vaccinated for HPV; 2) To examine the effect of higher versus lower levels of campaign exposure on HPV vaccination and related outcomes including social norms and vaccine hesitancy; 3) Determine whether the social media campaign followed its planned theory of change; 4) Identify which social media messages were most effective in promoting vaccination at the MNCH week events and in reducing vaccine hesitancy.

METHODS

Study Design

This study employed a quasi-experimental design in which we compared the effects of higher versus lower levels of paid advertising (boosted) social media campaign activity on HPV vaccination outcomes. This approach, known as a "heavy-up" design in advertising research, has been widely used, including studies in health promotion and public health. 22, 23

Ethical consideration

This research, including procedures, instruments, and informed consent form, was reviewed and Participants were contacted through the Meta and TikTok social media platforms through a paid advertising campaign. Interested participants clicked on a link and were taken to an informed consent form that provided details on the study procedures, delivered through the Facebook Messenger app. By clicking on an "accept" link after reading the consent, they were entered into a screening protocol for the study. Participants who were parents of girls age 9-14 and lived in Bauchi state were eligible and offered the survey.

Data collection began on 22 May 2026. Over the following 4 weeks prior to MNCH week, in May and

June 2026, we collected survey data from a baseline sample of parents of girls ages 9-14 in Bauchi. Then we ran the initial social media campaign (phase 1) at a defined level of boosted campaign activity for approximately 4 weeks. Following this campaign, MNCH week promoting HPV vaccination occurred in early July 2026. We then collected a 2 nd round of survey data (midline) from the same parents. Then we ran the campaign at approximately 3 times the level of boosted activity (3 times the advertising spending to generate exposure to content), which included the MNCH week events. After the end of MNCH week, we collected midline data from the same sample of parents. We then ran the phase 2 campaign to promote vaccination at other events and activities in Bauchi state in July and August 2026.

The endline survey was conducted immediately after the end of phase 2 in August 2026.

Intervention

The Bauchi Ministry of Health and local partners, in collaboration with our investigative team, developed social media content in English and Hausa. Content focused on the benefits of HPV vaccination, and used, in different social media videos, techniques including story telling (narratives), humor, and music.

Attendance at MNCH and opportunities to vaccinate were promoted. Trusted voices from across Bauchi State were featured in the social media. Health officials, imams, doctors, mothers, local journalists, poets and comedians all carried the same message: Bring your daughter for a free HPV vaccine. Inperson outreach events like MNCH Week then turned that intent into action, with free vaccines, free medical care and giveaways at health facilities across the state.

We used a social media campaign management and data collection platform called Virtual Lab to support study implementation. Virtual Lab was managed by the study team and used to recruit a panel of adult parent participants living in the Bauchi. The panel was recruited using Meta platform (Facebook and Instagram) paid posts using targeting persona-specific campaigns to survey respondents via remarketing and measure impacts. We collected a baseline sample of 2,206 individual Bauchi parents who met inclusion criteria.

Measures

An online, self-administered survey delivered via the Virtual Lab platform was used for data collection in each identified state. The survey was designed for completion using Facebook Messenger software (chat feature in which each individual chat contains a survey question), and required approximately 15 minutes to complete at each time point. We offered participants incentives of 1500 Naira in mobile phone credit per completed survey to maximize completion rates.

Evaluation measures included: demographics (e.g., age, gender, education, work role, income); psychographics (e.g., psychological traits, values, lifestyle variables); vaccination social norms; related vaccine/vaccination beliefs; vaccination history; acceptance/uptake of HPV vaccination; use of social media; exposure to vaccine-specific information (on social media and other sources). Measures were derived from previous studies by the investigators, and published in the peer-reviewed literature. 18, 20

Cost-effectiveness evaluation

Behavior change interventions that rely on social networks for their success are hypothesized to have greater impact than interventions that rely on changes in individual behavior. 24 Thus, in addition to the effect of treatment-on-the-treated, social network researchers have measured the effect of treatment on the untreated. Researchers who have evaluated door-to-door campaigns to get out the vote, for example, have measured a smaller, discernable effect of such campaigns on the untreated (i.e., effects on members of the household who are not directly exposed to the canvasser). This additive effect may be due to effects on social norms and other social influences (e.g., based on observation of those who receive treatment by those who do not). This has implications for cost-effectiveness of social network interventions. This evaluation examined these potential effects.

The cost to implement a social media campaign to reduce vaccine hesitancy is a key input for future decision-makers considering broader implementation of the strategy following this effectiveness trial.

The goal of the cost-effectiveness analysis component of the evaluation was to quantify the societal costs of implementing the social media campaign and to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of the intervention compared to the current standard of care.

We used a micro-costing approach to evaluate resources utilized for all program-related activities using a standard three-stage process of resource identification, measurement, and valuation. 25 The development of a model-based evaluation is beyond the scope of this project; we used a time horizon for the analysis consistent with the intervention time period. The intention-to-treat population was used for the primary cost-effectiveness analysis of the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) for the cost per HCP vaccinated at follow-up. We used bootstrap sampling with replacement from intervention and control groups to estimate 95% uncertainty intervals around ICERs. 26

Sample size determination

We conducted an individual-level power analysis using HPV vaccination rate as the outcome of interest.

Based on this analysis, we concluded that the total sample size required to achieve statistical power of 0.8 for testing the null hypothesis of no difference between the high exposure and low exposure study conditions in self-reported HPV vaccination at a given point in time (follow-up) using a two-sided test resulted in a required sample size of 2,000. Our final collected baseline sample was 2,206, exceeding power requirements.

Sampling strategy

The sample was drawn using the Virtual Lab procedures described below. We sampled from Facebook users who meet eligibility criteria of being a parent with a girl age 9-14 living at home in Bauchi state.

Data collection procedure

Data collection was conducted by Virtual Lab. The Virtual Lab is an open-source platform that uses social media such as Facebook and Instagram to recruit participants using advertising and creates panels of respondents for data collection. The data collection procedures may be summarized as follows.

All data in flight is encrypted via TLS and all data at rest is encrypted on encrypted block storage provided by Google Cloud Services. Data downloaded by researchers from the web portal is encrypted via TLS to their local machines at which point they must store all data which might contain personal information on encrypted disks. Respondents can request to see or delete their data at any time by sending an email to an address which is provided to them. Data deletion requests are handled within 30 days and fully removed from data archives in 60 days after the handling of the deletion request.

Participants were recruited via an ad that is shown both on Facebook and Instagram. The ad advertised that we want them to take a survey and also explains that they will receive a mobile credit as compensation for their participation.

Ads were targeted to potential respondents via Meta (Facebook and Instagram) targeting demographics including the geographic region in which they live, the industry in which they work (eg, healthcare), their job title, their education, their interests, their age, and their gender. This targeting was used in the study to ensure representativeness across the desired variables.

On clicking the ad, respondents were directed to a chat window within Facebook Messenger (FM). This chat window had an initial message which explains again that we would like them to participate in a study and that they will receive mobile credit for participating. They had the option to click "start" in the chat to start the survey.

Once the respondent clicked "start", Meta sent the Virtual Lab platform a notification that a user with a page-scoped user ID (account) number had started a chat. This ID was used to identify the user within the Virtual Lab platform and used to communicate with Facebook. The ID was usable for tracking all user interaction within the chat window and with the page more broadly, but not usable to track user interaction with any other page (thus, it is "page-scoped").

The page-scoped user id was used for "remarketing", by allowing "custom audiences" of 100+ ID's to be created and used for ad targeting. We used remarketing to test the impact of ad campaigns on "treated" groups of individuals.

For follow-up data collection, participants were sent a message through FM indicating that a new survey was available. Repeat messages were sent for 7 days until the respondent either completed the survey, or the participant was deemed lost to follow-up.

Data Analysis

We used both the survey data and quantitative social media metrics (reach, engagement) data drawn from the Meta platform, capturing views and interactions (likes, comments, shares) with the campaign HPV vaccination posts. Social media metrics characterize prevention message dissemination, reach, and network engagement, and provide an exogenous measure of participant exposure to content. Our analysis aimed to isolate the effects of exposure on vaccination and related attitudes and beliefs. SPSS version 31 was used for all analyses. 27 Based on baseline data, we conducted reliability analyses for all potential scales. In addition, also using baseline data we used confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to assess the dimensionality and construct validity of the various scales. The CFA used principal component analysis, varimax rotation and eigen value of one was the threshold to retain a factor. CFA yielded a four-factor solutions. Thus for the analyses of the campaign effects, only the participants who completed all three waves of data collection (Baseline, Midline and Endline) were included. The final total number participants was 546. Univariate repeated measures analyses were conducted as manipulation checks for the effects of campaign phase on attitude measures, self-reported exposure and vaccine uptake.

The campaign theory of change posits that engagement in the campaign predicts intervention effects on vaccination intentions and uptake. DISCUSSION

In this project, we implemented an HPV vaccination social media promotion campaign at scale in Bauchi state, Nigeria. The combined phase 1 and 2 campaigns reached over 1.1 million unique Meta accounts, generated more than 12 million 3-second views, and generated more than 3.1 million full views. Selfreported exposure, as expected, increased as the 2 campaigns were implemented, from some 69% at baseline (likely due to existing online content related to the HPV vaccine), to nearly 81% at endline. This suggests that the campaigns not only reached large numbers of people, but that there was increasing awareness and recall of the HPV vaccination promotion messages.

Quasi-experimental evaluation findings, based on the heavy-up design, 22 suggest that the campaign was effective in increasing HPV vaccination rates based on a quasi-experimental evaluation. Specifically, in a longitudinal sample, there was an increase in vaccination rates associated with higher levels of engagement with the campaigns. Engagement was measured through social media metrics, and there were over 825,000 individual engagements (eg, likes, shares, comments) with posts across the phase 1 and 2 campaigns. However, we did not see a relationship between self-reported recall of campaign posts and higher vaccination rates.

Overall, in our sample, vaccination rates increased from 69% at baseline to 83% and midline and 86% at endline. We also found that the pro-vaccine attitudes factor, derived from factor analysis of a series of items that asked about and reduced vaccine hesitancy also was associated with higher vaccination rates.

The relationship between positive vaccinated-related attitudes and subsequent vaccination has been examined in recent studies, with mixed results. 29 Researchers have found an attitude-uptake gap in regard to HPV vaccination. 30 Some studies, like ours, have found that substantial increases in provaccine attitudes may be associated with increases in HPV vaccine uptake. 31 But given variable results in such studies, there is no clear evidence base on the role of pro-vaccine attitudes, and related beliefs and intentions, in promoting uptake. This area, and how innovative methods such as social media campaigns to promote pro-vaccine attitudes and intentions, calls for more rigorous interventions and research.

Furthermore, we found that the campaign produced additional HPV vaccinations at low cost: Adjusted for campaign exposure cost of $0.81 per additional vax in campaign phase 1, $2.10 per vax in phase 2, and $2.40 for the combined campaigns. There is limited evidence on the effectiveness of social media campaigns to promote vaccination generally, and HPV vaccination specifically. 32 However, the present study adds to previous research in LMICs that suggests social media is a promising strategy to increase vaccine uptake. More rigorous interventions, combined with cost effectiveness analysis, should be conducted to build the evidence base in this area.

Overall, evidence from this study suggests that social media may be an effective strategy to promote HPV vaccination, and that it is cost effective compared to alternatives. More research is needed on the mechanisms and determinants of HPV vaccine uptake, including the role of psychosocial variables such as vaccine hesitancy, pro-vaccine attitudes, and social norms. 33, 34 Additionally, rigorous study designs, including randomized controlled trials, and dose-response experiments of social media campaigns, are needed to help interpret campaign impact on HPV vaccine uptake and behavioral determinants. 35 Such studies are also needed to build the evidence and allow for valid comparisons between studies on social media vaccination cost effectiveness analysis.

Overall, this research suggests that future interventions should utilize social media to promote vaccination, and that the types of campaign strategies used in this study may be worth using or adapting. These include use of storytelling narratives, humor, and messages delivered by trusted and influential voices (social influencers). While much more evidence is needed on these techniques, other studies have found them to be promising ways to frame health messages, and promote behavior change. 36 Despite the evidence provided by this study, it has some important limitations. First, we experienced high attrition of approximately 75%. While attrition is often high in social media-based studies, this level of attrition means that our findings should be judged with caution. The evidence from this study, while promising, requires corroboration in trials with higher retention. Second, there were other social influences and media messages both pro-vaccination, and misinformation, related HPV during the time of our study. Efforts were ongoing in Bauchi, and across Nigeria, to promote HPV vaccine uptake, and also unmeasured negative influences. These may present important confounds to our study, and future research should examine their potential impact. Third, this was not a randomized study, so while the heavy-up design provides a mechanism for causal inference, it has limitations. For example, attention to and engagement with the campaign may be attributable to other factors that would influence the likelihood of HPV vaccine uptake. Future studies should use randomized designs. Finally, the nature of Meta's social media metrics prevents individual level attribution of campaign exposure to outcomes. We have data on the exposure in the population, and can relate those metrics to our survey data, but not to individual study participants.

CONCLUSION

Social media campaigns show promise for HPV vaccination promotion. There is a small, but growing body of evidence that social media can increase vaccination rates, and that higher levels of exposure to and engagement with such campaigns is associated with vaccine uptake. These campaigns show evidence of producing high vaccine uptake at low cost. Future research should use rigorous designs and examine specific factors (eg, the role of pro-vaccine attitudes and social norms) that may predict uptake.
