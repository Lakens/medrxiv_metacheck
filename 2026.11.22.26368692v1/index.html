To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 3 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Introduction

Brain banks are an important resource for investigating the pathological basis of neurodegenerative diseases. As diagnostic criteria evolve, retrospective assessment of archival brain tissue offers opportunities to identify pathological changes that may not have been evaluated during the original examination. Chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE), for which consensus neuropathological diagnostic criteria were established relatively recently, represents a particularly relevant example [1, 2]. Reassessment of existing brain banks for CTE is of particular interest given the prevalence of Alzheimer's disease (AD) pathology in aging populations and the challenges associated with distinguishing these two diseases. AD and CTE can occur independently or coexist, and their shared involvement of hyperphosphorylated tau pathology can complicate disease-specific interpretation, particularly in advanced or mixed-pathology cases [3, 4, 5]. Computational tools that facilitate the characterization of such complex neuropathological profiles could support retrospective brain bank research and assist pathologists in the evaluation of overlapping disease processes.

A practical challenge in retrospective neuropathological research is the availability of the histological information needed for disease characterization. Additional sectioning or staining of archival brain tissue may be constrained by tissue availability and preservation, particularly years after the original examination [6, 7, 8, 9]. Furthermore, differences in tissue sampling and staining protocols across brain banks can result in systematic differences in the brain regions and histological preparations available for analysis [10]. These challenges are relevant to the study of neurodegenerative diseases, for which pathological assessment relies on multiple complementary histological measurements. In AD and CTE, hyperphosphorylated tau pathology, commonly visualized using antibodies such as AT8, is central to CTE diagnosis and contributes to AD staging [11, 1]; amyloid-β (Aβ) immunohistochemistry reveals amyloid plaque pathology [12]; and Luxol fast blue combined with hematoxylin and eosin (LHE) provides structural and myelin context. While these stains highlight distinct pathological and morphological features, they may also contain partially overlapping infor-2 mation relevant to disease characterization. Computational approaches capable of leveraging this complementarity without requiring uniform stain availability could facilitate the use of heterogeneous brain bank resources.

Whole slide imaging (WSI) has enabled high-resolution digitization of histological preparations and the development of computational approaches ranging from multiple instance learning to graph-and transformer-based aggregation [13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. However, most computational neuropathology pipelines remain stain-specific and focused on individual diseases or lesion types [20], while multimodal approaches often assume complete modality availability during training and inference [21, 22, 23, 24]. Existing strategies for missing-modality learning, including imputation, generative synthesis and modality-specific cascades, have been investigated primarily in radiological imaging [25, 26], with limited exploration in multi-stain WSI analysis. An important question is whether computational models can effectively integrate complementary information across histological stains and retain discriminative information when individual stains are unavailable.

This study addresses this question using AD and CTE classification as a proof-of-principle application within a single brain region. Although comprehensive neuropathological assessment of AD and CTE involves the evaluation of multiple brain regions [27, 1], restricting the anatomical scope provides a setting in which to investigate cross-stain information integration. A multimodal deep learning model was developed to classify cases into four mutually exclusive categories: AD, CTE, comorbid AD and CTE, and neither. Building on the sharedspecific feature-modeling paradigm for missing-modality learning [28], the model combines stain-specific transformer encoders with a shared encoder aligned through cross-modal knowledge distillation and employs random modality masking during training to accommodate absent stains at inference. The framework was developed and evaluated on 1, 607 WSIs from 602 donors spanning three neuropathology cohorts: the Understanding Neurologic Injury and Traumatic Encephalopathy (UNITE) brain bank, the Framingham Heart Study (FHS), and the Boston University Alzheimer's Disease Research Center (ADRC), each contributing AT8-, Aβ-, and LHE-stained sections of the dorsolateral frontal cortex, a region heavily affected in CTE and routinely sampled for neuropathological assessment of AD [3, 27]. To evaluate the benefits of multimodal integration and its robustness to missing stains, three overlapping study groups were defined, each using a different stain (AT8, Aβ, or LHE) as its defining stain. Within each group, a unimodal model using only the defining stain was compared with a multimodal model incorporating all available stains from the same cases. The multimodal model was further evaluated with the defining stain withheld to assess the extent to which predictive information could be retained through the remaining stains. This design enables matched comparisons across different single-stain baselines without privileging any particular stain as a universally required input. Associations between model-predicted disease probabilities and neuropathological staging measures for AD and CTE, as well as the stability of spatial attribution under defining-stain masking, were additionally evaluated to characterize model behavior beyond classification performance. Together, these investigations establish a basis for future computational neuropathology studies integrating histological information across multiple brain regions and brain banks with heterogeneous sampling and staining protocols.

Materials and methods

Study population

Whole slide images (WSIs) obtained from the dorsolateral frontal cortex region were analyzed from three brain banks focusing on AD and CTE pathology (Figure 1A): the Understanding Neurologic Injury and Traumatic Encephalopathy (UNITE), the Framingham Heart Study (FHS), and Boston University Alzheimer's Disease Research Center (ADRC). The dorsolateral frontal cortex was selected on the basis of its involvement as one of the earliest and most severely affected cortical regions in CTE and its inclusion among the standard sampling regions for neuropathological assessment of AD [3, 27]. The three datasets comprised WSIs derived from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) brain tissue samples, with staining protocols including AT8 for hyperphosphorylated tau, Aβ for amyloid-beta (Aβ) plaques, and Luxol-fast blue combined with hematoxylin and eosin (LHE) for general tissue morphology. The Boston University ADRC longitudinally tracks older research participants across the cognitive spectrum, from normal cognition to dementia, and includes diagnostic slides digitized at 20× magnification [29, 30]. The FHS employs identical staining and digitization protocols as the ADRC and is a multi-generational, community-based longitudinal cohort affiliated with the same brain bank [31]. The UNITE Brain Bank serves as a repository for individuals with histories of repetitive head impacts, such as from contact sports or military service [32]. The UNITE samples included in this study were selected with an emphasis on high-stage CTE to facilitate comparative histopathological analysis of AD and CTE. Intake and processing protocols for these cohorts were standardized across all brain banks, with neuropathology assessments performed by board-certified neuropathologists using the NIA-Reagan criteria for AD [33], and the second NINDS/NIBIB consensus criteria for CTE [1].

Both 20× and 40× stained WSIs were included. Cases were included if they had at least two different stains out of AT8, Aβ, and LHE, and no neurodegenerative pathologies other than AD and/or CTE. Other neurodegenerative pathologies were excluded to minimize potential confounding from overlapping histopathological features. This yielded a total of 602 cases (UNITE: n = 418, FHS: n = 83, ADRC: n = 101) and 1, 607 WSIs (AT8: n = 512, Aβ: n = 519, LHE: n = 576). Stain availability varied across cases, resulting in heterogeneous combinations of histological stains within the study population. Summary statistics and clinical features are reported in Table 1.

Diagnostic criteria

Neuropathological diagnoses of AD and CTE followed consensus criteria. AD pathology was assessed using the National Institute on Aging-Reagan criteria [33], which combine Braak staging of neurofibrillary tangle topography [11] with CERAD scoring of neuritic plaque density [34] to estimate the likelihood that AD pathology accounts for dementia. In this study, AD neuropathology was categorized into four Reagan categories (none, low, intermediate, and high). CTE was diagnosed according to the second NINDS/NIBIB consensus criteria [1], which require a characteristic perivascular accumulation of hyperphosphorylated tau in neurons, with or without glial involvement, at the depths of cortical sulci. CTE neuropathology was assessed using the McKee staging scheme, which defines four stages (I-IV) based on the extent and anatomical distribution of tau pathology [3]. Stages I-II and III-IV broadly correspond to low and high CTE staging, respectively, as described in the second NINDS/NIBIB consensus recommendations [1]. Cases meeting criteria for both AD and CTE were adjudicated as comorbid (CTE+AD). All diagnoses were rendered by boardcertified neuropathologists blinded to clinical data and brain bank membership, following the standardized intake and staging protocols shared across UNITE, FHS and ADRC.

WSI processing

An existing image segmentation pipeline was used on LHE WSIs to isolate tissue from background and artifacts [35]. For AT8-and Aβ-stained WSIs, dedicated tissue segmentation models were trained per stain using available gray-matter region annotations. Gray matter annotations were matched to corresponding WSIs, yielding 252 AT8 and 218 Aβ annotation-WSI pairs, split case-wise into training and hold-out test sets at a 4:1 ratio (seed 42), producing 203/49 cases for AT8 and 175/43 for Aβ. WSIs were downsampled to 10× magnification and annotation polygons were rasterized as binary tissue masks at the same resolution. Tiles of 512×512 pixels were extracted with stride 512 and a minimum tissue fraction of 0.15. A U-Net [36] with a ResNet-18 encoder pretrained on ImageNet was trained for binary tissue-versus-background segmentation. Training used a combined binary crossentropy and Dice loss, AdamW optimizer (learning rate 0.0001, one-cycle schedule), and early stopping with patience of 10 on the Dice score on the validation set. Segmentation performance is reported in Supplemental Figure S1. For study population cases with existing gray matter annotations (324 AT8 and 279 Aβ slides), annotations were used directly as tissue masks. For the remaining cases without annotations (188 AT8 and 240 Aβ slides), trained UNet models were applied to generate tissue masks.

Following tissue segmentation, tiles were extracted without overlap at 512×512 pixels for 20× WSIs and 1024×1024 pixels for 40× WSIs. Each tile was resized to 448×448 pixels and passed through a patch encoder pretrained on over 350k diverse H&E and IHC slides [37], producing a 1536-dimensional embedding f ∈ R 1×1536 used as input to the downstream model.

Modeling framework

The multimodal model was built upon the shared-specific representation learning framework introduced in ShaSpec [28]. It consists of a multimodal backbone that integrates information across stains and a linear classification head that predicts probabilities of each of four mutually exclusive diagnostic categories: Control, AD, CTE, or comorbid CTE+AD (Figure 1C).

The backbone comprises three modality-specific transformer encoders, one for each stain (AT8, Aβ, and LHE), and a shared transformer encoder with weights tied across stains. All encoders follow the TransMIL architecture [18], consisting of two Nyströmformer attention layers with pyramidal positional encoding between them. This architecture was selected for its effectiveness in WSI multiple-instance learning and its computationally efficient attention mechanism for processing large numbers of image patches.

For each available stain, WSI patch embeddings were projected into the transformer input space through a linear layer and processed with a custom input-dependent patch dropout module. During training, patches were ranked by the sigmoid-transformed mean of their embedding features and assigned nonlinearly increasing dropout probabilities, up to a maxi-84 mum of p. Selected patches were stochastically zeroed to introduce nonuniform regularization and reduce reliance on high-scoring patch representations. Dropout was disabled during in-86 ference. The modality-specific encoders learned stain-dependent representations, while the shared encoder learned representations across stains through shared parameters. For each stain, the modality-specific and shared embeddings were concatenated and passed through a linear projection followed by a ReLU activation. A residual connection from the shared embedding was then added to produce an integrated stain representation. The integrated embeddings from all three stain positions were concatenated into a case-level representation and passed through a linear classification head, followed by softmax to obtain four-class probabilities. When a stain was unavailable, its integrated embedding was replaced by the average of the shared embeddings from the available stains, preserving a fixed-dimensional 95 input to the classifier regardless of stain availability.

Experimental design

To systematically evaluate the benefits of multimodal integration relative to different singlestain baselines, three overlapping groups were defined within the study population based on stain availability (Figure 1B): Group AT8 (n = 512) included all cases with AT8, Group Aβ (n = 519) included all cases with Aβ, and Group LHE (n = 576) included all cases with LHE. Models were trained and evaluated separately for each stain-defined group. Within each group, the unimodal model used only the defining stain, whereas the multimodal model incorporated all available stains for the same cases. This design enabled paired comparisons between unimodal and multimodal models while controlling for differences in case composition. Repeating these comparisons across three defining stains allowed the benefits of multimodal integration to be evaluated without assuming any particular stain as a universally required input.

To improve robustness to incomplete stain availability, stochastic modality masking was applied during multimodal training. Each available stain was independently masked with a probability of 0.3, with masks resampled whenever all available stains were selected for removal so that at least one stain remained per case. Masked stains were represented by empty embeddings. In addition to full-input evaluation, each multimodal model was evaluated under a defining-stain-masked condition, in which the group's defining stain was withheld for every test case. This condition assessed the extent to which predictive performance could be retained through the remaining stains under systematic absence of the defining stain.

For each stain-defined group, cases from all three cohorts (UNITE, FHS, and ADRC) were pooled and evaluated using stratified five-fold cross-validation. Splits were constructed at the case level, jointly stratified by cohort and diagnostic label to preserve cohort-specific diagnostic composition across folds. In each iteration, three folds (60%) were used for training, one fold (20%) for validation, and one fold (20%) for testing. Validation and test assignments were rotated across the five iterations so that each case served as a validation case exactly once and as a held-out test case exactly once. Unimodal and multimodal models were trained independently for each iteration, with early stopping and checkpoint selection based on validation-set macro-averaged four-class AUROC. The same data partitions were used for unimodal and multimodal models within each group to enable paired comparisons.

Model performance was evaluated both within individual held-out test folds and using pooled out-of-fold predictions across all five iterations, as detailed in Performance metrics.

Models were trained using the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 0.0002 and weight decay of 0.0001. Standard dropout used a dropping probability of 0.25, and inputdependent patch dropout used a maximum dropping probability of 0.3. The transformer hidden dimension was set to 128, and the knowledge distillation loss weight was set to α = 0.2 for multimodal training. Training was performed for up to 200 epochs, with early stopping patience of 20 epochs. Batch size of 4 was used for all three stain groups based on hyperparameter tuning, with gradient accumulation applied to accommodate memory constraints. Class-balanced oversampling was applied during training to mitigate diagnostic label imbalance. A fixed random seed of 2025 was used for reproducibility.

Performance metrics

Models were evaluated as four-class classifiers over the mutually exclusive diagnostic categories (Control, AD, CTE, and CTE+AD). Classification performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), average precision (AP), accuracy, precision, sensitivity, and specificity. AUROC and AP were calculated separately for each class as a one-vs-rest problem against the remaining three classes, then macro-averaged across all four classes to obtain the overall (All) scores. Accuracy, precision, sensitivity, and specificity were computed from the argmax predictions of the four-class softmax outputs. Precision, sensitivity, and specificity were macro-averaged across the four one-vs-rest confusion matrices derived from these predictions.

Performance was evaluated at the individual-fold and pooled out-of-fold levels. For each cross-validation iteration, all classification metrics were calculated independently on the cor-responding held-out test set to characterize fold-level performance. In addition, classification metrics were calculated on a pooled out-of-fold evaluation population constructed by concatenating held-out predictions from all five iterations. The pooled evaluations were used for the primary model comparisons and statistical analyses, ensuring that results were not dependent on any single test fold.

Model comparisons were performed separately within each stain-defined group using differences in pooled macro-averaged four-class AUROC (∆AUROC). Three pairwise comparisons were evaluated: multimodal versus unimodal, defining-stain-masked multimodal versus unimodal, and defining-stain-masked multimodal versus multimodal. Standard errors and 95% confidence intervals were estimated using 10,000 class-stratified bootstrap resamples of the pooled test population. The 95% confidence intervals were obtained from the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distributions. Two-sided p-values were calculated using Wald tests based on the ratio of the observed ∆AUROC to its bootstrap standard error and the standard normal approximation. Combined estimates were obtained by averaging the three stain groups' ∆AUROC values and their corresponding bootstrap resample distributions with equal weight per group. Bootstrap resampling was coordinated at the donor level across the three overlapping stain groups to preserve dependencies arising from shared cases. Holm-Bonferroni correction [39] was applied across the three combined model comparisons, while group-level p-values were reported without adjustment. All statistical tests were two-sided with a significance threshold of 0.05.

For the neuropathological staging analyses, the predicted probability of AD pathology, P (AD), was defined as the sum of the softmax probabilities assigned to the AD and CTE+AD classes, while the predicted probability of CTE pathology, P (CTE), was defined as the sum of the probabilities assigned to the CTE and CTE+AD classes. These probabilities captured disease-specific predictions regardless of comorbidity. Associations between predicted disease probabilities and neuropathological staging categories were assessed using Spearman rank-order correlations. AD probabilities were evaluated across four Reagan categories (no AD, low, intermediate and high), while CTE probabilities were evaluated between cases without CTE and those with high-stage CTE (stages III-IV). Four-class classification of Control, AD, CTE, and CTE+AD was evaluated within three overlapping stain-defined groups (AT8, Aβ, and LHE; Figure 1). Within each group, the unimodal model used only the defining stain, whereas the multimodal model incorporated all available stains from the same cases. The multimodal model was additionally evaluated with the defining stain withheld at inference. Cases from the three cohorts were pooled within each group and evaluated using five-fold cross-validation, with case-level splits stratified jointly by cohort and diagnostic label. All reported results were obtained from held-out test data, including pooled out-of-fold predictions for aggregate analyses. Demographics, stain availability, and cohort composition for each stain-defined group are presented in Supplemental Table S1.

Multimodal classification performance

Each multimodal model was evaluated as a single four-class classifier over mutually exclusive diagnostic categories using pooled out-of-fold predictions spanning the study population of each stain-defined group (512 cases for AT8, 519 for Aβ, 576 for LHE). Overall macroaveraged classification AUROC reached 0.899 for AT8, 0.904 for Aβ, and 0.898 for LHE (Table 2). Per-class ROC and PR curves are shown in Figure 2A-F, and row-normalized confusion matrices are shown in Figure 2G-I. Across all three stain groups, the model achieved consistently high per-class AUROCs, ranging from 0.852 to 0.946, indicating broadly effective discrimination across the four diagnostic categories. Per-fold performance metrics were reported in Supplemental Table S2.

Consistent class-specific differences emerged across all three stain groups. Control and CTE generally achieved higher AUROCs and APs, whereas CTE+AD showed the lowest performance, particularly in AP. The confusion matrices revealed a similar pattern: all four diagnostic classes had their highest prediction proportions along the diagonal, although 16 CTE+AD showed lower correct classification proportions and greater confusion with AD and CTE across all three stain groups. Separating comorbid CTE+AD from either disease alone remained particularly challenging.

Multimodal integration improves predictive performance

The multimodal model consistently outperformed its unimodal counterpart in macro-averaged four-class AUROC across all three stain groups (Table 2). AUROC increased from 0.874 to 0.899 for AT8, from 0.859 to 0.904 for Aβ, and from 0.834 to 0.898 for LHE. Across the three groups, multimodal integration significantly improved four-class discrimination (∆AUROC = 0.044, adjusted p < 0.0001; Supplemental Table S3). Consistent improvements were also observed in overall AP and precision across all three groups, and in accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity for Aβ and LHE (Table 2). At the individual class level, multimodal integration improved AUROC in all 12 stain-group/class comparisons and AP in 11 of 12 comparisons, with the only decrease in AP observed for CTE+AD in the AT8 group.

The magnitude of improvement varied across stain groups, with larger gains observed for stains with weaker unimodal discrimination. The smallest gain occurred in the AT8 group (∆AUROC = 0.025, p = 0.029), followed by Aβ (∆AUROC = 0.045, p < 0.0001), while LHE showed the largest improvement (∆AUROC = 0.063, p < 0.0001; Supplemental Table S3).

These findings demonstrate that multimodal integration provides benefits beyond individual stains, with the greatest observed gain in the LHE group, where the unimodal model had the lowest baseline performance.

Multimodal models retain performance under missing-stain conditions

To evaluate robustness under systematic stain absence, each multimodal model was evaluated with its group's defining stain withheld for every test case. This removed 35.6% (AT8), 35.9% (Aβ), and 37.0% (LHE) of the total WSIs available to each group's multimodal model.

Despite this reduction in available inputs, the masked multimodal models maintained macroaveraged four-class AUROCs of 0.836 for AT8, 0.871 for Aβ, and 0.892 for LHE (Table 2).

Compared with the corresponding unimodal baselines, masked multimodal performance was lower for AT8 (∆AUROC = -0.038, p = 0.019), not different for Aβ (∆AUROC = 0.013, p = 0.397), and higher for LHE (∆AUROC = 0.057, p < 0.001; Supplemental Table S3).

The combined comparison across the three groups showed no differences between masked multimodal and unimodal performance (Supplemental Table S3). These results indicate that the ability to maintain discrimination using the remaining stains varies across stain groups, with masked multimodal performance approaching or exceeding the corresponding unimodal baseline for Aβ and LHE, though not for AT8.

Relative to the full multimodal models, withholding the defining stain reduced macroaveraged AUROC by varying magnitudes across the three groups. The largest reduction was observed when AT8 was withheld (∆AUROC = -0.063, p < 0.0001), followed by Aβ (∆AUROC = -0.033, p < 0.0001), whereas withholding LHE resulted in a smaller, nonsignificant reduction (∆AUROC = -0.006, p = 0.328). Across the three groups, masking significantly reduced discrimination overall (∆AUROC = -0.034, adjusted p < 0.0001; Supplemental Table S3). Together, these findings demonstrate that the impact of missing stains on predictive performance is not uniform, with the greatest observed dependence on AT8, intermediate dependence on Aβ, and minimal performance loss when LHE is withheld.

Predicted disease probabilities are associated with neuropathological staging

To examine whether model predictions reflected neuropathological characteristics beyond categorical diagnostic labels, predicted disease probabilities were evaluated against established staging measures. For AD, predicted probabilities were compared across Reagan categories, derived from Braak neurofibrillary tangle stage and CERAD neuritic plaque score [11, 34, 33]. Median predicted AD probabilities increased monotonically across Reagan strata in all three stain groups (Figure 3A-C). Spearman rank-order correlations were 0.686 for AT8, 0.714 for Aβ, and 0.702 for LHE (all p < 0.0001), consistently exceeding the corresponding unimodal correlations of 0.607, 0.665, and 0.584, respectively (Supplemental Figure S2A-C). The largest increase in correlation was observed in the LHE group. Pairwise Mann-Whitney comparisons further showed significant differences in predicted probabilities across all six pairs of Reagan strata for both multimodal and unimodal models in every group (Holm-Bonferroni-adjusted p < 0.05).

To determine whether the association between predicted AD probabilities and neuropathological severity was maintained under missing-stain conditions, the analysis was repeated with each group's defining stain masked at inference (Figure 3D-F). Spearman correlations declined from 0.686 to 0.618 for AT8, from 0.714 to 0.598 for Aβ, and from 0.702 to 0.693 for LHE. Pairwise Reagan stratum comparisons remained significant after masking, except for the no AD versus low comparison in the Aβ group. The smallest reduction was observed in LHE, consistent with its minimal loss in four-class discrimination under masking. Together, these results demonstrate that the association between predicted AD probabilities and neuropathological severity largely persists under missing-stain conditions, although its strength varies with the withheld stain.

CTE neuropathological staging is commonly categorized as low stage (I-II) and high stage (III-IV) [1]. The UNITE samples included in this study were selected with an emphasis on high-stage CTE (stages III-IV) to facilitate comparative histopathological analysis of AD and CTE. Predicted CTE probabilities were significantly higher in the high-stage group across all three stain groups (Figure 3G-I), with Spearman correlations of 0.634 for AT8, 0.634 for Aβ, and 0.629 for LHE (all p < 0.0001). These correlations exceeded the corresponding unimodal values of 0.614, 0.575, and 0.568 (Supplemental Figure S2D-F). Under definingstain masking, the associations remained significant, with correlations of 0.568, 0.587, and 0.601 for AT8, Aβ, and LHE, respectively (Figure 3J-L). Thus, predicted CTE probabilities distinguished high-stage CTE from the absence of CTE across all model conditions.

Two complementary visualization approaches were used: modality-specific encoder attention maps and shared-encoder class attribution maps. Modality-specific attention maps are shown in Figure 4A. Discussion

This study demonstrates the feasibility of integrating complementary histopathological information under incomplete stain availability for joint characterization of AD and CTE within a single brain region. Across three overlapping stain-defined populations, multimodal integration consistently improved four-class discrimination compared with matched unimodal baselines, while defining-stain masking demonstrated that predictive information could be retained through the remaining stains. Model-predicted disease probabilities were associated with neuropathological staging measures for AD and CTE, and spatial attribution patterns on the remaining stains were largely preserved under masking. Together, these findings support the integration of complementary neuropathological information without requiring complete stain panels.

The performance gains from multimodal integration and the effects of defining-stain masking revealed a consistent but asymmetric pattern across stain groups. AT8 showed the strongest unimodal discrimination and the smallest gain from multimodal integration, yet withholding AT8 caused the largest reduction in multimodal performance. In contrast, LHE showed the weakest unimodal discrimination and the largest benefit from additional stains, while withholding LHE had little effect on full multimodal performance. Aβ occupied an intermediate position in both comparisons. These observations suggest that the contribution of a stain within a multimodal framework depends not only on its individual discriminative capacity but also on the extent to which relevant information can be complemented by or recovered from other stains. The sensitivity to AT8 removal is biologically plausible given the central role of hyperphosphorylated tau in CTE diagnosis [1] and AD neurofibrillary pathology [11]. AT8 directly visualizes tau pathology that is not equivalently measured by Aβ or LHE, whereas LHE primarily provides structural and morphological information that may overlap with tissue features represented in the other stains. The smaller performance loss upon Aβ removal suggests that some information relevant to AD classification remains accessible through tau pathology and tissue morphology, although these stains do not di-22 rectly measure amyloid plaques. These findings demonstrate that multimodal integration can leverage complementary information across stains to improve disease discrimination while exploiting partially overlapping information to mitigate the impact of missing stains.

Despite strong overall discrimination, comorbid CTE+AD remained the most challenging diagnostic category, with predictions frequently assigned to either CTE or AD alone. Unlike single-pathology classification, identifying comorbidity requires sufficient evidence for both pathological processes, which may differ in burden, spatial distribution, and detectability within the sampled tissue. CTE is characterized by focal perivascular hyperphosphorylated tau pathology at cortical sulcal depths [1], whereas AD neuropathological assessment incorporates neurofibrillary tangles and amyloid plaque pathology [11, 34, 33]. The difficulty of recognizing CTE+AD may therefore reflect incomplete or imbalanced evidence for the two disease components. Because neuropathological assessment of AD and CTE considers the distribution of pathology across multiple brain regions, the restriction to dorsolateral frontal cortex in this study may have limited the evidence available to distinguish these disease components in comorbid cases [27, 1]. These findings illustrate that multimodal integration within a single brain region can address only part of the complexity of neuropathological classification and underscore the importance of extending this approach to integrate information across anatomically distinct regions.

Methodologically, this work addresses a setting that differs from conventional singlestain computational pathology and multimodal fusion of histology with non-imaging data.

Existing WSI classification approaches commonly aggregate patch-level information from individual stains through attention-based multiple-instance learning, graph-based modeling, or transformer architectures [13, 43, 41, 18, 19]. Other multimodal pathology frameworks combine histology with molecular or clinical measurements [22, 44, 45, 46]. Incompletemodality learning has also been investigated in medical imaging through approaches such as HeMIS [47], SMIL [48], and ShaSpec [28], while emerging multi-stain pathology frameworks have begun addressing variable stain availability. Building on ShaSpec's shared-specific representation learning and missing-modality feature substitution, this study adapts these mechanisms to gigapixel multi-stain WSIs using TransMIL-based encoders for slide-level representation learning. The three stain-defined comparisons provide a systematic evaluation of multimodal integration relative to different single-stain baselines while controlling for differences in case composition within each comparison. Furthermore, withholding the defining stain for every test case evaluates model robustness under systematic stain absence, a condition relevant to differences in staining protocols across brain banks. This experimental framework provides a basis for evaluating multimodal integration under varying patterns of histopathological information availability.

Although the models were trained using categorical diagnostic labels, their predicted probabilities showed associations with neuropathological staging measures. For AD, predicted probabilities showed strong ordinal associations with Reagan neuropathological categories. Across all stain groups, full multimodal predictions were more strongly correlated with Reagan categories than their unimodal counterparts, with the largest improvement observed for LHE. Importantly, the predicted probability of AD incorporates AD-only and CTE+AD class probabilities, allowing the analysis to reflect AD-related pathology regardless of CTE comorbidity. Because Reagan categories are derived from Braak neurofibrillary tangle stage and CERAD neuritic plaque score [11, 34, 33], the observed association is consistent with the models capturing histological features related to the underlying components of AD neuropathological severity. For CTE, predicted probabilities similarly distinguished cases without CTE from those with high-stage CTE (stages III-IV), with stronger associations for multimodal than unimodal predictions across all stain groups. The associations between predicted probabilities and neuropathological status persisted when the defining stain was withheld. For AD, all but one pairwise Reagan-category comparison remained statistically significant under masking across the three stain groups. Similarly, predicted CTE probabilities continued to distinguish cases without CTE from those with high-stage CTE under masking. Together, these findings extend the evidence of missing-stain robustness beyond categorical classification, showing that associations between model predictions and neuropathological measures can persist when a key stain is unavailable.

Spatial interpretation provided a complementary perspective on the learned representations and their behavior under missing-stain conditions. Modality-specific attention maps showed spatial patterns broadly consistent with the histological characteristics of the corresponding stains. AT8 and Aβ attention was concentrated in relatively localized regions, consistent with the focal appearance of tau-and amyloid-associated pathological features, whereas LHE attention more frequently followed the cortical gray-matter ribbon (Figure 4A).

These qualitative patterns suggest that modality-specific encoders emphasize different aspects of tissue morphology, although individual attention hotspots were not independently validated against pathologist-annotated lesions. Importantly, shared-encoder class attribution showed high spatial rank correlation between full-input and defining-stain-masked conditions across the remaining stains, both in representative cases and at the population level (Figure 4B; Supplemental Table S5). This indicates that removing one stain generally preserved the relative spatial distribution of attribution within the other stains, complementing the observed prediction-level robustness.

Our work has a few limitations. First, CTE-positive cases were available only from UNITE, whereas AD cases were represented across all three cohorts. Although crossvalidation was stratified jointly by cohort and diagnostic label, the ability to generalize CTE classification to independent CTE-positive populations remains untested. Furthermore, tissue processing and neuropathological assessment protocols were standardized across the three cohorts. As a result, whether the framework generalizes to independent brain banks with different tissue sampling, staining, and processing protocols remains to be established.

Second, the anatomical scope of this study was restricted to the dorsolateral frontal cortex. Neuropathological assessment of AD relies on the regional distribution of amyloid and tau pathology, while consensus criteria for CTE recommend sampling multiple brain regions given the patchy distribution of its pathognomonic lesions [27, 1]. The present findings therefore reflect only a subset of the histopathological information relevant to AD and CTE, and more comprehensive neuropathological characterization will require integration of information across multiple brain regions. Third, direct supervision on disease-specific severity measures was not investigated, and the restriction to high-stage CTE precluded assessment across the full spectrum of CTE severity. Fourth, the spatial interpretation analysis was restricted to cases with all three stains available for full-versus-masked comparisons, and attention localization was not validated against expert lesion annotations. In addition, AT8 and Aβ patches were restricted to gray matter, whereas LHE included both gray and white matter.

Whether these differences in tissue coverage influence the shared encoder's spatial attribution, particularly for LHE, remains unexamined.

In conclusion, our model's tolerance to absent stains could facilitate retrospective reassessment of archival brain bank material, including cases in which CTE was not evaluated during the original examination and for which additional tissue sampling or staining may no longer be feasible. Such applications can potentially expand the effective sample size available for epidemiological inference. Also, the ability to accommodate varying stain combinations provides a methodological basis for future studies on brain banks with different tissue sampling and staining protocols.
