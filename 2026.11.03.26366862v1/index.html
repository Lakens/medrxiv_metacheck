Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Objective: Urban neighbourhoods across Europe are rapidly transforming through gentrification, with Portuguese cities particularly affected. Although the health impacts of these changes are increasingly recognised, validated instruments to assess perceptions of neighbourhood change remain scarce. This study adapted and validated a Portuguese version of the Perceptions About Change in Environment and Residents (PACER-PT) scale.

Methods: A cross-sectional online survey was conducted among 255 adults living for ≥5yrs in the Lisbon and Porto Metropolitan Areas. PACER was translated and culturally adapted to create PACER-PT. We evaluated psychometric properties using exploratory factor analysis (EFA), internal consistency, and convergent and criterion validity.

Results: EFA identified a three-factor structure explaining 47.5% of the variance: Built, social and economic changes, Positive attitude towards neighbourhood changes, and Displacement concerns and neighbourhood disruption. Internal consistency was acceptable (Cronbach's alpha=0.70-0.78). Convergent/criterion validity were supported by significant associations with amount and pace of change, self-rated health, and quality of life. Younger and wealthier participants and those with greater residential satisfaction reported more favourable perceptions.

Conclusions: PACER-PT demonstrated it is a valid instrument for assessing gentrificationrelated neighbourhood changes in Portugal.

INTRODUCTION

Decades of research have established neighbourhood environments as critical determinants of health outcomes [1]. Neighbourhoods are dynamic systems shaped by population movement, public and private investment, policy interventions, and broader socioeconomic processes [2].

Among these transformations, gentrification has emerged as one of the most widely studied forms of urban change. Gentrification involves an influx of residents with greater economic power than the existing population, accompanied by rising property values and the potential displacement of long-term, lower-income residents [3]. Consequently, gentrification can influence population health and health inequalities by transforming neighbourhoods [3].

Gentrification has traditionally been measured using objective census-based indicators, including changes in income, education, housing costs and demographic composition [4, 5].

While such objective measures provide valuable contextual information, they fail to capture the lived experiences of residents undergoing neighbourhood transformation. Residents' perceptions of neighbourhood change may represent an important mechanism linking urban change to health outcomes [6]. Such perceptions -residents' subjective awareness and appraisal of changes occurring in their neighbourhood -may influence psychological stress, sense of belonging, social relationships, and housing security. By contrast, residents' experiences encompass the broader ways in which neighbourhood transformation affects their everyday lives. Qualitative studies have provided important insights into these lived experiences [6] [7] [8], but their findings are typically limited in scalability and generalizability [9]. A key gap in the literature is therefore the lack of scalable measures of perceived neighbourhood change and of a clear conceptualization of how these perceptions may influence health [9].

Recognizing these limitations, Hirsch and colleagues developed the Perceptions About Change in Environment and Residents (PACER) scale to measure residents' experiences of gentrification-related neighbourhood change relevant for health [9]. The development of PACER followed a rigorous multi-stage process including literature review, expert consultation, cognitive interviews, and pilot testing. The resulting instrument captured multiple dimensions of neighbourhood change, including built environment changes, social dynamics, affordability, and residents' emotional responses to transformation. Psychometric evaluation demonstrated acceptable internal consistency and evidence of criterion-related validity across several domains.

PACER provides an important tool for measuring perceived gentrification-related neighbourhood change and has been developed, validated and applied in North American settings, including the original study in Philadelphia, its subsequent validation in MESA, and its application in the Canadian INTERACT study [9] [10] [11]. Its development incorporated input from the National Neighbourhood Indicators Partnership (NNIP), reflecting the challenges of capturing diverse experiences of neighbourhood change even within the United States [9]. and gentrification in Portuguese urban contexts to inform urban planning and public health strategies.

Data were collected through an online survey administered by Pureprofile using its established panel. Eligible participants were aged 18 years or older, fluent in Portuguese, and had lived in their current neighbourhood in either metropolitan area for at least five years. Neighbourhood was defined as the area surrounding participants' homes, without a fixed geographical boundary, allowing respondents to use their own understanding of the term. Unlike the original PACER validation, which included participants with varying lengths of residence, this study focused on longer-term residents to ensure sufficient exposure to neighbourhood change.

The questionnaire included PACER-PT and questions characterising participants' residential histories, housing situations, and sociodemographic characteristics. These covered duration of residence in the neighbourhood and current dwelling, housing tenure, intentions regarding future residence, reasons for choosing the neighbourhood, age, gender, educational attainment, employment status, household size, and household income. These variables were used to describe the sample and contextualise variation in scale responses.

Based on the recommended minimum ratio of 10 participants per item for scale validation [18], the 25-item scale required at least 250 valid responses. A total of 255 valid responses were obtained from a convenience sample. Comparison with the 2021 Census population of the Lisbon and Porto metropolitan areas (Supplementary File S1, Table 1) indicated a similar distribution of self-reported gender to the Census distribution of sex. However, younger and more highly educated individuals were overrepresented, while older adults and those with lower educational attainment were underrepresented.

Translation and cultural adaptation

The original PACER scale was translated and culturally adapted following established guidelines for instrument validation [19]. One bilingual native speaker/translator and a public health researcher fluent in English independently translated the original items, and a consensus version was produced. A back-translation into English was then performed by one bilingual native speaker unfamiliar with the original scale to ensure conceptual equivalence. To assess face validity, the research team reviewed the items for clarity, relevance, and cultural appropriateness. Particular attention was given to ensuring that terminology describing housing dynamics, neighbourhood change, commercial activities, and social relationships reflected Portuguese linguistic conventions and urban realities. The pre-final version was pilot-tested with a small sample from the target population to verify comprehensibility (n=10).

Measures

As in the original PACER scale, the Portuguese version (PACER-PT) was used to assess residents' perceptions of neighbourhood change occurring over the previous three to five years.

The PACER-PT scale is provided in the supplementary material (Supplementary File S2).

The original PACER questionnaire comprises nine items assessing the perceived occurrence of neighbourhood changes. Responses to these items were coded as follows: "It does not happen" (-1), "Unsure" (0), "It happens a little" (1), and "It happens a lot" (2). Thus, "Unsure" was assigned an intermediate numerical value between "It does not happen" and "It happens a little".

A further ten items assess emotional and cognitive responses to neighbourhood change using a five-point agreement scale. In the final survey analysis, responses were coded from -2 ("Strongly disagree") to +2 ("Strongly agree"), with 0 corresponding to "Neither agree nor disagree". "Not applicable" responses were also coded as 0. As done by Hirsch and colleagues, four of the items -signalled by (R) in Table 2 -were reverse-coded [9]. The items cover changes commonly associated with gentrification, including housing and commercial transformation, population change, neighbourhood tensions, belonging, trust and displacement concerns [9].

The Portuguese translation and cultural adaptations made to these items are described below.

During cultural adaptation, particular attention was given to terminology relating to housing tenure, commercial change and neighbourhood governance. Wording and examples were modified where necessary to ensure their relevance and comprehensibility in the Portuguese context, while preserving the intended meaning of the original items. In addition to these items, contextual indicators were included to measure the perceived amount and pace of neighbourhood change (questions 13, 14). Participants rated the overall magnitude of neighbourhood transformation and the speed at which changes had occurred using numeric scales ranging from 1 to 10. These indicators were used to support the evaluation of convergent validity.

Questions 10, 11, 12, and 15 were included as additional items to capture complementary dimensions of perceived neighbourhood change not covered by the core PACER items.

Specifically, these questions assessed perceived demographic and socioeconomic differences between long-term residents and newcomers, perceived changes in neighbourhood naming practices, newcomers' engagement with existing local establishments and organisations, and residents' perceptions of the primary drivers of neighbourhood transformation, including tourism. Moreover, health-related measures were included to assess general health and quality of life.

Participants rated their overall quality of life on a five-point scale ranging from "Very poor" to "Very good". Self-rated general health was assessed using a single item with five response options: "Excellent", "Very good", "Good", "Fair", and "Poor". Perceived change in health status was also evaluated by asking participants to compare their current general health with that of three years earlier, with response options ranging from much better to much worse. These measures were used to examine criterion validity between PACER-PT scores and health-related outcomes.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted following established procedures for psychometric validation of measurement instruments [19] and followed the steps of the authors of the original PACER scale [9]. The number of factors to retain was determined based on eigenvalues greater than 1 (Kaiser criterion [20]), inspection of the scree plot, and the interpretability of the resulting factor structure. An oblique rotation (oblimin with kaiser normalization) was applied, as correlations between factors were expected. Items with factor loadings ≥0.40 were considered significant contributors to a factor. Items exhibiting cross-loadings ≥0.30 on more than one factor were examined carefully, and decisions regarding item retention were based on both statistical and theoretical considerations. Several alternative models were examined, and the final solution was selected based on statistical performance and theoretical plausibility.

Internal consistency acceptability was evaluated using Cronbach's alpha coefficients calculated separately for each identified factor. Values of α ≥ 0.70 were considered indicative of acceptable internal consistency [21]. Subscale scores were calculated as unweighted averages of items loading onto each factor. Convergent validity was assessed by calculating Spearman correlation coefficients between PACER-PT subscale scores and contextual indicators of perceived neighbourhood change, specifically perceived amount and pace of change ("On a scale of 1-10, with 10 being the most and 1 being the least, overall how much change has happened in your neighbourhood during the last three to five years?" and "On a scale of 1-10, with 10 being the fastest and 1 being the slowest, how quickly have changes been happening in your neighbourhood during the last three to five years?"). Additional Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated between PACER-PT subscale scores and selected indicators of perceived quality of life and self-rated health to further assess criterion validity. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 30.0.0.0, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Mean PACER-PT factor scores were compared across participants' sociodemographic and contextual characteristics using independent-samples T test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). To ensure adequate group sizes, several sociodemographic and contextual variables were aggregated into broader categories.

RESULTS

Sample characteristics

Sample characteristics are shown in Table 1. The sample included 255 participants (51.0% male), with a mean age of 40.9 years (SD=13.8). Most participants reported household incomes between €20,000 and €40,000 (35.7%), had completed secondary (37.3%) or bachelor's level education (38.8%), were employed full-time (72.2%), and owned their homes (63.9%). Over half were somewhat likely to recommend their residential area (53.7%), and 40.8% intended to remain in their current home for at least 10 years. Most participants rated their health as good or very good (60.8%), with 52.2% reporting no change in health compared with three years earlier.

Regarding the PACER-PT questions, descriptive analyses, depicted in Table 2, indicated that respondents reported varying degrees of perceived neighbourhood transformation across multiple domains. Regarding the types of change, the highest mean scores were observed for items related to housing cost increases (Housing, mean = 1.78, SD = 0.64) and housing market activity (Flipping, mean = 1.55, SD = 0.73), while the lowest mean score was reported for items reflecting the opening of expensive and sophisticated stores (Grocery, mean = 0.45, SD = 1.20) and tension and conflict between neighbours (Neighbours, mean = -0.04, SD = 1.19).

With respect to feelings about changes, the highest mean score was observed for feeling welcome in commercial establishments (Welcome, mean = 0.80, SD = 0.90), whereas the lowest mean score was found for perceived ability to afford housing within the neighbourhood (Afford, mean = -0.46, SD = 1.34). Intermediate mean scores (around 0 on average) were observed for items related to support for neighbourhood change (Support, mean = 0.15, SD = 1.08), trust in newcomers (Trust, mean = 0.07, SD = 0.95), and displacement concerns (Pushed, mean = 0.11, SD = 1.22).

Exploratory factor analysis

Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) was conducted to examine the dimensional structure of the PACER-PT scale. Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy was 0.774, indicating good adequacy for factor analysis. Bartlett's test of sphericity was significant (χ²(171) = 1399.025, p < 0.001), supporting the suitability of the data for factor analysis.

The final factor solution supported a three-factor structure, which explained approximately 47.5% of the total variance in the data. The eigenvalues associated with the three retained factors were 3.70, 3.18, and 2.15, respectively. The cumulative variance explained by the three factors reached 19.5%, 36.2%, and 47.5% at each successive step, indicating that the selected structure captured a substantial proportion of the variance in responses.

Table 3 shows the factor loadings of the final solution. The first factor captured observable changes in the built, social and economic environment. These items included the construction of new buildings, property renovation activities, increases in housing costs, the arrival of new residents, and commercial transformation. This factor was interpreted as representing "Built, social and economic changes", reflecting structural shifts associated with gentrification processes.

The second factor included items reflecting residents' positive attitudes toward neighbourhood change. This dimension included items related to feeling welcome in new businesses, trusting new residents, feeling that neighbourhood changes were meant for people like oneself, supporting neighbourhood improvements, and perceiving that one had a voice in neighbourhood transformation processes. This factor was interpreted as representing positive responses to neighbourhood change and was therefore labelled "Positive attitude towards neighbourhood changes".

The third factor included items reflecting uncertainty and perceived risk related to neighbourhood transformation. Items loading onto this factor included concerns about displacement, fear of being pushed out of the neighbourhood, perceptions of changes in neighbourhood character, and reported tensions between neighbours. This factor was interpreted as representing "Displacement concerns and neighbourhood disruption", although due to the reverse-coding of the corresponding items, the greater its value, the smaller these fears and concerns are perceived.

Reliability analysis

Internal consistency was acceptable across all factors, with Cronbach's alpha values of 0.77 (Built, social and economic changes), 0.78 (Positive attitude towards neighbourhood changes), and 0.70 (Displacement concerns and neighbourhood disruption).

Convergent and criterion-related validity

Correlation analyses provided further support for convergent and criterion validity (Table 3). The Built, social and economic changes factor showed significant positive correlations with perceived amount of change (ρ=0.317, p<0.001) and pace of change (ρ=0.337, p<0.001), indicating that higher perceptions of structural and economic transformation were associated with greater perceived intensity of neighbourhood change.

Consistent with these findings, the "Displacement concerns and neighbourhood disruption" factor was negatively associated with the perceived amount (ρ = -0.334, p < 0.001) and pace of neighbourhood change (ρ = -0.257, p < 0.001). As higher factor scores indicate fewer concerns, these associations suggest that greater perceived change and a faster perceived pace of change were associated with greater concerns about displacement and neighbourhood uncertainty. The "Positive attitude towards neighbourhood changes" factor showed a weak but significant association with perceived amount of change (ρ = -0.145, p<0.05) suggesting that positive perceptions about the changes decline with perceived intensity of neighbourhood change.

The "Positive attitude towards neighbourhood changes" factor was positively correlated with quality of life (ρ = 0.272, p < 0.001), self-rated health (ρ = 0.192, p < 0.01), and perceived changes in health status (ρ = 0.314, p < 0.001). The "Displacement concerns and neighbourhood disruption" and "Built, social and economic changes" factors were also positively, but less strongly, associated with quality of life (ρ=0.139, p<0.05 and ρ=0.153, p<0.05, respectively), although the associations with self-rated health and perceived changes in health status did not reach significance, suggesting that they had a more limited effect in perceived health outcomes.

PACER-PT factor scores according socioeconomic and demographic factors

Table 4 shows the mean PACER-PT factor scores according to sociodemographic and contextual characteristics. Overall, Factor 2 (Positive attitude towards neighbourhood changes) showed the greatest variation across groups. Significant differences were observed according to age (p = 0.016), household income (p = 0.033), likelihood of recommending the residential area (p < 0.001), and desired length of time to remain in the current home (p < 0.001). Younger participants (<45 years) and those with higher household income (≥ €60.000) tended to report more positive attitude towards neighbourhood changes than the others. Participants who were very likely to recommend their residential area and those intending to remain in their home for longer periods also reported significantly more positive attitudes towards neighbourhood changes.

The only significant association found for Factor 1 (Built, social and economic changes) was relative to educational level (p = 0.006), where participants who did not complete secondary education reported fewer changes in their neighbourhoods than participants with secondary or higher education.

For Factor 3 (Displacement concerns and neighbourhood disruption), significant differences were observed for housing status (p = 0.030), likelihood of recommending the residential area (p < 0.001) and desired length of time to remain in the current home (p < 0.001). Homeowners reported fewer concerns about displacement and neighbourhood disruption than renters. The same happened with those who were very likely to recommend their neighbourhood and those desiring to remain in their current residence for longer periods.

DISCUSSION

This study successfully translated and culturally adapted the PACER questionnaire into Portuguese and demonstrated that the resulting Portuguese version (PACER-PT) has satisfactory psychometric properties. Overall, the findings support the validity and reliability of the adapted instrument for assessing perceptions of gentrification-related neighbourhood change in Portugal. The PACER-PT demonstrated a coherent three-factor structure with acceptable internal consistency, meaningful associations with neighbourhood change and health-related outcomes, and the ability to discriminate between groups expected to differ in their perceptions of neighbourhood change.

Compared with the original PACER study, which identified four dimensions (Feelings, Built Environment, Social Environment and Affordability), the Portuguese adaptation yielded a more parsimonious three-factor structure. The original Feelings factor was largely preserved, suggesting that emotional responses to neighbourhood change are stable across cultural contexts. In contrast, the original Built Environment and Social Environment dimensions merged into a single factor reflecting Built, social and economic changes, suggesting that Portuguese respondents perceive physical, commercial and social transformations as interconnected aspects of the same urban process. Additionally, items from the Feelings, Social Environment and Affordability domains clustered into a third factor representing Displacement concerns and neighbourhood disruption, highlighting residential stability and neighbourhood identity as a distinct underlying dimension in the Portuguese context.

Despite the reduction in the number of dimensions, the PACER-PT demonstrated satisfactory psychometric performance. All three factors showed acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach's alpha=0.70-0.78), supporting the reliability of the adapted instrument. Furthermore, the pattern of correlations with contextual indicators of neighbourhood change and healthrelated outcomes was conceptually coherent. The factor reflecting "Built, social and economic changes" was most strongly associated with the perceived amount and pace of neighbourhood change, whereas "Positive attitude towards neighbourhood changes" was positively associated with self-rated quality of life and health, providing additional evidence of construct and criterionrelated validity.

The comparison of PACER-PT factor scores across participant characteristics provided further support for the instrument's construct validity. "Positive attitude towards neighbourhood changes" (Factor 2) varied according to age, household income, residential satisfaction and intended length of residence, with more favourable perceptions generally reported by younger participants, those with higher incomes, and respondents expressing greater satisfaction with and stronger intentions to remain in their neighbourhood. "Built, social and economic changes" (Factor 1) differed only by educational level, whereas "Displacement concerns and neighbourhood disruption" (Factor 3) was primarily associated with housing tenure, residential satisfaction and future residential intentions. Overall, contextual and residential characteristics explained greater variation in PACER-PT scores than most sociodemographic characteristics, supporting the instrument's ability to discriminate between groups expected to differ in their perceptions of neighbourhood change.

The observed differences in PACER-PT factor scores provide evidence of the instrument's construct validity by showing that perceptions of neighbourhood change vary according to relevant sociodemographic and residential characteristics. Younger participants were more likely to report positive attitudes about neighbourhood changes than older adults. This finding may reflect greater openness to urban transformation and a higher perceived ability to benefit from neighbourhood improvements, as younger adults are often more closely aligned with the lifestyles, amenities, and housing preferences associated with processes of "youthification", which often occur alongside gentrification and involve urban redevelopment increasingly attracting and catering to younger populations [22]. In contrast, older residents may be more sensitive to the disruption of long-established social ties, place attachment, and local identity that can accompany neighbourhood change. This interpretation is consistent with previous studies showing that gentrification may generate social exclusion, loss of familiar social spaces, and diminished sense of belonging among older adults, even in the absence of physical displacement [23]. Furthermore, participants who were more satisfied with their neighbourhood, as reflected by their willingness to recommend it and their intention to remain living there, consistently reported more positive perceptions of change and fewer concerns related to displacement and neighbourhood disruption. However, these relationships may vary according to the nature of the changes and their consequences for residents, including housing affordability and security of tenure [24].

Several strengths of this study should be acknowledged. First, the adaptation process preserved the conceptual integrity of the original PACER instrument while ensuring linguistic and cultural appropriateness for the Portuguese context. Second, the use of exploratory factor analysis enabled an empirical evaluation of the dimensional structure of the Portuguese version, ensuring that the resulting factors reflected meaningful constructs within the study population.

Third, the inclusion of contextual indicators of neighbourhood change allowed the assessment of convergent and criterion-related validity, strengthening the evidence supporting the scale's validity. Moreover, the inclusion of health indicators enabled an initial exploration of the scale's relevance for urban health research, which represents its intended field of application. Although the sample was not fully representative of the adult population of the Porto and Lisbon Metropolitan Areas, comparison with Census 2021 data indicated a broadly comparable gender distribution. The overrepresentation of younger and more highly educated participants, which is common in voluntary surveys, may limit the generalisability of the findings. However, the inclusion of participants with diverse sociodemographic and residential characteristics provides support for the applicability of the PACER-PT across a broad range of urban residents.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Although PACER-PT captures residents' retrospective perceptions of neighbourhood change, which are relevant, the cross-sectional design does not allow us to assess how these perceptions evolve over time. Longitudinal studies would provide valuable insights into how perceptions evolve over time and how they relate to changes in health outcomes. Additionally, the use of online data collection methods may have introduced selection bias, potentially limiting participation among individuals with lower digital literacy or restricted internet access [25].

Future studies should consider mixed-mode data collection strategies and/or correction strategies to enhance representativeness. Another limitation relates to the geographic scope of the study, which included only residents of the Metropolitan Areas of Porto and Lisbon. While these cities represent major urban centres undergoing rapid transformation, the findings may not be fully generalizable to smaller cities or rural settings [26].
