Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. Abstract

Health advice circulates through everyday relationships. The sources of informal health authority are less clear: the adviser's knowledge and conduct, or their network position. We examine personal network data from 89 adults in a rural community (1,625 respondent-contact relationships). We distinguish contacts consulted for health advice from contacts who tried to change the respondent's eating, model both relations with multilevel and penalised regressions, and test their differences. Both relations are embedded in frequent, close, and commensal ties and point to contacts more educated than the respondent. We find no characteristic that is more strongly associated with one relation than with the other. Health advice seeking also correlates with co-residence, the marital relationship, and responsibility for cooking. Respondents perceive their advisers as healthier, but advisers' own reports give no evidence of healthier diets or lower body mass. Informal health authority here is constituted by relational position (intimacy, domestic food work, and a contact's higher education relative to the respondent). We find no evidence that it rests on demonstrated conduct. Interventions that work through nominated advisers should not assume that being consulted implies expertise. Our design is cross-sectional and single-site. Future dyadic and longitudinal studies should test whether advice is followed.

Introduction

Health-related practices are embedded in everyday social relationships. Individuals discuss health with relatives, friends, and other members of their personal networks, often before professional advice is sought (Freidson, 1960). Help-seeking is socially organised (McKinlay, 1973; Pescosolido, 1992), relationships are mobilised selectively for different purposes (Perry and Pescosolido, 2010), and health-advice relationships only partially overlap with close personal relationships (Reiner et al., 2025). Adviser selection depends on assessments of others' knowledge and opportunities to access it (Borgatti and Cross, 2003). Understanding informal health advice therefore requires attention to the relationships through which particular people become sources of guidance, not only to individuals' characteristics.

Unlike professional authority, grounded in credentials, informal advisory standing is established within relationships. It may reflect experiential knowledge of illness and healthcare (Prior, 2003), exemplary health conduct (Valente and Pumpuang, 2007), or relational position, such as being a spouse, cook, or educated relative. These possibilities matter: advisers chosen for their conduct may model healthier practices, whereas those chosen for their position need not. Distinguishing perceived conduct from reported conduct requires information from both the person seeking advice and the adviser. Overlapping personal network interviews can provide both.

Dietary practices are especially relevant because eating is organised through households, shared meals, and routines (Sobal et al., 2002). Food preparation is gendered caring labour (DeVault, 1991), and women often monitor family health (Gregory, 2005). Cooking may create opportunities for consultation without conferring control over consumption, challenging the notion of women as household food gatekeepers (McIntosh and Zey, 1989). Health-related social control also differs from support (Lewis and Butterfield, 2007): helping, persuading, and pressuring someone about food may have different consequences (Stephens et al., 2013).

These observations highlight a distinction that research on personal networks has paid little attention to: the difference between being consulted for health advice and trying to change another person's eating. The first concerns the selection of a source of guidance. The second concerns a reported effort to modify behaviour. Both may occur within the same relationship, but they need not involve the same people, and both may be associated with gender, education, and relational proximity. Research on advice networks suggests that informal status, including relative expertise, affects advice relationships (Brooks et al., 2015). Educational differences within a relationship offer one observable dimension of such asymmetry, although education cannot be equated with health knowledge. Distinguishing advice from attempts at influence also matters for research on dietary behaviour in networks. Connected people often eat alike, but similarity can arise through influence, through choosing similar companions, or through shared circumstances (McPherson et al., 2001). Evidence from rural Eastern Europe documents such similarity in processed-food consumption within personal networks (Hâncean, Lerner et al., 2025). It shows that eating is socially patterned, but not who is influencing whom.

A rural community in Romania is an informative site for our questions. Rural populations in Eastern Europe are older than urban ones, carry a high burden of diet-related cardiovascular disease, and have more limited access to health services (Hâncean, McCarty et al., 2026). Informal advice is, therefore, likely to be a routine part of managing health. Households are frequently multigenerational, meals are shared, and residents know one another across overlapping ties. Personal network studies in this setting have documented the social clustering of smoking and diet (Mihăilă et al., 2024; Hâncean, Lerner et al., 2025).

Our study asks three questions: (1) Which contact and relationship characteristics are associated with health advice seeking and attempted dietary influence? (2) How much do the two relations overlap, and do their associations differ in direct tests? (3) Are advisers healthier than those consulting them, according to respondents' perceptions and advisers' own reports? Addressing these questions, we make a threefold contribution. First, by examining whether advisory standing reflects perceived or self-reported health conduct or relational position. Second, by connecting gendered care and cooking responsibility to advice and attempted dietary influence. And, thirdly, by comparing these relations within the same personal networks. We underline that our article focuses on reported relationships and attempts, not on whether advice was followed or behaviour changed. Consequently, we cannot establish causality.

Methods

Study setting and design

This study uses personal network data collected in a rural commune of approximately 4,330 residents (3,808 adults) in Arge County, Romania, as part of an EU longitudinal project on primary cancer prevention (the 4P-CAN project; https://4p-can.eu/). The project follows an open-cohort longitudinal design with four waves of personal network interviews between September 2023 and September 2025 (Hâncean, McCarty et al., 2026). The analysis focuses on Wave 3 (March-April 2025), in which both health advice seeking and attempted dietary influence were recorded. Wave 4 (September 2025) examines the consistency of the advice findings under a different elicitation procedure and, for respondents interviewed in both waves, change over time.

Recruitment followed a link-tracing procedure from six purposively selected seeds who varied in sex, age, education, income, and employment. Participants recommended other eligible residents, whom the research team contacted. Eligibility required participants to be at least 18 years old, have lived in the community for at least six months, and provide informed consent. The sample is non-probability-based and not intended to represent the adult population. Wave 3 included 89 respondents, who described 1,625 respondent-alter relationships. Wave 4 included 87 respondents (67 returning, 20 first-time) and 2,529 relationships.

Personal network data collection

Interviews were conducted face-to-face by trained interviewers using Network Canvas (Birkett et al., 2021), with name generators whose design evolved across waves (Hâncean, Lerner et al., 2026). Wave 3 asked for at least 25 contacts without a maximum cap. In Wave 3, respondents (egos) nominated adults (alters) living in the community with whom they communicated frequently, with separate prompts for household members, colleagues, relatives, and friends (with a distinction between contacts met face-to-face daily or weekly and those contacted daily or weekly by telephone or internet). Returning respondents generated their networks without being shown their earlier nominations. The median network contained 17 alters (range 2-48). In Wave 4, returning respondents reviewed their full historical roster. Namely, they reported contact frequency, emotional closeness, village residence, and years of acquaintance for each alter and answered the health-related items (shared meals, cardiovascular condition, and processed-food consumption) only for alters they met daily or weekly. First-time respondents followed the Wave 3 protocol, and only they were asked the dietary-influence item.

Individuals appearing across multiple networks or waves were tracked using harmonised identifiers matched manually from names, sociodemographic, and relational information. If an alter was nominated by two respondents, they contributed two observations while remaining identifiable as one person. For alters who also participated as respondents, interview data was linked to the reports about them, keeping perception and self-report distinct. Co-residence and kinship were taken as the last recorded value up to Wave 3 if not re-asked. The explicit kin relation was recorded only in Wave 1 for alters first nominated then. While households were not recorded as units, respondents reported whether they lived in the same house as each contact. Because individuals carried a single identifier across networks, we chained these reports: two respondents living with the same person were assigned to the same household, along with anyone else linked this way. Respondents with no co-residence links formed one-person households.

Outcome measures

Two directed, respondent-reported relations were recorded for every alter in the Wave 3 roster. "Health advice seeking" indicates whether the respondent turned to this alter for health advice: a reported advisory relationship, not its frequency, content, or quality. "Attempted dietary influence" indicates whether this alter tried to change how the respondent ate: a reported attempt, regardless of whether the respondent changed their eating. Neither measure establishes the temporal sequence of the two activities.

Explanatory variables

Alter characteristics, as reported by the respondent, were sex, age, education (up to eight years; nine years to post-secondary; tertiary), retirement, a cardiovascular condition, participation in sport, and whether the alter was also a Wave 3 respondent. Perceived health was measured by a ten-point rating of the alter's health and by whether the alter was described as eating processed food daily or weekly. Relationship characteristics were co-residence, kinship outside the household, daily or weekly face-to-face contact, emotional closeness, sharing meals daily or weekly, and same sex. Wave 4 added years of acquaintance and village residence. Respondent characteristics were sex, age, education, household size, body mass index (from self-reported height and weight), and the number of eight processed-food categories consumed daily or weekly. Details on the study's variables are available in the Supplementary Material (SM, Section 4.1).

Domestic roles.

Among co-residents, we identified spouses and household cooks. Where available, Wave 2 supplied the cook's person identifier. Otherwise, a respondent's description (e.g., "my wife" or "my mother") was matched to co-residents by relation, sex, and age. A unique match identified the cook, and ambiguous cases remained unassigned. Relative education classified alters as more, equally, or less educated than egos.

Self-reported differences. For 353 of the Wave 3 relationships, the contact had also been interviewed as a respondent, so both parties had reported their own processed-food consumption and body mass index. For each of these relationships, we subtracted the respondent's value from the contact's. A positive difference means that the contact reported eating more processed food or having a higher body mass index than the respondent. This allows advisers to be compared with the people who consult them.

Additionally, two-way clustering and bootstrapping yield the same conclusions.

The household cook and the spouse

Within the 133 co-resident ties (Table 3), the cook is consulted in 30 of 37 cases (81%), against 26 of 96 other household members (27%). Net of alter sex, age, and spousal status, being the cook raises the probability of being the ego's adviser by 40 percentage points (Model 3a: 95% CI 20, 60), and being the spouse by 46 points (95% CI 27, 64). Respondent-clustered estimates (Model 3b) are practically identical. All 13 spouses who cook are advisers, compared with eight of the 11 who do not. The corresponding estimates for attempted dietary influence (Model 3c) are positive but imprecise (cook: +14, 95% CI -7, 34; spouse: +15, 95% CI -6, 35). Among the 1,330 ties with a recorded kin relation, only the spouse has an association of its own: net of alter sex, education, age, contact, and closeness, a spouse is 46 points more likely than a non-kin alter to be consulted (95% CI 21, 72), whereas the associations for parents (+28 to +3) and children (+16 to +1) disappear once contact and closeness are controlled (SM, Table S6).

Are advisers healthier than those who consult them?

Egos rate their advisers 0.22 points higher on a ten-point health scale (z = 2.5) and tend to describe them less often as eating processed food weekly (-9.5, 95% CI -20.0, 1.0; Wave 4: -10.7, 95% CI -20.9, -0.6; SM, Table S10). In the 353 ties in which both parties are respondents (SM, Table S7), the difference in self-reported processed-food consumption is not associated with being consulted (-0.7 per item, 95% CI -1.9, 0.5) and only weakly, in the expected direction, with attempted influence (-0.9, 95% CI -1.9, 0.1). The difference in BMI is associated with neither. With 55 and 35 nominations, these models are imprecise. At the ego level, the share of a network attempting dietary influence rises with the ego's BMI (+0.3 points per unit, 95% CI 0.0, 0.5) and is unrelated to a cardiovascular condition (SM, Table S8).

Three further analyses are reported in full in the SM. Among respondents already in contact, advice ties concentrate within households (coefficient 2.94, SE 0.62) and show no evidence of reciprocity (-0.44, SE 0.82) or triadic closure (0.27, SE 0.38), although with 55 ties these tests have limited power (SM, Sections 12 and 15.9). In Wave 4, the same characteristics predict nominations, with larger associations for co-residence and closeness and a non-zero contact-level variance (SM, Table S2). Among the 20 first-time respondents, tertiary education (+12.5) and closeness (+12.0) again predict attempted influence (SM, Table S9). Among the 61 egos interviewed in both waves, those who reported an influencer in Wave 3 (n = 24) did not differ from the others in the change in processed-food consumption, BMI, or dieting by Wave 4. However, the design has a power of only 0.21 to detect a reduction of half a processed-food item and 0.61 for one item (SM, Sections 14 and 15.10).

Discussion

Three findings stand out. Health advice seeking and attempted dietary influence were associated with the same social and relational characteristics (education, frequent interaction, closeness, shared meals), and advice seeking was also associated with co-residence, marital relationship, and responsibility for cooking. Although the two activities differed in prevalence and only partially overlapped, no characteristic was more strongly associated with one than the other. Respondents perceived their advisers as healthier, but the advisers' own reports gave no evidence that they were. Informal health authority thus appears to rest on relational position and perceived characteristics rather than on demonstrably healthier conduct.

The relational organisation of health advice

Most respondents named an adviser, yet only one in ten ties involved advice. This selective mobilisation is consistent with work on functional specificity in personal networks (McKinlay, 1973; Pescosolido, 1992; Perry and Pescosolido, 2010; Reiner et al., 2025).

Two features of the selection deserve emphasis. First, respondents were more likely to consult contacts more educated than themselves and less likely to consult those less educated, controlling for co-residence, kinship, contact, closeness, and shared meals. This pattern also held in Wave 4. This is compatible with the idea that advice seeking involves assessments of relative competence (Agneessens and Wittek, 2012; Blau, 1954), although educational differences may also carry social hierarchies, confidence in communication, or the perceived legitimacy of offering guidance rather than health expertise. Second, shared meals showed an association of their own after controlling for co-residence, contact frequency, and closeness. Also, in Wave 4, commensality was the strongest relationship feature among daily contacts. Meals create repeated occasions on which eating is visible and can potentially affect health (Sobal and Nelson, 2003), although we cannot establish the direction of the association here. Health advice was not confined to the household: nearly two-thirds of advisers lived elsewhere, and relatives living elsewhere were consulted more often than non-kin.

Gendered care, domestic work, and advisory standing

Female contacts were more likely to be nominated as advisers, controlling for education and relational characteristics. This is consistent with the gendered distribution of domestic care, food provision, and responsibility for family health (Umberson, 1992; Clark, 1995). Similar results were also reported in other cultural settings (Reiner et al., 2025). The household analyses provide more direct evidence. Among co-resident contacts, the cook and the spouse were much more likely to be nominated, controlling for the alter's sex and age. The kin analysis reinforces the point: the spouse retained a substantial association after controlling for contact and closeness, whereas parents and children were consulted only because they were everyday, close contacts.

Our findings do not suggest that food preparation confers authority over dietary decisions. The distinction between performing food-related care and controlling consumption is supported in the literature (Blissett and Haycraft, 2008). Cooking responsibility and spousal status were strongly associated with being consulted, whereas their associations with attempted dietary influence were positive but too imprecise to characterise. Whether domestic roles also carry attempts to regulate another person's eating remains open.

Advice seeking and attempted dietary influence: distinct activities, similar relationships

The two relations differed in prevalence and only partially overlapped. Yet the overlap was far greater than their prevalence alone would produce. Being an adviser raised the probability of being reported as an influencer from under five per cent to about twelve per cent, controlling for other characteristics. Some relationships thus accommodate several forms of health-related involvement, even though someone may not request or welcome an attempt to change their eating.

None of the 14 predictor-specific differences was statistically significant after Holm adjustment. The two effects that were suggestive before adjustment (the alter's participation in sport, associated with influence but not advice, and the respondent's household size) were not significant after it. The findings therefore do not establish that the two activities have different relational foundations, nor does the absence of significant differences demonstrate equivalence. Among the measured characteristics, both activities were associated with the same social and relational contexts. The same relationships create opportunities for both guidance and attempts at change. Whether an interaction is experienced as advice, care, persuasion, or pressure may depend on unmeasured content.

One asymmetry appeared on the respondent side. The share of a network reported attempting dietary influence rose with the respondent's body mass index and tended to rise with processed-food consumption, but it was unrelated to a cardiovascular condition. Among firsttime respondents in Wave 4, body mass index was again associated with being an influence target. Research on health-related social control has found that attempts to regulate a partner's behaviour are directed at those perceived to need it and are shaped by the target's weight (Novak and Webster, 2011; Brunson et al., 2014). Our results suggest that dietary pressure in this community responds to visible body size rather than diagnosed risk, although the estimates are based on 89 respondents.

Perceived health, self-reported behaviour, and the basis of informal authority

Respondents rated nominated advisers as healthier than other contacts and tended to describe them as eating processed food less often. This is consistent with the possibility that perceived conduct contributes to advisory standing (Valente and Pumpuang, 2007). The analyses using independently reported characteristics did not provide corresponding evidence. Namely, among relationships in which both parties were interviewed, differences in self-reported processed-food consumption and body mass index were not associated with being consulted, and only weakly with attempted influence. This should be interpreted with care, as self-reports were available only when both individuals participated, nomination counts were small, and the two measures only partially capture health-related knowledge or exemplary conduct. The absence of an association is a lack of evidence that advisers differ, not evidence that they are alike. A US survey found that people who give health advice to many others report somewhat healthier behaviours (Colón-Ramos et al., 2009). Our comparison, using the reports of both parties to the same relationship, found no such difference.

Professional authority is institutionally credentialled, whereas informal advisory standing must be established within relationships (Freidson, 1960) and may reflect perceived knowledge, experience of illness, educational position, trust, familiarity, or responsibility for care (Prior, 2003). Our findings do not isolate these sources, but they show that the three positional ones (i.e., relative education, relational proximity, and domestic responsibility) are each associated with being consulted, while the exemplary one (healthier conduct) is perceived and not confirmed. Informal health authority in this community is thus not an attribute of the adviser but a feature of where the adviser stands in relation to the person asking. Identifying frequently consulted individuals reveals socially recognised sources of guidance, not people who hold accurate information or follow healthier practices. Interventions working through personal networks therefore need to account for what advisers communicate and not assume that standing indicates expertise.

Network structure, consistency across waves, and change over time

Advice ties among respondents clustered within households but showed no clear reciprocity or triadic closure. Nevertheless, the small network limits inference. Wave 4 showed broadly similar correlates, but its changed roster and overlapping respondents make it a within-sample consistency check, not an independent replication. The 61-person panel found no association between reported influence and later dietary or BMI outcomes. On the other hand, this analysis had limited power and recorded attempts rather than their intensity or reception. Consequently, it does not establish that influence is ineffective.

Limitations

The respondents were recruited by link-tracing in one rural community, not by probability sampling. The main findings come from comparisons among the contacts within each respondent's network. Thus, the two outcomes and the contact characteristics were recorded for every contact named. The contacts are those the respondents named under the generator's rules, so they represent frequent local relationships rather than all of a person's ties. The outcomes are what respondents reported, not observed interactions, and the characteristics they reported about their contacts may be biased by the relationship itself (e.g., advisers may be described more favourably). The cross-sectional design cannot establish causal direction. Attempted dietary influence was reported for only 78 relationships. Penalised estimation addressed this, but several estimates for this outcome remained imprecise. Some analyses are based on subsets or reconstructed measures, i.e., the cook was identified for 38 respondents, kin relations only for contacts first named in Wave 1, and self-reports only for the 353 relationships in which both parties were interviewed. Future research could interview both parties in each relationship, ask respondents directly how knowledgeable and trustworthy they consider each contact, and follow networks over longer periods.

Conclusion

Health advice seeking and attempted dietary influence differ in prevalence and overlap only partially. Yet they share the same social and relational correlates. Health advice seeking is connected to relative educational standing, relational proximity, and domestic roles, including the marital relationship and responsibility for cooking. Attempts at dietary influence seem to be directed at heavier respondents (higher BMI) and not at those with diagnosed conditions. Respondents perceive advisers as healthier, but advisers' own reports do not confirm this.

Informal health authority in this community rests on where people stand in relation to one another more than on their observable health-related conduct.
