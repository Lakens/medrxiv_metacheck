Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract Introduction

Crimean-Congo haemorrhagic fever (CCHF) is endemic in Afghanistan, but multi-hospital data characterising reported exposure patterns, healthcare-worker clustering, and ribavirin-mortality associations after accounting for treatment timing are lacking.

Methods

A retrospective cohort study included 737 laboratory-confirmed CCHF patients admitted to six tertiary hospitals in Afghanistan between January 2022 and December 2025. Reported exposures were assessed by occupation and tick-contact history. Clustering of healthcare-worker (HCW) infections during high caseload was assessed by permutation testing. Ribavirin-mortality association was assessed using multivariable Cox regression adjusted for clinical severity, age, sex, hospital, and comorbidity, with sensitivity analyses addressing treatment timing immortaltime bias.

Results

Among 737 patients, the median age was 35 years and 78.3% were male. The hospitalization fatality ratio (HFR) was 27.7% (204/737). Of 574 patients with recorded occupation: 33.6% were livestock handlers, 12.9% slaughterhouse workers or butchers, 24.9% household meat preparers, and only 11.1% reported tick exposure. Nineteen patients were HCWs, and 18 became infected during periods of concurrent CCHF admissions, and clustering exceeded chance (p=0.012), consistent with healthcare-associated exposure during surges. Personal protective equipment (PPE) was used in 728 (98.6%) admissions, but its composition was incomplete: 532 (72.2%) comprised mask and gloves without a gown, and only 205 (27.8%) with mask, gloves, and gown.

Isolation rooms were available for only 274 of 737 admissions (37.2%). Among 727 patients in the ribavirin analysis, 85.0% received treatment. Conventional analysis suggested lower mortality (adjusted HR 0.57) but affected by immortal-time bias. The association weakened for same-day treatment versus no treatment (HR 0.63, 95% CI 0.41-0.96; sensitive to model specification) and was absent in time-varying analysis (HR 0.93).

Introduction

Crimean-Congo haemorrhagic fever (CCHF), caused by an orthonairovirus transmitted primarily by Hyalomma ticks, is one of the most widely distributed viral haemorrhagic fevers, with documented human infections across more than 30 countries in Africa, the Balkans, the Middle East, and Central Asia [1] [2] [3] [4] [5]. Case fatality rates in hospitalised patients range from 5% to 40%, varying by clinical setting, severity distribution, and access to supportive care [6] [7]. The global burden of CCHF is substantially underestimated due to surveillance gaps in endemic low-income countries, where diagnostic capacity and reporting infrastructure are limited [3, [8] [9].

The geographic range of CCHF is expanding due to climate change, rising temperatures, land-use change, and migratory bird dispersal of the Hyalomma tick vectors, which have driven range expansion into previously non-endemic regions, including autochthonous transmission in Spain since 2016 [10] and the detection of established Hyalomma marginatum populations across southern Europe [11] [12]. Climate projections indicate continued northward spread under moderate warming scenarios [13] [14]. In parallel, endemic regions face escalating case burdens: Afghanistan [15] [16], Türkiye [17] [18], Iran (Islamic Republic of) [19] and Pakistan [20], have all reported increasing incidence over the past decade, attributed to growing unregulated movement and unsafe handling of livestock populations, disrupted vector control programmes, and fragile health systems during periods of conflict and political instability.

Afghanistan has reported an increasing burden of CCHF in the past decade. CCHF has been endemic since at least 1998, with spring-summer epidemic cycles driven by overlapping livestock handling and tick activity seasons [15] [16]. National surveillance data from Afghanistan report crude case fatality rates ranging from approximately 8% to over 40%, depending on period and case definition with marked heterogeneity across provinces and documented nosocomial outbreaks [16, [21] [22] [23]. The 2025 season has produced the largest recorded CCHF outbreak in Afghanistan to date, with 1,407 suspected cases and 97 deaths reported by late October (CFR 6.9%), substantially exceeding the 2022-2024 three-year average and compounding pressure on a fragile healthcare system serving a population with limited access to diagnostics, intensive care, and uninterrupted drug supply chains [22].

Three interrelated clinical and epidemiological questions remain inadequately characterised in the Afghanistan context. First, although Hyalomma tick bites are classically implicated in CCHFV transmission, the relative contribution of direct contact with infected animal blood and tissues through occupational slaughter, butchering, and domestic meat preparation has not been documented. In resource-limited settings where tick surveillance is minimal to absent, understanding reported exposure pattern is essential for designing prevention programmes while acknowledging that tick bites may be unnoticed or unrecalled. Second, healthcare-associated CCHF infection is well-documented in other countries in the region, but formal evidence on outbreak-period clustering and associated health-system constraints in Afghanistan hospital settings remains limited [23] [24] [25]. This is the first multi-centre hospital study including data from all CCHF-treating hospitals in Afghanistan. Third, ribavirin is widely recommended for CCHF treatment by WHO, but observational evidence remains mixed and is vulnerable to confounding, facility-level differences, and immortal-time bias. Severity-stratified ribavirin-mortality associations from routine clinical data in Afghanistan have not previously been reported [26] [27] [28].

The objectives of this study are to characterize reported CCHF exposure patterns, healthcareworker infection clustering, and ribavirin-mortality associations in a multi-centre hospital setting.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a retrospective analysis of multi-hospital surveillance and hospital medical records data of all admitted patients with documented CCHF from six public tertiary hospitals in Afghanistan: Balkh Regional Hospital (n=101), Herat Infectious Disease Hospital (n=103), Kunduz Regional Hospital (n=47), Kandahar Regional Hospital (n=39), Nangarhar Regional Hospital (n=52), and the National Infectious Disease Hospital, Kabul (n=395). These hospitals are the regional designated centres for high threat pathogens in Afghanistan. Patients admitted between January 2022 and December 2025, were included. Diagnosis was established by RT-PCR, antigen detection, or ELISA IgM/IgG serology, following the WHO cascade diagnostic pathway.

Ethics approval was obtained from Institutional Review Board of Afghanistan National Public Health Institute [A-3-475]; all data were de-identified prior to analysis (see case report form in Supplementary File 1).

Participants

All 737 hospitalised patients with laboratory-confirmed CCHF were included. The study was a census of all eligible cases during the study period; no formal sample-size calculation was performed. Demographic information, occupation, residential setting, tick exposure history, comorbidities, clinical features, supportive treatments received, and final outcome (survival or inhospital death) were abstracted from standardised case record forms. Occupation was recorded for 574 of 737 patients (77•9%); free-text entries were mapped to five pre-specified categories (farmer or livestock handler; healthcare worker; housewife or household caregiver including meat handling; slaughterhouse worker or butcher; jobless or other). Clinical severity was classified by attending physicians according to clinical criteria as mild, moderate, or severe based on clinical symptoms, haemorrhagic manifestations and laboratory parameters at admission. The severity grading score was used as the method for severity classification as based on earlier studies in Turkey [29].

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were conducted in R Statistical Software (Version 4.3; R Core Team, 2026). Survival analyses used the survival package. Receiver operating characteristic analyses used the pROC package. Spatial analyses used the sf and ggplot2 packages. Continuous variables are reported as median (interquartile range) and categorical variables as count (percentage). Denominators are stated for all proportions and are restricted to patients with the variable recorded; the number of missing observations for each variable is given in the footnote to Table 1. Date fields were recorded in two encodings within the source database and were parsed with explicit day-first interpretation before any interval was derived.

Binomial proportions are reported with 95% confidence intervals computed using the Wilson score method. A two-sided p-value below 0•05 was considered statistically significant throughout. All models were fitted on complete cases, and the analytic denominator is reported for each. No adjustment was made for multiple comparisons: the primary hypothesis was pre-specified, and the severity-stratified and subgroup analyses are reported as exploratory. Because these comprise a substantial number of comparisons, nominally significant subgroup findings carry an inflated probability of type I error and are interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory throughout.

Geographic analysis

Patient residence data were mapped across Afghanistan's 34 provinces using administrative boundary shapefiles from the Global Administrative Data (GADM) database via the geodata package in R. Provinces were grouped into six geographic regions based on Afghanistan's established administrative structure. Regional hospitalization fatality ratios (HFRs) were defined as the proportion of deaths among all hospitalized laboratory-confirmed patients, were calculated for patients residing in each region across all study hospitals combined (Supplementary Figure 1). Spatial analyses were conducted in R (Version 4.3; R Core Team, 2026) using the sf and ggplot2 packages.

Reported exposure route analysis

Tick exposure was self-reported by patients or proxies at admission. Occupational categories were pre-specified in the case record form: farmer or livestock handler; healthcare worker (HCW); housewife or household caregiver including meat handling; slaughterhouse worker or butcher; and jobless or other. To describe the reported exposure, we calculated the proportion reporting tick exposure within each occupational group and compared aggregate exposure patterns across hospitals (Supplementary Table 1).

Healthcare associated infection analysis

Healthcare workers were identified by occupational classification in the case record form. For each healthcare worker case, we defined a likely exposure window as the 5-6 days preceding symptom onset, consistent with the reported CCHF incubation period following contact exposure [1, 5, 7, 24]. We enumerated all non-healthcare worker CCHF patients concurrently hospitalised at the same facility during each healthcare worker's exposure window. To test whether healthcare worker infections clustered temporally during periods of elevated CCHF patient volume, we performed a permutation test (1,000 iterations) comparing the observed mean number of concurrent CCHF inpatients during healthcare worker exposure windows to a null distribution generated by randomly reassigning healthcare worker symptom-onset dates within the observed range of patient hospitalisations at each facility. A two-sided p-value below 0•05 was considered evidence of nonrandom temporal clustering.

Ribavirin-mortality association analysis

Ribavirin receipt was defined using the date of ribavirin initiation recorded in the case record form; patients with a recorded ribavirin start date were classified as treated, including those who received only an initial dose or were treated for one day or less. Ribavirin timing was calculated as days from hospital admission to treatment initiation Treatment duration was not used as an exposure variable because it is truncated by death and is therefore partly a consequence rather than a determinant of outcome. Ribavirin dose was not analysed because dose units were inconsistently recorded across sites.

The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality. We estimated the association between ribavirin receipt and mortality using a multivariable Cox proportional hazards model with time from hospital admission to death or discharge as the survival time. The conventional model adjusted for clinical severity classification (i.e., mild, moderate, severe, further detailed below), age, sex, hospital, and pre-existing comorbidity. Hospital was included as a fixed effect to control for facility-level case-mix confounding identified through exploratory analysis (Supplementary Table 3). The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals to assess the temporal stability of specific covariate effects.

To investigate discordance between the unadjusted and adjusted mortality estimates, we conducted a stratified log-rank test stratifying by hospital, which provides a non-parametric assessment of the ribavirin survival association after controlling for hospital case-mix without parametric model assumptions (Supplementary Table 4). A logistic regression model was used to examine whether the probability of ribavirin receipt differed by hospital., ruling out reverse causation as an alternative explanation for the adjusted estimate.

Unadjusted Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated for ribavirin-treated and untreated patients overall and within each clinical severity stratum, with log-rank tests used to assess unadjusted differences in survival. These unadjusted analyses are presented in Supplementary Methods and Supplementary Figures 3 4to illustrate the extent and source of discordance between crude and adjusted estimates.

Sensitivity and bias analyses

A large proportion (85%) of patients received ribavirin and treatment was defined by a recorded start date, classification as treated required survival to the point of treatment, creating a risk of immortal-time bias. It was addressed with a hierarchy of pre-specified analyses. The primary analysis compared patients treated on the day of admission (for whom exposure and follow-up both began at time zero, so that no immortal time can arise) with patients who never received ribavirin. Additionally, ribavirin was modelled as a time-varying exposure using the Mantel-Byar approach, splitting each patient's follow-up at the recorded treatment time so that person-time before treatment was classified as untreated; this analysis was repeated with the time origin set at symptom onset with left truncation at admission. Landmark analyses at days 1, 2, and 3, reclassifying exposure according to treatment status at each landmark, were performed as further sensitivity analyses. Effect modification by the interval from symptom onset to admission was tested to assess a possible therapeutic window. For all models the estimate with and without the treating-hospital term is reported, and baseline characteristics of treated and untreated patients were compared to establish the direction of confounding by indication. The conventional model treating ribavirin as a fixed baseline covariate is reported only to quantify the magnitude of immortal-time bias

Mortality predictor analysis

To identify independent admission-level predictors of in-hospital mortality, we fitted a multivariable Cox proportional hazards model using only variables available at the time of hospital admission, excluding ribavirin and all treatment variables to avoid reverse causality. Candidate predictors were selected based on clinical plausibility and published CCHF prognostic literature: age, sex, clinical severity category, admission platelet count (per 10,000/μL), Aspartate Aminotransferase (AST) greater than 200 U/L, any haemorrhagic symptom at admission (haematemesis, melaena, epistaxis, haematuria, or petechiae or purpura), neurological involvement (confusion or seizures), and pre-existing comorbidity. White Blood Cell (WBC) count below 3×10⁹/L was identified as a candidate but was present in only three patients and was therefore excluded from the multivariable model due to insufficient power. The model was additionally adjusted for hospital as a fixed effect. Discriminatory performance was assessed using the concordance statistic. To confirm that identified predictors reflected intrinsic disease severity rather than differential treatment access, we assessed hazard ratio stability by comparing estimates with and without ribavirin in the model and repeated the analysis in the ribavirin-treated cohort only.

Severity classification analysis

To assess whether haemodynamic and laboratory markers improve mortality discrimination beyond the clinical severity classification, we constructed a series of logistic regression models with in-hospital death as the outcome. Models were built incrementally: clinical severity classification alone; plus circulatory failure markers (admission platelet count, vasopressor requirement, blood transfusion, and platelet transfusion); plus hepatic and haematological laboratory markers (AST greater than 200 U/L, WBC below 3×10⁹/L, haemoglobin below 10 g/dL); and plus bedside clinical markers (haemorrhagic symptoms, neurological involvement, oxygen requirement, and comorbidity). Hospital was excluded from all classification models as it is not a patient-level severity criterion. Discriminatory performance was quantified using the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. Differences between models were assessed using DeLong's test for paired receiver operating characteristic curves. A separate withinstratum analysis was conducted restricted to patients classified as moderate by clinical criteria to assess the extent of severity heterogeneity within this classification.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 737 patients were hospitalized across the six public hospitals between January 2022 and December 2025. The median age was 35 years (IQR 24-48); 577 (78•3%) were male. Significant variation in patient age, sex distribution, and occupational profile was observed across hospitals (all p < 0•05, Table 1 and Figure 1A). Among 204 CCHF deaths, documented causes (n=195, 95%) predominantly involved overlapping pathophysiologic mechanisms, with sepsis-multiorgan failure being the most common combination (45, 23%), followed by isolated hemorrhagic shock (35, 18%) and the triad of sepsis, hemorrhagic shock, and multiorgan failure (27, 14%) (Figure 1a). The hospitalization fatality ratios (HFRs) which is distinct from the population case fatality rate, and cannot be compared directly with it, differed markedly by hospital, ranging from 8•7% at Herat Infectious Disease Hospital to 40•6% at Balkh Regional Hospital (overall 27•7%; p < 0•001; Table 1). Only 64 study subjects (8•7%) reported having any known tick exposure.

All 737 patients underwent RT-PCR, which was positive in 622 (84•4%). Of the 115 RT-PCRnegative patients, 98 were confirmed by IgM serology and 6 by antigen detection alone; a further 6 were IgG-positive only, and 5 had no positive confirmatory result (Table 1). Overall, 100 patients (13•6%) were IgM-positive and 7 (0•9%) antigen-positive, including 3 patients positive on more than one assay. RT-PCR confirmation rates varied substantially by hospital, ranging from 44•6% at Balkh Regional Hospital to 100•0% at Herat Infectious Disease Hospital. Other facilities achieved RT-PCR confirmation rates of 97•9% (Kunduz Regional Hospital), 89•9% (National Infectious Disease Hospital), 89•7% (Kandahar Regional Hospital), and 73•1% (Nangarhar Regional Hospital). IgM serology served as the predominant diagnostic modality at Balkh Regional Hospital (55/101 patients, 54•5%), compensating for the low RT-PCR confirmation rate at this facility. IgM testing also contributed to case confirmation at Nangarhar Regional Hospital (14/52, 26•9%), Kandahar Regional Hospital (4/39, 10•3%), and National Infectious Disease Hospital (26/395, 6•6%). Antigen detection was performed exclusively at the National Infectious Disease Hospital, confirming 7 of 395 cases (1•8%) at this facility (Table 1).

Seasonal trends of CCHF hospitalization

Marked seasonality was observed across all three epidemic seasons, with peak admissions consistently occurring between May and August (Figure 1A). Admissions increased substantially over the study period: monthly admissions peaked at 3 patients in May 2022, 38 patients in July 2023, 117 patients in June 2024, rising to 127 in June 2025. Among 195 of 204 fatal cases with documented cause of death, sepsis (26•2%), multi-organ failure (20•5%), and haemorrhagic shock (8•8%) were the principal attributed causes, with the majority of deaths involving comorbidities (Figure 1B). HFRs varied substantially by hospital and year (Figure 1C). Balkh Regional Hospital recorded the highest mortality ratio across all seasons (approximately 40%), whereas Herat Infectious Disease Hospital consistently recorded the lowest (approximately 9%).

Clinical presentations and treatments

Of the symptoms, fever (734/737, 99.6%), headache (713/737, 96.7%), myalgia (669/737, 90.8%), and fatigue (625/737, 84.8%) were the most prevalent, which were jointly present in 566 patients (76.8%), of whom 180 ultimately died (31.8% mortality rate). Haemorrhagic manifestations were common: epistaxis was reported in 230 patients (31.2%), haematemesis in 99 (13.4%), petechiae or purpura in 259 (35.1%), melena in 67 (9.1%), and haematuria in 38 (5.2%). Any hemorrhagic manifestation was present in 499 patients (67•7%) with 176 deaths (35•3% mortality).

Neurological features including confusion (337/737, 45•7%) with 131 deaths (38•9% mortality) and seizures (161/737, 21•8%) (Figure 2A). Symptom intersections involving confusion, seizures, petechiae, and haematemesis were significantly more commonly associated with fatal outcomes (Figure 2A). High-risk of death combinations included confusion plus seizures (80 patients, 49•7% mortality, RR 1•6) and petechiae plus haematemesis (36 patients, 66•7% mortality, RR 2•1) compared to the constitutional tetrad (31•8% mortality). Patients with both hemorrhagic and neurological symptoms had the highest mortality risk (274 patients, 44•9% mortality, RR 3•6) compared to those with constitutional symptoms only (137 patients, 12•4% mortality). Treatment patterns showed that supportive therapies were commonly used in combination, with ribavirin administration frequently accompanied by intravenous fluids, oxygen support, and other supportive measures (Figure 2B).

Healthcare system challenges

Documented healthcare system challenges were nearly universal and reported amongst almost all hospitalized patients (733/737, 99•5%). Ribavirin stockouts were experienced by 119 patients (16•1%), with the highest burden at the National Infectious Disease Hospital (92/395, 23•3%) and Herat Infectious Disease Hospital (17/103, 16•5%). A documented PPE shortage was recorded for 625 patients (84•8%), reported at every site and universally at Nangarhar (52/52) and Mirwais (39/39). A documented ICU shortage for 399 (54•1%), and cultural barriers to care for 207 (28•1%). The scarcity of organ support was near-absolute rather than relative: among 736 patients with the field recorded, one (0•1%) was admitted to intensive care, and none received mechanical ventilation or dialysis at any site during the four-year period. Supportive care therefore consisted almost entirely of (731/735, 99•5%), antibiotics (722/736, 98•1%), oxygen (282/735, 38•4%), vasopressors (103/736, 14•0%), and blood products (235/734, 32•0%). Access to blood products was the one component of supportive care that differed substantially between sites, ranging from 74•3% of admissions at Balkh to 11•5% at Nangarhar. These systemic deficits likely contributed to the elevated HFRs observed at Balkh Regional Hospital (40•6%) relative to other sites (Table 1). The elevated HFR at Balkh Regional Hospital (40•6%) relative to other sites (Table 1) did not follow the pattern of documented resource deficits, as Balkh had the highest access to blood products and the lowest documented PPE shortage, and may reflect differences in case mix, referral pattern and diagnostic practice.

Patient-reported risk factors

Although only a minority of patients reported exposure to ticks (64/737, 8•7%), more than half of patients with recorded occupation reported potential occupational or domestic exposures involving regular contact with animal blood or tissues. Occupation was recorded for 574 of 737 patients (77•9%); the following proportions are expressed over that denominator, including: farming or handling of livestock (193/574, 33•6%); slaughterhouse or butchering work (74/574, 12•9%); or other household exposure involving domestic meat preparation (143/574, 24•9%). Tick exposure was most commonly reported by among farmer-livestock handlers (31/193, 16•1%) and shepherds (1/7, 14•3%), followed by slaughterhouse workers (9/74, 12•2%), butchers, and housewives (12/143, 8•4%), who nonetheless comprised a substantial fraction of cases (Supplementary Table 1). Because occupation was missing for 163 patients (22•1%) and tick-exposure context was missing for 681 (92•4%), these distributions describe reported exposures among patients with available data and should not be interpreted as confirmed transmission routes. Spatial variation in reports of tick exposure was substantial. More than half of patients at Kunduz and Kandahar hospitals reported tick bites (53•2% and 51•3%, respectively) compared to none at Balkh and Nangarhar regional hospitals (p < 0•001).

Healthcare associated infection clustering among healthcare workers

Nineteen of 737 patients (2•6%) were Health Care Workers (HCWs). Infections were geographically concentrated: 13 of 19 (68•4%) occurred at Balkh Regional Hospital, despite that facility treating only 13•7% of the total cohort (101/737; Table 1). Two distinct outbreak periods were observed at Balkh: June 2024 (2 HCW infections during 22 admissions, attack rate 9•1%) and April-July 2025 (11 HCW infections during 47 admissions, attack rate 23•4%). At the National Infectious Disease Hospital, four HCWs were infected during a May-June 2025 surge with 111 admissions over the two-month period (attack rate 3•6%) and up to 49 concurrent CCHF patients.

Single cases occurred at Kandahar Regional Hospital (June 2025; 1 HCW infection during 25 admissions, attack rate 4•0%) and Nangarhar Regional Hospital (June 2024; 1 HCW infection during 4 admissions, attack rate 25•0%).

Temporal clustering analysis demonstrated that 18 of 19 HCW infections (94•7%) occurred while at least one CCHF patient was concurrently hospitalized at the same facility (exposure window 3-10 days before HCW symptom onset). The median number of concurrent CCHF patients during HCW exposure windows was 8 (IQR 3-14; range 0-49). The single HCW with no concurrent patient admissions during their exposure window had no reported tick exposure and died. The permutation test indicated that HCW infections clustered significantly in periods of elevated patient volume (observed mean concurrent patients: 11•4 vs. expected: 8•7; p = 0•012), consistent with increased healthcare-associated exposure during outbreak surges; specific transmission events were not documented, and needlestick, splash and mucosal exposures were not recorded (Supplementary Table 2, Supplementary Figure 2).

Ribavirin-morality association

Of 737 patients with confirmed CCHF admitted across six hospitals, 10 were excluded due to missing admission or outcome dates (n=8 missing date of outcome, n=2 missing date of admission), and a further 2 because ribavirin status was not recorded, leaving 725 patients in the ribavirin analysis. Of these, 618 (85•2%) received ribavirin and 107 (14•8%) did not. Among 617 treated patients with both admission and treatment start dates recorded, ribavirin was initiated on the day of admission in 442 (71•6%; median day 0, IQR 0-1). The median interval from symptom onset to ribavirin initiation was 5 days (IQR 3-7), mirroring the median onset-to-admission interval of 5 days (IQR 3-7). Treatment duration, reported descriptively, was 10 days or longer in 202 of 618 treated patients (32•7%), 5-9 days in 250 (40•5%), 2-4 days in 106 (17•2%), and 1 day or less in 45 (7•3%), with 15 (2•4%) not recorded. Mortality rose steeply as recorded duration fell (42/45, 93•3% among those treated for ≤1 day versus 25/202, 12•4% for ≥10 days), a gradient that reflects truncation of treatment by death rather than a dose-response relationship. The conventional multivariable Cox proportional hazards model, adjusted for clinical severity, age, sex, hospital, and comorbidity, treating ribavirin as a fixed baseline covariate suggested lower mortality hazard (HR 0•57, 95% CI 0•38-0•85; p=0•006; C-statistic 0•753) (Figure 4), but this estimate is vulnerable to immortal-time bias and is presented for comparability with prior studies. The proportional hazards assumption was satisfied for all covariates and globally (Schoenfeld residual test, global p=0•50, Figure 3). Unadjusted Kaplan-Meier curves showed crossing of survival functions and a non-significant log-rank test overall (p=0•30) and within the Moderate clinical severity stratum (p=0•348), consistent with hospital-level differences in case mix and resources.

The unadjusted log-rank test was non-significant (p=0•30), reflecting hospital-level case-mix confounding. Balkh Regional Hospital contributed 34 patients classified as moderate by clinical criteria who had universal vasopressor requirements (100%), a median admission platelet count of 23,500/μL, and a HFR of 61•8% (21/34) that substantially exceeds that of patients classified as severe at other institutions (39•6%) and is consistent with haemodynamic instability not captured by the haemorrhage-based clinical severity criteria. Ribavirin receipt did not predict hospital allocation within the moderate clinical severity stratum (logistic regression p=0•607), indicating that the between-hospital contrast was explained by differential prescribing within that stratum (Supplementary Table 3 4).

Severity-stratified ribavirin analyses

These subgroup hazard ratios derive from the conventional baseline-covariate model and share its immortal-time bias; they are presented as exploratory and are not adjusted for multiple comparisons. Untreated patients were not systematically more severely ill than treated patients at presentation. Among the 111 patients who did not receive ribavirin, none required vasopressors (0% versus 16•5% of treated patients), median admission platelet count was higher (35,000 versus 26,500/μL), median AST was lower (113 versus 166 U/L), and a smaller proportion were classified as severe (38•7% versus 44•1%). Haemorrhagic manifestations (65•8% versus 68•1%), neurological involvement (45•9% versus 45•7%), and comorbidity (6•3% versus 5•0%) were comparable.

Confounding by indication in this cohort therefore operates in the direction of biasing the ribavirin estimate towards the null rather than away from it (Table 2).

The conventional estimate was substantially attributable to immortal-time bias. Among treated patients, ribavirin was recorded as starting after the day of admission in 175 (28%), yet the median length of stay among patients who died was 2 days (IQR 1-4), with 82 deaths within 24 hours of admission; classifying ribavirin at baseline therefore credited the treated group with survival time that necessarily preceded treatment. Discussion

Exposure and the One Health interface

Tick exposure was reported by only 8•7% of patients, against 51-72% in Turkish clinical series [30] [31] [32] and 26-70% in smaller Iranian series [33] [34]. This difference can be interpreted as recalled exposure rather than proof of a different transmission ecology: Hyalomma nymphs detach unnoticed, and severely ill or fatally affected patients often cannot provide an exposure history.

What the data show positively is that occupational and household animal contact dominated the reported exposures -livestock handling, slaughter, and domestic meat preparation together accounting for most patients with an occupation recorded. Because infected livestock remain asymptomatic, these enzootic cycles go undetected, and interventions at the animal-human interface should therefore be strengthened alongside, not instead of, tick-bite prevention. The rise in monthly admissions over the study period, peaking each year in the one to two weeks after Eidal-Adha, is consistent with intensified livestock contact during animal sacrifice and meat handling, but this interpretation remains ecological and should be tested prospectively [35] [36].

Nosocomial risk and the health-system context

The permutation analysis provides formal statistical evidence that healthcare-worker infections clustered with patient volume during surges (p=0•012), extending descriptive reports from Iran, Türkiye, and Pakistan [24] [25] [37] [38]. The distribution of infections suggests that factors beyond PPE supply were relevant. Balkh Regional Hospital contributed 13 of 19 healthcare-worker cases despite treating only 13•7% of the cohort, and recorded the highest HFR (40•6%), the highest attack rate during the April-July 2025 surge (23•4%), and no isolation capacity (0 of 101 admissions). However, because specific exposure events were not documented, these findings establish temporal clustering consistent with healthcare-associated exposure, not a proven transmission route. Possible mechanisms include patient-to-HCW exposure during care, contaminated sharps, blood products, shared equipment and laboratory exposure, which could not be distinguished in these data. PPE composition remains relevant: across the cohort, protective equipment was usually recorded but often incomplete, with mask and gloves without a gown in 72•2% of admissions and no recorded respirator or face-shield use at any site. Completing PPE composition is low-cost and immediately actionable, whereas isolation capacity requires capital investment and lead time; both are needed and neither substitutes for the other.

The context in which this care was delivered warrants emphasis, because it bears directly on interpretation. Among 737 patients with HFR of 27•7%, one patient was admitted to intensive care and none received mechanical ventilation or dialysis at any site over four years. Organ support was, in effect, unavailable. This shapes what the mortality data can and cannot say: differences in intensive care between "better-resourced" and other centres cannot confound our comparisons, because no such gradient existed, and the fatality ratio reported here describes CCHF managed with supportive care alone.

Ribavirin

Our unadjusted-for-immortal-time estimate (HR 0•57) closely mirrors the pooled relative risk of 0•56 (95% CI 0•35-0•90) from a meta-analysis of observational studies, which was graded as very low certainty because of confounding [26]. The only randomised trial, by contrast, found no mortality benefit (RR 1•13, 95% CI 0•29-4•32), although with 136 participants it was too small to exclude a clinically important effect [39], and a second meta-analysis likewise found no survival advantage (RR 1•06, 95% CI 0•97-1•16) [27]. That a conventional analysis of our cohort reproduces the observational literature so closely suggests that much of the apparent benefit reported previously may reflect the same biases.

We do not interpret these findings as evidence that ribavirin is ineffective. The point estimate in the least-biased analysis remained below one, its significance was borderline and specificationdependent, and with only 34 deaths among untreated patients the study was powered to detect only large effects. The appropriate conclusion is an absence of evidence of large benefit, not evidence of absence; residual confounding by indication -which here would bias towards the null, since untreated patients were marginally less severe -cannot be excluded. Adequately powered randomised trials remain necessary, and observational analyses should model antiviral exposure as time-varying before informing ribavirin allocation policy.

Severity grading

The clinical severity classification discriminated mortality only moderately (AUC 0•65).

Admission AST above 200 U/L was independently associated with death (adjusted HR 4•13, 95% CI 2•87-5•93), consistent with Turkish cohorts in which markedly raised AST predicted death [40], but admission platelet count was not, and we do not propose it as an additional criterion. We had considered vasopressor requirement as a candidate severity marker but withdraw that proposal: vasopressor use was recorded in 99•0% of admissions at Balkh and in 0-2% elsewhere, indicating that this variable reflects site-level documentation practice rather than a patient-level severity gradient and cannot be separated from treating hospital in these data. Prospective, protocolstandardised validation is required before any revision of CCHF severity grading.

Limitations

This retrospective analysis of routinely collected records supports only observational associations.

Several limitations bear on interpretation.

First, the ribavirin comparison rests on a small untreated group (34 deaths), is powered only for large effects, and is subject to residual confounding by indication and to the immortal-time bias detailed above; we therefore report a hierarchy of specifications rather than a single casual estimate. Second, exposure classification is subject to substantial misclassification: tick exposure was self-reported and frequently unrecalled, occupation was missing for 163 patients (22•1%) and tick-exposure context for 681 (92•4%), Reported livestock, slaughtering, butchering, household meat preparation, and occupational exposures should be interpreted as reported histories rather than confirmed routes, and the data cannot distinguish tick exposure, animal exposure, shared household exposure, or secondary household transmission. Third, the healthcare-worker analysis establishes temporal clustering, not a mechanism -needlestick, sharps, splash, and mucosal exposures were not recorded, no serological screening of asymptomatic staff was undertaken, and the biosafety level of sample processing was not documented, so the route of transmission remains unexplained and the attack rate is likely underestimated. Fourth, severity was assigned by attending physicians without central adjudication or a validated instrument, and marked between-site heterogeneity indicates inconsistent thresholds. Fifth, hospital resource differences may have confounded treatment allocation, mortality, and healthcare-worker risk, including differences in isolation capacity, blood products, diagnostic methods, staffing, laboratory capacity, and ribavirin availability. Sixth, all models were complete case, missingness was non-uniform and plausibly related to outcome, and ribavirin dose could not be reported because units were inconsistent across sites. Reasons for non-treatment and for treatment discontinuation were not available, and diagnostic methods differed between sites (IgM serology alone confirmed 54•5% of cases at Balkh). Seventh, this hospitalised tertiary-care cohort excludes pre-hospital and communitymanaged cases, so the 27•7% fatality ratio reflects ascertainment, referral pattern, case-mix and health-system constraints and should not be compared directly with population case-fatality rates of 5-10% from settings with broader surveillance. Generalizability to non-tertiary settings and to other endemic countries with different referral systems and health-system capacity is therefore limited. Finally, no sample-size calculation was performed, as this was a census of eligible cases; confidence intervals are reported throughout so that precision can be judged directly, and subgroup analyses, unadjusted for multiple comparisons, should be regarded as hypothesis-generating.

Conclusion

Three priorities follow. First, CCHF prevention in Afghanistan should strengthen animal-human interface interventions while maintaining tick-bite prevention, because reported tick exposure was likely under-ascertained. Practical measures include protective equipment for slaughter workers and butchers, community education for household meat handlers, veterinary surveillance, and pre-Eid-al-Adha risk reduction under a One Health framework spanning the Ministries of Public Health and Agriculture and Environment. Second, healthcare-associated prevention requires investment in dedicated isolation capacity, adequate staffing, complete PPE, safe sharps and bloodexposure practices, and surge protocols at high-volume regional hospitals; PPE supply alone is insufficient. Third, the apparent survival association of ribavirin in observational data should be interpreted cautiously: these findings do not establish efficacy, and treatment policy should rest on adequately powered trials and analyses that account for treatment timing. Afghanistan's accelerating epidemic and unregulated cross-border livestock movement underscore the need for regional coordination of surveillance, laboratory capacity, isolation capacity, and uninterrupted drug supply.

Personal protective equipment was used in 726 of 736 admissions (98•6%), but its composition was incomplete at most sites: 532 admissions (72•2%) were managed with mask and gloves alone, and only 205 (27•8%) with mask, gloves, and gown. No use of respirators or face shields was recorded at any site. Gown provision was universal at Balkh (101/101) and, Kandahar (39/39), partial at Herat (65/103), and absent at Kunduz, Nangarhar, and the National Infectious Disease Hospital. Isolation rooms were available for 274 of 736 admissions (37•2%), almost all of them at the National Infectious Disease Hospital (259/394, 65•7%); no isolation room was available at Balkh (0/101), Kandahar (0/39), or Nangarhar (0/52). Hand hygiene, waste disposal, disinfection, and injection safety procedures were recorded as in place for between 99•5% and 100% of admissions. The distribution of HCW infection did not follow the distribution of PPE provision. Balkh, where 13 of 19 HCW infections occurred, recorded universal gown use and the lowest documented PPE-shortage rate in the cohort (23/101 admissions, 22•8%), but had no isolation capacity and the highest patient volume-adjusted attack rate. Conversely, the National Infectious Disease Hospital recorded no gown use and near-universal documented PPE shortage, yet four HCW infections across 395 admissions. Fourteen of the 19 infected HCWs were managed at sites where gowns were in use. Taken together, these site-level observations suggest that isolation capacity and caseload may be more closely related to healthcare-associated risk than PPE supply alone, although this was not formally tested and staffing, invasive procedures and sharps handling were not recorded.
