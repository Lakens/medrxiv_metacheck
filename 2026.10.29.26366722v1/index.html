To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 2 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Full text

Abstract

Malaria and schistosomiasis are co-endemic across much of sub-Saharan Africa, yet it remains unclear whether co-infections reflect shared risk factors, unexplained biological determinants, or chance cooccurrence. We analysed data collected at recruitment from 4290 individuals aged 5-90 years across 52 villages in rural Uganda within the SchistoTrack cohort. A bivariate copula model was developed to separate shared and disease-specific associations from residual dependence. Prevalence was 41.47% for Schistosoma mansoni, 41.19% for Plasmodium falciparum, and 18.51% for co-infection. Prevalence overlapped in school-aged children with co-infection prevalence peaking at 13 years. Greater distance to a government health centre was the strongest shared determinant, apart from age, male gender and living in western districts, increasing the risk of both. Local water ecology influenced each pathogen in opposing directions. Fishing was associated with higher S. mansoni and co-infection risks, but not P. falciparum risk. A modelled scenario combining household drinking-water treatment, handwashing facilities and protected water sources with cessation of freshwater contact reduced co-infection prevalence by 32%. Our findings support integrated control around shared demographics and limited healthcare access and household water infrastructure, while highlighting the need for non-integrated approaches for ecological interventions aiming to reduce parasite-specific transmission determinants.

Introduction

Malaria and schistosomiasis are two of the most common parasitic diseases across sub-Saharan Africa with an estimated 500 million people living in co-endemic areas [1, 2, 3]. Co-infection, defined here as concurrent infection with both parasites in the same individual, has been linked to anaemia and impaired growth in children [4, 5]. Individuals living in co-endemic areas often have limited access to health services and to water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) infrastructure [5]. Integration of schistosomiasis control with malaria programmes has been advocated since at least 2005 [6, 7], and the latest World Health Organization (WHO) neglected tropical disease (NTD) roadmap 2030 emphasises the need for integrated control [8, 2]. Integration can proceed by targeting shared determinants, so that co-infection falls as both infections decline, or by identifying determinants of co-occurrence itself, so that programmes reach individuals carrying both parasites directly. Which route is more appropriate depends on whether substantial dependence between the two infections remains after accounting for measured shared and disease-specific determinants. Because both infections are endemic in sub-Saharan Africa, a fraction of co-infection is expected by chance alone [4, 9, 5]. However, additional co-infections can arise if the same individuals share risk factors such as treatment being unavailable or similar demographics, or if there is biological dependence where one infection might alter susceptibility to the other [10, 11]. Yet, for malaria and schistosomiasis, which determinants are shared and which are disease-specific has not been established [12]. Further, how much dependence remains once such determinants are accounted for is unknown, despite being essential for effective integrated control [13, 4]. This study focuses on identifying parasite-specific and shared risk factors for Plasmodium falciparum and Schistosoma mansoni infection.

Although malaria and schistosomiasis overlap geographically, their transmission routes fundamentally differ. P. falciparum is transmitted by a mosquito vector, whereas S. mansoni is acquired through contact with freshwater harbouring cercariae shed by intermediate-host snails [14, 15, 3, 16]. For example, in lakeside communities of rural Uganda, malaria is caused predominantly by P. falciparum [17], transmitted by Anopheles mosquitoes that breed in vegetated standing water near dwellings, whereas intestinal schistosomiasis is caused by S. mansoni, whose intermediate hosts are freshwater snails of the genus Biomphalaria [18]. Both species lead to severe morbidity in this region. P. falciparum is the main cause of severe malaria and anaemia [19], while chronic S. mansoni infection leads to periportal fibrosis and portal hypertension [20].

Existing malaria-schistosome co-infection investigations have focused on limited populations, mostly drawn from schools and health clinics, with nearly no quantification of shared risk factors and community-level risk profiles unexplored [12]. Co-infection risk is highest in older children and adolescents around 8-17 years of age [21, 11, 4], and pooled estimates suggest higher odds among males in community settings [12]. Yet, treatment campaigns for malaria and schistosomiasis have much narrower age targets. Malaria control has historically concentrated on children under five years of age [19] and in Uganda community-based integrated case management is still restricted to this age group, although the national strategy plans to extend it to all ages [17, 22].

Preventive chemotherapy for schistosomiasis, by contrast, is delivered mainly to school-aged children between roughly 5 and 14 years, typically through school-based mass drug administration (MDA) [23].

Each parasite has a distinct ecological niche [15, 18], so conditions that decrease transmission of one parasite may sustain or increase transmission of the other, with implications for the effectiveness of any shared environmental intervention [16]. Prevention therefore depends on identifying which determinants are shared and which act in opposite directions. Beyond prevention, integrated control also concerns case management. Integrated treatment for co-infection may be agnostic of shared risk factors if diagnostics are readily available to screen all individuals and there is no need for targeting who is diagnosed [19, 24]. In the absence of blanket diagnostics, demographic profiles would be needed at the least to target likely co-infected individuals. Case management may also need to change if S. mansoni alters P. falciparum parasitaemia [10, 11], or if co-infected individuals carry a greater burden of anaemia [24, 25]. Each of these hypothetical scenarios remains unaddressed without separating shared from parasite-specific determinants.

Co-infection has thus far been studied only as a binary outcome, ignoring the representation of the two pathogens as two dependent outcomes. As such, empirical evidence on the joint distribution of the two infections is scarce [9, 12]. Pooled estimates nonetheless show co-infection occurring more often than chance, though with substantial heterogeneity. Schistosome-infected individuals were around 27% more likely to also be infected with malaria [12]. Methodologically, this crude excess cannot be interpreted without separating shared determinants from residual dependence, and existing co-infection analyses with binary outcomes cannot make that separation. All 30 studies in the recent review collapsed four possible joint infection states (uninfected, malaria only, schistosomiasis only, co-infected) into a single binary outcome and primarily fitted univariate logistic regression [12]. A broader review of multi-response methods for co-infection found that most epidemiological studies use multinomial models, which similarly conflate marginal effects with cross-pathogen dependence [13]. Neither univariate logistic nor multinomial regression can identify which determinants are shared between pathogens, which determinants are pathogen-specific, or whether residual dependence remains after accounting for shared determinants.

We examined 4290 individuals from the SchistoTrack cohort [26, 27], a population-based prospective cohort study spanning 52 villages across three rural districts of Uganda. Cohort participants were aged 5 to 90 years. Malaria infection was ascertained by rapid diagnostic test (RDT), with almost all infections being P. falciparum [17]. Only intestinal schistosomiasis, specifically S. mansoni, is prevalent in Uganda and was diagnosed here by duplicate Kato-Katz slides from one stool sample [16]. Detailed socio-demographic, behavioural, household WASH, and environmental data were collected on the same individuals. We fitted a bivariate copula model to separate parasite-specific determinants from residual co-occurrence. From the fitted model we used Gcomputation to quantify these effects as absolute risk differences and population attributable fractions (PAFs) to estimate how much co-infection each modelled population action could, under strict assumptions, avert. The major aim of this study was to address the following. Which determinants influence both P. falciparum and S. mansoni versus one of these species, and how much residual dependence remains after accounting for shared determinants?

Results

There were 4290 participants out of 4330 assessed individuals at the point of recruitment within the SchistoTrack cohort with complete diagnostic and covariate data (Supplementary Fig. S1).

The sample composition is shown in Table 1. The prevalence of S. mansoni infection was 41.47% (1779/4290) and 41.19% (1767/4290) for P. falciparum infection. Co-infection prevalence was 18.51% (794/4290) (Table 1). The RDT (Bioline Malaria Ag P.f /Pan) detects P. falciparum-specific histidine-rich protein 2 (HRP2) and pan-Plasmodium lactate dehydrogenase (pLDH) [28]. Of the 1767 positive tests, 1403 (79.4%) showed the HRP2 line only, 359 (20.3%) both the HRP2 and pLDH lines, and 5 (0.3%) the pLDH line only. Microscopy did not show pLDH-only as P. falciparum positive. However, given the very low prevalence of pLDH-only, the possibility that pLDH lines also reflect P. falciparum, and the low sensitivity of microscopy, we therefore interpret RDT positivity as P. falciparum infection. The two infections were positively associated without adjustment for other variables (χ 21 = 14.87, p < 0.001). The crude crossproduct odds ratio (OR) was 1.27 (95% confidence interval (CI) 1.13 to 1.44), corresponding to a Yule's Υ of 0.06 (95% CI 0.03 to 0.09). Determinants of malaria and S. mansoni infection

Covariate-adjusted age-prevalence curves from the bivariate copula model differed in shape between the two infections (Fig. 1, unadjusted curves are shown in Supplementary Fig. S2).

P. falciparum prevalence peaked at age 10 years (95% CI 9 to 12) and declined gradually through adolescence and early adulthood, levelling off at around 22% from the late twenties. S. mansoni prevalence peaked later, at age 16 years (95% CI 15 to 18), and declined gradually through adulthood. Co-infection prevalence was highest at age 13 (95% CI 12 to 14) in between the two mono-infection peaks. Both age-penalised splines in the P. falciparum and S. mansoni model margins were non-linear (p < 0.001). In the S. mansoni model margin, four factors were associated with higher infection odds.

Reported open-water fishing carried the largest of these effects when compared to no reported fishing (OR 1.63, 95% CI 1. 25 to 2.14). An individual belonging to the majority religion of their village had higher odds than an individual who did not (OR 1.27, 95% CI 1.07 to 1.51), as did an individual belonging to the majority tribe of their village (OR 1.27, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.52).

Odds also increased with distance to the nearest government health centre (OR 1.32 per oneunit increase in log(1+d), with d in metres; 95% CI 1.12 to 1.56), equivalent to roughly 1.57-fold higher odds at 5 km than at 1 km. Female gender was associated with lower odds of S. mansoni infection when compared to male gender (OR 0.85, 95% CI 0.72 to 1.00), and each additional year of education was associated with lower odds of S. mansoni infection (OR 0.96, 95% CI 0.93 to 0.99). Distance to the nearest water-contact site showed a similar association, with lower odds among households living farther from that site (OR 0.80 for each one-unit increase in log(1 + d), d in metres; 95% CI 0.71 to 0.91). The nearest water landform being a pond or swamp (OR 0.65, 95% CI 0.48 to 0.88) or a river or river marsh (OR 0.67, 95% CI 0.48 to 0.94) was associated with lower odds when compared to a lake beach.

In the P. falciparum model margin, higher odds were associated with geographic and environmental features. Relative to a lake beach, lake marsh (OR 1.61, 95% CI 1.24 to 2.10), pond or swamp (OR 1.78, 95% CI 1.31 to 2.41), and river or river-marsh (OR 1.81, 95% CI 1.28 to 2.56) all were positively associated with P. falciparum infection. Higher vegetation surrounding the home, measured by the normalised difference vegetation index (NDVI), was associated with higher odds of P. falciparum infection (OR 2.50 per one-unit increase in NDVI, 95% CI 1.00 to 6.22). The odds of P. falciparum infection increased with the distance to the nearest government health centre (OR 1.34 per one-unit increase in log(1 + d), 95% CI 1.15 to 1.57), corresponding to roughly 1.61-fold higher odds at 5 km than at 1 km. Female gender was associated with lower odds of P. falciparum infection when compared to male gender (OR 0.76, 95% CI 0.64 to 0.90) and individuals who referred to their occupation as farmers had lower odds of P. falciparum infection than people who referred to their occupation as none/other (OR 0.76, 95% CI 0.59 to 0.98). No water-contact activity was associated with P. falciparum infection when compared to not reporting that activity. Self-reported antimalarial treatment in the month before recruitment was likewise not associated with the odds of P. falciparum infection when compared to no reported treatment (OR 1.16, 95% CI 0.89 to 1.52).

Residual co-infection dependence beyond shared determinants

The estimated conditional cross-product OR from the copula model was 1.18 (95% CI 1.01 to 1.39), corresponding to a Yule's Υ of 0.04 (95% CI 0.00 to 0.08). For malaria status ascertained via RDT, the CI for ψ excluded 1, indicating weak positive residual association between the two infections conditional on the determinants included in the marginal equations. The magnitude was small, conditional on the fitted marginal risks. The probability of joint infection was only modestly greater than would be expected under conditional independence. For comparison, the crude cross-product OR was 1.27 and the crude Yule's Υ was 0.06.

We tested whether ψ varied with any single covariate by fitting nine dependence-modulation specifications. None improved on the constant ψ baseline by Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) (∆AIC ≥ 1.86 in every case; Supplementary Table S2). Refitting the full model separately within each district gave ψ = 1.04 in Mayuge, 1.26 in Buliisa and 1.18 in Pakwach, which were homogeneous on the log scale (Cochran's Q = 0.69, df = 2, p = 0.71; Supplementary Table S3).

Model performance and sensitivity analyses

The pooled 10-fold cross-validated area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was 0.712 for S. mansoni, 0.786 for P. falciparum, and 0.759 for co-infection (Supplementary Fig. S3). Backward elimination dropped five covariates from the candidate set (home ownership, home quality score, household social status, household size, and log-distance to the nearest tap or latrine), in decreasing order of AIC improvement (Supplementary Table S4).

Among the 4290 participants in 2160 households, the household intraclass correlation was 0.039 for S. mansoni and was estimated at zero for P. falciparum. Refitting the full bivariate model within each cluster-free subsample gave ψ = 1.23 (95% CI 0.97 to 1.56) among adults and ψ = 1.20 (95% CI 0.96 to 1.51) among children, both close to the pooled estimate and homogeneous on the log scale (Cochran's Q = 0.02, df = 1, p = 0.88; Supplementary Table S5). Adjusted ORs were broadly similar when the model was fitted separately to children aged 5-14 years and to individuals aged 15 years or older (Supplementary Fig. S4). We also compared agreement between RDT and microscopy results and found that they agreed for 80.05% of participants (Cohen's κ = 0.58; Supplementary Table S6), and when refitting the model under alternative malaria outcome definitions requiring detectable parasitaemia, ψ was 1.06 (95% CI 0.89 to 1.26) and 1.03 (95% CI 0.88 to 1.21), compared to 1.18 under the primary definition (Supplementary Table S7). Fishing and distance to health centre were moderately robust (E-values 1.57 to 1.88). Discussion

Integration of malaria and schistosomiasis control has been advocated for two decades and is reinforced by the WHO roadmap for neglected tropical diseases [6, 7, 8, 2]. Whether control can be jointly targeted depends in part on whether the same individuals are at risk of both infections, and thus on the extent to which P. falciparum and S. mansoni share individual-level determinants rather than merely co-occur geographically. We examined the shared and parasitespecific determinants of P. falciparum and S. mansoni infection in 4290 individuals in rural Uganda using a bivariate copula model that estimates each parasite model margin with its own covariate set, evaluates the direction of each effect on both model margins, and quantifies the residual dependence between the two infections in a single fit. We found that the two infections share several upstream determinants whilst retaining parasite-specific risk factors for infection acquisition. Residual dependence between the two infections after inclusion of easily observable covariates was weak.

The burden of both infections was strongly age-dependent with age being an important shared determinant. P. falciparum prevalence peaked at 10 years of age and S. mansoni prevalence at 16 years of age. The early P. falciparum peak is consistent with evidence that school-aged children retain high susceptibility and act as a community transmission reservoir [17, 29], while parasitaemia was highest at 5 years of age, consistent with immunity acquired through repeated exposure [30]. Co-infection prevalence peaked at 13 years of age between the two parasite-specific peaks. Given the weak residual dependence between the two infections, this co-infection age peak is expected if co-infection largely reflects the overlap of two separate risks. Despite differences in the exact age year of peak prevalence, both parasites overlapped substantially within schoolaged children. Notably, a substantial burden of both infections remained in adults, who are less often the focus of control programmes [19, 23].

The age patterns we observed have implications for mass treatment campaigns. Both infections and co-infection were most common among school-aged children. Praziquantel has been delivered as preventive chemotherapy through school and community-based MDA in the districts of this study since 2003 [23, 16]. Malaria chemoprevention in school-aged children has been shown to reduce infection prevalence [29], resulting in revised WHO guidance that has widened the age groups eligible for malaria chemoprevention [19] as well as updates to the Uganda national malaria plan to extend community case management for malaria to all age groups [22].

Adding malaria chemoprevention to school-based MDA or providing praziquantel through primary healthcare facilities where antimalarials are distributed requires further research. Realising any integration strategy will depend on platforms that are already under strain, as community case management has been limited by drug stock-outs and uneven coverage across regions [31] and praziquantel distribution through MDA has been interrupted due to the loss of international aid. Additional benefits of treatment integration may include specific effects of artemisinin-based treatments against juvenile schistosomes that are not affected by praziquantel [32]. Both parasites having reservoirs in the adult population and need treatment beyond where the peak prevalence is observed in school-aged children [16, 17]. Other non-modifiable participant aspects such as male gender and living in a western district were shared risk factors for both infections; this information could be used in addition to age to build profiles of individuals requiring targeted treatment or other interventions.

Female gender was associated with lower risk of both infections. Given that the two parasites are acquired through different transmission routes, the effect of shared gender may plausibly be the sum of parasite-specific exposures rather than a single shared behaviour. For S. mansoni, the male excess risk is consistent with occupational freshwater contact, as fishing is almost exclusively undertaken by men in these communities and was associated with S. mansoni infection in our data [16]. By contrast, none of the water-contact activities we measured were associated with P. falciparum infection, so the lower P. falciparum risk in females cannot be attributed to the same domestic versus occupational division of water contact. The female risk for P. falciparum infection might reflect other exposures, such as outdoor activity during the evening hours when Anopheles bite, or differences in care-seeking [33, 34]. Males therefore appear to be a priority group for both infections, and because they attend health facilities less often for routine or preventive care [33, 34], reaching them may depend on community-based rather than facility-based delivery.

Using our bivariate modelling framework, we were able to identify that improved access to primary healthcare services could jointly benefit malaria and schistosomiasis control efforts, thereby offering an opportunity for integration. Greater distance to a government health centre was associated with higher risk of both infections, and reducing log-distance by one standard deviation resulted in the largest single-covariate co-infection PAF observed, at 21.25%, alongside PAFs of 12.27% for S. mansoni and 11.47% for P. falciparum. Antimalarials reach individuals both at health centres and door-to-door through community case management, whereas praziquantel is not available through routine health centres and is administered only through MDA, as donated tablets are allocated to vertical campaigns delivered by teachers and community medicine distributors rather than government health workers [35]. When campaigns are delayed, communities along Lake Albert therefore seek treatment from private drug shops, village health team members, or traditional healers [36]. The association of distance with S. mansoni infection is unlikely to reflect access to treatment and more plausibly captures the remoteness of households that campaigns are more likely to miss [34, 37]. For P. falciparum, by contrast, antimalarials are available at health centres, so greater distance may plausibly hinder access. WHO recommends that all infected individuals, regardless of age, be treated with praziquantel at health facilities [23].

Making praziquantel available in primary healthcare facilities would provide an accessible route to treatment between MDA rounds and allow co-infected individuals to be treated for both infections at a single visit. Our findings add population-level support for this recommendation, and we would expect the greatest gains for schistosomiasis, which currently has no routine treatment route within local health systems. Health workers in our study districts were often uncertain about praziquantel dosing outside MDA, and called for its inclusion on the national essential medicines list [35]. Additional research is needed to evaluate whether stocking praziquantel in health centres, together with training in diagnosis and dosing, increases treatment coverage for both infections, and whether managing both infections at the same facility reduces co-infection.

We found that no single WASH component consistently lowered the risk of both infections. However, providing drinking-water treatment, handwashing facilities and access to protected water sources, together with no water contact, could reduce co-infection by almost a third (31.52%).

WASH could therefore serve as a shared platform for both programmes. For schistosomiasis, the benefit is well established, as water supplies reduce contact with open water, and high community coverage may protect even those without household access [38]. Households that adopt WASH practices may also use bed nets and live in better housing, which is itself associated with lower malaria risk [39], although the housing quality score we considered did not improve our model. A household WASH package of this kind would be a complex intervention, but comparable approaches have succeeded for both infections. An integrated strategy in China combining safe water, sanitation, health education and the removal of cattle from snail habitats reduced Schistosoma japonicum prevalence to below 1% [40], and environmental management, including drainage and housing modification, has substantially reduced malaria morbidity and mortality [41]. Efforts to integrate the two programmes have so far focused on co-administering praziquantel and antimalarials or on health education, and few studies of co-infection have 20 considered WASH at all [9, 12]. Eventually there is a need to test complex household WASH interventions in randomised trials for reducing P. falciparum and S. mansoni co-infections to identify whether such interventions are a valid approach for integrated control of malaria and schistosomiasis.

Any water contact has been proposed to increase the odds of co-infection [12, 21]. However, we found that fishing in open water was the only water-contact activity associated with infection, and only with S. mansoni, even though fishing sites in marshy areas also provide habitats suitable for mosquitoes [18, 42]. Policymakers in the future will be confronted with open questions about which interventions can be integrated and where parasite-specific control remains necessary. Our framework separates determinants that act on both infections, from those that act on one parasite, such as fishing and the type of water site, which require targeted measures. Reducing S. mansoni infection at water sites, through focal snail control at frequently visited sites near homes [43] and praziquantel treatment of the people who use them, would also prevent coinfection, but would not reduce malaria. Such measures should therefore be planned with malaria in mind, as changes at water sites can create or remove mosquito breeding sites [41]. In agricultural and irrigation areas, integrated vector management combining environmental management with chemical and drug-based measures, including mollusciciding and larval source management, has been a successful approach to controlling both mosquito and snail-borne diseases [44]. Future research should test whether snail control and mosquito larval source management can be delivered jointly at the same water sites, and whether schistosomiasis measures can be designed to also reduce mosquito breeding or, at a minimum, avoid creating new breeding sites.

For occupations, only farming was associated with either infection, with lower P. falciparum risk than in individuals without occupation and no difference in S. mansoni infection risk, consistent with earlier findings in this cohort [17]. Occupational water contact rises among males from adolescence onwards [16], so those at highest risk fall outside school-based praziquantel programmes [23]. Community-wide treatment in fishing communities, for example at landing sites, should therefore reach everyone who fishes, and could also offer malaria testing and treatment.

Combined delivery of this kind has precedent. When bed nets and malaria treatment were added to the work of community ivermectin distributors, coverage of both more than doubled [45]. The farming category used in our analysis, however, combined subsistence farmers with rice farmers and papyrus gatherers (given the few individuals in the latter categories), whose work in standing water and wetlands could expose them to both parasites. Future studies should examine these occupations separately and test whether combined outreach at landing sites can deliver treatment for both infections to adults.

The landform of the nearest water site of a household was the clearest divergent determinant.

Compared to lake beaches, ponds, swamps, and rivers and river marshes were associated with lower S. mansoni but higher P. falciparum risk. The two parasites have distinct niches, and one cannot be inferred from the other, which makes their ecology difficult to target jointly [18, 14].

Knowing where exposure occurs for each parasite therefore remains a prerequisite for targeting, even where the ecology is not shared. Distance to the nearest water-contact site is central to locating schistosome exposure but far less relevant to malaria [43]. In this dataset, satellitederived shoreline distance performed similarly to field-mapped distance in the S. mansoni model, suggesting that it may serve as a suitable proxy indication in comparable lakeside settings. The satellite-derived shoreline measure could extend the field-mapped water-site component of the S. mansoni margin to unsurveyed parts of the Lake Albert and Lake Victoria basins, using freely available Sentinel-2 imagery [46]. This approach may offer a scalable source of parasite-specific ecological information that could be used in programme planning.

Through our descriptive analysis, we found that co-infected individuals did not necessarily have higher parasite loads. Co-infected individuals carried modestly higher S. mansoni egg counts than those with schistosome infection alone, but lower P. falciparum parasitaemia. This difference was limited to those with the heaviest schistosome infection. Community-based paediatric cohorts in Mali and Senegal found low-to-moderate Schistosoma egg counts were associated with lower parasitaemia and fewer febrile episodes [10, 47, 48], with reduced regulatory T-cell responses and dampened Th1 cytokine production proposed as the mechanism [49]. It is unclear whether there is a biological interaction to explain the results in our study, i.e. the lack of heavy co-infection clustering, as we did not have immunological data and this area would require additional studies. From a clinical perspective, if co-infected individuals carried a greater burden of anaemia, its management would need to account for co-infection [24, 25]. We found little evidence for this, as anaemia associated with infection seemed to be primarily due to P. falciparum.

Co-infected individuals were no more anaemic than those with P. falciparum alone and S. mansoni mono-infection accounted for only 0.8% of anaemia. There was no interaction between the two infections on haemoglobin or on the additive scale for anaemia. Hookworm, a common cause of anaemia, was uncommon outside Mayuge, and adjusting for it in this analysis did not change the estimates.

Our analysis addresses a methodological gap identified in recent systematic reviews of coinfection [13, 12]. All 30 studies in our recent review of malaria and schistosomiasis co-infection collapsed the four infection states into a single binary outcome, which obscures through which infection a determinant acts [12]. Our framework models the two infections as separate margins coupled through a Plackett copula, with a dependence parameter that captures whether coinfection is more common than expected given each infection risk [50, 51]. This framework allows us to identify which determinants are shared and which are parasite-specific, and thereby to distinguish the measures that a joint programme can deliver from those each individual programme must retain, which will become crucial for integration efforts advocated for by the WHO.

The empirical strengths of this study are its scale, wide age range, and diverse geographic setting within rural Uganda. Methodologically, the copula model proposed here is able to return marginal effects, residual dependence, and joint co-infection probabilities in a single fit; the G-computation approach keeps the projected scenarios internally consistent with the fitted margins. Several limitations should be noted. The cross-sectional design cannot establish temporal ordering of exposures or distinguish prevalent from incident infection, and because recruitment took place in January and February, we may have captured malaria near its seasonal high and S. mansoni below its seasonal peak [52, 53]. We did not adjust for bed-net use because it was not recorded for participants recruited in 2022, two thirds of the sample, so the associations of household WASH may partly reflect differences in bed-net use, although unmeasured shared exposures of this kind would also be expected to appear as residual dependence, which was weak. Individuals were paired within household, but residual household clustering was negligible (design effect 1.04 for S. mansoni and 1.00 for P. falciparum) and estimates were unchanged in cluster-free subsamples. Residual dependence was smaller with malaria defined based on detectable parasitaemia, so the primary estimate is the largest of those considered. The projected PAFs depend on assumptions of conditional exchangeability, positivity, consistency, and correct model specification that cannot be verified in observational data, so the combined scenario should be read as a ceiling and not a forecast [54]. Finally, the cross-product OR used to summarise residual dependence is non-collapsible, so future comparisons across populations or specifications must separate differences in dependence from aggregation artefacts [55].

Methods

Study design and population

We conducted a cross-sectional study using data from all participants at the point of recruitment into SchistoTrack, a community-based prospective cohort in Uganda established in 2022 [26, 27].

The cohort is in three districts: Pakwach along the River Nile and Lake Albert, Buliisa along Lake Albert, and Mayuge along Lake Victoria. Households were randomly sampled from local village registers across 52 villages. Recruitment and data collection took place in January and February of each study year (2022, 2023, 2024), during the dry season following the high-transmission rainy period [52]. Households were eligible if they included at least one child and one adult, who had resided in the community for at least six months of the year. One adult (aged ≥ 18 years) and one child (aged 5 to < 18 years) per household were enrolled using a paired sampling approach. Comparison of the clinical cohort against the full household census has shown no demographic bias with respect to age or gender [26]. The final analytic sample included 4290 individuals, after removing individuals with missing diagnostic results for either P. falciparum or S. mansoni and incomplete covariate data. Each participant contributed a single observation.

Outcome ascertainment P. falciparum infection was ascertained by RDT (Bioline Malaria Ag P.f /Pan), which detects P. falciparum histidine-rich protein 2 (HRP2) and pan-Plasmodium lactate dehydrogenase (pLDH), on a finger-prick blood sample [28]. We encoded positive RDT results hereafter with Y M = 1 for a positive result and Y M = 0 otherwise. Parasitaemia intensity was assessed by thick and thin blood film microscopy following WHO guidelines [19].

S. mansoni infection was confirmed via Kato-Katz stool microscopy on duplicate slides prepared from one stool sample [56]. Infection was coded as Y S = 1 if EPG exceeded zero on any slide and Y S = 0 otherwise. We retained the infection intensity data of both infections for descriptive comparisons between mono-and co-infected individuals, although they were not used in the primary bivariate analysis for which the main outcome was the pair (Y S , Y M ) ∈ {0, 1} 2 . As secondary outcomes we compared infection intensity between mono-and co-infected individuals as well as altitude-adjusted haemoglobin and anaemia across the four infection groups, for which we also considered an interaction between the two infections.

Covariates

At the individual level we included age (years), self-reported gender (male as reference), years of formal education (0 to 14), and occupation coded as a four-level categorical variable including none/other (including students and unemployed), farmers, fishers, and fishmongers. The none/other category was chosen as the reference category for analysis [20, 26]. Occupation is defined as the primary income-generating activity. Additionally, binary indicators recorded whether each individual belonged to the majority tribe or majority religion within their village [20].

We additionally considered six water-contact behaviours in both marginal models as binary indicators and included fishing, swimming, fetching water from open water bodies, washing clothes in open water, fishmongering at water sites, and bathing in open water. These indicators were also previously used in an analysis by Reitzug et al. [16] focusing on the association between water contact and schistosomiasis. The behaviours were recorded by structured household interviews with the household head or spouse as a respondent for each household member, and referred to typical weekly activity, as described by Reitzug et al. [16]. All water contact indicators record activity irrespective of occupation, as individuals engage in activities such as fishing without it being their main source of income.

Household-level variables were as follows. To account for socio-economic status we included a composite home-quality score (3 to 12 scale) based on wall material, roof type, and floor quality used in previous analysis [20, 17]. We also considered access to electricity, home ownership, number of rooms, and household size. Household social status was coded as positive if any member held a recognised local leadership position.

Since WASH access may affect infection of either parasite, we assessed at the household level the availability of a handwashing facility with soap and water, sanitation facility type, classified as improved or unimproved per WHO/UNICEF Joint Monitoring Programme criteria [57], any water-treatment practice (bleach or chlorine, solar disinfection, or cloth straining) and access to a protected water source (tap or borehole).

Based on infrastructure field surveys in 2022 and 2023, health facility locations were available, allowing us to calculate the Euclidean distance from each household to the nearest government health centre and to the nearest private drug shop. For modelling purposes we applied to both variables a log transformation and included them in the model as log(1+d) with d in metres [26].

Additionally, we included binary indicators of self-reported praziquantel treatment in the preceding year before recruitment and self-reported antimalarial treatment during the month before recruitment in the respective model margins.

To capture environmental exposure relevant for transmission of both parasites, we derived the NDVI from Sentinel-2 Level-2A surface reflectance imagery, which was further processed by hexagonal grid aggregation and Gaussian-weighted neighbourhood smoothing as described in Lang et al. [17]. We also included the water-body type of the nearest water-contact site (lake beach, lake marsh, river or river marsh, pond or swamp, rice paddy) to capture ecological features that may influence transmission [18]. In the S. mansoni margin we also considered the distance from the household to the nearest water-contact site as a spatial covariate. We also included district (Mayuge, Buliisa, Pakwach) and recruitment year (2022 to 2024) in both margins to account for unobserved differences between study sites and recruitment years [26]. Because the two parasites have distinct transmission routes, the marginal models use overlapping but not identical covariate subsets.

Measure of residual dependence

We summarised dependence between S. mansoni and P. falciparum infection using the crossproduct OR as the measure of association. Co-infection of two parasites in the same individual may arise from shared exposure or upstream covariates, from residual association that persists after those determinants are accounted for, or from a mix of both. In observational data, crude co-infection prevalence does not distinguish these two sources. We therefore require a measure of association that does not change with the marginal prevalences of either infection, so that comparisons across populations or model specifications can capture whether co-infection is more or less likely than by chance given the marginal prevalences. For two binary outcomes, the joint distribution is characterised by the two marginal probabilities and one additional degree of freedom, which can be captured by the cross-product OR ψ = p 11 p 00 p 10 p 01 (1) binary outcomes, therefore the Plackett copula here denotes a convenient odds-ratio parameterisation of the bivariate Bernoulli distribution. The closed-form expression, its derivation, and further discussion are provided in Supplementary Note 1.

We estimated the model by penalised maximum likelihood jointly with the GJRM package (version 0.2.6) in R (version 4.2.1) [59], with the smoothing parameters selected automatically by minimising an approximate AIC within the fitting algorithm. Our analysis operates within an empirical Bayes framework that links penalised likelihood and Bayesian smoothing [60, 58], such that each penalty term is equivalent to a Gaussian prior distribution on the corresponding coefficients, β ∼ N (0, S - λ ), where S - λ is a (pseudo-)inverse of the penalty matrix [60]. The penalised estimate β is the corresponding maximum a posteriori estimate and instead of assigning the smoothing parameters hyper-prior distributions, we estimated them directly from the data [60, 58]. Conditional on the estimated smoothing parameters, the coefficients then have the approximate Gaussian posterior β | y ∼ N ( β, V b ) from which intervals approximately attain nominal frequentist coverage across the function [61, 59].

We drew B = 5000 samples from the posterior of the fitted coefficients [58] and evaluated each estimand at every draw. For AMEs, PAFs and the age-prevalence curves, the 95% intervals are the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the draws (Supplementary Note 1). ORs and ψ have Wald intervals from V b . Given the close nominal frequentist coverage we report them as 95% confidence intervals (CIs) throughout [61]. All tests were two-sided.

We assessed out-of-sample discrimination by 10-fold cross-validation, during which the folds were stratified on the cross-classified schistosomiasis-by-malaria outcome, so that each fold carried a comparable share of co-infected individuals. Within each fold the full model, including age splines and village random intercepts, was refitted on the nine training folds and from the refitted model the infection probabilities for S. mansoni and P. falciparum for the held-out observations were obtained. We pooled the predictions across folds and computed a single AUC.

We obtained classification thresholds using Youden's J in the training folds only, so that no held-out observation contributed to its own threshold [62].

Covariate selection and model evaluation

Covariates were included in the marginal models based on prior work in this cohort [16, 17]. We retained a locked subset irrespective of model fit which included age, district, recruitment year, gender, occupation, education years, praziquantel or antimalarial treatment status, the six watercontact behaviours, the four WASH variables, electricity access, and the spatial covariates of primary interest (mean NDVI, log-distance to nearest private drug shop, log-distance to nearest health centre). The remaining covariates, listed in Supplementary Table S4, were selected via joint backward elimination on the bivariate model. Inference on the retained terms depends on the selection path, although the extent of this concern is limited by the pre-specified set of covariates.

Risk differences and population attributable fractions

From the fitted model we obtain three predicted probabilities for each individual. A marginal S. mansoni risk pS,i estimate, marginal P. falciparum risk pM,i estimate, and joint co-infection risk estimate p11,i = C ψ (p S,i , pM,i ). From these estimates we derived standardised risk differences via G-computation [54]. For each binary covariate Z, we generated two scenario datasets in which Z was set to 1 and to 0 for every individual while all other covariates were held at their observed values, to obtain the predicted infection probabilities from the model. For each setting these predicted probabilities were averaged across the population and then differenced. For continuous covariates, the scenario was a one-standard-deviation increase from each individual's observed value. We provided full definitions in Supplementary Note 1 and emphasise that these estimates rest on conditional exchangeability, positivity, consistency, and correct specification of the outcome model [54].

We report the AME over the full sample, the PAF, and the conditional AME among the relevant subgroup (currently exposed for the six water-contact behaviours, currently unexposed for the four WASH variables). Under the stated assumptions, the conditional AME among the unexposed quantifies the predicted risk reduction if an intervention were extended to those who currently do not have access to it. Each covariate was classified according to the signs of its two marginal AMEs and the stability of those signs across the posterior draws. We calculated the sign stability as the proportion of the B = 5000 draws in which an AME takes the same sign as its point estimate, ranging from 0.5 to 1, and considered a margin as directionally stable when sign stability was at least 0.975. A covariate was convergent if both margins were directionally stable and agreed in sign, divergent if both were directionally stable with opposite signs, singular if exactly one was directionally stable, and unclassified if neither was. Multi-level factors and intervention groups were classified by the same rule applied to setting all individuals to the reference level or to a specific scenario. Full definitions are in Supplementary Note 1.

We also computed joint PAFs for three blocks of covariates. The three blocks included changing all four WASH variables together to either full coverage or the reference level for individuals (WASH block), changing all water-contact variables together (water-contact block), and a combined block between the two. The combined block paired full WASH coverage with no measured water contact, and its reverse paired no WASH with universal water contact. We repeated the WASH and combined blocks excluding sanitation, and excluding both sanitation and handwashing facilities.

Sensitivity analyses

We conducted six sensitivity analyses. First, we replaced the field-mapped distance to the nearest water-contact site with the satellite-derived shoreline distance and removed the field-derived water-body type variable, since both depend on the same site mapping. Our goal was to assess a model specification that requires no field-based snail or water-contact site data, that we can compare to the main model. We derived water bodies from Sentinel-2 imagery using the Modified Normalised Difference Water Index on the same hexagonal grid as the NDVI [63, 17, 46], classified water and land by Otsu's threshold [64], and defined shoreline hexagons as those adjacent to the opposite class. Distance to the nearest shoreline hexagon entered the S. mansoni margin as log(1 + d). The entire pipeline and its validation against field-mapped sites are described in Supplementary Note 2 and Supplementary Fig. S6. Second, we refit the full bivariate model separately within each district, allowing both margins and the dependence parameter to vary, to describe district-specific residual dependence (Supplementary Table S3). We also evaluated the homogeneity by comparing the constant-ψ baseline to a model allowing log ψ to vary by district via likelihood ratio test, holding both margins common so that the comparison isolates dependence. We also report pairwise Wald contrasts on the log scale in Supplementary Table S3 as a descriptive summary of heterogeneity. Third, to test whether residual dependence concentrates in particular subgroups, we modelled ψ as a function of covariates on a third linear predictor, log ψ i = w ⊤ i γ + k g k (z k,i ), where w i is a low-dimensional subset of the marginal covariates and g k are penalised splines, which we specified with nine differing choices of w i (age, gender, district, log-distance to the nearest water-contact site, and combinations of these). We compared these specifications against the constant-ψ baseline by AIC, for which the full specifications and results can be found in Supplementary Table S2. Fourth, because each household contributed one adult and one child, individuals were paired within household and all household-level covariates were shared within pairs. We quantified residual clustering by fitting intercept-only mixed-effects logistic models with village and nested household random intercepts to each infection separately, and computed the household intraclass correlation on the latent scale as ρ = σ 2 household /(σ 2 household + σ 2 village + π 2 /3). Additionally, we refitted the full bivariate model separately among adults and among children. Fifth, because HRP2-based RDTs remain positive for several weeks after parasite clearance whereas microscopy detects only circulating parasites, we refitted the model under two alternative malaria definitions, positivity on both assays, and microscopy positivity alone. Agreement between the two assays was summarised by Cohen's κ.

All three specifications used identical marginal equations and differed only in the definition of Y M . Finally, we assessed robustness of the significant marginal associations to unmeasured confounding by computing E-values with the EValue R package [65, 66]. Methods for the analyses of infection intensity, haemoglobin and anaemia are given in Supplementary Note 3.
