Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

P-Value Text
p≥.20 The two arms were balanced on demographic, baseline pain, and cannabis use characteristics (Table 1, all p≥.20).

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 14 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Cannabis use is rising fastest among older adults in the U.S., and pain is among the most common reasons they use it. No trial, however, has measured the trade-off between the analgesic and abuse-related effects of Δ9-tetrahydrocannabinol (Δ9-THC) in this population. In a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, 20 adults aged 65 and older were assigned to oral Δ9-THC (placebo, 5, 10 mg) or vaporized Δ9-THC (placebo, 2, 4 mg) across three eighthour sessions in counterbalanced order. We assessed laboratory-evoked pain sensitivity using quantitative sensory testing, abuse liability using subjective drug effects and reinforcement value, and also sampled plasma Δ9-THC and 11-OH-THC. Analgesia and abuse liability outcomes were measured repeatedly within each session. On a pain sensitivity index, oral 5 mg relieved pain relative to placebo (p=.040), although the oral arm's overall dose effect was not significant (p=.108); oral 10 mg and both vaporized doses did not differ from placebo. Subjective effects increased with dose and were greatest after vaporization, whereas reinforcement value increased only after vaporized 4 mg. After oral 5 mg, analgesia exceeded placebo at every assessment (60-360 min), while its pleasurable effects peaked at 150 min. Vaporized Δ9-THC produced pleasurable effects within 30 min but did not significantly change thermal pain sensitivity. While abuse liability tracked Δ9-THC exposure, analgesia showed no significant association, and all adverse events occurred at vaporized 4 mg. These findings suggest dose and route selection may help maximize Δ9-THC analgesia while minimizing abuse liability, a strategy that warrants evaluation in clinical populations.

1. Introduction

Chronic pain affects over a third of adults aged 65 and older [33], and many older adults have turned to cannabis to relieve their pain. Older adults are now the fastest-growing group of people who use cannabis: past-year use has risen 20-fold in two decades [22, 23, 40, 47], and more than half of older adults who use cannabis cite pain as a reason [6, 44, 47, 55]. Between 2015 and 2019, the share of older adults who see regular use as a great risk fell from 53% to 43% [21]. Chronic pain is also a risk factor for cannabis use disorder (CUD), and CUD diagnoses are rising fast among older adults [25].

Δ9-tetrahydrocannabinol (Δ9-THC) is a partial agonist at cannabinoid type 1 (CB1) receptors [46] and reliably produces analgesia in preclinical models [24, 35, 36]. Clinically, however, cannabis and Δ9-THC relieve chronic pain modestly: a minimally important improvement in 10% more patients than placebo [54]. Laboratory findings from quantitative sensory testing (QST), which evokes pain with calibrated stimuli [48], are inconsistent. A systematic review of 39 placebo-controlled QST studies of inhaled cannabis, dronabinol, nabilone, Δ9-THC:CBD formulations, and other cannabinoid agonists, almost all in younger adults, found them too heterogeneous to pool [42]. Controlled studies have evaluated the pharmacokinetics, tolerability, or dementia-related agitation associated with cannabis in older adults, but no studies have evaluated analgesia [2, 3].

Aging reshapes both the nociceptive system and the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of Δ9-THC [15]. Pain thresholds tend to increase with age, whereas pain tolerance changes are more modest [34]. CB1 receptor availability declines with age in preclinical models [19], and in human frontal cortex post mortem, receptor density and function fall by roughly 10% per decade [39]. Emerging data indicate that the cognitive effects of acute Δ9-THC are blunted in older adults [15]. Greater adiposity and reduced hepatic blood flow also prolong exposure to Δ9-THC and its active metabolite 11-OH-THC [14, 30, 38]. These changes may attenuate, potentiate, or leave unchanged Δ9-THC's analgesic and abuse-related effects, and not necessarily in the same direction for each [15].

Dose and route can also affect analgesia and abuse liability differently [13]. Δ9-THC's dose-response relationship is non-linear and often U-shaped [9]: in healthy volunteers, an intermediate dose of smoked cannabis reduced capsaicin-induced pain, whereas a higher dose increased it [53]. In contrast, abuse liability, tends to increase with dose [50] and with the speed at which a drug reaches the brain [1]. Route sets that speed: among older adults, vaporized Δ9-THC produced peak concentrations roughly nine-fold higher than matched oral doses, at 3 minutes rather than 60 to 90 minutes [14]. Taken together, these results suggest the benefit-risk profile of cannabis in older adults could favor lower doses (i.e., greater analgesia and lower abuse liability).

Both outcomes therefore have to be measured together. To identify this trade-off in older adults, we conducted a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of oral (5 and 10 mg) and vaporized (2 and 4 mg) Δ9-THC, measuring both outcomes repeatedly in each session. We hypothesized an inverted-U dose-response for analgesia, placing the most favorable benefit-risk balance at the lower dose of each route. We also asked whether analgesia tracks total exposure while abuse liability tracks peak concentration.

2. Methods

We prospectively registered this study (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT05906511) and the protocol was approved by the Yale University and VA Connecticut Healthcare System Institutional Review Boards. The study was conducted at the Biological Studies Unit of the VA Connecticut Healthcare System (West Haven, CT). All participants provided written informed consent before any study procedure, and reporting follows the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) 2025 statement [27]. This report covers laboratory-evoked pain and its balance against abuse liability. All other outcomes are reported in the companion manuscript [14].

2.1 Study Design

The trial design is described in full in the companion manuscript [14]. Briefly, participants were randomized to one route of Δ9-THC, oral or vaporized, and within that arm completed three eight-hour test sessions, one per dose (oral: placebo, 5 mg, 10 mg; vaporized: placebo, 2 mg, 4 mg). Dose order was randomized and counterbalanced, and sessions were separated by at least 72 hours to mitigate carryover.

2.2 Participants

Participants were healthy adults aged 65 years or older who had prior lifetime cannabis exposure and no DSM-5 psychiatric or substance use disorder, other than tobacco use disorder, in the past year. We required lifetime cannabis exposure to enhance the safety and tolerability of the study. Because the present analyses depend on intact nociception, eligibility excluded conditions that alter pain sensation or balance (for example, stroke or peripheral neuropathy identified on a neuro-sensory examination), contraindications to nociceptive stimulation such as untreated hypertension, and regular use of analgesics or of strong CYP2C9, CYP3A4, or UGT1A9 modulators. A negative urine test for THC-COOH (50 ng/mL cutoff) was required for enrollment. Medical exclusions included hepatic enzyme elevation and renal dysfunction.

Psychiatric exclusions included a personal or family history of primary psychotic or mood disorders with psychotic features. Full eligibility criteria are listed in Supplementary Section S1.

2.3 Study Medication and Administration

Oral Δ9-THC was administered as dronabinol (synthetic Δ9-THC; McKesson Corporation, Irving, TX), an FDA-approved Schedule III oral formulation, in capsules containing 0, 5, or 10 mg. Vaporized Δ9-THC was delivered as pharmaceutical-grade Δ9-THC (99% purity, compounded as a 10 mg/mL ethanolic solution) using the Volcano Hybrid® vaporizer (Storz & Bickel, Tuttlingen, Germany) [32] with a standardized paced-puffing procedure [8, 17] described in the companion manuscript. Active vaporized doses of 2 and 4 mg were selected to approximate the systemic exposure of the oral 5 and 10 mg doses (Supplementary Section S3). The research pharmacy held the randomization sequence and dispensed visually identical placebo and active preparations, maintaining the blind for participants, raters, and investigators (Supplementary Section S2); blinding procedures and integrity are reported in the companion manuscript [14].

2.4 Pharmacokinetic Assessments

Plasma Δ9-THC and its metabolites were sampled serially and quantified by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry; the full pharmacokinetic characterization is reported in the companion manuscript [14].

2.5 Quantitative Sensory Testing

Laboratory-evoked pain sensitivity was assessed by a multimodal quantitative sensory testing (QST) battery based on the standardized protocol of the German Research Network on Neuropathic Pain (DFNS) [48]. The QST battery comprised seven threshold and tolerance measures across three stimulus modalities: heat pain threshold (HTH), heat pain tolerance (HTO), cold pain threshold (CTH), and cold pain tolerance (CTO), delivered by contact thermode (TSA-II; Medoc, Ramat Yishai, Israel); pressure pain threshold (PTH) and pressure pain tolerance (PTO), by computer-controlled algometer (AlgoMed; Medoc); and mechanical pain threshold (MTH), by calibrated pinprick stimulators (MRC Systems, Germany). Each measure was obtained twice and averaged. QST was administered at a pre-dose baseline and at five post-dose timepoints (oral arm: 60, 120, 180, 240, and 360 min; vaporized arm: 5, 60, 120, 180, and 360 min). The DFNS protocol governed stimulus application and threshold determination; the tolerance measures and the summary measures defined below were specific to this study. Baseline clinical pain, which was not an eligibility criterion, was characterized at screening with the Brief Pain Inventory-Short Form (BPI-SF) [10].

For each measure, the effect of Δ9-THC was summarized two ways: the session peak change from baseline (the maximum signed deviation across the session) and the session AUC, the area under the change-from-baseline curve integrated by the trapezoidal rule across the post-dose observation window (oral 60-360 min; vaporized 5-360 min). For individual QST measures, session-AUC estimates and confidence intervals were divided by the window duration and are reported as mean changes from baseline in each measure's original units. For the cold measures (CTH, CTO), the change score was sign-inverted so that positive values denote analgesia (greater cold tolerated) across every measure.

2.6 Pain Sensitivity Index

As a data reduction strategy, we combined the four thermal QST measures (HTH, HTO, CTH, CTO) into a single pain sensitivity index. This approach is not only consistent with previous integration of QST measures into composite indices [7, 26], but it also limits multiplicity across correlated measures and averages out measurement noise. The combined measures all share a transduction substrate: noxious heat and cold are detected by TRP channels on Aδ and C nociceptors [28]. CB1 receptors are co-expressed with TRPV1 on those same afferents, where a locally applied CB1 agonist attenuates thermal hyperalgesia [5]. We built the pain sensitivity index on session AUCs rather than peak changes because, in laboratory-evoked pain models, analgesic effects are detected more robustly by the area under the pain curve than by peak pain intensity [51]. For each session, the four session AUCs were sign-inverted for the cold measures so that positive values denote analgesia, standardized within measure as z-scores across all sessions of both arms. The pain sensitivity index is therefore in SD units, one unit being an average change of one standard deviation across its four component measures. All QST measures are also reported separately.

2.7 Abuse Liability

Subjective drug effects were the primary measure of abuse liability and behavioral reinforcement value served as a secondary measure. To assess subjective drug effects, we used the Pleasurable composite of the Drug Effects Questionnaire (DEQ), a visual analog scale with established psychometric properties [41]. The Pleasurable composite was the mean of three items (like the drug effects, feel good effects, want more drug), which map onto measures named in the FDA guidance Assessment of Abuse Potential of Drugs [52], and was summarized as the peak change from baseline (E max ), the summary that guidance recommends for primary analyses of abuse potential, and as AUC 0-8h , parallel to the QST outcomes.

To assess reinforcement value, we administered the Multiple-Choice Procedure once per session, at 480 min after dosing. Participants made a series of choices between a repeat of that session's dose and increasing amounts of money, and the outcome was the crossover point: the lowest amount of money chosen over the assigned dose for that test session [20].

2.8 Statistical Analysis

QST outcomes were summarized as the area under the change-from-baseline curve across the session and analyzed using linear mixed models. The oral and vaporized arms were analyzed separately. Dose and period (first, second or third session) were fixed effects, the latter to account for practice effects, and dose was a within-participant repeated factor under an unstructured variance-covariance matrix. Denominator degrees of freedom used the Kenward-Roger approximation [29]. The pain sensitivity index was analyzed identically. For peak DEQ Pleasurable and the Multiple-Choice Procedure crossover point, we report the companion manuscript's estimates, from the same model under a heterogeneous compound-symmetry variance-covariance matrix. Pairwise dose contrasts are reported as least-squares mean differences with 95% confidence intervals in original units, accompanied by Hedges' g computed from the model-estimated standard deviations (SDs) of the two doses compared [31].

A within-arm low-versus-high contrast tested the biphasic-dose hypothesis. Given the exploratory, proof-of-concept design, pairwise contrasts are reported for every outcome regardless of the omnibus test, and p-values were not corrected for multiple comparisons [49].

Analyses were performed in R version 4.5.2 with two-sided α = 0.05.

Additionally, we asked whether analgesia and abuse liability track drug exposure rather than administered dose. We related both outcomes to each session's peak concentration (C max ) and total exposure (AUC 0-8h ) of Δ9-THC plus 11-OH-THC [12, 57], using linear mixed models with a random intercept for participant, which accounts for the correlation among each participant's sessions (Supplementary Section S8).

Finally, we compared the conditions of both routes on their model-estimated analgesia and abuse liability using a benefit-risk Pareto frontier, an approach that has guided dose selection in tuberculosis [37] and cancer combination therapy [18]. We refer to our application as the Analgesia-Abuse Liability Frontier (AALF; Supplementary Section S5). Time courses are shown descriptively and were not tested statistically (Supplementary Section S9).

3. Results

>Results

3.1 Participants

Twenty older adults were enrolled, 10 per arm (Table 1). Overall, mean age was 70.0 (SD: 5.1) years, 30% (n=6) were female, and mean body mass index (BMI) was 28.6 (SD: 4.2) kg/m². Baseline clinical pain was low on the BPI-SF: mean severity 1.5 (SD: 1.6) and interference 1.1 (SD: 2.0). The two arms were balanced on demographic, baseline pain, and cannabis use characteristics (Table 1, all p≥.20). In the oral arm, 20% (n=2) were female, mean age was 70.5 (SD: 4.7) years, and mean BMI was 27.6 (SD: 4.2) kg/m². In the vaporized arm, 40% (n=4) were female, mean age was 69.5 (SD: 5.7) years, and mean BMI was 29.6 (SD: 4.2) kg/m². One vaporized-arm participant was withdrawn after a vasovagal episode during the first test session.

3.2 Pain Thresholds and Tolerance

We observed a dose effect of Δ9-THC on four of the seven QST measures. In the oral arm, dose significantly affected heat pain threshold (p=.025) and pressure pain threshold (p=.020; Table 2, Figure 1). The 5 mg dose raised heat pain threshold above both placebo (g=0.75, p=.041) and 10 mg (g=0.57, p=.046), and pressure pain threshold above 10 mg (g=0.86, p=.018). In the vaporized arm, dose significantly affected heat pain tolerance (p=.017) and mechanical pain threshold (p=.011). The 2 mg dose raised heat pain tolerance above 4 mg (g=0.55, p=.006) and mechanical pain threshold above placebo (g=1.25, p=.010). Across both arms, all five significant pairwise contrasts favored the lower doses, and neither higher dose differed significantly from placebo on any measure.

3.3 Pain Sensitivity Index

We observed no significant overall dose effect on the pain sensitivity index in either arm (oral p=.108; vaporized p=.800; Figure 2). Within the oral arm, however, the pairwise contrast of 5 mg against placebo was significant (mean difference: 0.44 SD, 95% CI 0.03 to 0.86; g=0.71, p=.040), whereas 10 mg did not differ from placebo (0.05 SD; g=0.07, p=.885). Neither vaporized dose differed from placebo (2 mg: g=-0.18, p=.611; 4 mg: g=-0.15, p=.763).

3.4 Abuse Liability

The dose effect on peak DEQ Pleasurable was significant in both arms (oral p=.038; vaporized p=.019; Supplementary Figure S2). In the oral arm, both doses raised it above placebo (5 mg: +19.1 points, 95% CI 3.4 to 34.8; g=0.84, p=.021; 10 mg: +22.8, 95% CI 2.0 to 43.6; g=0.77, p=.034). In the vaporized arm, 2 mg did not differ significantly from placebo (+21.0, 95% CI -9.1 to 51.0; g=0.68, p=.153), whereas 4 mg did (+35.1, 95% CI 12.2 to 58.1; g=1.49, p=.007). DEQ Pleasurable rose faster and higher after vaporization than after oral administration (Figure 3). Reinforcement value showed the same route asymmetry. In the oral arm, neither the dose effect (p=.670) nor contrast against placebo (5 mg: -$0.4, p=.911; 10 mg: +$2.1, p=.533) was significant. In the vaporized arm, the dose effect was significant (p=.018): 2 mg did not differ significantly from placebo (+$4.8, 95% CI -0.1 to 9.7; g=0.70, p=.052), whereas 4 mg raised the crossover point above placebo by $9.0 (95% CI 3.6 to 14.5; g=1.05, p=.004). When both outcomes were considered together, no other condition produced more analgesia than oral 5 mg at no greater abuse liability (Supplementary Section S5).

3.5 Time Course of Analgesia and Subjective Effects

After oral 5 mg, the pain sensitivity index showed more analgesia than placebo at every assessment from 60 to 360 min (by 0.39 to 0.73 SD; Figure 3). DEQ Pleasurable peaked at 150 min and was 4.5 points above placebo at 360 min. After oral 10 mg, the pain sensitivity index showed more analgesia than placebo through 180 min (by 0.09 to 0.29 SD) and less thereafter (by 0.17 and 0.09 SD). DEQ Pleasurable peaked at 90 min and was 10.1 points above placebo at 360 min. After vaporized 2 mg, the pain sensitivity index showed less analgesia than placebo at every assessment (by 0.02 to 0.33 SD). DEQ Pleasurable peaked at 30 min and was 0.5 points below placebo at 360 min. After vaporized 4 mg, the pain sensitivity index differed from placebo by -0.28 to +0.08 SD. DEQ Pleasurable peaked at 30 min and was 10.9 points above placebo at 360 min.

3.6 Relationships Between Exposure, Analgesia, and Abuse Liability

Both abuse liability measures increased with combined Δ9-THC and 11-OH-THC exposure. DEQ Pleasurable increased by 14.5 points per SD of C max (p<.001) and by 10.6 points per SD of AUC 0-8h (p=.003). Reinforcement value increased by $1.97 per SD of C max (p=.045) and by $1.98 per SD of AUC 0-8h (p=.044). No significant association between analgesia and either exposure metric was detected (p=.585 and p=.903; Supplementary Section S8).

3.7 General Safety and Adverse Events

Adverse events occurred only at vaporized 4 mg. Five events were recorded in two participants, including a severe vasovagal episode that prompted withdrawal. No adverse events were recorded across the 30 oral sessions. Detailed safety, cardiovascular, and tolerability outcomes are reported in the companion manuscript [14].

4. Discussion

In this randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in adults aged 65 and older, analgesic effects were detected on selected QST measures at the lower dose of each route.

Oral 5 mg increased heat pain threshold and the pain sensitivity index relative to placebo, whereas vaporized 2 mg increased mechanical pain threshold. Neither higher dose, oral or vaporized, differed significantly from placebo on any QST measure. Subjective pleasurable drug effects increased at both oral doses and at vaporized 4 mg, whereas reinforcement value increased significantly only after vaporized 4 mg. Oral 5 mg showed the most favorable observed balance between analgesia and abuse liability. It was the only condition whose pain sensitivity index showed more analgesia than placebo at every assessment, and its peak pleasurable response was the smallest of any active condition. It was also the only active condition on the benefit-risk Pareto frontier, which needs no weighting of one outcome against the other (Supplementary Section S5). This result highlights the relative advantage of the oral 5 mg compared to the other tested dose-route conditions, but should not be viewed as evidence of an optimal dose. All recorded adverse events occurred after vaporized 4 mg.

4.1 Implications for Understanding the Analgesic Effects and Abuse Liability of Δ9-THC

Among Older Adults

The analgesic response pattern was suggestive of an inverted U, rather than a monotonic increase with dose. Every significant low-versus-high contrast favored the lower dose: oral 5 mg over 10 mg on heat and pressure pain thresholds, and vaporized 2 mg over 4 mg on heat pain tolerance. Biphasic dose-response has been documented for Δ9-THC's anxiety-related effects: in healthy young adults, 7.5 mg of oral Δ9-THC reduced subjective distress after a psychosocial stressor, whereas 12.5 mg increased anxiety and negative mood [9]. For analgesia, the evidence is sparser but suggests a similar relationship. In healthy volunteers, smoked cannabis containing 4% Δ9-THC reduced capsaicin-induced pain, whereas 8% increased it and 2% had no effect [53]. A single 20 mg oral dose of Δ9-THC likewise produced hyperalgesia rather than analgesia on heat and cold testing [43].

The abuse liability of Δ9-THC among older adults is contingent not only on dose, but also on route of administration. Vaporization produced the faster and larger subjective response in this trial and, at 4 mg, the only increase in reinforcement value. In a laboratory study of adults with a mean age of 68.7 years, smoked cannabis likewise kept subjective ratings elevated for five hours [16]. A review of cannabis and aging found few and inconsistent studies in older adults [56], and no prior controlled study in adults aged 65 and older had assessed analgesia and abuse liability together across doses and routes. Taken together, these findings demonstrate measurable abuse liability of Δ9-THC and cannabis in older adults, and support jointly assessing analgesia and abuse liability of cannabinoids in this population.

4.2 Comparing Doses and Routes on Analgesia and Abuse Liability

After oral Δ9-THC, the duration of analgesia extended beyond the peak of abuse-related effects. Pleasurable effects after oral 5 mg peaked at 150 min and had nearly returned to placebo levels by 360 min. The pain sensitivity index, by contrast, showed more analgesia than placebo from 60 through 360 min, and the difference was largest at 180 min. An earlier study that compared oral and smoked Δ9-THC on both outcomes in 30 adults who used cannabis daily (mean age 27 years) found the same pattern [13]. In that study, abuse-related ratings after oral dronabinol peaked at 60 to 90 min, whereas analgesia peaked at 180 min. Peak analgesia on the cold pressor test was similar for oral dronabinol and smoked cannabis, but dronabinol's analgesia lasted longer and its abuse-related ratings were lower [13].

Our results differed from those of the earlier study on dose. Both outcomes changed at a lower oral dose in our study: 5 mg raised pleasurable effects and produced analgesia. In the earlier study, dronabinol raised abuse-related ratings and reduced pain sensitivity only at 20 mg [13]. Tolerance from daily cannabis use [11] and age-related changes in the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of Δ9-THC [15] may both contribute. The pain tests also differed. The earlier study measured pain while participants held one hand in 4 °C water (the cold pressor test). Our pain sensitivity index combined heat and cold pain thresholds and tolerances measured with a contact thermode.

4.3 Implications for Understanding the Relationship Between Exposure to Δ9-THC,

Analgesia, and Abuse Liability Outcomes

Both abuse liability measures tracked peak and total exposure to Δ9-THC and its active metabolite, whereas no significant association between analgesia and either exposure metric was detected. A significant association for abuse liability and a null for analgesia do not establish that the two outcomes depend on different features of the concentration-time profile: the analgesic estimates were imprecise, and the study was not designed to separate peak (C max ) from total (AUC 0-8h ) exposure, which co-vary within each route and participant. The data show only that the active condition with the lowest C max , oral 5 mg, was associated with the most analgesia, while abuse liability rose with exposure. Whether analgesic effects are relatively insensitive to exposure differences within this range may be evaluated in a trial designed to dissociate peak from total exposure, for example by varying the rate of delivery at a fixed dose.

4.4 Limitations

Several limitations bound our results. First, the sample was small, predominantly male and White, and composed entirely of healthy volunteers with prior cannabis exposure, although all tested negative for recent use at enrollment; generalizability to cannabis-naive older adults is therefore limited. Second, the pain model: evoked pain thresholds may not always translate into relief of chronic pain [45], although a systematic review found broadly similar cannabinoid response patterns in healthy volunteers and patients with chronic non-cancer pain [42]. Third, the design: between-participant variability may confound cross-route comparisons, and singledose sessions cannot establish sustained efficacy or characterize tolerance [11]. In the earlier study, smoked cannabis produced analgesia that peaked at 15 min [13]. The QST schedule here had no assessment between 5 and 60 min after vaporization, so it could have missed an effect this brief. Finally, the analysis: with seven QST measures and multiple contrasts, some significant results may be chance findings, because analyses were exploratory and uncorrected for multiplicity; the consistent direction of effects across measures, rather than any single pvalue, is the finding to weigh.

4.5 Future Directions

These findings support evaluating oral 5 mg Δ9-THC in repeated-dose trials involving older adults with chronic pain. Such trials should determine whether the observed laboratory analgesic effects translate into sustained improvements in clinical pain and function, while assessing how abuse-related responses change with continued treatment and how they differ between cannabis-naive and cannabis-experienced older adults. Given the association between chronic pain and CUD [25], such trials should also measure the emergence of problematic use alongside analgesia and tolerability. Additional doses below and around 5 mg would help determine whether the favorable observed balance extends across a dose range. Studies that distinguish the contributions of peak and total exposure could also guide delivery strategies. If analgesia is preserved while abuse liability is reduced by lowering peak concentration independently of total exposure, a delivery profile that maintains total exposure while limiting peaks could improve the benefit-risk balance. Repeated low doses of inhaled Δ9-THC could, in principle, approximate the profile of oral 5 mg where rapid onset matters, as in breakthrough pain [4]; whether they would preserve analgesia is unknown. The benefit-risk frontier could also be applied to other cannabinoids and to balanced Δ9-THC:CBD formulations.

5. Conclusion

In this randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in adults aged 65 and older, we compared two doses each of oral and vaporized Δ9-THC on laboratory-evoked pain and abuse liability, measured together across each session. Significant analgesic effects were confined to the lower dose of each route, consistent with the hypothesized inverted-U dose-response; oral 5 mg offered the most analgesia for the least abuse liability; and adverse events occurred only at vaporized 4 mg.
