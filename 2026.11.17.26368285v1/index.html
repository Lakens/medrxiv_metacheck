Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Cyberbullying research is constrained by inconsistent measurement, and the instruments most widely used were developed and validated in European and Asian school samples rather than in African higher-education settings. This study evaluated the factor structure, reliability, construct validity and sex measurement invariance of a 22-item measure of cybervictimisation and cyberperpetration based on the European Cyberbullying Intervention Project Questionnaire among health-professions students in Kenya. In a multicampus cross-sectional survey conducted between September and November 2025, 406 students (215 women, 191 men) enrolled at Kenya Medical Training College campuses completed 11 victimisation and 11 perpetration items together with measures of depression, anxiety and stress symptoms, problematic social-media use, self-esteem and well-being. Items were modelled as ordered categorical indicators by confirmatory factor analysis with the WLSMV estimator. A one-factor model was compared with a correlated two-factor model; reliability was estimated using ordinal alpha, McDonald's omega and composite reliability; convergent validity used Spearman correlations with bootstrap intervals and Holm adjustment; and invariance across sex was tested sequentially. Every item showed a pronounced floor effect. The correlated two-factor model fitted markedly better than the one-factor model (scaled difference χ²(1) = 48.06, p < .001) and, after three content-matched residual covariances were released, fitted the data well (CFI = .984, TLI = .982, RMSEA = .049, 90% CI .042 to .056, SRMR = .044).

Standardised loadings ranged from .627 to .928, and the latent correlation between dimensions was .849 (95% CI .803 to .895). Omega was .951 for cybervictimisation and .975 for cyberperpetration. Both dimensions correlated positively with distress and problematic social-media use and negatively with self-esteem, but showed negligible correlations with well-being. Configural, threshold and loading invariance across sex were 48 supported. The findings support scoring the instrument as two reliable, sex-comparable but 49 strongly overlapping dimensions.

Introduction

Digitally mediated communication now underpins the academic, professional and social lives of young adults, and with it has come sustained concern about aggression carried out through digital media. Cyberbullying is generally understood as intentional and repeated aggressive behaviour enacted through electronic means against a target who finds it difficult to defend themselves [1], and it is conventionally separated into cybervictimisation, the experience of being targeted, and cyberperpetration, the enactment of such behaviour towards others [2]. The two roles are neither mutually exclusive nor independent: a substantial minority of young people report both, and the resulting bully-victim group is consistently associated with the poorest psychosocial profile [3].

Because cyberbullying is associated with depressive and anxiety symptoms, lowered self-esteem and impaired academic functioning [4, 5], the credibility of the evidence base rests on whether the instruments used to quantify it measure what they claim to measure in the populations to which they are applied.

That condition is not reliably met. Systematic reviews of cyberbullying assessment repeatedly document substantial heterogeneity in how the construct is operationalised: instruments differ in whether a definition is presented to respondents, in the reference period (from the past month to the whole of life), in whether the response format is binary or frequency-based, in the threshold used to classify involvement, and in whether repetition and power imbalance are represented at all [6, 7]. These differences are not merely stylistic. They generate prevalence estimates that vary several-fold across studies, they make cross-study and cross-country comparison hazardous, and they leave open the possibility that apparent group differences reflect differential item functioning rather than genuine differences in the underlying behaviour [7]. Progress therefore depends less on the proliferation of new scales than on rigorous evaluation of existing ones in new populations.

The European Cyberbullying Intervention Project Questionnaire (ECIPQ) was developed to address precisely this problem. It comprises 22 items, 11 addressing victimisation and 11 addressing perpetration, arranged as matched pairs so that each behaviour is asked about from both perspectives. Items are answered on a five-point ordered frequency scale ranging from never to more than once a week within a defined reference period. The original validation, in 5,679 secondary school students from six European countries, supported a two-dimensional conceptualisation and reported comparable factor structures across the national subsamples [8].

The paired structure is a deliberate design feature that supports direct comparison of the two roles, but it also creates the conditions for shared method variance between matched items, an issue that becomes visible only when residual structure is examined explicitly.

Subsequent psychometric work has broadly supported a correlated two-factor structure while diverging on important details. Validations in Colombian and Chinese adolescents reproduced the two dimensions, although the Chinese adaptation retained only 14 of the 22 items after cultural review [9, 10]. A large Spanish study likewise supported two factors but reported markedly unequal reliability for the two subscales and established only configural invariance across gender [11], and a subsequent brief form combined cyberbullying items with traditional bullying items [12]. Reported factor correlations vary considerably, and studies differ in whether items were modelled as ordered categorical indicators using polychoric correlations or treated as continuous.

This distinction matters: with strongly skewed, low-base-rate items, treating ordered categories as continuous attenuates loadings and distorts fit statistics [13, 14], so evidence from the two modelling traditions is not directly comparable.

Evidence from African higher-education settings remains limited. Most ECIPQ validation work has been conducted with school-aged adolescents in Europe, Latin America and East Asia.

Kenyan research on cyberbullying has established that the phenomenon is common among university students and has described the forms it takes [15, 16], but published studies have generally relied on locally constructed item sets or on instruments used without formal psychometric evaluation in the sampled population. Health-professions students are a particularly relevant group: they are emerging adults in demanding training environments, they use digital platforms intensively for both study and social life, and their psychological well-being has direct implications for the future health workforce [17]. We are not aware of published evidence on the measurement properties of the ECIPQ in Kenyan health-professions students, although the absence of such evidence cannot be established with certainty from the published literature.

Sex is among the most frequently examined correlates of cyberbullying involvement, and metaanalytic evidence suggests small but systematic differences that vary with age [18]. Any such comparison presupposes that the instrument functions equivalently in women and men.

Measurement invariance formalises this requirement: configural invariance establishes that the same latent constructs are represented by the same items in both groups, threshold invariance that the points at which respondents move between response categories are comparable, and loading invariance that items relate to the latent variables with the same strength [19] [20] [21]. Where these conditions do not hold, observed differences between women and men confound genuine differences in behaviour with differences in how the items are answered. For ordered categorical indicators the sequence and its identification conditions differ from the familiar continuous case, and require the thresholds to be handled explicitly [22, 23]. The present study therefore set out to evaluate the factor structure, reliability, construct validity and measurement invariance by sex of the 22-item ECIPQ-based instrument administered to health-professions students at Kenya Medical Training College. The specific objectives were to describe the item-response distributions; to evaluate the factorability of the item set; to compare a one-factor with a correlated two-factor measurement model; to estimate the reliability of each dimension; to evaluate convergent validity against measures of psychological distress, problematic social-media use, self-esteem and well-being; to evaluate discriminant validity between the two dimensions; to test measurement invariance across female and male students; and to determine whether the complete 22-item set could be retained. We expected the items to be strongly skewed towards the lowest response category, the correlated two-factor model to outperform a single-factor specification, both dimensions to reach acceptable reliability, both dimensions to correlate positively with distress and problematic social-media use and negatively with self-esteem and well-being, and at least configural and loading invariance to be supported across sex.

Materials and methods

Study design, setting and reporting

This was a psychometric analysis nested within a multicampus cross-sectional survey of students at Kenya Medical Training College (KMTC), a state corporation that is the largest middle-level health training institution in Kenya and delivers pre-service and in-service training in nursing, clinical sciences, public health, diagnostic sciences, pharmaceutical sciences and related fields across campuses distributed nationally. Data were collected between 8 September and 1 November 2025. Participants were drawn from campuses in the eight KMTC sports zones used as the sampling frame (Port Reitz, Manza, Imenti, Nyeri, Kaptumo, Vihiga, Migori and Molo) together with Kabarnet campus; a small number of respondents reported other campuses. Because this manuscript addresses the performance of the instrument rather than the epidemiology of cyberbullying, campus and faculty composition are not analysed or tabulated here. Reporting follows the STROBE statement for cross-sectional studies [24] (S1 Checklist), with additional reference to COSMIN terminology for measurement properties [25, 26] and to the SAMPL recommendations for statistical reporting [27].

Participants and sampling

The target population was students enrolled in health-professions training programmes at the participating KMTC campuses. Students were eligible if they were aged 18 years or older, had completed at least one semester of study, and gave consent. The parent study used multistage cluster sampling in which the eight KMTC sports zones served as clusters, one campus was selected at random from each zone, and the target number of participants was allocated across campuses in proportion to campus enrolment; students within campuses were then approached at random. The sample size for the parent survey was determined using the Yamane formula for a finite population at 95% confidence and 5% precision, giving 377 participants, inflated by 20% to 452 to allow for non-response. A total of 406 eligible students consented and provided complete responses to all 22 instrument items, and this full set was used for every analysis reported here.

No separate sample-size calculation was performed for the psychometric analysis, and no retrospective power calculation is reported. The adequacy of a sample for ordinal confirmatory factor analysis depends jointly on the number of indicators, the number of factors, the magnitude of the loadings, the distribution of responses across categories, model complexity, group size and the estimator [28]; these considerations are returned to in the Discussion, particularly in relation to the multigroup models, where the female and male subsamples comprised 215 and 191 participants respectively.

Data collection

The questionnaire was self-administered electronically using KoBo Toolbox. Participants received a link to the instrument, which opened with a participant information page describing the purpose of the study, the voluntary nature of participation, the right to withdraw at any point without consequence, the confidentiality safeguards in place and the arrangements for referral to the college chaplain, the counselling department or the nearest health facility should any participant become distressed. Consent was recorded electronically by the participant confirming agreement before any study item was displayed; all 406 records in the analytic dataset carry an affirmative consent response. No names, admission numbers or other direct identifiers were collected, and records were identified only by an automatically generated study number.

Research assistants were drawn from student support services at the participating campuses and received two days of online training covering use of the electronic data-capture tool, ethical conduct, data management and security, and the counselling and referral pathway. Electronic capture enforced permitted response ranges at the point of entry. The instrument was administered in English, the language of instruction at KMTC.

Measures

Cyberbullying instrument

The instrument comprised 22 items -11 addressing cybervictimisation and 11 addressing cyberperpetration -based on the European Cyberbullying Intervention Project Questionnaire [8]. The 11 items of each dimension form matched pairs covering the same 11 behavioural content domains from the target and actor perspectives: insult and name-calling, threats, denigration to others, causing fear, password misuse and impersonation, rumour spreading, sharing embarrassing material, deliberate exclusion from online groups, offensive private messages, public ridicule, and identity misuse. Each item was answered on a five-point ordered scale:0 = never,1 = once or twice,2 = once or twice a month,3 = once a week,4 = more than once a week. Higher categories therefore indicate more frequent behaviour. Dimension totals range from 0 to 44, with higher scores indicating greater involvement. The full administered wording of every item is reproduced in S6 Table.

The correspondence between the administered items and the published ECIPQ warrants explicit statement. The internal content architecture of the administered instrument -11 matched victimisation-perpetration pairs spanning the content domains characteristic of the ECIPQ -is fully documented in the study records and is reproduced in S13 Table . However, the source version used, the permissions obtained and any translation, back-translation, expert-panel review, cognitive interviewing or pilot-testing procedures are not documented in the materials available for this analysis. We therefore make no claim that the administered items are semantically equivalent to the published ECIPQ, and we do not describe the instrument as a formally translated or cross-culturally adapted version [29]. Throughout this paper the measure is described as ECIPQ-based. The 22 items were treated as ordered categorical indicators in all factor analyses, and the two dimension totals were used in the external-validity analyses.

External construct measures

Depression, anxiety and stress symptoms were assessed with the 21-item Depression Anxiety Stress Scales (DASS-21), which comprises three seven-item subscales rated from 0 to 3 over the preceding week and scaled to a 0-42 range for each domain, with higher scores indicating greater symptom severity [30, 31]. DASS-21 scores are treated here as dimensional measures of symptom severity and psychological distress; they are not diagnostic categories and no diagnostic interpretation is made. Problematic social-media use was assessed with the six-item Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale, each item rated from 1 (very rarely) to 5 (very often), giving a total from 6 to 30, with higher scores indicating more problematic use [32, 33]. Consistent with the dimensional interpretation adopted here, elevated scores are described as problematic socialmedia use or elevated risk rather than as a diagnosis of addiction. Self-esteem was assessed with the 10-item Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale, with the five negatively worded items reverse-scored and a total ranging from 0 to 30, higher scores indicating higher self-esteem [34]. Well-being was assessed with the five-item World Health Organization Well-Being Index, rated from 0 to 5 over the preceding two weeks, summed and multiplied by four to give a percentage score from 0 to 100, higher scores indicating better well-being [35]. All four external measures were analysed as continuous total scores.

Data management

Each record carried a unique study identifier. Before analysis the prepared datasets were verified against prespecified expectations: record count, uniqueness of identifiers, absence of duplicates, sex distribution, absence of missing item responses, item values within the permitted 0-4 range, correct assignment of 11 items to each dimension, and exact correspondence between the fivecategory dataset and a four-category dataset in which the two highest categories were combined.

The results of these checks are reported in S1 Table. Involvement-role classifications were held in separate datasets and played no part in any analysis reported here, so that the measurement analysis is independent of any classification rule. Responses were complete for all 22 items and all four external measures in all 406 participants, so no missing-data procedure was required.

Item-level analysis

Each item was summarised by the valid number of responses, the frequency and percentage in each response category, the median and interquartile range, the mean and standard deviation for descriptive comparability only, skewness and the corrected item-total correlation with the remaining items of the same dimension. A floor effect was defined as the percentage of participants selecting the lowest category and a ceiling effect as the percentage selecting the highest category, with values above 15% conventionally regarded as notable [36]. A response category was defined as sparse when fewer than five participants selected it, either overall or within a sex group. Categories were recorded as observed overall and separately in women and men. The five original ordered categories were retained for all principal analyses; the fourcategory coding was used only for the prespecified sensitivity analyses, including the operational check on measurement invariance.

Factorability and factor retention

The polychoric correlation matrix of the 22 items was estimated by the two-step procedure of Olsson, with thresholds estimated from the marginal distributions and each correlation by bivariate maximum likelihood [37]. Factorability was assessed using the overall and item-level Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin measures of sampling adequacy [38] and Bartlett's test of sphericity [39], both computed on the polychoric matrix. The number of dimensions suggested by the data was examined by parallel analysis [40] adapted to ordinal data [41]: each item column was independently permuted, which preserves the observed marginal distributions while removing between-item association, and the polychoric matrix and its eigenvalues were recomputed for each of 1,000 permuted datasets (random seed 20260801). A factor was retained where its observed eigenvalue exceeded the 95th percentile of the corresponding permutation distribution.

Parallel analysis was treated as supplementary evidence and did not replace the confirmatory model comparison.

Confirmatory factor analysis

Confirmatory models treated all 22 items as ordered categorical indicators and were estimated by diagonally weighted least squares with robust standard errors and a mean-and variance-adjusted (scaled-shifted) test statistic (WLSMV) [42, 43]. The delta parameterisation was used with latent variances fixed at one, so that standardised loadings and the factor correlation are reported on the latent-response metric and the residual variance of each latent response variable equals one minus its squared standardised loading.

Analyses were conducted in R version 4.3.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) using lavaan 0.6-17 for polychoric correlations, confirmatory factor analysis and multigroup models, semTools 0.5-6 for measurement-invariance syntax and the heterotraitmonotrait ratio, and psych 2.4.1 for the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin statistic and Bartlett's test. The complete analysis code is provided in S1 File, and the reproducibility record, including random seeds and every analytical decision, is provided in S14 Table.

Three models were specified. Model 1 loaded all 22 items on a single general cyberbullying involvement factor. Model 2, the prespecified structure, loaded the 11 victimisation items on a cybervictimisation factor and the 11 perpetration items on a cyberperpetration factor, allowed the two factors to correlate, and fixed all cross-loadings and all residual covariances to zero. Model 3 was a theory-adjusted version of Model 2 in which a small number of residual covariances were released. Model 3 was fitted after estimation of Model 2 and is reported as a post-estimation, theory-supported modification rather than as a prespecified model. A residual covariance was released only where the modification index was among the largest observed, the two items shared documented behavioural content, and a maximum of three modifications in total was respected.

For each released parameter the item pair, the modification index, the estimated residual correlation and the content justification are reported in S11 Table.

Model fit and item retention

Model fit was evaluated using the scaled chi-square statistic, the comparative fit index (CFI), the Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) with its 90% confidence interval, and the standardised root mean square residual (SRMR), together with inspection of standardised loadings, residual variances, the factor correlation and any improper estimates. Conventional benchmarks (CFI and TLI at or above .95 strong and .90 minimally acceptable; RMSEA at or below .06 strong and .08 minimally acceptable; SRMR at or below .06 strong and .08 minimally acceptable) were treated as interpretive guidelines rather than as automatic pass-fail rules [44, 45], recognising that these benchmarks were derived for continuous data and that indices from categorical estimators tend to be optimistic. Nested models were compared using the scaled chi-square difference test appropriate to the estimator; unadjusted chisquare values were not subtracted directly [46]. Item retention was judged jointly on loading magnitude and its confidence interval, residual variance, the corrected item-total correlation, conceptual relevance and content coverage, content redundancy, local dependence, stability across sex, and the sensitivity analyses. Items were not deleted solely to improve global fit, and removal was considered only where several diagnostics agreed.

Reliability

Reliability was estimated separately for each dimension. Ordinal Cronbach's alpha was computed as standardised alpha from the polychoric correlation matrix of the items of that dimension [47] [48] [49]. McDonald's omega and composite reliability were computed from the standardised loadings of the accepted model, with omega additionally incorporating the released within-dimension residual covariance [50]. Average variance extracted was computed as the mean squared standardised loading. Corrected item-total correlations and alpha with each item removed were also computed. Confidence intervals for omega, composite reliability and average variance extracted were delta-method intervals obtained by defining these quantities as functions of the model parameters, and the interval for ordinal alpha was a percentile bootstrap interval from 1,000 case-resampling replications (seed 20260801). Omega was treated as the principal reliability coefficient, and a combined 22-item alpha is deliberately not reported as a headline value because the instrument is two-dimensional and a single coefficient would misrepresent that structure [51, 52].

Convergent and discriminant validity

Convergent validity was evaluated against six external measures with prespecified directions: positive associations of both ECIPQ dimensions with depression, anxiety and stress symptoms and with problematic social-media use, and negative associations with self-esteem and with wellbeing. Associations were quantified using Spearman's rank correlation between each dimension total and each external score, with percentile bootstrap 95% confidence intervals from 2,000 case-resampling replications using a fixed random seed of 20260801; all 2,000 replications were valid. Tests were two-sided and p-values were adjusted using the Holm procedure across the six external measures separately within each dimension [53, 54]. All correlations used the complete sample of 406 participants, since no external measure had missing values.

Discriminant validity between cybervictimisation and cyberperpetration was assessed using five converging approaches rather than any single criterion: the scaled difference test comparing the one-factor and correlated two-factor models; the magnitude of the latent factor correlation; the confidence interval for that correlation and whether it excluded unity; the Fornell-Larcker comparison of the squared latent correlation with each dimension's average variance extracted [55]; the heterotrait-monotrait ratio computed from the polychoric matrix [56]; and the observedscore correlation between the two dimension totals. No single threshold was treated as decisive.

Measurement invariance by sex

Invariance across female and male students was examined in a sequence appropriate to ordered categorical indicators [22, 23]. Separate single-group models were first fitted in women and in men to confirm that the accepted structure was interpretable within each group. Multigroup models were then identified following Wu and Estabrook, which is designed for ordered categorical indicators and avoids the untestable constraints implied by conventional identification: a configural model with the same pattern of loadings in both groups and with loadings, thresholds and factor correlations freely estimated; a threshold-invariance model in which all item thresholds were constrained equal across sex; and a model constraining both thresholds and loadings. Residual (strict) invariance was not tested because it is not required for comparing latent means. Each added level of constraint was evaluated with the scaled chi-square difference test and with working criteria of a change in CFI no worse than -.010 and an increase in RMSEA of no more than .015 [57, 58]; a significant difference test alone was not treated as sufficient grounds for rejecting invariance [59]. Partial invariance would have been considered had any parameter been non-invariant, retaining at least two invariant indicators per factor. Latent means were compared only after threshold and loading invariance had been established, with the female group as reference (latent mean fixed at zero, latent variance fixed at one). Because three item categories were unobserved among women under the five-category coding, and multigroup estimation requires every category to be observed in each group, the invariance models used the prespecified four-category coding, in which the two highest categories were combined and no category was empty in either sex.

Sensitivity analyses

Eight sensitivity analyses were specified. Prespecified analyses compared the five-category with the four-category coding, the prespecified with the theory-adjusted model specification, ordinal with continuous (Pearson) treatment of the items using robust maximum likelihood, and the three reliability estimators. Conditional analyses covered exclusion of exceptional response patterns.

Exploratory analyses covered exclusion of participants who scored zero on every item and estimation of a supplementary bifactor model. Two prespecified analyses were not undertaken and the reasons are stated: an exploratory refined item set was not estimated because the prespecified condition for item deletion was never met, and latent external-validity correlations were not modelled because the external measures were available only as total scores, so that a latent model would have required single-indicator constructs with assumed reliabilities. Results are summarised in S10 Table.

Discriminant validity

Evidence on the separability of the two dimensions was mixed but on balance supportive of two related factors ( S7 Table). The correlated two-factor model fitted materially better than the onefactor model (scaled difference χ²(1) = 48.06, p < .001; CFI .979 versus .954). The heterotrait-monotrait ratio was .842, below both the .90 and .85 thresholds. The observed-score correlation between the dimension totals was ρ = .655 (bootstrap 95% CI .586 to .717), substantial but far from unity. Against these supportive findings, the Fornell-Larcker criterion was met for cyberperpetration but not for cybervictimisation: the squared latent correlation of .721 exceeded the victimisation average variance extracted of .656 while remaining below the perpetration value of .780. Taken together with the single-factor result from parallel analysis, this indicates that a strong general involvement dimension underlies both subscales, so discriminant validity is classified here as partially supported.

Sensitivity analyses

Collapsing the two highest response categories reproduced the five-category solution 599 closely (CFI .984 versus .989; RMSEA .049 versus .045; factor correlation .849 versus .848).

600 Excluding the single participant with an identical non-zero response to all 22 items changed no 601 conclusion (CFI .984 versus .987; factor correlation .849 versus .839). The three reliability estimators agreed to within .003. Two analyses altered the picture in informative ways. Treating the items as continuous with robust maximum likelihood attenuated the loadings (range .508 to .845 versus .627 to .928), lowered the factor correlation (.807 versus .849) and fitted poorly (CFI = .872, RMSEA = .097), confirming that ordinal estimation is necessary for items distributed as these are. Excluding the 128 participants who scored zero on every item (four-category coding, because several upper categories became empty) weakened fit slightly and lowered the factor correlation and the smallest loading (CFI .981; RMSEA .052; factor correlation .785 versus .849; lowest loading .416 versus .627); these are valid uninvolved respondents whose exclusion restricts the range of the latent variables, so this specification is reported for completeness only and was not adopted. The supplementary bifactor model converged without improper estimates but fitted less well than the accepted model (CFI = .980, RMSEA = .057), with general-factor loadings of .557 to .878 and specific-factor loadings of .020 to .479. Latent external-validity modelling was not undertaken, for the reasons given in the Methods.

In summary, the accepted model was the theory-adjusted correlated two-factor ordinal model with three content-matched residual covariances; all 22 items were retained; configural, threshold and loading invariance across sex were supported, with residual invariance not identified in this parameterisation; and, because the correspondence between the administered items and the published ECIPQ could not be verified from the available records, the measure is described throughout as an ECIPQ-based instrument rather than as the ECIPQ itself.

Discussion

In 406 health-professions students at Kenya Medical Training College, a 22-item ECIPQ-based measure of cybervictimisation and cyberperpetration performed well as a two-dimensional ordered categorical instrument. A correlated two-factor model fitted markedly better than a single general factor, and after three content-matched residual covariances were released the model fitted well on conventional indices. All 22 items loaded substantially on their intended factor, both dimensions were highly reliable, and configural, threshold and loading invariance across female and male students were supported. Convergent validity was supported against distress, problematic social-media use and self-esteem but not against well-being, and discriminant validity was only partially supported because the two dimensions shared most of their variance.

No item warranted deletion, so the complete item set is retained.

Structural validity

The two-dimensional result aligns with the original European validation and with subsequent work in Colombian, Chinese and Spanish samples [8] [9] [10] [11]. That the same broad structure emerges in Kenyan health-professions students -emerging adults rather than school-aged adolescents, in a sub-Saharan African rather than a European, Latin American or East Asian setting -is a meaningful extension of the instrument's evidence base. Two differences from earlier work deserve comment. First, the latent correlation of .849 observed here is at the upper end of published estimates, and parallel analysis retained only a single factor. Second, the loadings were uniformly high, and the reliability coefficients exceeded .95 for both dimensions, substantially above the omega values of .82 for victimisation and .68 for aggression reported in the largest recent Spanish adolescent study [11]. Several features of the present study plausibly contribute: the use of polychoric correlations and a categorical estimator rather than continuous methods, which recovers larger loadings for skewed items [13, 14, 43]; an older and more homogeneous sample; and the retention of the complete 22-item set. We cannot determine from these data which of these explanations predominates, and none should be treated as established.

Factor correlation and local dependence

The strong latent correlation and the single-factor parallel-analysis result point the same way: a general involvement dimension accounts for much of the shared variance among these items in this population. This is conceptually intelligible. The ECIPQ items are constructed as matched pairs describing the same behaviour from the target and actor perspectives, so students who are involved in cyberbullying at all tend to endorse items from both sets, and the bully-victim overlap documented across the cyberbullying literature is substantial [3]. The supplementary bifactor solution was consistent with this reading, fitting less well than the correlated two-factor model and producing small specific loadings for several items (7 of 22 below .30), a pattern that typically indicates limited unique dimension-specific variance [61]. The three released residual covariances reinforce the point rather than undermining it: two of the three link matched victimisation-perpetration pairs describing exactly the same behaviour (public ridicule; insult and name-calling), and the third links two victimisation items whose content overlaps. These modifications were made after estimation, were limited to three, and were justified by documented content rather than by fit improvement alone; nonetheless they remain post hoc and require confirmation in an independent sample. The practical implication is that the two subscales should be reported separately, as the model comparison supports, while researchers should expect them to behave similarly and should not treat them as independent constructs.

Item distributions

Every item showed a pronounced floor effect, with between 63.5% and 84.7% of students selecting never and all medians at zero. Under conventional criteria such distributions would raise concern about item quality [36], but that reading would be mistaken here. These items ask about harmful behaviours that are genuinely uncommon in a general student population, so a low base rate is the expected finding rather than a defect, and the corrected item-total correlations of .512 to .803 confirm that the items continued to discriminate. What the distributions do require is appropriate estimation: with medians of zero and sparse upper categories, mean-based summaries would be misleading if used inferentially, and treating the items as continuous attenuated the loadings in our own sensitivity analysis. The distributions also constrain the invariance analysis, since the highest thresholds are estimated with limited precision and three item categories were unobserved among women. This is why the four-category coding was used for the multigroup models, and the close agreement between the five-category and four-category single-group solutions supports that choice.

Reliability and item retention

Both dimensions showed internal consistency well above conventional thresholds, and the three estimators agreed closely. We report omega as the principal coefficient because alpha assumes essential tau-equivalence and is a poor summary when a model-based estimate is available [51, 52], and we deliberately avoid a single 22-item coefficient, which would misrepresent a twodimensional instrument. High internal consistency should not be over-read. Coefficients of this magnitude partly reflect the general involvement dimension and the concentration of responses at the floor, and they raise the possibility of content redundancy among matched items. They are not evidence of unidimensionality, of structural validity or of construct validity, all of which rest here on the model comparison, the loading pattern, the invariance analysis and the external correlations. Because no loading fell below .40, no deletion improved dimension reliability and no item behaved inconsistently across sex, the complete content domain was preserved. Retaining all 22 items also keeps the instrument comparable with the international literature, which matters more for a measure whose principal value is cross-study comparability than a marginal gain in parsimony would.

Convergent validity

Both dimensions correlated moderately and positively with depression, anxiety and stress symptoms and with problematic social-media use, and negatively with self-esteem, in line with the associations reported for cyberbullying involvement across the international literature [3, 4] and in nursing and health-professions student samples [17]. These are cross-sectional associations and no causal interpretation is warranted in either direction. The negative association with selfesteem, while clearly present, was modest in magnitude. The clearest departure from expectation was well-being: correlations with the WHO-5 were essentially zero for both dimensions and remained non-significant after adjustment. Several readings are possible. The WHO-5 measures positive subjective well-being over the preceding two weeks rather than symptom burden, and positive and negative affect are not simple opposites, so an instrument capturing the presence of good feeling may be less sensitive to a low-frequency adverse exposure than symptom scales are [35]. The two-week reference window is also shorter than the reference period for the cyberbullying items. We cannot adjudicate between these explanations, and the finding is reported as a limitation of convergent validity rather than explained away.

Discriminant validity and sex invariance

Integrating five sources of evidence, discriminant validity is best classified as partially supported.

The model comparison, the confidence interval for the latent correlation excluding unity, the heterotrait-monotrait ratio below .90 and the observed-score correlation of .655 all support two distinguishable dimensions; the failure of the Fornell-Larcker criterion for victimisation and the single-factor parallel-analysis result qualify that conclusion. Reliance on any one criterion would have produced a different and less defensible verdict [55, 56]. The invariance findings are more straightforward. Configural, threshold and loading invariance were supported and required no partial-invariance model. This is a stronger result than the configural-only invariance reported in the largest recent Spanish adolescent validation [11], and it means that scores can be compared between female and male students in this population without item-level bias. The latent mean differences observed here are consequently interpretable as differences in the underlying constructs rather than as measurement artefacts. Residual invariance was not tested and is not a prerequisite for comparing latent means.

Implications

For research in Kenyan higher education, these findings support using the instrument with its complete 22-item set and scoring it as two separate subscales rather than as a single total, while reporting the latent correlation so that readers can judge how far the dimensions diverge in a given sample. Because measurement is invariant across sex, studies in comparable populations can compare women and men directly without first re-establishing invariance, although researchers examining other groupings -campus, faculty, programme or year of study -should not assume that invariance extends to them. For multicampus surveys, the close agreement between the five-category and four-category codings offers a practical fallback where sparse upper categories threaten estimation. For health-professions education, the associations with distress and with problematic social-media use suggest that cyberbullying is worth measuring within existing student support and counselling structures, though the instrument is a research measure of behavioural frequency and is not a diagnostic or clinical screening tool, and should not be used as one. Methodologically, the attenuation observed when items were treated as continuous is a concrete argument for categorical estimation whenever cyberbullying items with low base rates are modelled.

Strengths and limitations

The study has several strengths. The sample was drawn from multiple campuses distributed across the country, the sex groups were close to balanced, and data were complete for all 22 items and all external measures in all 406 participants. The complete item set was modelled as ordered categorical indicators using polychoric correlations and a robust categorical estimator, the model comparison was prespecified, three reliability coefficients and five forms of discriminant-validity evidence were reported rather than a single favourable statistic, measurement invariance was tested with an identification strategy appropriate to ordered categories, and every model modification, substitution and non-undertaken analysis is documented explicitly.

The limitations are equally clear. The design is cross-sectional, so no causal inference is possible from the validity correlations, and no test-retest or longitudinal invariance evidence is available.

All measures were self-reported and collected in a single sitting, so recall bias, social-desirability bias and common-method variance may all have influenced the associations reported, most plausibly by inflating them. The pronounced floor effects and sparse upper categories mean that the highest thresholds are imprecisely estimated, and three item categories were unobserved among women under the five-category coding; the invariance analysis therefore relied on the four-category coding, which mitigates but does not remove this constraint. The subsample sizes for the multigroup models (215 women, 191 men) are modest for a 22-indicator two-factor model, and while the models converged without improper estimates, the invariance conclusion would benefit from replication in a larger sample [28]. Invariance across campus, faculty and year of study was not examined. No behavioural or informant-based criterion was available, so criterion and predictive validity remain untested. The three residual covariances were released post hoc. Documentation of the instrument's provenance and of any adaptation procedure was not available, so semantic equivalence with the published ECIPQ could not be assessed and no cognitive interviewing was conducted to establish how Kenyan students interpret the items [62].

Finally, participants were students at participating KMTC campuses and the findings should not be read as representative of Kenyan students nationally, of students at other institutions, or of non-student young adults.

Future research

Independent replication in other Kenyan institutions, including universities and non-health programmes, would establish how far these findings generalise. Priorities include test-retest reliability and longitudinal invariance; invariance across campus, faculty and year of study in samples large enough to support it; cognitive interviewing and qualitative work on how students interpret individual items in this setting; item-response-theory analysis, which is well suited to the sparse upper categories observed here; and criterion and predictive validity against outcomes measured independently of self-report. Confirmation of the three residual covariances in an independent sample would establish whether the local dependence observed here is a stable property of the paired item design. Assembling and publishing the instrument's provenance and adaptation record would allow a future study to state directly whether the measure is the ECIPQ or an adapted derivative of it.

Conclusion

Among health-professions students at Kenya Medical Training College, a 22-item ECIPQ-based measure functioned as a reliable two-dimensional instrument. A correlated two-factor model representing cybervictimisation and cyberperpetration was clearly preferable to a single general factor, all 22 items loaded substantially on their intended dimension and were retained, and both dimension scores showed high internal consistency. Construct validity was supported against measures of psychological distress, problematic social-media use and self-esteem, but not against well-being. Discriminant validity was partially supported: the two dimensions were reliably distinguishable but strongly correlated, and a general involvement factor accounted for much of their shared variance.

Measurement was invariant across female and male students at the configural, threshold and loading levels, so scores can be compared between women and men in this population without item-level bias. The evidence supports using the complete 22-item set and reporting the two dimensions as separate but related subscales in research applications; it does not support use as a diagnostic or clinical screening instrument. Because the correspondence between the administered items and the published ECIPQ could not be verified from available records, the measure should be described as ECIPQ-based until provenance documentation is available.

Independent replication, test-retest and longitudinal evidence, and validation against criteria measured independently of self-report remain necessary before these conclusions can be extended to other Kenyan student populations.
