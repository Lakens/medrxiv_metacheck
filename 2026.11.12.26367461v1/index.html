To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 2 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

Abstract

Background: Unhealthy food marketing negatively affects children's dietary patterns. However, little is known about the extent and nature of unhealthy food marketing to children on YouTube due to reliance on manual assessment methods.

Objectives: To use a novel artificial intelligence (AI) system (CHild Oriented food Product Promotion Evaluation and Detection system, CHOPPED) to examine the extent and nature of unhealthy food marketing in YouTube videos suggested to Avatar accounts created for children and adults based on age and gender.

Methods: Over two months of continuous monitoring, CHOPPED collected marketing instances played before, during, and after videos suggested by YouTube's algorithm to six child and two adult accounts that were not signed in and differed by age (2-4, 5-8, 9-12, ≥18 years) and gender across four Canadian cities (Calgary, Vancouver, Toronto, Halifax). CHOPPED characterized the extent and nature of monthly child-targeted unhealthy food marketing instances during 24-hour and typical 2-hour daily viewing periods.

Results: CHOPPED collected 60,633 marketing instances. No food marketing was shown to child accounts 2-4 years. On average, child accounts (5-12 years) were shown 25 monthly unhealthy food marketing instances during a 2-hour daily viewing period, 24 of which were child-targeted. The boy's account (9-12 years) was shown a significantly higher monthly frequency of child-targeted unhealthy food marketing instances (340, 70.2%) than the boy's account (5-8 years; 241, 66.2%), the girl's account (5-8 years; 261, 60.6%), the man's account (224, 57.7%), and the woman's account (263, 54.7%) during a 24-hour (p<0.05) but not a 2-hour daily viewing period. Monthly frequencies of some core child-targeted techniques shown to the boy's account (9-12 years) were also higher than other accounts during a 24-hour (p<0.05) but not a 2-hour daily viewing period.

Conclusions: This study pioneered AI-driven analysis of the extent and nature of unhealthy food marketing to children on YouTube. Children's potential exposure to such marketing on YouTube may be low. There was some evidence that the older boys' account was shown more monthly child-targeted unhealthy food marketing instances and of some core child-targeted techniques than other accounts. On YouTube, food marketing may target a general audience rather than children by age and gender.

BACKGROUND

Dietary patterns develop in childhood and can persist into adulthood [1]. Globally, children's dietary patterns are concerning, with more than half of children consuming poor quality diets [2].

In Canada, children's dietary patterns are also poor, with average scores of just 40 out of 80 on the Healthy Eating Food Index-2019 [3]. Additionally, children's diets are high in ultraprocessed foods and low in fruits and vegetables [4, 5]. A key environmental factor that shapes children's dietary patterns is the marketing of unhealthy foods and beverages (hereafter "food"), such as those high in sodium, sugar, and/or saturated fats [6]. Unhealthy food marketing influences children's dietary patterns and body weight by influencing their product awareness and preferences, and subsequently their food requests, food purchases, and consumption [6].

Food companies are increasingly shifting their marketing spending towards digital marketing, where the influence on children's food preferences, attitudes, and consumption may be greater than on non-digital media [7, 8]. YouTube, the most popular online video sharing platform among children [9] [10] [11], has been shown to drive nearly three times more product purchases than other platforms [10].

The extent of unhealthy food marketing to children on digital media refers to the frequency of unhealthy food marketing they may encounter [12]. A study of YouTube's most popular childtargeted videos found that foods were marketed more frequently than any other product within these videos (38.0% of marketing instances), and a majority of these marketing instances promoted unhealthy foods (56.3%) [13]. Another study in Canada showed that children were exposed to 1.96 food marketing instances in 30 minutes of watching YouTube videos, with 87.0% promoting unhealthy foods [14]. The nature of food marketing, which refers to the persuasive marketing techniques used to target children, plays a key role in increasing sales of marketed foods [12]. One study that examined energy drink marketing to children on YouTube, Facebook, and Twitter found that the most employed marketing techniques were thrill-seeking themes, presence of celebrities, and product health and personal benefit claims [15].

A key limitation of prior studies is that they all used manual methods to assess the extent and nature of unhealthy food marketing to children on YouTube and other digital media. Although manual methods can provide detailed assessments of marketing content and persuasive techniques, they are labour intensive, highly subjective and limit the volume of digital content that can feasibly be analyzed [16]. This is particularly the case for digital media due to the persistent and highly targeted nature of digital marketing.

Artificial intelligence (AI), which refers to computer systems capable of executing tasks typically associated with human intelligence [17], can help to analyze large volumes of marketing by automating difficult, time-consuming, or sometimes unattainable tasks for researchers. We recently developed a novel AI system capable of monitoring the extent and nature of food marketing on digital media. The CHild Oriented food Product Promotion Evaluation and Detection system (CHOPPED) consists of seven modules and uses commercially available computing services and custom-made machine learning models. These seven modules include marketing data collection, feature extraction, food and brand identification, healthfulness classification, marketing technique classification, policy adherence classification, and aggregation and visualization. An initial prototype of the AI system [18] performed poorly, and thus, it was abandoned and a new system-CHOPPED-was developed by a new team co-led by DLO and TW. This new system was used in the present study. A forthcoming publication describes development and validation of the CHOPPED AI system in further detail (Al Sharani et al, unpublished observations).

The aim of this study was to use our newly developed AI system (CHOPPED) to examine the extent and nature of unhealthy food marketing in YouTube videos suggested to Avatar accounts created for children and adults based on age and gender.

METHODS

Study Design

This study collected and analysed marketing instances from YouTube using our AI system, CHOPPED. It used publicly available data and did not involve human participants; therefore, it was deemed exempt from ethical review by the Conjoint Health Research Ethics Board at the University of Calgary.

Data Collection

In order to collect marketing instances from YouTube's recommended videos, one boy and one girl Avatar account were created for each of three age categories including preschool (2-4 years), younger (5-8 years), and older (9-12 years) children informed by the Advertising Standards Authority's previous work in the UK (Table 1) [19]. We also created two adult accounts for one woman and one man (≥18 years) to assess whether there were differences in the extent and nature of food marketing instances shown to child and adult accounts. The process of data collection is described in Figure 1.

For each account, 15 search terms were employed that were tailored to the preferences of each of the eight accounts based on age and gender (e.g., Fortnite videos for the boy's account aged 9-12 years, craft ideas for the girl's account aged 9-12 years; Supplementary Tables 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8 9, Additional File 1). These search terms were selected based on seven studies examining YouTube video preferences and digital media use among boys, girls, men and women across particular age groups between 2018 and 2022 [20] [21] [22] [23] [24] [25] [26]. These search terms were essential because the accounts initially lacked browsing history that the YouTube algorithm could use to suggest videos. By using a designated search term as an initial filter, this process continuously generated new browsing data that aligned with each account's intended age and gender profile, enabling the YouTube algorithm to deliver appropriately tailored content. CHOPPED collected all skippable (allows viewers to skip after 5 seconds) and non-skippable (must be watched before a video can be viewed) video marketing instances played before, during, and after each video and moved on to the next video recommended by YouTube's algorithm for each of the eight accounts. All marketing instances were collected from Avatar accounts that were not signed into YouTube. Each account was assigned a dedicated device to prevent viewing and search histories from carrying over between accounts. Each account was permitted to accumulate a short-term browsing history as it searched and viewed videos, consistent with how YouTube's algorithm adapts recommendations and marketing instance delivery in real time for users who are not signed in. This approach was necessary because during a pilot test, we found that no food marketing instances were delivered to signed-in child accounts due to YouTube's policy which automatically redirects accounts for children under 13 years to YouTube Kids unless a parent explicitly enables a supervised experience on regular YouTube, an opt-in step our pilot accounts did not use [27].

The AI system began by searching a given term on each account's search bar using YouTube's built in search functionality. It then compared the video title to the search term and proceeded to select a recommended video from the list of results that was most similar to the search term using fuzzy matching, a technique used to identify strings or terms that are approximately similar rather than exact matches. After selecting a random recommended video with the highest similarity, the system viewed it for up to 20 minutes, or in full if the video was shorter, and collected the URL for all marketing instances in the video within the 20 minutes period, excluding marketing instances that appeared after the 20-minute cut off. A 20-minute limit was imposed to prevent long videos from dominating the viewing time for each search term. This approach assumed that children view each consecutive video without an ad-blocker. After the video was complete, it parsed through the titles of subsequent videos recommended by the algorithm, compared them to the search terms, and clicked consecutive videos that were most similar to the search terms. If the AI system encountered a video with a similarity percentage of ≤50%, it restarted the search from the search bar and selected a more relevant video. We confirmed that this limited browsing patterns that did not align with the intended preferences of each account by manually viewing 6 hours' worth of videos shown to each of the eight accounts. Without these search terms, the algorithm tended to drift toward unrelated video recommendations which did not reflect the viewing patterns of each age and gender group. A marketing instance was defined as a video advertisement of a product, service, or brand displayed before, during, or after a video. Food-related marketing instances were defined as those that promoted a food product, main dish, multiple food products, or a multi-component meal through images, text, or auditory cues [28, 29]. For marketing instances promoting multiple food products or a multi-component meal, each individual product was evaluated separately. The system classified the healthfulness of foods using the University of Toronto Food Classification List that includes nutrition information for over 80,000 pre-packaged products and 20,000 restaurant menu items up to 2020 [30]. Foods were deemed 'unhealthy' if they contained added saturated fats, sodium, or free sugars that exceeded Health Canada's thresholds for foods that would be restricted under their proposed policy to restrict unhealthy food marketing to children (Table 2) [28, 29]. Food-related marketing instances were classified as unhealthy if at least one of the promoted products was unhealthy. Only when all individual products were classified as healthy was the entire marketing instance considered healthy. Brand marketing instances where no food products were featured were excluded.

For each marketing instance, the AI system also classified all of the marketing techniques present using Health Canada's criteria for classifying food marketing techniques on digital media [28, 29]. Health Canada's marketing techniques include: presence of branded characters; presence of licensed characters; presence of celebrities/public figures; presence of other cartoon characters; child themes/visual design; use of child language; appeals to fun/cool; games and activities; price promotions, incentives, or giveaways; cross-promotions; unusual product flavour/shape; presence of children; adult-child situations; appeals to health/nutrition; displays of corporate and social responsibility; promotion of product convenience; appeals to flavour/texture; viral marketing; calls-to-action; special effects; and songs/music.

A child-targeted marketing instance was defined as a marketing instance that employed marketing techniques designed to capture the attention of children to influence their food preferences, desires, or purchasing intentions [28, 29]. To identify child-targeted techniques, we considered a subset of Health Canada's techniques based on the Child Appealing Packaging Tool [31] and a recent review that examined marketing techniques used to target children across multiple platforms [32]. Based on these studies, marketing techniques were classified as core child-targeted, broad child-targeted, or non-specific techniques as described in Supplementary Table 10, Additional File 1. We created these three classifications because Health Canada's marketing techniques include techniques that may be present in the majority of marketing instances and were not categorized as child-targeted in previous research [28, 29]. In the current study, marketing instances were considered child-targeted if they employed at least one corechild targeted marketing technique including: child themes/visual design; use of child language; appeals to fun/cool; games and activities; unusual product flavour, unusual product appearance; and adult-child situations. The presence of branded characters, licensed characters, celebrities/public figures, or other cartoon characters were excluded from the definition of corechild targeted techniques as CHOPPED did not have the capacity to capture these techniques at the time of the analyses. However, following the development of this aspect of the system, we subsequently conducted additional analyses that included these techniques, as reported in the sensitivity analyses.

The early version of CHOPPED used in this study was validated separately using a manually labelled sample of 647 marketing instances collected using the same child and adult accounts and search protocol described above. This validation sample was collected separately from the study dataset with an oversample of food marketing instances to ensure reliable estimation of sensitivity and specificity. Manual labels were assigned independently by two trained coders following a standardized coding manual, with disagreements resolved by consensus discussion.

Briefly, CHOPPED demonstrated high sensitivity (0.84, 95% CI: 0.77-0.89) and specificity (0.83, 95% CI: 0.79-0.86) for identifying food marketing instances, and high sensitivity (0.85, 95% CI: 0.81-0.89) but lower specificity (0.58, 95% CI: 0.47-0.70) for identifying unhealthy food marketing instances. For the majority of core child-targeted marketing techniques (e.g., child themes/visual design, appeals to fun/cool, adult-child situations), the system showed high sensitivity but low specificity, indicating a tendency toward false positives rather than false negatives. Data Analysis 282

To characterize the extent of unhealthy food marketing that targeted children, the total monthly 283 number of unhealthy food marketing instances that used one or more core child-targeted 284 techniques was expressed as frequencies and proportions of the total number of monthly food 285 marketing instances collected from each of the eight accounts. To characterize the nature of 286 unhealthy food marketing that targeted children, the total monthly number of unhealthy food 287 marketing instances that used each core child-targeted technique was expressed as frequencies 288 and proportions of the total number of monthly unhealthy food marketing instances that used any of the core-child targeted techniques collected from each of the eight accounts. Following further AI system development, the nature of unhealthy food marketing that targeted children was also characterized based on the presence of branded characters, licensed characters, celebrities/public figures, or other cartoon characters in a subsample of the data.

Descriptive statistics including frequencies and proportions of the extent and nature of food marketing were calculated. Poisson regression models were performed to assess whether the mean monthly frequencies of: food marketing instances, unhealthy food marketing instances, healthy food marketing instances, child-targeted food marketing instances, child-targeted unhealthy food marketing instances, and the use of each of the marketing techniques differed across the eight accounts. The unit of observation was the monthly frequency count for each account in each of the 2 months of data collection (February and March), yielding two observations per account (n=16 total). Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the impact of including the marketing techniques outlined by Health Canada that we did not classify as core child-targeted. First, the frequency and proportion of all unhealthy marketing instances that were child-targeted were calculated and compared to those from our primary analysis under two alternative definitions of child-targeted food marketing: using core and broad child-targeted techniques; and using core and broad childtargeted techniques along with non-specific techniques. Because the same marketing instances were reclassified under each of the three nested definitions rather than representing independent samples, Friedman tests were used to compare mean monthly frequencies of child-targeted unhealthy food marketing instances across the three definitions, with pairwise Wilcoxon signedrank tests used for post-hoc comparisons where the omnibus test was significant.

In the second sensitivity analysis, we calculated the frequency of child-targeted food marketing instances and then applied the same approach to examine the impact on our findings when branded characters, licensed characters, celebrities/public figures, and other cartoon characters were included as core-child targeted techniques and compared to the two alternative definitions of child-targeted food marketing: using core and broad child-targeted techniques; and using core and broad child-targeted techniques along with non-specific techniques. These frequencies were also compared within each definition when including and excluding the presence of characters and celebrities/public figures. This analysis was performed on a smaller subset of the available data (n= 2,599 food marketing instances) because the corresponding videos of some marketing instances were no longer available on YouTube at the time of the analysis.

Sensitivity Analyses

In sensitivity analyses, we compared two alternative definitions of child-targeted unhealthy food marketing to our primary definition which included only core child-targeted techniques (Supplementary Figure 1 and 2, Additional File 1). Under our primary definition, almost all unhealthy food marketing instances were considered child-targeted (child accounts: 283, 94.0%; adult accounts: 243, 91.0%). Under the first alternative definition which included core and broad child-targeted techniques, we found that the mean monthly frequency and proportion of childtargeted unhealthy food marketing were identical to results from our primary analyses (child accounts: 283, 94.0%; adult accounts: 243, 91.0%; p > 0.05). However, under the second definition which included core, broad, and non-specific techniques, all unhealthy food marketing instances were classified as child-targeted (child accounts: 301, 100.0%; adult accounts: 267, 100.0%; p > 0.05). These frequencies did not significantly differ from those in our primary analyses or when using the first alternative definition.

In the second sensitivity analysis of a subsample of 2,599 food marketing instances (23.0% of total sample), the presence of branded characters, licensed characters, celebrities/public figures, or other cartoon characters was very low across the subsample with proportions ranging from 1.0% to 16.0% during a 24-hour viewing period, with branded characters being the most prevalent and licensed characters the least (Supplementary Table 12, Additional File 1). The prevalence of these characters was not significantly different across accounts.

When the presence of characters and celebrities/public figures were included as child-targeted techniques, the majority of unhealthy food marketing instances in the subsample were considered child-targeted under our primary definition (child accounts: 145, 79.2%; adult accounts: 151, 77.4%) and under the first alternative definition (child accounts: 145, 79.2%; adult accounts: 151, 77.4%) with no differences between them (Supplementary Figure 2, Additional File 1).

Under the second alternative definition, all unhealthy food marketing instances were classified as child-targeted (child accounts: 183, 100.0%; adult accounts: 195, 100.0%). These frequencies did not significantly differ from those in our primary analyses or when using the first alternative definition. Additionally, there were no significant differences in the frequency of child-targeted marketing instances within each definition when including or excluding the presence of characters and celebrities/public figures (Supplementary Figure 2, Additional File 1 ). Therefore, the classification of unhealthy food marketing as child-targeted was not meaningfully influenced by whether the presence of characters or celebrities/public figures was included as a qualifying criterion, supporting the robustness of our main findings.

DISCUSSION

This study pioneered the use of AI to assess the extent and nature of unhealthy food marketing that children may encounter on YouTube. Of all the food marketing instances shown to child accounts, 64% were child-targeted marketing instances for unhealthy foods. Five key findings emerged from this research. First, child accounts between 5-12 years were shown a mean of 25 unhealthy food marketing instances per month, or less than one per day during children's typical 2-hour daily viewing period on YouTube. Second, younger child accounts (aged 2-4 years) were not shown any food marketing instances. Third, the boy's account 9-12 years was shown more monthly instances of child-targeted unhealthy food marketing and of some core child-targeted techniques than most other accounts based on a 24-hour time frame. Fourth, the inclusion of characters and celebrities/public figures did not change our findings. Lastly, most or all unhealthy food marketing instances were classified as child-targeted regardless of the definition used.

Our first key finding was that child accounts between 5-12 years were shown 25 unhealthy food marketing instances per month, or less than one per day during a 2-hour daily viewing period.

Twenty four of these marketing instances were child-targeted. Our AI-based analysis showed lower monthly potential exposure to unhealthy food marketing compared to previous studies.

One prior study that used manual methods to examine children's exposure to unhealthy food marketing on YouTube estimated that children may be exposed to 44 unhealthy food marketing instances per month for the same viewing period of 2-hours per day [13]. Our results suggest a monthly potential exposure that is somewhat lower than the previously reported estimate.

Moreover, given that the AI system had a low specificity in detecting unhealthy food marketing instances and a low sensitivity in identifying healthy food marketing instances we may have overestimated children's potential exposure. This inconsistency in estimates of the frequency of unhealthy food marketing instances may be explained by differences in methodology. First, the prior study manually monitored potential exposure using incognito mode, which does not account for personalized algorithms and likely does not capture the tailored marketing content that children may encounter, possibly misestimating children's potential exposure. Additionally, the prior study was conducted over a limited period of 3 weeks, with only 54 hours and 35 minutes of recorded video, offering a limited view of the marketing landscape. By comparison, we collected 5,376 hours of marketing data over 2 months across the four child accounts that were shown ads. It is also important to note that our study did not specify the exact timing of the 2-hour viewing period. Instead, we assumed that the frequency of marketing instances remained consistent throughout a 24-hour period and proportionally calculated the mean number of marketing instances for a 2-hour window. Previous studies have shown that marketing may fluctuate based on the time of day [34], potentially driving this inconsistency in findings.

However, it is possible that the difference in estimates could be entirely due to the fact that the prior study was conducted in Malaysia, whereas our study was conducted in Canada. Previous studies have shown that children's food marketing exposure varies by country [35].

Nevertheless, while we found that children's exposure to unhealthy food marketing on YouTube may be relatively low, YouTube represents only one digital media platform on which children may encounter such marketing and exposure across platforms is cumulative. Moreover, a high proportion of the instances observed were child-targeted, which could potentially have a negative impact on children's dietary patterns [12].

Our second key finding was that younger child accounts (aged 2-4 years) were not shown any food marketing instances. This is likely because the search terms that were used for these accounts allowed YouTube's algorithm to identify these accounts as belonging to young children. As a result, the algorithm redirected these accounts to videos with YouTube's "madefor-kids" designation, which prohibits food marketing on these videos [27]. Our findings do not align with previous research that found food marketing instances displayed before, during, and after popular "made-for-kids" videos on YouTube [37]. However, that study was conducted before YouTube implemented its new regulations which restrict food marketing on "made-forkids" content [27]. Thus, our findings suggest that YouTube's current protective measure, the "made-for-kids" designation, may effectively protect younger children (aged 2-4 years) from food marketing, but leaves older children (aged 5-12 years) exposed to unhealthy food marketing. Importantly, this finding should be interpreted with caution, as in real-world settings young children may be exposed to marketing through shared devices or by using others' accounts. If researchers and policymakers only monitor unhealthy food marketing on "made-forkids" videos, they risk underestimating children's potential exposure to unhealthy food marketing, as older children often watch general-audience content [23, 24].

Our third key finding was that the boy's account (9-12 years) was shown more monthly unhealthy food marketing instances and more child-targeted unhealthy food marketing instances than most other accounts based on a 24-hour time frame. Moreover, these marketing instances more often included adult-child situations, child language, child themes/visual design, and appeals to fun/cool. However, these differences were not significant when considering children's typical viewing period of 2-hours per day and may therefore lack public health relevance as it is unrealistic for a child to spend 24 hours a day watching YouTube. Additionally, the AI system's low specificity in identifying unhealthy food marketing instances suggests that the frequency of monthly unhealthy food marketing instances may be overestimated. Our findings do not align with a previous study in the UK, which found that a child account 9-12 years was shown a similar number of child-targeted unhealthy food marketing as an adult account on YouTube over a 24-hour monitoring period [36]. This discrepancy may be explained by differences in the monitoring period which was limited to one week in the UK study or by variations in how marketers target children across the UK and Canada. With respect to our findings that the boy's account was shown marketing instances that used different marketing techniques compared to other accounts, a previous study showed that exposure to child-targeted marketing techniques on television was higher for girls than for boys in Calgary, Canada, especially for the marketing technique of use of child language [38]. Another study on social media that included children between 12-16 years found that there was no difference in the frequency of food marketing instances shown to boys and girls [39]. These differences in findings could be due to differences in age and gender targeting on different platforms. Overall, although we found some differences in the frequency and nature of unhealthy food marketing shown to the older boy's account, the extent and nature of unhealthy food marketing during typical use appears relatively similar by age and gender on YouTube. This could indicate that food marketing on YouTube aims to maximize overall reach and may not target children by age or gender.

Our fourth key finding was that the inclusion of characters and celebrities/public figures did not change our findings. Although characters and celebrities/public figures are widely cited as prominent child-targeted marketing techniques [32], they appeared in only 1.0 to 16.0% of child-targeted unhealthy food marketing instances in this study. Consistent with this, sensitivity analyses confirmed that including or excluding these techniques did not meaningfully change the frequency of child-targeted unhealthy food marketing instances identified, suggesting that excluding them from our primary analysis did not meaningfully affect our findings. Our findings are broadly consistent with a recent study that similarly found that character-based techniques accounted for approximately 8% of food marketing instances across multiple digital media platforms [14].

Our last key finding was that most or all unhealthy food marketing instances were classified as child-targeted regardless of the definition we used. The frequency of child-targeted unhealthy food marketing did not change whether we considered only core child-targeted techniques or included both core and broad techniques. This was because the inclusion of broad techniques did not introduce additional instances classified as child-targeted, resulting in an unchanged overall frequency of child-targeted food marketing instances. When non-specific techniques were included, all marketing instances were classified as child-targeted since the technique calls-toaction appeared in every food marketing instance. However, the AI system's lower performance in identifying broad child-targeted marketing techniques and mixed performance for non-specific techniques may have rendered some aspects of our sensitivity analyses less meaningful.

This study has several limitations. First, although we used evidence-based search terms to simulate browsing patterns based on age and gender, these terms may not fully reflect the search habits of Canadian users. We also only captured video marketing instances and excluded image marketing, banner marketing, in-feed marketing, influencer marketing, and brand marketing instances, possibly underestimating children's potential exposure to food marketing on YouTube.

Additionally, we analyzed YouTube using non-signed-in accounts. If children were signed in, they would not be exposed to any food marketing instances. CHOPPED assessed the healthfulness of foods using the 2020 version of the University of Toronto Food Classification List [28], possibly leading to some misclassification of food healthfulness if some foods have since been reformulated. However, this is unlikely to have materially affected our findings as most unhealthy food marketing was for items that were clearly unhealthy and unlikely to have significantly changed in nutritional composition over time (e.g., soda, chocolate bars, confectionary). Data collection spanned 2 months and therefore cannot fully represent children's potential longer-term exposures to food marketing on YouTube. However, we collected data for 10,752 hours for all eight child and adult accounts combined (i.e., 8,064 hours for child and 2,688 hours for adult accounts) over a period of 2 months which is substantially longer than prior studies that collected no more than 336 hours of food marketing data on YouTube [13, 36].

Additionally, our AI system collected 60,633 total marketing instances, 11,324 of which were unique. These values far surpass those of previous studies which collected at most 187 unique marketing instances on YouTube [13, 36]. Finally, based on performance metrics, CHOPPED may have overestimated children's potential exposure to unhealthy child targeted food marketing instances. Although CHOPPED could not identify branded and licensed characters, celebrities/public figures, or other cartoon characters children at the time of this analysis, it does not appear that this limitation influenced our findings. The sensitivity analysis did not show a significant difference in the extent or nature of child-targeted marketing instances when including versus excluding these techniques in the definition of child-targeted marketing.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, our findings suggest that the frequency of unhealthy food marketing shown to children on YouTube may be low, with less than one unhealthy food marketing instance per day over a 2-hour daily viewing period. Although we found some evidence that the older boys' account was shown more monthly instances of child-targeted unhealthy food marketing and of some core child-targeted techniques than other child and adult accounts, these differences may be of limited public health relevance. Overall, there was limited evidence that marketing targeted children by age or gender on YouTube. As the first study to use AI to monitor food marketing to children on digital media, we demonstrated that AI can effectively monitor the digital food marketing landscape over extended periods. Our findings highlight the potential of AI-driven surveillance to inform policies to restrict unhealthy food marketing to children, which may help to protect children from the negative impacts of unhealthy food marketing.
