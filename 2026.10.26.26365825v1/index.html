To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 3 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Verticality perception is the sense of one's body orientation relative to gravity. It can be disrupted by neurological diseases or during aviation and space flight, resulting in falls and accidents. This perception is thought to arise from integration of vestibular and visual signals, yet where this multisensory computation is spatially implemented in the human brain remains unresolved. Room tilt illusion (RTI), in which the visual environment abruptly reorients relative to gravity (typically by 90° or 180°), has been proposed as a model disorder for studying verticality perception. Here, we leverage focal brain lesions (predominantly ischemic or hemorrhagic strokes) causing RTI to localize this multisensory computation. We assembled a total of 37 patients with brain lesions causing RTI from a systematic literature search and local cases across two centers. Clinical data were tabulated and lesions were analyzed using multimodal voxel-and connectome-based lesion mapping methods. Lesion findings were further tested against an independent dataset of 509 direct electrical stimulation sites associated with vestibular or non-vestibular percepts. The RTI lesions were distributed across cortical and subcortical locations, with the cerebellum representing the most frequent lesion site (49%). Despite anatomical heterogeneity, lesion connectivity converged on two hub regions: one in the posterior cerebellum and another in the mid-cingulate cortex. This pattern was robust to methodological variations, separation of RTI subtypes, and exclusion of cerebellar lesions. This pattern was also specific to RTI when compared against 185 phenotypically matched lesions from patients with milder disturbances of verticality perception and 1,110 lesions associated with 27 other neuropsychiatric syndromes. Finally, independent direct electrical stimulation data converged on both lesion-derived RTI hubs (mid-cingulate cortex: g=0.62, p<.001; posterior cerebellum: g=0.34, p=.014, 10,000 label permutations). In conclusion, anatomically diverse RTI lesions localize to a specific cerebello-cingulate network, providing an anatomical framework for verticality perception in the human brain. Our findings generate testable hypotheses for experimentally modulating or protecting verticality perception in neurological patients or pilots.

INTRODUCTION

Gravity shapes the physical world and how organisms interact with it. Across species, perceiving its direction is a fundamental neural computation that underpins gaze, posture, and spatial orientation. In humans, experimental observations indicate that spatial orientation relative to gravity depends on the reliability-weighted integration of vestibular, visual, and other sensory cues into a coherent, global estimate of verticality [1] [2] [3] . Errors in the underlying computations have real-world consequences, contributing to falls on the ground 4 and potentially fatal aviation mishaps due to spatial disorientation in pilots 5 . Disordered verticality perception is also a frequent and disabling feature of neurological conditions such as stroke, Parkinsonism, or vestibular disorders, where it contributes to poor clinical outcomes, mostly related to falls and their sequelae 4, 6, 7.

Notably, verticality perception can be modulated through both invasive and non-invasive neuromodulation, suggesting that it may represent a tractable network-level target, both in neurological contexts [8] [9] [10] [11] [12] and for mitigating spatial disorientation in aviation 13 . Although the multisensory computations underlying verticality perception have been examined in detail through psychophysical experiments 1, 3, 14 , where these computations are implemented and spatially encoded in the human brain remains incompletely resolved 15 . Room tilt illusion (RTI) is considered a unique model disorder to localize the neural substrates mediating verticality perception, as proposed by Alberts and colleagues 14 . During RTI, patients experience an abrupt and extreme rotation of the entire visual environment relative to gravity, most commonly by 90° or 180°, while the scene itself remains internally coherent and most other aspects of perception intact 16, 17 . In contrast to the more common, milder disturbances of verticality perception (e.g., tilts of the subjective visual vertical, SVV [18] [19] [20] ) that produce a continuously tilted percept, RTI appears quantized, collapsing onto a small set of stable spatial configurations 16 . Albeit also described in peripheral vestibular disorders, RTI is most commonly caused by brain lesions 16, 21 . These lesions have been reported across remarkably distributed locations, spanning cortical and subcortical regions linked to vestibular, visual, and multisensory processing 16, 22 . This observation underpins the longstanding hypothesis that RTI reflects a brain network disorder characterized by a transient mismatch between vestibular and visual coordinate maps of space 23, 24 . Although the clinical and imaging features of RTI have been systematically reviewed 16 , RTI-causing lesions have not been systematically analyzed at the voxeland network level, leaving unresolved whether shared network-level substrates exist, and if so, where they are localized. Lesion network mapping provides a means of identifying shared circuitry across anatomically heterogeneous lesions, and it has already revealed convergent networks underlying specific disorders of higher-order vestibular, visual, and multisensory processing [25] [26] [27].

Based on a combined local and literaturebased cohort of 37 lesion-induced RTI cases, we applied functional and structural connectivity analyses across independent lesion and brain stimulation datasets, each modeling complementary aspects of verticality and vestibular perception. We show that anatomically heterogeneous RTI lesions converge onto a specific cerebello-cingulate network that is embedded within the canonical central vestibular network, but is dissociable from the network underlying milder disturbances of verticality perception. Together, these findings can provide a causal neuroanatomical framework for localizing multisensory computations in the human brain, generating testable hypotheses for understanding and modulating human verticality perception.

Syndromic definition and case collection

Disturbances of verticality perception span a clinical spectrum defined by the magnitude of the perceived deviation from true vertical. Common manifestations, such as the ocular tilt reaction (OTR) or isolated pathological deviations of the SVV, are typically associated with tilt angles above 2.5° but well below 30° 28, 29 . In contrast, RTI is an extreme misperception of verticality that is characterized by tilts of the visual environment reaching 45°, 60°, 90°, or 180°, most commonly in the roll plane. For the purpose of this study and in line with prior literature, we operationally defined the latter category as RTI, irrespective of the heterogeneous terminology used in the original reports (e.g., "upside-down vision", "environmental tilt illusion", or "reversal of vision metamorphopsia") 16 . The study was based on two independent lesion datasets: retrospective local cases and cases identified through a systematic literature search following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines 30 . Lesion network mapping

To approximate the brain-wide network connections of a given lesion location, we used the most recent and validated iteration of the lesion network mapping methodology 26, [34] [35] [36] , which encompasses the following steps.

Lesion segmentation

Lesion locations were manually transferred from published figures or local neuroimaging documentation onto a reference brain atlas in standard space (Montreal Neurological Institute 152 [MNI152] with 2 mm isotropic resolution) using MRIcroGL (www.nitrc.org/projects/mricrogl), in line with previous studies 26, 34, 37 . To ensure reproducibility, two assessors (MUF, CWZ) segmented all lesions independently for subsequent comparisons, consistent with current best practice recommendations 38.

Interassessor agreement was quantified as the voxel-wise Pearson correlation between the lesion network maps derived from the two assessors' independent segmentations. For cases derived from the literature, lesion locations were traced on all published neuroimaging slices, irrespective of number or orientation, and each lesion was traced to its full visible extent on every available slice. Of note, previous work demonstrated that twodimensional (2D) lesion traces are sufficient for lesion network mapping 25, 39 . For the local cases, the full clinical imaging dataset was available, and lesions were manually segmented in their entire threedimensional extent.

Lesion mapping

To integrate the anatomical distribution of lesion locations associated with different severities of verticality misperception, lesion overlap maps were generated for lesions causing RTI and lesions associated with milder SVV tilts (<30°). Lesion masks were binarized and combined to create voxel-wise overlap maps representing the fraction of cases involving each voxel. To identify neuroanatomical regions preferentially associated with RTI compared with milder SVV tilts, we performed voxel-wise lesionsymptom mapping (VLSM) using the MATLAB R2018a package NiiStat, as previously described 37, 40 . Voxel-wise Liebermeister tests were used to contrast lesions associated with RTI against those associated with milder SVV tilts (<30°). Lesion volume was included as a nuisance covariate to account for potential effects of lesion size, as well as a minimum overlap of 5, 10, and 20 lesions, in line with our previous study 37 . Statistical significance was assessed using family-wise error (FWE) correction based on 2,000 Freedman-Lane permutations, as implemented in NiiStat.

Using the human functional connectome to compute lesion networks

Functional connectivity between each lesion and the rest of the brain was estimated using a normative resting-state functional connectome derived from 1,000 healthy adults (50% female; age range 18-35 years) from the Brain Genomics Superstruct Project (GSP), acquired at Harvard University and Massachusetts General Hospital using a 3-T Siemens Magnetom TrioTim magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scanner with a 12-channel phased-array head coil 41, 42 . Structural images consisted of high-resolution T1weighted scans (1.2 mm isotropic resolution; repetition time [TR] = 2200 ms; inversion time [TI] = 1100 ms; flip angle = 7°; acquisition matrix = 192 × 192 with parallel imaging (GRAPPA) acceleration factor = 4), whereas resting-state functional images were acquired using gradient-echo echo-planar imaging (47 interleaved slices (foot-head); slice thickness = 3 mm with no interslice gap; TR = 3000 ms; TE = 30 ms; flip angle = 85°; 120 usable volumes per run following removal of the first four volumes). Participants completed one or two restingstate runs (72.6% completed two runs).

Standard preprocessing included regression of motion, cerebrospinal fluid, white matter and global signal fluctuations, as described previously 41, 42 . The resulting dataset provided a normative estimate of whole-brain functional connectivity, allowing the connectivity profile of each lesion location to be inferred in a common reference space. For each lesion mask, the mean resting-state time course was correlated with every other brain voxel across all subjects, yielding a voxel-wise connectivity map that was converted into a T-statistic, thus reflecting the consistency of connectivity across the normative cohort. Positive and negative values indicated regions showing correlated and anticorrelated spontaneous bloodoxygenation-level-dependent (BOLD) fluctuations with the lesion location, respectively 43 . To evaluate the robustness of our findings to connectome preprocessing choices, the complete connectivity analysis was repeated using both currently established preprocessing pipelines for the GSP connectome 41, 42, 44.

Lesion network overlap

In line with validated pipelines for lesion network mapping, we thresholded the lesion network maps at |T|≥ 5 (corresponding to a voxel-wise FWEcorrected p< .05) to assess the consistency of lesion connection in the RTI cohort, creating binarized maps of brain regions significantly connected to the lesion location. We repeated this process at |T|≥ 7 (voxel-wise FWE-corrected p< 10 - 6 ), and |T|≥ 9 (voxel-wise FWE-corrected p< 10 -11 ) to verify robustness against threshold variations. Finally, we overlaid the binarized lesion network maps to identify peak connectivity regions across the anatomically heterogeneous lesions causing RTI. As an alternative, thresholdfree measure of network consistency 45 , voxel-wise connectivity values from all individual lesion network maps were entered into a one-sample t-test against a null hypothesis of zero mean connectivity. This analysis retains continuous voxelwise connectivity values, thereby providing a complementary measure of convergence across lesions.

Specificity testing

As lesion network overlap alone is insufficient to detect connections specific to a group of lesions of interest 35 , we conducted two independent specificity analyses. First, we contrasted the unthresholded lesion network maps from the 37 RTI lesions with 1,110 lesion network maps derived from lesions causing 27 other neuropsychiatric disorders as previously reported 26 , including other central visual and/or perceptual disorders (e.g., Alice in Wonderland syndrome 26 , hallucinations 46 , prosopagnosia 47 , or cortical blindness 25, 48 ). Second, to test for connections specific to different degrees of misperceptions of verticality, we performed a similar two-sample t-test comparing RTI to lesion network maps associated with milder disturbances of verticality perception as a control cohort phenotypically matched for disturbances in verticality perception (n = 185). All of these tests accounted for lesion size as a nuisance variable and were implemented using open software for statistical analysis of neuroimaging data (FSL's permutation analysis of linear models, PALM 49 ). Results were corrected for multiple comparisons using voxelbased FWE correction in FSL-PALM. To characterize the magnitude of group differences within the data-driven RTI network hubs, we additionally extracted mean connectivity values from each individual lesion network map and calculated Hedges' g. Group differences were also assessed using non-parametric label permutation testing with 10,000 permutations 35, 50 . Because the regions of interest (ROIs) were derived from the RTI dataset itself, these ROI-based analyses were considered descriptive and complementary to the whole-brain voxelwise inference rather than an independent test of specificity. An analogous ROI-based permutation framework was applied to connectivity maps derived from direct electrical stimulation (DES) sites. This was done to test whether the lesion-derived RTI network hubs were preferentially connected to stimulation sites eliciting vestibular compared with non-vestibular percepts. As the stimulation and lesion datasets in that case were independent, this analysis provided an independent test of the functional specificity of the RTI hubs. Label permutation testing was used to assess whether these group differences exceeded those expected by chance, in line with current methodological recommendations emphasizing permutation-based specificity testing in connectome-based brain mapping studies 35, 50.

Deriving a lesion-based network for RTI

A conjunction map was created by combining the lesion network overlap map and the specificity map using the logical "and" operation in Python 3.0's nibabel library. This combination identified brain regions consistently (i.e., connected in ≥89% of cases) and specifically (i.e., regions with FWE-corrected p< .05 in the specificity analysis) connected to RTI lesions. We will refer to the areas resulting from this conjunction analysis as RTI "network hubs". As an exploratory robustness analysis, we additionally used permutation-based null distributions to estimate false-positive rates across a range of lesion network overlap, specificity, and conjunction thresholds. We compared the location of these hubs to advanced structural brain atlases including the brainnetome atlas 51 , the Spatially Unbiased Infra-tentorial Template (SUIT) cerebellum atlas 52 , and the Harvard-Oxford cortical atlas 53 . In the case of cortical regions of interest, hub locations and atlas boundaries were compared by overlaying both on a highresolution MRI template (400 µm isotropic resolution, Juelich EBrains) in FSLeyes 54 , while in the case of cerebellar regions of interest, neuroimaging findings were projected onto SUIT's cerebellar flatmap visualization 55 , as per the instructions supplied with the SUITPy package in Python 3.0 (https://suitpy.readthedocs.io/en/latest/flat map.html).

By definition, positive connectivity to the positive RTI hub and negative connectivity to the negative RTI hub defined a distributed brain network encompassing the heterogeneous lesion locations associated with RTI. To visualize this network, we computed functional connectivity with the two RTI network hubs, thresholded each connectivity map at |T|= 5, and identified all voxels that satisfied the above criteria.

Direct electrical stimulation network mapping

To further test whether the lesion-derived RTI network hubs participated in vestibular processing, we utilized a convergent brain mapping approach by analyzing an independent dataset of DES sites associated with vestibular and nonvestibular percepts, as previously reported 25, 56 . Cases were identified from a retrospective analysis of 354 patients with medication-resistant focal epilepsy, who underwent presurgical evaluation with stereo-electroencephalography (SEEG) and electrical brain stimulation 57, 58.

Specifically, DES was performed between two adjacent contacts of the SEEG electrodes (inter-contact space = 0.5 mm) at 50 Hz (pulse width: 0.5-1 ms; train duration: 3-8 s) and at 1 Hz (pulse width: 1-2 ms; train duration: 20-60 s). The intensity was increased progressively by 0.2 mA steps from 0.2 mA (maximum: 5 mA, rarely exceeding 3 mA). Patients were blinded to stimulation timing and systematically asked for subjective experiences 57 . The DES sites were modelled as 4 mm spheres centered at the midpoint between the two stimulated contacts. Brain-wide connectivity was analyzed using the same normative functional connectome and processing pipelines. Vestibular sites comprised DES locations eliciting sensations of vertigo, dizziness, rotations, tilts, and swaying (n = 57). All remaining DES sites eliciting motor, autonomic, language, affective, perceptual (e.g., somatosensory, visual, or auditory) effects as well as sites producing no effect served as controls (n = 452). To identify connections preferentially associated with vestibular stimulation sites, voxel-wise two-sample t-tests were performed using FSL-PALM, comparing vestibular and non-vestibular stimulation-derived connectivity maps. As an independent validation analysis, mean connectivity within lesion-derived RTI network hubs was compared between vestibular and non-vestibular DES sites using nonparametric label permutation testing (10,000 permutations).

Disconnectome mapping

We generated structural disconnectome maps for each lesion using BCBtoolkit 59 . To this end, a normative structural connectome from 7-T diffusion MRI data of n = 178 healthy controls was used, as previously described 37, 59 . This connectome reflected the inter-individual variability of fiber pathways in the healthy human brain. In line with previous work, the individual tractography maps associated with each lesion were then binarized at >0.5 (reflecting disconnection in the majority of connectome subjects) and collapsed across individuals, resulting in a disconnectome map for each lesion 37, 59 . These maps reflected the probability of anatomical disconnection between the lesion location and each other brain voxel in the normative fiber connectivity dataset. To test for disconnections specific to either subgroup of verticality misperceptions (i.e., lesions causing RTI and those causing <30° verticality deviations), a two-sample t-test, accounting for lesion size as a nuisance variable, was implemented in FSL-PALM 49 . All results were corrected for multiple comparisons using voxel-based FWE correction with a significance level set at p< .05.

Statistical computations and visualization

Spatial correlations, permutation testing, and combination of lesion network maps (e.g., for conjunction map) were conducted using Python (version 3; https://www.python.org) with Pandas, NumPy, Nibabel, and SciPy packages. Data distributions (e.g., demographic factors, clinical case features) were determined using Shapiro-Wilk testing or inspection of interquantile ("Q-Q") plots. Depending on the data distribution, parametric (t-test) or non-parametric (Mann-Whitney U) tests were used for continuous variables, and Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. Results from voxel-wise analyses were corrected for multiple comparisons using voxel-wise FWE correction in FSL-PALM. Data are reported as mean ± standard deviation or median and interquartile range (IQR). The significance level was set at p< .05. Main findings are visualized on the Ebrains BigBrain template normalized to MNI152 space 60 , or on a cerebellar flatmap visualization as implemented in Python using the SUITPy package 55.

RESULTS

Demographic and clinical characteristics of RTI cases

Combining local cases and our systematic literature search yielded a total of 37 cases of RTI attributable to focal brain lesions (14 right, 15 left, 8 bilateral, Supplementary Table 1, Supplementary Figure 3). Of these, 22 (59%) presented with a 180° tilt/inversion, 14 (38%) with a 90° tilt, and one case (3%) with a 45° tilt. Across 180° and 90° subgroups, vascular etiologies predominated, accounting for 64% of 180° and 93% of 90° cases, followed by inflammatory (14% vs. 0%) and other causes (22% vs. 7%). Patients with 180° RTI were significantly younger than those with 90° RTI (p = 0.035). Conversely, vestibular symptoms and signs, including vertigo or dizziness (74% vs. 64%), the ocular tilt reaction (29% vs. 57%), and nystagmus (69% vs. 50%), were common in both RTI subgroups, as was ataxia (65% vs. 77%). Among cases with documented nystagmus, gazeevoked nystagmus occurred at similar frequencies in 180° and 90° RTI (25% vs. 40%), whereas spontaneous nystagmus was reported exclusively in three 180° RTI cases. Nevertheless, nystagmus subtype did not differ significantly between groups (all Fisher's exact p> 0.2). All RTI episodes were generally transient, with a median duration of 5 minutes (IQR 29.0; range 0.1 -4320 minutes; duration not reported in 8/37 cases). Among cases with available contextual information, episodes occurred almost exclusively indoors (20/21 cases, 95%). Detailed cohort characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Lesion topography

Across the RTI cohort, lesions were distributed throughout various brain regions, most frequently involving the cerebellum (18 cases, 49%), pons (5 cases, 14%), occipital cortex (5 cases, 14%), midbrain (3 cases, 8%), and medulla (3 cases, 8%). Thalamic or temporal cortex involvement each was observed in 2 cases (5%), while the frontal cortex was affected in a single case (3%). Within the cerebellum, posterior lobules (lobules VIII-X, Crus II) were involved in the majority of cases (13/18, 72%), while anterior/intermediate regions (lobules IV-VI) were less frequently affected (6/18, 33%), with overlapping involvement in one case. Lesions causing RTI were commonly located in subcortical structures (73%, 27/37) and equally distributed across both brain hemispheres (15 left, 14 right, 8 bilateral, Supplementary Figure 3). Cortical lesions were significantly more common in 180° RTI (9/ 22 vs. 0/ 14, p = .006). Consistent with this regional heterogeneity, descriptive voxel-wise overlap was low, with a maximum of 4/ 37 lesions (11%) located in the left paramedian aspect of cerebellar lobule IX (Supplementary Figure 4).

In the cingulate cortex, a peak of 89% of lesion locations was significantly connected to the midcingulate cortex (area A23c 51 ) with neighboring areas occupying its border zones with the anterior and posterior cingulate cortex, respectively (Figure 3C3). Direct electrical stimulation sites causing vestibular sensations are preferentially connected to RTI network hubs

Current pathophysiological models of RTI invoke dysfunctional interaction of higherorder vestibular and visual networks 16, 24, 31, 64 . Because our lesion network analyses revealed substantial differences between RTI and milder disturbances of verticality perception -as established disorders of lower-tointermediate-level vestibular processing 4 -we next asked whether the lesion-derived RTI hubs are preferentially embedded within vestibular cortical networks. To test this directly, we leveraged an independent retrospective dataset of DES sites associated with vestibular sensations (including vertigo, dizziness and selfmotion illusions; n = 57) and nonvestibular effects or no perceptual effects (n = 452). Using the lesion-derived RTI hubs as independently defined ROIs, label permutation testing showed that vestibular DES sites were significantly more strongly connected to both RTI hubs than control stimulation sites (10,000 permutations; cingulate hub: p < .001, Hedges' g = 0.62; posterior cerebellar hub: p = .014, Hedges' g = 0.34; Figure 6A-B). As a supplementary whole-brain analysis, we treated DES foci analogously to lesions within the network mapping framework, confirming specific connectivity in the midcingulate cortex, encompassing the RTI hub region (voxel-wise FWE-corrected p < .05, Supplementary Figure 9).

DISCUSSION

Reliable perception of verticality is fundamental for spatial orientation and postural stability, and its disruption contributes to falls and disability across neurological disorders or during aviation and space flight. Specifically, RTI is a transient disorder in which the orientation of the visual environment is perceived as significantly tilted relative to gravity, most often quantized at 90° or 180° 65 . In this regard, RTI has been proposed as a model disorder to probe the multisensory computations underlying verticality perception, but its neural basis has remained unresolved, in part because causative lesions can be anatomically scattered throughout cortical as well as subcortical brain regions. In 37 cases of lesion-induced RTI, we found clinical and anatomical characteristics broadly consistent with the largest previous systematic review, including a male predominance, a predominantly vascular etiology, transient symptom duration and marked heterogeneity of gross lesion locations 16 . However, by spatially reconstructing lesions and applying multimodal lesion mapping methods, we extend these previous observations to show that this anatomical heterogeneity conceals a highly convergent network architecture centered on two hub regions: one in the posterior cerebellum and another in the mid-cingulate cortex. This hub profile distinguished RTI lesions from 185 lesions with comparable topography and phenotypically closely related, but milder (≤30°) and graded disturbances of spatial orientation relative to gravity, as well as 1,110 lesions across 27 other neuropsychiatric conditions. Convergence across connectivity modalities and independent datasets provided complementary support for this network architecture: structural connectivity converged on the posterior cerebellar hub, while independent DES data revealed preferential connectivity of vestibular stimulation sites to both lesion-derived RTI hubs. Together, these findings suggest a two-fold circuit-level substrate for RTI. The anatomical localization of the hubs identified by our multimodal brain mapping approach provides context for this interpretation. The posterior cerebellar hubfunctionally connected to 91% of RTI lesionschiefly occupies two vestibulo-cerebellar territories: a (para-)vermal region, linked to internal models of spatial orientation and selfmotion relative to gravity 66, 67 , and a (peri-)floccular region, involved in integrating vestibular and visual signals for gaze stabilization in three-dimensional (3D) space 68 . Notably, structural disconnectome mapping independently converged on the (peri-)floccular component of this functionally defined hub, providing cross-modal support for its association with RTI. These anatomical findings align with experimental and clinical studies implicating the posterior cerebellum in the integration of vestibular, visual, somatosensory, and ocular motor information relevant to estimating spatial orientation relative to gravity 66, [69] [70] [71] . More specifically, Bayesian models provide a computational account of this integration, suggesting that vestibular and visual panoramic cues are dynamically weighted against each other according to their relative reliability 14 . Consistent with this framework, pathologically unreliable vestibular gravity signalssuch as in bilateral vestibulopathy or during exposure to altered gravitational conditions in aviation and spaceflightincrease susceptibility to visual reorientation illusions that are characterized by abrupt 90° or 180° reorientations of the perceived environment, closely resembling the RTI phenomenology [72] [73] [74] [75] . Similarly, RTI can be induced by vestibular stimulation in patients with brainstem lesions only in illuminated conditions 31 , while visual information has been shown to dominate verticality estimates in people with cerebellar degeneration 70 . Within this framework, we hypothesize that in lesion-induced RTI, disconnection of posterior vestibulo-cerebellar circuitry may degrade the reliability of vestibular verticality estimates, leading to a shift of perceptual inference toward visual scene information. But why should such a shift produce the discrete, cardinal reorientations characteristic of RTI rather than a graded perceptual tilt? Environmental structure may provide one explanation. Natural and particularly built environments contain strong cardinal cues such as the horizon, walls or ceilings, and these cardinal orientations are preferentially encoded over oblique orientations in the visual cortex 76, 77 . Together, these external and internal constraints could bias perceptual inference toward a limited set of alternative but internally coherent interpretations of the visual world during RTI. Consistent with this hypothesis, eye closure has been reported to abolish RTI in some cases 16 , and among our cases with available contextual information, episodes occurred almost exclusively indoors (20/21), where rectilinear environmental cues provide particularly strong cardinal references for upright. The cerebellar association alone, however, appears insufficient to explain RTI. The majority of RTI lesions (19/37) were located outside the cerebellum while converging on the same network. More generally, RTI remains rare relative to the frequency of cerebellar strokes 16, 78 . Moreover, lesions to closely neighboring cerebellar sites linked to tremor 79 , cervical dystonia 80 or cognitive-affective syndromes 81 are not typically associated with RTI. Together, these observations suggest a potentially two-fold network architecture. Indeed, a second RTI-specific hub was found in the mid-cingulate cortex. This hub, chiefly corresponding to area A23c 51 , was negatively connected in up to 89% of RTI lesions and independently supported by causal evidence from DES. Importantly, this cingulate territory was one of the two regions most strongly distinguishing RTI from phenotypically related, milder disturbances of verticality perception, suggesting that on a network level, RTI cannot simply be understood as an extreme form of graded verticality perception errors. Converging evidence from our stimulation mapping analyses and previous neurophysiological studies implicates the cingulate cortex in two multisensory computations: higher-order vestibular processing and representation of one's own body and self-location 10, [82] [83] [84] [85] [86] [87] [88] [89] . More specifically, large-scale studies showed that DES to the mid-cingulate cortex (including A23c) most frequently induces body-anchored and gravityrelated sensations, such as a sense of weightlessness or floating of single body parts or the body as a whole 86, [89] [90] [91] . Based on this evidence, we hypothesize that the cingulate hub may contribute to anchoring gravity-referenced information within a coherent representation of self-orientation relative to the external world 92 . Therefore, we propose a tentative circuit-level model in which the posterior cerebellar hub may contribute to multisensory estimation of verticality, whereas the cingulate hub may relate this estimate to a body-centered representation of self-orientation within space. From this perspective, RTI may emerge when a specific disruption of this cerebello-cingulate network destabilizes the gravity-referenced relationship between body and external space to allow a dramatically erroneous estimate of spatial orientation to become transiently stabilized 10, 90 . Notably, canonical cortical vestibular regions such as the posterior insula did not emerge as principal RTI hubs. Although the posterior insula was associated with milder verticality disturbances and also showed weaker effects in functional and structural specificity analyses with respect to RTI, these were substantially less pronounced than the posterior cerebellar and cingulate findings. Thus, while RTI appears to involve regions embedded within the broader vestibular network 10, 93 , its network anatomy differs from that associated with vertigo and graded verticality errors, which have been linked more strongly to posterior insular circuitry 25 . This dissociation may therefore provide an anatomical framework for the concept of distinct "higher vestibular functions" as proposed by Brandt and colleagues 23 . Several limitations warrant emphasis. First, our data are primarily drawn from published case reports, which are subject to ascertainment and publication bias. We aimed to mitigate this by extracting detailed clinical characteristics beyond the presence of RTI, which grossly aligned with results from a published systematic review. Second, lesions were traced from 2D figures of variable quality, which might have affected accuracy. We mitigated this by following best practice standards for lesion segmentation 38 , and also by quantifying inter-assessor reliability, which was high (mean r = 0.90). Third, lesion network mapping relied on a normative functional connectome rather than patientspecific connectivity and has recently been the subject of methodological debate regarding reproducibility and specificity 63 . We therefore deliberately combined multiple connectivity methods, independent datasets, recent validated analytical pipelines, and permutationbased assessment of false-positive rates to test the robustness of the thresholds used to define the RTI network 35, 50, 94 . Fourth, the mid-cingulate hub was defined by negative connectivity, whose physiological meaning remains debated, as anticorrelations may reflect inhibitory network interactions but are also influenced by global signal regression 43 . Nevertheless, its robustness across preprocessing pipelines and convergence with vestibular DES support its biological relevance. In conclusion, beyond investigating a rare neurological syndrome, RTI provides a lesion-based model for localizing the neural circuitry supporting verticality perception. By identifying a convergent and specific cerebello-cingulate network, our findings provide an anatomical framework for the multisensory computations thought to underlie verticality perception and body-anchored self-orientation. By localizing this circuitry, our findings generate testable hypotheses for experimentally probing and modulating human verticality perception and the underlying multisensory computations, with potential translational applications 8, 13, 95, 96.
