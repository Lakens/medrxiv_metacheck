Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Toxoplasma gondii exhibits marked variation in genetic diversity, yet the relationships between parasite genotype and clinical presentation in humans remain poorly understood. We investigated the diversity of T. gondii strains infecting humans and their associations with different forms of toxoplasmosis.

We retrospectively analyzed 2,467 T. gondii-positive human clinical cases collected in France through a nationwide network of hospital parasitology diagnostic units over more than four decades. Cases included patients receiving care in mainland France, including residents, travelers, and immigrants, as well as patients managed in French overseas territories in South America, the Indian Ocean, and Oceania. Parasite genotyping was performed using a standardized panel of 15 microsatellite markers. Complete genotypes were obtained for 1,269 cases, revealing 13 distinct genetic groups, including clonal lineages and non-clonal populations, alongside genotypes that could not be assigned to any recognized group. Type II strains predominated overall (82.6%) and were particularly frequent in congenital toxoplasmosis (869/955 cases, 91.0%). Despite being predominantly associated with infraclinical or mild congenital infections, type II strains were also responsible for severe congenital infections following maternal infection during all three trimesters of pregnancy, as well as acquired ocular toxoplasmosis. Haplogroup 16, encompassing strains previously assigned to multiple PCR-RFLP genotypes, showed an association with severe congenital toxoplasmosis, with six of seven cases presenting severe manifestations. Type I strains were remarkably rare (n = 5) and showed no signs of increased clinical severity compared to type II. Amazonian strains, another genetically diverse group, were identified almost exclusively in acquired toxoplasmosis in immunocompetent patients, all of them severe, in keeping with previous reports of the exceptional pathogenicity of this genetic group. In immunodeficient patients, Africa 1 strains (equivalent to ToxoDB#6) were more frequently.

1. Introduction

Toxoplasmosis is a ubiquitous parasitic infection caused by Toxoplasma, a zoonotic protozoan that infects warm-blooded animals, including humans [1]. It is estimated to affect approximately one third of the world's population [2]. To date, only one species, Toxoplasma gondii, is recognized as responsible for human toxoplasmosis; however, the disease presents with highly diverse clinical manifestations. Several factors influence clinical presentation, including host immune status [3], parasite inoculum [4, 5], parasite genetic diversity [6] [7] [8], age, ethnicity of the host [9, 10], and gestational trimester in congenital toxoplasmosis [11]. Knowledge of T. gondii diversity has considerably expanded over the past four decades. Initial studies [4, 12, 13] identified three clonal lineages (types I, II, and III). Subsequently, broader sampling encompassing a greater diversity of host species and geographic origins, together with the development of multilocus genotyping approaches, revealed a much broader global genetic diversity. These approaches primarily relied on microsatellite (MS) [14, 15] and restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP) markers [4, 16].

These genetically diverse strains are associated with markedly different phenotypes in both mice and humans [17, 18]. The frequent use of mouse bioassays for strain isolation, together with the ease of performing experimental infections in mice, has enabled detailed characterization of the relationship between T. gondii genotypes and toxoplasmosis severity [17]. In mice, strains are generally classified as non-virulent when infection remains asymptomatic, virulent when it results in acute lethal infection, and intermediate when both phenotypes are observed. In humans, however, the situation is far more complex. In addition to the existence of several clinical forms of toxoplasmosis, the spectrum and severity of clinical manifestations within each form are highly heterogeneous, reflecting the influence of multiple factors beyond the intrinsic characteristics of the parasite [10, 18]. Furthermore, reports describing T. gondii genotypes in clinical samples remain sparse, are often limited to individual case reports or small case series, and rely on heterogeneous genotyping methods and genotypes designation schemes [19], including the still frequent use of genotyping techniques with insufficient discriminatory power to distinguish between T. gondii lineages and populations (e.g., single-locus marker genotyping) [20] [21] [22] [23] [24] [25]. Consequently, associations between parasite genotypes and clinical manifestations are still poorly understood and remain difficult to synthesize and harmonize.

. In France, a nationwide network of hospital parasitology diagnostic units has developed for more than three decades and has been constituted in a National Reference Centre for Toxoplasmosis (NRCT) since 2006, that has among several missions the molecular surveillance of T. gondii strains in pregnant women undergoing follow-up and in patients diagnosed with toxoplasmosis throughout France through genotyping approaches (https://cnrtoxoplasmose.chu-reims.fr/?lang=en). Until 2023, the NRCT and the associated network of parasitology diagnostic laboratories collected 2,467 toxoplasmosis cases reported in France, including cases from mainland France, French overseas territories, travellers, and immigrants. The corresponding strains originated predominantly from mainland France, but also from other European countries, Africa, Asia, Oceania, and South America.

In the present study, we performed a retrospective analysis of the genotypic and genetic diversity of T. gondii strains collected for the surveillance of toxoplasmosis in France, with the aim of evaluating associations between T. gondii genotypes and the clinical presentations of patients with toxoplasmosis.

2.1. Sample and Data collection

In France, epidemiological surveillance of human toxoplasmosis has officially been coordinated by the NRCT since 2006, following the implementation of the national online toxoplasmosis reporting system, ToxoSurv [26]. Surveillance primarily focuses on cases of congenital toxoplasmosis (CT) identified through routine prenatal screening, for which systematic monitoring was established to reduce the risk of undiagnosed maternal infections. However, surveillance also includes other clinical forms of toxoplasmosis, such as acquired toxoplasmosis (as opposed to congenital) in immunocompetent (ATIC) or immunodeficient patients (ATID) and isolated ocular toxoplasmosis (OT). Within this framework, the Laboratory of Parasitology of the Limoges University Hospital is responsible for molecular surveillance through the genotyping of T. gondii directly from DNA extracts from clinical samples or from strains isolated from patients. Nevertheless, this genotyping activity had already begun several years before the establishment of the NRCT, through a nationwide network of hospital correspondents sending samples to the Limoges laboratory for genotyping. Samples were sent by parasitology diagnostic laboratories in hospitals across mainland France, as well as from French overseas territories in Latin America and the Caribbean (French Guiana, Martinique, and Guadeloupe), in the Indian Ocean region (Réunion Island) and Oceania (French Polynesia and New Caledonia).

Samples consist either of DNA extracted from pathological products using the DNA extraction protocols routinely implemented in the participating hospital laboratories (Ct < 33 with quantitative PCR), or of T. gondii strains isolated by mouse bioassay from these pathological products. In the latter case, isolates are sent by NRCT network correspondents in the form of infected mouse tissues (brain or ascitic fluid). DNA extraction from these materials is subsequently performed at the Limoges laboratory using the QIAamp DNA Minikit® (QIAGEN®, Germany).

Correspondents also provide epidemiological (country of residency or country of origin, travel history outside France, potential sources of contamination) and clinical data (immune status and main clinical manifestations). In the present study, samples sent between January 2006 and December 2023 by the correspondents of the NRCT had their associated data entered through ToxoSurv. In addition, earlier cases referred to the Limoges laboratory before 2006 were also incorporated into the analysis, with the same types of epidemiological data available. Overall, the dataset comprised 1,975 toxoplasmosis . cases collected from 2006 onwards, 433 cases collected before 2006, and 59 cases for which the year of sampling could not be retrieved.

2.2. Ethical statement

The analysis of biological samples obtained through standard clinical practices was classified as noninterventional research in accordance with Article L1221-1.1 of the French Public Health Code, requiring only the absence of patient objection at the time of sample collection (as per Article L1211-2 of the Public Health Code). At participating centers, mothers and patients were informed about the anonymized reporting of CT cases, the collection of clinical and epidemiological data, and the study of T. gondii isolates from different clinical forms of toxoplasmosis. Mothers and patients had the right to refuse participation in this survey. All collected data were anonymized before samples were sent to the Limoges laboratory. Anonymized clinical and epidemiological data associated with T. gondii strains collected through the French NRCT were reported by the 37 participating specialized laboratories via a dedicated online data-entry system (http://cnrtoxoplasmose.chu-reims.fr/ ), using the specifically developed ToxoSurv software (EpiConcept). The software and associated data collection procedures were approved by the French National Data Protection Authority (Commission Nationale de l'Informatique et des Libertés, CNIL; approval reference DR-2016-186).

2.5. T. gondii genotyping and genotype classification 231

Genotyping of samples within the framework of NRCT activities at the Limoges laboratory is based on multilocus analysis of MS markers. This MS typing method, originally developed at the Limoges laboratory, has evolved over time with regard to the number of markers used: from 5 MS markers between 2000 and 2006 [28], to 6 MS markers between 2006 and 2009 [29], and finally to a multiplex panel of 15 MS markers after 2009 [15, 30]. To ensure methodological consistency and comparability over time, samples initially genotyped using the 5-or 6-marker panels were progressively reanalysed using the 15-marker panel. Among the 15 MS markers, 8 are considered typing markers, and the combination of alleles at these loci allows classification of strains into clonal lineages (e.g., clonal types I, II, and III, Africa 1-4, and Caribbean 1-3). For type II and Africa 4, a difference of one repeat unit (2 bp) at a single typing marker was considered compatible with assignment to the respective lineage. This criterion accounts for the greater microsatellite variability of type II and Africa 4, two lineages whose longer evolutionary history, suggested by whole-genome analyses [31], may have allowed greater accumulation of mutations compared with other clonal lineages.

However, some genotypes belong to partially clonal or non-clonal T. gondii populations, which display allele polymorphism at typing loci and therefore lack a unique allelic signature. Some of these genotypes could not be assigned to recognized clonal lineages based on their typing profiles but corresponded to genetic groups previously characterized by whole-genome sequencing, namely haplogroup 16 and the Amazonian group [31, 32]. Strains previously assigned to these groups by wholegenome analyses retained their classification. A neighbour-joining tree was built from all 15 MS markers using Bruvo's genetic distance and the BRUVO.BOOT function of the poppr package in R. Additional genotypes were assigned to haplogroup 16 or to the Amazonian group when they clustered with the corresponding WGS-characterized strains in a branch separated from all other genotypes in the neighbour-joining tree constructed from the 15 MS markers. Genotypes that could not be assigned according to these criteria were classified as unclassified. Because this category is heterogeneous by construction and does not correspond to a genetic group, it was described but not included in comparisons between genetic groups.

2.6. Associations between T. gondii genetic groups and clinical presentations

The evaluation of associations between T. gondii genetic groups and the clinical presentations of patients with toxoplasmosis included only strains successfully genotyped at all 15 MS markers. Unless otherwise stated, proportions of clinical forms were calculated among cases with documented clinical data. First, the proportions of type II genotypes versus non-type II genotypes were compared pairwise between the four clinical categories (CT, ATIC, ATID, and OT) using Chi-squared or Fisher's exact tests, as appropriate. Odds ratios (ORs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) were obtained by conditional maximum likelihood. Both uncorrected p-values and p-values after Bonferroni correction for the number of comparisons within each family of tests are reported. Then, within each clinical category, multiple correspondence analysis (MCA) were performed as a hypothesis-generating approach to explore relationships between T. gondii genetic groups and clinical forms, using the FactoMineR package in R (version 4.3.0; Vienna, Austria). MCA is an extension of correspondence analysis (CA) and a generalization of principal component analysis (PCA). This multivariate analysis technique allows the investigation of potential relationships between multiple categorical variables [33] [34] [35] [36]. MCA results are presented graphically in two-dimensional graphs, defined by the dimensions permitting the largest proportion of inertia. Because complete disjunctive coding mechanically deflates the raw proportions of inertia of an MCA, which are therefore not comparable to the proportions of variance of a PCA, adjusted inertias are reported. Only genetic groups represented by at least three toxoplasmosis cases were included. The analysis also accounted for the geographic origin of infection and for category-specific variables, such as the gestational trimester in CT cases and the type of immunosuppression in ATID cases. In some CT cases, it was not possible to determine the precise date of maternal infection because of late performance of the initial serological test or irregular monthly serological monitoring; so infections occurring between 0 and 6 weeks of gestation were classified as periconceptional, while those falling between two trimesters were classified as "1st-2nd trimester" and "2nd-3rd trimester". In a few CT cases, maternal infection was acute but preconceptional or the mother had a chronic infection prior to pregnancy. All such CT cases where the date of maternal infection could not be determined during pregnancy or who were infected prior to pregnancy were excluded from the MCA and pairewise comparisons.. Hypotheses generated by the MCA were subsequently evaluated using pairwise Chi-squared or Fisher's exact tests, as appropriate. Because the same dataset generated and tested these hypotheses, the corresponding p-values are reported as exploratory.

Among the 238 ATID cases, disseminated (126/238, 52.9%) and isolated cerebral forms (99/238, 41.6%) predominated, whereas only 13 cases (5.5%) were paucisymptomatic or asymptomatic ( S7 Table). 3.5.3. Acquired toxoplasmosis in immunocompetent patients

Among the 42 ATIC cases, 33 (78.6%) presented with severe clinical forms and 9 (21.4%) were paucisymptomatic ( S8 Table). All 14 Amazonian-associated cases were severe; 12 had been acquired in French Guiana and 2 in Suriname. Twenty-three cases (54.8%) involved genotypes that could not be assigned to any recognized genetic group; 18 of these were severe and 19 had been acquired in Latin America and the Caribbean, a clinical and geographical distribution indistinguishable from that of the whole-genome-characterized Amazonian strains. Conversely, all three type II-associated cases and the single Africa 1-associated case were paucisymptomatic, and the single haplogroup 16-associated case was severe. Because the Amazonian group was defined on the basis of strains previously characterized by whole-genome sequencing, most of which originate from previously reported series of severe acquired toxoplasmosis in French Guiana, and because several ATIC strains from French Guiana shared identical 15-marker profiles within the same year and correspond to documented common-source outbreaks, no statistical comparison between genetic groups was performed for this clinical category and results are presented descriptively. One of these ATIC cases was the mother of a CT case included in the present series, the same strain having been recovered from the mother's blood and from the fetal brain.

3.5.4. Ocular toxoplasmosis

Among the 23 isolated OT cases, 8 lacked detailed clinical information or had no signs suggestive of severity, such as extensive inflammation (e.g., panuveitis), bilateral involvement, recurrence, or incomplete clinical improvement ( S9 Table). These cases included 6 type II strains and 2 unclassified genotypes. In contrast, fifteen OT cases presented at least one clinical feature suggestive of severity. In the 7 IC patients infected with type II strains, severe clinical presentations included bilateral granulomatous panuveitis, extensive retinitis, and persistent inflammation. These patients experienced recurrent episodes, sometimes over multiple years, or incomplete clinical improvement despite treatment. Because of the limited number of OT cases and the high diversity of genetic groups represented, no statistical analyses were performed for this clinical category.

4. Discussion

The nationwide French network of hospital parasitology diagnostic laboratories, officially constituted with the NRCT in 2006 for the epidemiological survey of congenital toxoplasmosis (CT), has collected 2,467 T. gondii-positive human clinical cases recorded between 1962 and 2023. The 1,269 T. gondii genotypes successfully characterized using 15 MS markers revealed a remarkably high genetic diversity, corresponding to 13 distinct genetic groups, including both clonal lineages and non-clonal populations, together with 79 genotypes that could not be assigned to any recognized group and that probably encompass a variety of rare lineages. Several of these genetic groups have previously established correspondences with ToxoDB PCR-RFLP genotypes, considering that correspondences between microsatellite lineages and ToxoDB genotypes are not always one-to-one [37]. Type I, II, and III correspond to ToxoDB#10, #1-3, and #2, respectively. Africa 1 corresponds to ToxoDB#6, Africa 4 to ToxoDB#20 and #137, and haplogroup 16 to multiple genotypes including ToxoDB#15, #17, and #202. Caribbean lineages correspond mainly to ToxoDB#13 (Caribbean 1), #12 and #31 (Caribbean 2), and #25 and #141 (Caribbean 3), whereas Amazonian strains encompass multiple genotypes including ToxoDB#60, #95, #97, #98, #193, and #194. The Mascarene 1 lineage corresponds to ToxoDB#99 [38].

This high level of genetic diversity can be explained by several factors. First, the nationwide French network of hospital parasitology diagnostic laboratories collect clinical samples not only mainland France, but also French overseas territories and collectivities located in South America (French Guiana), the Caribbean archipelago (Guadeloupe and Martinique) the Indian Ocean (Réunion Island) and Oceania (French Polynesia and New Caledonia). The numerous genotypes identified in residents of French overseas territories and collectivities reflected the local genetic diversity of these areas, including Caribbean 1, 2, and 3 lineages in the Caribbean islands and French Guiana, Amazonian strains . in French Guiana, the Mascarene 1 lineage in Réunion Island, and the Polynesian 1 lineage in French Polynesia in Oceania. Second, clinical samples collected in mainland France also included samples from immigrants and travelers receiving care at French hospitals. Because a substantial proportion of immigrants living in France originate from Africa [39], African lineages, mainly Africa 1 but also Africa 2 and Africa 4, were well represented among the T. gondii genotypes characterized through the activities of the French network. Notably, most of these lineages were described for the first time through the activities of the French nationwide network [29, 37, 40]. Third, clinical samples collected in mainland France also included residents with no history of travel but with confirmed or suspected exposure to imported food products such as horsemeat. Notably, the association between toxoplasmosis and consumption of imported horse meat has been previously documented through the NRCT activities [8, 41]. This particularly high genetic diversity observed in human samples contrasts markedly with that reported in domestic and wild animals in mainland France, in which T. gondii strains are overwhelmingly represented by the type II lineage, with only a single type III strain reported to date [42] [43] [44] [45]. However, among human cases with Europe and Central Asia as the reported place of infection, genetic diversity was substantially lower and more closely resembled that documented in animal reservoirs, with type II accounting for 93.3% of cases.

Within each clinical category (CT, ATID, ATIC, and OT), the number of collected and successfully genotyped T. gondii-positive clinical samples and isolates, as well as the diversity of T. gondii genotypes identified, varied substantially. Cases of CT accounted for the largest proportion of genotyped samples. In France, public health policies recommend systematic screening of pregnant women at the beginning of pregnancy, followed by regular serological monitoring throughout gestation in seronegative women, even in the absence of clinical symptoms [46, 11]. This emphasis on CT surveillance is reflected in the activities of the NRCT, which was established primarily to conduct epidemiological surveillance of CT infections. Most T. gondii genotypes identified in cases of CT belonged to the type II lineage, probably reflecting local T. gondii diversity in mainland France. However, a wide range of other genetic groups was also detected, reflecting infections acquired in overseas territories, among travelers and immigrants, cases associated with the consumption of imported horse meat, and infections for which no clear source of exposure could be identified. Cases of ATID accounted for a substantial proportion of genotyped samples. Although type II remained the predominant lineage in this clinical category, the Africa 1, Africa 2, Africa 4, and type III lineages were also well represented. These genotypes were frequently identified in patients originating from Africa who were living in France and presented with reactivation of a latent T. gondii infection. This pattern is consistent with the circulation of these lineages in several African regions and with the higher burden of HIV infection reported in some of these settings. Cases of ATIC were less frequent, reflecting the fact that T. gondii infection is most often asymptomatic or paucisymptomatic in immunocompetent individuals in mainland France [47]. Most genotyped strains from this category were acquired in French Guiana and belonged either to the whole-genome-characterized Amazonian population or to unassignable genotypes sharing the same geographical origin; this population is associated with exposure to the wild environment of French Guiana and is known to cause severe disseminated toxoplasmosis in immunocompetent hosts [48, 49]. Cases of isolated OT were also uncommon. This likely reflects both the limited success of genotyping directly from ocular samples and the near impossibility of isolating viable strains from these specimens. Although type II accounted for more than half of the genotyped OT cases (60.9%), its proportion was markedly lower than that observed in CT (91.0%). A variety of other genetic groups were also represented. This pattern may reflect differences in the higher propensity of certain T. gondii genetic groups to cause OT compared with type II strains, although the limited number of OT cases precluded meaningful statistical comparisons.

The results of the present study, based on the largest collection of human T. gondii genotypes assembled to date, indicate that a given T. gondii genetic group can be associated with a wide spectrum of clinical presentations. Nevertheless, significant differences in the distribution of clinical manifestations and in the frequency of severe forms were observed between certain genetic groups. Among CT cases, type II strains were predominantly associated with infraclinical or mild forms. However, a substantial proportion of type II-associated CT cases presented with severe manifestations (16.8% of cases with documented clinical data). These included infections acquired before conception or during early pregnancy, when clinical severity is generally considered to be driven primarily by gestational timing rather than parasite genotype, but also infections acquired during later stages of pregnancy, where parasite genetic factors may play a more important role in determining clinical outcome [50, 51]. Nine CTcases followed maternal infection acquired before conception or in the periconceptional period, or occurred after maternal reactivation, and six of these were severe; although based on small numbers, this observation is of interest given that the previously reported haplogroup 16 case of severe CT also followed maternal reinfection during pregnancy [8]. Compared with type II strains, haplogroup 16 strains were significantly associated with severe CT, whereas no such association was observed for type III strains. This association rests on seven cases only, with very wide confidence intervals, and require confirmation in larger series. Haplogroup 16 is a poorly described genetic group; it was first defined through whole-genome phylogenetic analysis of 62 T. gondii genomes from diverse geographic origins, in which it formed a well-supported, genetically distinct branch [32]. Among the strains assigned to this haplogroup were three from patients investigated through the French NRCT: the previously reported IPP-URB strain (TgH20005), responsible for a severe CT case following maternal reinfection during pregnancy associated with imported horsemeat consumption [52], and two additional strains (TgH26044 and TgH21016) isolated from patients with no history of travel or reported consumption of imported horse meat. Additional haplogroup 16 strains have repeatedly been identified through NRCT microsatellite genotyping, all carrying the characteristic TUB2 allele 287. Although its geographical origin remains elusive, several observations nevertheless point towards a South American origin for this genetic group, even though all ten haplogroup 16 infections of the present series were acquired in Europe. CASTELLS, the only strain in the original whole-genome dataset with a documented geographic origin outside the French NRCT, was isolated from a sheep in Uruguay [53]. Moreover, although haplogroup 16 forms a distinct phylogenetic branch, whole-genome analyses indicate genetic proximity to South American genomes [31]. These observations are of particular interest given the high burden and severity of toxoplasmosis reported in South America [6]. Although parasite genetic diversity is thought to contribute to this pattern, the genetic groups involved remain poorly characterized [54]. Our findings suggest that haplogroup 16 may represent one such group and warrant further investigation, particularly in the context of CT.

The associations discussed so far concern the severity of CT once the parasite has reached the fetus. Whether genetic groups also differ in their ability to reach the fetus at all is a distinct question. In the present study, all nine cases in which CT was initially suspected but mother-to-child transmission was ultimately excluded correspond to a strain isolated from the placenta of a child who was not infected (no-CT), and their genotype distribution is unbalanced: six arose among the 875 type II strains recovered from suspected or confirmed CT, whereas three arose among the five type I strains of the entire collection. Taken together, these five strains are too few to support any inference on the transmissibility of type I strains to the fetus, or to argue that they cross the placenta less efficiently than type II strains. Elsewhere, in a previous study, the rate of CT has been reported higher in Martinique than in mainland France, suggesting that certain T. gondii lineages from Caribbean islands (i.e., Caribbean 1, 2, and 3) could be more efficiently transmitted to the fetus than type II strains [55].

. In cases of acquired toxoplasmosis in immunodeficientpatients (ATID), compared with type II strains, which were more frequently associated with disseminated forms of toxoplasmosis, Africa 1 strains were more frequently associated with isolated cerebral forms, whereas type III strains showed a distribution of clinical forms similar to that observed for type II strains. A previous study based on a smaller series of ATID cases from the French NRCT and the associated nationwide network attributed the high frequency of cerebral forms among patients infected with Africa 1 strains to the predominance of HIV infection in this group [29], as HIV-positive patients are more likely to present with cerebral toxoplasmosis than patients with other causes of immunodeficiency. This hypothesis was supported by the fact that HIV infection was significantly more frequent among patients infected with Africa 1 strains (80.0%) than among those infected with type II strains (32.7%). The association between cerebral forms and HIV infection was subsequently supported by another study [53]. However, the present study provides additional insight. Although HIV infection was also the predominant cause of immunodeficiency among patients infected with type III strains (52.9%), no significant difference in the distribution of causes of immunodeficiency was observed between Africa 1 and type III infections, although with 25 and 17 patients respectively this comparison cannot exclude differences of substantial magnitude. Despite these comparable immunodeficiency profiles, isolated cerebral forms remained significantly more frequent among patients infected with Africa 1 strains than among those infected with type III strains. This observation suggests that differences in immunodeficiency status alone are unlikely to fully explain the clinical differences observed between these two genetic groups. Taken together, these findings support an association between Africa 1 strains and isolated cerebral forms in immunodeficient patients and suggest that T. gondii genotype may play a role in determining the clinical presentation of toxoplasmosis even in the context of immunodeficiency. Nevertheless, this interpretation should be considered with caution, given the relatively small sample sizes for these lineages and the potential influence of other factors that could not be assessed in the present study, including host genetic background or ethnicity, which have previously been implicated in the patterns observed for OT and ATIC cases [10, 18].

In cases of acquired toxoplasmosis in immunocompetent patients (ATIC), type II strains were uncommon and were identified only in paucisymptomatic cases. In contrast, most cases were associated with strains acquired in French Guiana, which belong to a T. gondii population with several unique characteristics. This population is almost exclusively associated with the wild environment of French Guiana and displays exceptionally high genetic diversity. Despite this diversity, WGS studies have demonstrated that Amazonian strains constitute a well-defined discrete genetic group [31, 32]. The present study shows, however, that this group cannot be delimited with 15 microsatellite markers, and a majority of the ATIC cases of this series involved genotypes that could not be assigned to any recognized group but whose geographical origin was indistinguishable from that of whole-genomecharacterized Amazonian strains. Taken together, these strains were almost invariably associated with severe disseminated toxoplasmosis, in keeping with the case reports and case series coordinated by members of the French network that have documented the exceptional pathogenicity of this population, which can be life-threatening if left untreated in immunocompetent individuals exposed to the Amazonian rainforest environment [48, 49]. In addition, haplogroup 16 was also associated with one severe disease case in an immunocompetent patient. While haplogroup 16 was more strongly associated with severe CT in the present study, this observation suggests that its pathogenic potential may not be restricted to CT. Of note, the previously reported case of severe CT caused by a haplogroup 16 strain occurred following maternal reinfection during pregnancy in a woman who had been infected before conception and experienced a paucisymptomatic clinical presentation [52]. Although based on very limited numbers, these observations raise the possibility that haplogroup 16 may exhibit an intermediate level of pathogenicity between type II and Amazonian strains. The single Africa 1 strain identified among ATIC cases was associated with a paucisymptomatic presentation. Interestingly, although the Africa 1 lineage is primarily associated with ATID cases, suggesting a lower apparent pathogenicity in immunocompetent hosts, a small case series of ATIC associated with Africa 1 strains has previously been published by the French nationwide network in immunocompetent immigrants from Africa and travelers returning from Africa [56]. Of note, one immunocompetent patient infected with an Africa 1 strain from this study had severe disseminated toxoplasmosis; however, that infection was associated with an incomplete genotype and could therefore not be included in the present analyses.

In patients with OT, most ocular samples could not be successfully genotyped, likely because of the low T. gondii DNA burden typically present in ocular specimens, in particular aqueous humor; only 7.1% of the 325 OT cases of the series yielded a complete genotype. This previously observed limitation [57, 58] introduces a major source of bias when investigating associations between T. gondii genotype and clinical presentation. Among successfully genotyped cases, type II strains accounted for more than half of the genotypes identified, but a substantial proportion of OT cases also encompassed a broad range of other genetic groups (Africa 1, Caribbean 1, haplogroup 16, Mascarene 1 and unclassified genotypes), collectively accounting for a substantial proportion of cases, although each group was represented by only a small number of cases. Although the limited number of successfully genotyped samples precludes firm conclusions, this observation may suggest a greater propensity of certain nontype II genotypes to proliferate in ocular tissues. Conversely, several OT cases associated with type II strains in both immunocompetent and immunodeficient patients presented features commonly associated with severe disease, including bilateral involvement, extensive inflammation, recurrent episodes, or incomplete clinical improvement despite treatment. This observation is consistent with previous findings from the French nationwide network [57], which demonstrated that type II strains can cause severe forms of OT. It should be noted, however, that most cases classified as severe met the criterion of recurrent or reactivated disease (14 of 15). Because recurrence can only be observed in patients followed long enough, and follow-up duration was not documented in this dataset, patients seen on a single occasion cannot be identified as severe on this criterion. The relative frequency of severe OT according to T. gondii genetic group therefore remains largely unknown. Addressing this question will require larger datasets and improved genotyping success rates, while also accounting for the multiple other factors that may influence disease severity.

The present study has several limitations related to its retrospective design and to the fact that the dataset was not initially established for genotype-clinic association analyses. Consequently, data collection was not fully standardized over time, resulting in missing data for several variables. In addition, analyses were performed separately for each clinical category, leading to marked imbalances in sample sizes across categories and genetic groups. This limited the statistical power of certain analyses and contributed to wide confidence intervals and large odds ratios for some comparisons. Furthermore, due to the nature of the dataset, which was derived from cases analyzed by the French NRCT, some genetic groups of T. gondii that are rare or exotic in France were represented by only a small number of cases within each clinical category. This limited representation reduced the statistical power of the analyses and precluded robust assessment of whether certain rare genetic groups are associated with increased disease severity. Missing data also prevented the implementation of multivariable analyses and may have introduced residual confounding. Although potential confounding factors were partially explored through MCA and subsequent pairwise statistical comparisons, the influence of unmeasured variables cannot be excluded. An additional limitation relates to the strong correlation observed between certain T. gondii genetic groups and the geographic origin of infection. Several lineages showed highly structured geographic distributions, including Amazonian strains in French Guiana, Africa 1 strains in sub-Saharan Africa, and Caribbean lineages in the French Caribbean territories. Consequently, the respective contributions of parasite genotype, geographic origin, environmental exposure, host genetic background, and other region-specific epidemiological factors could not be fully disentangled. Although geographic origin was incorporated into the MCA, the absence of multivariable models prevented formal adjustment for these potentially confounding factors; the comparisons restricted to infections acquired in Europe and Central Asia provide only a partial answer to this question. Similarly, some host-related variables were unevenly distributed across genetic groups. For example, HIV infection predominated among patients infected with Africa 1 strains, whereas haematological malignancies were more frequent among patients infected with type II strains. Although these factors were considered in the exploratory analyses and further investigated through targeted pairwise comparisons, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Moreover, because the multiple correspondence analyses and the confirmatory tests were performed on the same dataset, the corresponding p-values should be interpreted as exploratory. Another important limitation relates to genotyping success, which varied substantially according to the type of clinical sample analyzed. While genotyping success was high for isolated strains, it was considerably lower for primary clinical specimens and particularly limited for ocular samples. This differential genotyping success may have introduced selection bias if certain T. gondii genotypes are associated with higher parasite burdens or are more readily amplified from clinical specimens. This recurring limitation in T. gondii molecular epidemiology highlights the need to develop improved parasite enrichment and purification methods for low-parasite-load clinical samples. Finally, it should be noted that the French NRCT is a surveillance network rather than a population-based cohort. As a result, uncommon, severe, or clinically atypical cases are more likely to be investigated and genotyped than mild infections. Consistent with this, complete genotyping was more frequent for severe or clinically overt presentations in all clinical categories except ocular toxoplasmosis, so that the clinical spectrum associated with each genetic group may be shifted towards its more symptomatic end.This potential recruitment bias may be particularly relevant for ATIC patients, for which severe forms are more likely to be referred to specialized centers and included in the NRCT database.

5. Conclusion

In conclusion, this study, based on the largest collection of human T. gondii genotypes assembled to date, highlights the remarkable diversity of parasite strains infecting humans receiving care in France, and illustrates the value of long-term national surveillance networks for investigating the epidemiology and clinical impact of toxoplasmosis. By combining standardized microsatellite-based genotyping with clinical and epidemiological information collected over more than four decades, the French network of hospital parasitology diagnostic laboratories provided a unique opportunity to investigate the relationships between T. gondii genotypes and the clinical manifestations of different forms of human toxoplasmosis in patients infected in France or in other regions of the world. Our findings indicate that although a given T. gondii genetic group can be associated with a broad spectrum of clinical manifestations, certain genetic groups display preferential associations with specific disease presentations. Type II strains, while predominantly associated with infraclinical or mild CT, were also responsible for severe CT following maternal infection in all three trimesters of pregnancy, as well as for severe ocular disease. Type I strains were remarkably rare in this collection and showed no signs of increased clinical severity compared to type II. Amazonian strains were identified almost exclusively in immunocompetent patients with severe acquired toxoplasmosis, in keeping with previous reports of the exceptional pathogenicity of this genetic group. Haplogroup 16 emerged as a genetic group associated with severe CT. In immunodeficient patients, Africa 1 strains showed a particular association with isolated cerebral forms compared to type II and type III strains. Taken together, these . findings suggest a role for parasite genotype in shaping the clinical expression of toxoplasmosis, even in the presence of strong host-related determinants such as immunodeficiency or gestational timing of infection. Finally, our results emphasize the need to expand genetic surveillance internationally and pursue whole-genome investigations across diverse geographic regions to better characterize the global diversity of T. gondii and understand the biological determinants underlying the pathogenicity of its different genetic groups.
