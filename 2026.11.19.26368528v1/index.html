What we found

We found 2 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Abstract

Biological rhythms modulate symptoms of brain disorders, yet brain stimulation therapies largely overlook the temporal organization of pathological brain states. Harnessing these rhythms could improve therapeutic efficacy. Here, we show that circadian coordination of hippocampal network connectivity is a biomarker of therapeutic response to responsive neurostimulation in epilepsy. Using longitudinal electrocorticography from individuals with bitemporal epilepsy, we find that alignment of circadian connectivity rhythms between the left and right hippocampus predicts seizure reduction. The daily distribution of stimulation also predicts how these rhythms evolve over months. We capture these dynamics using a nonlinear Kuramoto oscillator model and demonstrate that optimizing stimulation timing in silico can improve predicted circadian network alignment. Our findings establish a cycle-based, control-theoretic framework for modelling and potentially reshaping brain network rhythms, offering new opportunities for personalized neurostimulation in epilepsy and other neurological and psychiatric disorders.

Introduction

Epilepsy operates on a clock. Epileptic seizures, like many neurological and psychiatric symptoms such as mood, anxiety, and pain, are not entirely random events; they are modulated by rhythmic brain activity spanning circadian and multidien (multi-day) timescales 1, 2 . Yet, existing closed-loop brain stimulation systems treat pathological activity as though they were temporally isolated events: detect an abnormal pattern, deliver stimulation, and repeat. Although effective for some patients, this reactive paradigm does not explicitly account for cyclical brain activity that influence emission rates of pathologic events or state-dependent effects of stimulation [3] [4] [5] [6] . Circadian rhythms are believed to create recurring changes in brain excitability that influence both seizure likelihood and therapeutic effects of stimulation 7, 8 . Cycle-aware brain stimulation offers the possibility of moving beyond interrupting discrete pathological events towards reshaping the underlying rhythms that modulate their occurrence 9.

In drug-resistant focal epilepsy, a striking temporal disconnect separates the delivery of closed-loop stimulation from the emergence of clinical benefit. The NeuroPace Responsive Neurostimulation (RNS) System delivers brief bursts of electrical stimulation in response to epileptic activity detected through intracranial electrocorticography (ECoG). While RNS benefits many patients, treatment responses remain variable, seizure reduction can take years to emerge, and complete seizure freedom is uncommon 10 . Originally thought to terminate incipient seizures, RNS is increasingly thought to exert its therapeutic effects through repeated bursts of stimulation that cumulatively alter brain network dynamics 11 . Circadian rhythms offer a potential link between these timescales, as stimulation bursts are distributed across daily cycles of fluctuating brain states. Yet, whether the circadian patterning of stimulation bursts reinforces beneficial network plasticity and ultimately drives clinical improvement remains poorly understood 12, 13 . Bridging this knowledge gap requires computational models that link stimulation timing, longitudinal network dynamics, and treatment response, laying the foundation for cycle-guided control of brain activity.

Development of such models presents three challenges. First, how circadian cycles shape seizure likelihood within the epileptic brain network remains poorly understood. Functional connectivity (FC) of the bilateral hippocampi, which captures synchronized brain activity that reflects communication spanning these structures, has been established as a candidate biomarker of seizure likelihood 14 and long-term seizure reduction 12 . It remains unclear whether FC itself follows circadian rhythms, how these rhythms align across brain regions, and whether their coordination reflects treatment response. Second, the effects of time-dependent stimulation on FC reorganization and treatment response must be resolved. Previous studies examined how stimulation effects depend on seizure risk states 3, 15 but have not established how the daily patterning of stimulation bursts shapes circadian variation of FC. Third, capturing the nonlinear evolution of network dynamics demands interpretable computational models capable of learning from sparse longitudinal recordings available in clinical practice.

Here, we develop a cycle-guided computational framework integrating circadian brain network dynamics, daily stimulation patterns, and clinical outcomes in people with bilateral mesial temporal lobe (bitemporal) epilepsy. We first examine how circadian cycling of FC provides a dynamic marker of treatment response. We next investigate whether stimulation timing contributes to the long-term evolution of these circadian cycles. Lastly, we develop a dynamical oscillator model to explore how stimulation could be tuned to reshape circadian network dynamics and design individualized treatment strategies in silico. Together, we demonstrate a novel approach for moving beyond reactive, event-triggered stimulation towards deliberate, directed modulation of circadian brain network dynamics in epilepsy. Results

We studied 38 adults (21 males; Supplementary Table S1) with bilateral mesial temporal lobe (bitemporal) epilepsy who were implanted chronically with the RNS System with bilateral hippocampal depth leads that recorded neural activity (Fig. 1a). During routine clinical encounters, monthly patient-reported seizure frequency was compared to seizure frequency during a 3-month pre-implantation baseline period. Analysis was restricted to visits for which records were available.

Network concordance of circadian cycles

To measure time-dependent, intrahippocampal communication, we computed a functional connectivity (FC) metric called the phase locking value (PLV) between RNS contacts in the anterior and the posterior hippocampus. Left-and right-sided hippocampal FC was obtained for 99,349 scheduled electrocorticography (ECoG) recordings collected by the RNS device over 66,732 participant-days. Recordings were acquired at participant-specific predetermined intervals (Fig. S1).

We modelled circadian dynamics in left and right hippocampal FC separately using Lomb-Scargle (LS) spectral analysis, a method that detects repeating patterns in irregularly sampled timeseries (Fig. 1b). LS models with a 24-hour period were fitted to FC values over time to obtain the amplitude and phase of network circadian dynamics. LS models were fitted independently for each frequency band and channel pair within non-overlapping six-month windows (epochs) anchored to the date of implantation. Across the cohort, network circadian cycles were evident across frequency bands, with peak phases spanning the 24-hour clock (Fig S2).

We anticipated that the circadian cycling of FC in the left and right hippocampi would rise and fall at the same times of the day. Surprisingly, in an example individual, we discovered that the left-and right-sided circadian cycles were initially distinct and dynamically reorganized during RNS treatment (Fig. 1c). Specifically, circadian cycling within the left and right hippocampi became more aligned, or concordant, with greater seizure reduction (Fig. 1d). We hypothesized that (1) seizure reduction is linked to circadian reorganization of hippocampal FC, and (2) circadian reorganization is driven in part by the relative timing of stimulation bursts over the course of the day (Fig. 1e). To test these hypotheses, we evaluated circadian cycle concordance as a candidate biomarker of treatment response across the study cohort.

Biomarker of ongoing seizure reduction

Longitudinal seizure outcomes varied across participants (Fig. 2a). Progressive seizure reduction was observed across the cohort over multiple years, but seizure frequency fluctuated over time. For example, participants demonstrated periods of worsening seizure frequency following intervals of near-complete seizure freedom. Given these fluctuations, we posit that there may be biological signals that underlie the RNS treatment course. We therefore asked whether hippocampal network circadian dynamics could predict ongoing seizure reduction. We found that greater alignment of circadian cycles between the left and right hippocampus differentiated visits with high seizure reduction (> 0.90) from visits with low seizure reduction (< 0.50) (Mann-Whitney U test P = 0.0009; Fig. 2b).

To further investigate circadian network dynamics as a predictor of treatment response, we constructed multivariate regression models to predict longitudinal seizure reduction from time-matched circadian network features. Model performance was evaluated using leave-one-participant-out cross-validation (LOPO CV), and mean prediction errors were computed on held-out test sets (Methods) (Fig. 2c).

Circadian features improved the prediction of time-matched single participant treatment response by 14.3% above chance (Fig. 2d). We observed weakened gamma-band circadian amplitude and strengthened alpha-band circadian concordance as predictors of longitudinal seizure reduction (Fig. 2e). Higher circadian concordance in the alpha band was associated with improved ongoing seizure reduction (Fig S3). We rigorously assessed the robustness of this finding across stricter detection criteria of circadian cycles (Fig. S4). Further, the connectivity-based marker of circadian concordance outperformed power-based markers of circadian concordance in predicting clinical seizure reduction by 57.5% (Fig. S5). Henceforth, circadian concordance (CC) in the remainder of this paper refers specifically to concordance in the alpha frequency band due to its strong predictive effect.

Clustering of peak hours of network circadian cycles

We next asked whether the circadian rhythms spanning the hippocampal network have generalizable and stable organization across participants and time. Pooling circadian peak hours across participants, left and right hemispheres, and epochs, we observed a markedly non-uniform distribution (Kuiper test, n = 466, P = 5.2e-6) (Fig. 3a), with peak hours forming one distinct nocturnal cluster (centered at 3:39AM ± 4.1 mins) and another distinct diurnal cluster (centered at 3:02PM ± 3.5 mins).

We investigated the longitudinal stability of cluster membership within each participanthippocampus in six-month epochs and found that cycles tended to remain within the same nocturnal or diurnal cluster to which it was originally assigned at the beginning of RNS treatment (Fig. 3b). Transitions between clusters were relatively infrequent and could occur in either direction (diurnal-to-nocturnal or nocturnal-to-diurnal), with individual cycles potentially visiting both clusters multiple times over the recording period (Fig. S6). State transition analysis revealed that cycles were approximately four times more likely to remain within their current cluster than to transition to the other (Fig. 3c; within-cluster transition probability: 0.83 and 0.82 for diurnal and nocturnal states, respectively; inter-cluster transition probability: 0.17 and 0.18). The near symmetry of transition probabilities across clusters indicates that neither phase state is inherently more stable than the other.

Finally, we examined whether intrinsic properties of a circadian cycle could prospectively predict the type of transition it would undergo. Circadian amplitude was significantly lower in cycles that subsequently underwent inter-cluster transitions compared to those that remained within the same cluster (2-sample t-test; n₁ = 282, n₂ = 62; P = 4.8e-6; Fig. 3d). A logistic regression model trained to predict transition type from cycle amplitude and phase achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.689 in leave-one-participant-out cross-validation (Fig. 3e), confirming the prospective utility of this feature.

Taken together, these findings reveal that hippocampal network circadian cycles organize around nocturnal or diurnal attractors. Low-amplitude cycles are more susceptible to transitions between clusters centered at these attractors. Crucially, cycle amplitude can identify, six months in advance, those participants whose circadian concordance is likely to undergo a substantial reorganization, opening a potential window for prospective therapeutic intervention.

Circadian patterning of stimulation events

We next sought to test whether RNS therapy drives changes in circadian cycles underlying treatment response. Based on our finding that the circadian rhythm predicts treatment response, we reasoned that the temporal patterning of stimulation events over the course of the day may also relate to seizure control. Total stimulation count did not predict treatment response (Fig. S7). The stimulation profile curve, which quantifies the normalized distribution of hourly stimulation event counts per participant, differentiated super responders (seizure reduction > 90%) from poor responders (seizure reduction < 50%; Fig. 4a). After aligning each participant's stimulation profile curve to their peak hour, super responders exhibited a temporally narrower stimulation profile compared to poor responders (2-sample t-test at each hour; n1 = 8, n2 =10, P uncorrected < 0.05 between 1h and 7h relative to stimulation peak hour).

To quantify the temporal concentration of stimulation, we defined stimulation profile area (SPA) as the area under the normalized stimulation profile curve (Fig. 4b). Intuitively, lower SPA indicates stimulation is concentrated within fewer hours of the day, whereas higher SPA indicates that stimulation remains elevated over a broader portion of the day. Thus, SPA is sensitive to the relative temporal distribution of stimulation over a 24-hour period rather than the amount of stimulation. SPA over 24 hours, defined as SPA(24h), was negatively correlated with average seizure reduction across participants (n = 38, r = 0.399, P = 0.013; Fig. 4c), suggesting that participants with more temporally concentrated stimulation profiles over a 24-hour period experienced greater seizure reduction. We further tested whether stimulation during particular times of day was more strongly associated with treatment response. SPA was computed for all hourly intervals spanning a 24-hour period and correlated with average seizure reduction (Fig. 4d). A systematic pattern emerged: greater seizure reduction was associated with stimulation concentrated into a narrower portion of the day. Specifically, participants with lower SPA within the window beginning at 11 AM and ending at 7 AM the following morning experienced greater seizure reduction (n = 38, r = 0.504, P uncorrected = 0.0013; Fig. 4e). This finding indicates that less broadly distributed stimulation across afternoon, evening, and overnight hours is associated with better treatment response.

We compared the utility of stimulation features, i.e., SPA and stimulation peak phase, with network circadian dynamics features for predicting seizure reduction. Multivariate regression models were trained with RFE and evaluated using LOPO CV (Fig. S8). Network circadian features alone outperformed stimulation features in predicting longitudinal seizure reduction (Fig. 4f). Combining stimulation and circadian network features yielded the strongest overall model, reducing prediction error by 21.3% relative to the baseline mean predictor.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that the daily timing of RNS stimulation carries important information about treatment response. Greater seizure reduction was associated with stimulation concentrated around a narrower daily peak, rather than sustained across much of the day, particularly outside the afternoon, evening, and overnight hours. Circadian organization of the stimulation profile curve captures therapeutic information complementary to that encoded in hippocampal network dynamics.

Bidirectional control of network circadian cycles

What drives reorganization of the circadian cycle and determines the change in peak phase? An answer to this question would provide mechanistic insight into how effective long-term neurostimulation re-aligns circadian rhythms across the hippocampi. We trained a multivariate regression model to forecast directional changes in the peak hour of the circadian cycle observed six months ahead (Fig. 5a). Models incorporated the initial state of the circadian cycle and stimulation features during the ensuing six months as predictors.

Temporal patterning of stimulation forecasted the directional change in peak hour (LOPO CV; n = 292, r = 0.187, P = 0.0013; Fig. 5b, Fig. 5c). Stimulation that was temporally concentrated in the morning predicted an anti-clockwise shift in circadian peak hour, whereas stimulation distributed broadly across the afternoon, evening, and night predicted a clockwise shift.

This finding demonstrates that the timing of neurostimulation actively shapes hippocampal network circadian dynamics over months, supporting the hypothesis of a stimulation mechanism operating at circadian timescales. Kuramoto oscillators model long-term dynamics of network circadian cycles

Such a model would move beyond empirical correlations toward a mathematical description of network circadian evolution under the influence of stimulation. Discussion

We develop a novel cycle-based control-theoretic framework for modelling the effect of brain stimulation on network circadian dynamics and leveraging this understanding for in silico optimization of stimulation in epilepsy. Using longitudinal ECoG recordings from individuals with bitemporal epilepsy undergoing RNS therapy, we demonstrated that the alignment of circadian network activity across the left and right hippocampus is a biomarker of ongoing seizure reduction. More temporally concentrated stimulation was associated with greater seizure reduction, revealing a previously uncharacterized relationship between the circadian patterning of stimulation and therapeutic outcome. Critically, longitudinal changes in hippocampal network circadian dynamics were predicted by the 24-hour temporal structure of stimulation. Together, these findings support a novel mechanism of RNS action operating at the circadian timescale. We formalized this mechanism using a Kuramoto oscillator model, which accounted for the six-month evolution of hippocampal network circadian dynamics under stimulation. Virtual interrogation of a population-level framework with the Kuramoto model identified optimized stimulation timing strategies that, in silico, doubled the proportion of epochs in which participants achieved near-complete seizure freedom relative to current clinical practice. Beyond its immediate application to hippocampal RNS in epilepsy, the proposed framework has broader relevance for the design of brain stimulation therapies across neurological and psychiatric conditions where symptoms fluctuate with regular periodicity 9, 16.

We found that bilateral alignment of circadian dynamics in the hippocampal network predicts ongoing seizure reduction. We established this biomarker by investigating the circadian dynamics of network connectivity based on the finding that FC captures longterm therapeutic response and forecasts 24-hour seizure risk 12, 14 . Our study extends prior work developing biomarkers of treatment response 7, 17 by tracking circadian network dynamics and seizure reduction in parallel over years of follow-up, capturing the longitudinal heterogeneity in patient-reported outcomes that is characteristic of realworld RNS therapy 18, 19 . The closest precedent for a circadian biomarker of ongoing response to brain stimulation comes from the literature on brain stimulation for psychiatric disorders. A prior study of deep brain stimulation in obsessive-compulsive disorder 16 reported that circadian cycles of 9 Hz power tracked symptom severity and therapeutic efficacy. Our findings in epilepsy provide further evidence that the relationship between circadian neural organization and stimulation response may be a general principle applicable across neuropsychiatric conditions.

Effects of brain stimulation are thought to be state-dependent, though the precise mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Brain states related to seizure likelihood are known to modulate the effect of stimulation 3, 4 on long-term seizure control with RNS 15 . Brain states related to sleep and wakefulness, which are observable manifestations of an endogenous circadian oscillator 20 , may similarly modulate the effect of stimulation delivery 21.

Consistent with this reasoning, we found that stimulation that was more temporally concentrated between 7AM and 11AM was associated with greater seizure reduction. This relationship has both prognostic and prescriptive value: it provides a novel indicator of likely treatment response and suggests a potential target for stimulation redesign. Relative to super-responders, poor responders tended to receive disproportionately more stimulation at night than during the day, resulting in a temporally wider distribution of stimulation across the 24-hour cycle. While RNS-delivered stimulation has not been found to disrupt sleep architecture per se 22 , excessive nocturnal stimulation may nonetheless interfere with sleep in a manner analogous to interictal epileptiform discharges, which are known to disrupt sleep 23 . Conversely, more temporally uniform stimulation across the non-morning hours may promote long-term reorganization of the epileptic network by engaging synaptic consolidation mechanisms active during sleep 24, 25 , thereby contributing to the sustained therapeutic efficacy observed in RNS 12, 13 . As sleep was not tracked in this retrospective cohort, we could not assess whether sleep-wake state mediates the link between stimulation timing and outcome. Future prospective studies should track sleep to disentangle its contribution from that of circadian timing.

Circadian concordance in the alpha band emerged as the strongest predictor of ongoing treatment response, consistent with prior work linking alpha band dynamics in the therapeutic effects of brain stimulation. In RNS for epilepsy, early post-implantation increases in daily alpha oscillatory power have been associated with long-term seizure reduction 7 , and in DBS for OCD circadian cycling in alpha band power predicted both symptom severity and treatment response 16 . Together, these findings suggest that alpha band activity may play a privileged role in the neural response to chronic brain stimulation, perhaps because alpha rhythms support mesoscopic communication between neuronal populations 26 . In epilepsy, bilateral hippocampal circadian misalignment may reflect partial isolation of seizure-generating tissue from broader synchronizing networks 27 , leading to attenuated circadian power in the tissue 28 and allowing local network rhythms to drift independently. Greater concordance may therefore index a more integrated hippocampal circuit capable of distributing epileptiform activity across connected networks rather than permitting its concentration within an isolated seizure-generating zone 29 . Such alignment may also increase the reach of stimulation-evoked activity across hemispheres, enhancing the capacity of RNS to reshape pathological circuits 30, 31 . An alternative interpretation is that concordance is not causal, but instead reflects successful stimulation-driven reconnection of an isolated epileptic network. Distinguishing whether circadian concordance actively mediates seizure reduction or serves as a downstream marker of network repair will be an important direction for future work.

Our principal methodological contribution is demonstrating that the Kuramoto oscillator framework can mathematically describe the longitudinal evolution of hippocampal network circadian dynamics under the influence of responsive neurostimulation. The model serves three complementary purposes: to move beyond empirical correlations toward a process-level account of how circadian network organization evolves over time; to provide mechanistic insight into how stimulation timing reshapes network oscillatory dynamics; and to furnish a predictive framework for optimizing stimulation delivery by deliberately steering circadian peak phases toward bilateral alignment.

Our study offers a platform for in silico optimization of the temporal patterning of neurostimulation. RNS parameter tuning is a high-dimensional problem, encompassing over 12 million possible parameter combinations with no principled basis for selection 32 and currently relies on time-consuming empirical adjustment 33, 34 . Critically, agreement between virtually optimized stimulation and verum stimulation predicted better outcomes, supporting the biological validity of the framework. By virtually identifying stimulation timing strategies that promote circadian concordance, the Kuramoto framework offers a principled approach to expediting this search and improving individual treatment outcomes. More broadly, the simplicity, neurophysiological interpretability, and established applicability of Kuramoto models across neuroscience 35 positions this framework as a promising tool for studying the effects of brain stimulation across a wide range of timescales: from seconds to circadian and multiday periods.

Prior computational approaches have largely focused on either differential equationbased models or probabilistic state-space models 36, 37 . Differential equation-based approaches, including Epileptor https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?9bHW7v , Virtual Brain 39, 40 , and related biophysical models 41 have described fast seizure dynamics. Probabilistic state-space models provide a complementary view by capturing neural variability and autoregressive dynamics, including the acute neural effects of stimulation 42 and multiday cycling for seizure forecasting 43 . These models characterize dynamics at either the seconds or multiday timescale and are primarily concerned with the evolution of seizure risk rather than the network reorganization that underlies chronic treatment effectiveness. Our approach is distinct. We employ non-linear coupled differential equations to model how stimulation delivery interacts with circadian hippocampal network organization over months-long timescales. In doing so, the Kuramoto framework bridges the gap between the fast dynamics captured by seizure-onset models and the slow multiday trends captured by state-space forecasting approaches, providing a principled account of how chronic stimulation progressively reshapes the network organization that governs seizure susceptibility.

This study has limitations. Circadian concordance was established retrospectively in a limited-sized cohort of 38 participants with bilateral hippocampal RNS implants, and the generalizability of the biomarker to a larger and more diverse cohort of RNS implant configurations remains to be established. The closed-loop design of RNS precludes separability of stimulation from the interictal epileptiform activity (IEA) that triggers it. Therefore, it is possible that individuals whose IEA is more temporally concentrated over a day are intrinsically more responsive to treatment. Additionally, we did not account for effects of stimulation parameters or medication, which may independently influence treatment outcomes beyond the temporal patterning of stimulation examined here 4, 44 . RNS ECoG snapshots are sampled intermittently, so sampling bias is possible, although novel application of sliding-window LS analysis provided a temporally coarse representation of long-term dynamics. Future work should explore Kalman filtering approaches which capture more fine-grained temporal dynamics by estimating the instantaneous phase of circadian cycles from longitudinal recordings 43, 45 . Kuramoto modelling relied exclusively on the initial and final phase observations of each six-month epoch, restricting higher-resolution view of circadian phase trajectories and the use of more expressive oscillator models, such as the Stuart-Landau model 46 . Next generation devices may permit deliberate temporal patterning of stimulation and programming of cycle-based network biomarkers that would enable prospective validation of our in silico stimulation optimization pipeline for clinical translation.

Our study identifies circadian stimulation timing as a new dimension of therapeutic control for neurostimulation. Clinically, the in silico optimization framework offers a principled pathway towards personalized treatment and accelerated stimulation parameter tuning. This could advance contemporary practice, in which clinicians empirically navigate a vast stimulation parameter space without knowing whether or where more effective settings may exist. Hippocampal network circadian dynamics could also inform pre-implantation surgical planning and device placement by identifying recording or stimulation sites whose rhythms most closely track seizure risk. More broadly, these findings suggest that effective neurostimulation may require tuning not only where 47 , how strongly, or under what detection threshold stimulation is delivered, but also when it is delivered relative to the brain's endogenous rhythms.

Key mechanistic questions remain for future investigation. First, does circadian concordance protect against seizures or reflect stimulation-induced network repair 29, 48 ? Second, do alpha band dynamics actively mediate the therapeutic effects of stimulation? Finally, how do circadian rhythms bridge the acute effects of stimulation with the chronic reorganization of brain networks, potentially through sleep-dependent plasticity? Although developed in epilepsy on the circadian timescale, our framework may generalize to other neurological and psychiatric disorders with rhythmic fluctuations on circadian and multidien timescales.

Methods

Participant cohorts and clinical outcomes

Study participants were individuals with drug-resistant epilepsy who had been implanted with bilateral hippocampal leads connected to a responsive neurostimulation (RNS) device (NeuroPace). Clinical outcomes were assessed longitudinally at scheduled clinic visits throughout the course of treatment. At each data-complete visit, clinical seizure frequency was assessed as the participant-reported number of seizures experienced in the preceding month. Seizure reduction at each visit was computed by comparing the fractional change in clinical seizure frequency compared to pre-implantation 3-month baseline, and was defined on a continuous scale from 0 (no reduction relative to baseline) to 1 (complete seizure freedom). In cases where the reported seizure rate exceeded the baseline rate, seizure reduction was assigned a value of 0 to reflect the absence of therapeutic benefit. Average seizure reduction over the full treatment course was computed as a weighted average of the longitudinal seizure reduction values, with weights proportional to the number of days elapsed between successive data-complete visits. Responder status was assigned both at the level of individual clinic visits and for each participant overall, based on average seizure reduction. Three categories were defined: super responder (seizure reduction > 0.9), intermediate responder (0.5 ≤ seizure reduction ≤ 0.9), and poor responder (seizure reduction < 0.5).

Device data acquisition

Each participant was implanted with two intracranial leads, one in the left hippocampus and one in the right hippocampus. Each lead contained four electrode contacts arranged in a bipolar montage, yielding two bipolar channels per lead and four channels per participant in total. Channels 1 and 2 corresponded to the left hippocampus and Channels 3 and 4 to the right hippocampus.

The RNS device continuously acquired two types of data throughout the course of therapy. First, scheduled electrocorticography (ECoG) recordings were collected as 90second clips at six-hour intervals, at times of day predetermined by the treating epileptologist for each participant (Fig. S1). The number and times of Scheduled ECoGs were generally evenly distributed within participants. These recordings were predominantly interictal and were acquired longitudinally across the full duration of device implantation. ECoG signals were sampled at 250 Hz. Second, the device recorded the cumulative number of stimulations delivered during each hour of the day, providing a continuous log of stimulation activity over the entire treatment course.

ECoG data pre-processing and feature extraction

Each 90-second Scheduled ECoG clip was preprocessed independently through three sequential steps: bandpass filtering, stimulation artifact removal, and functional connectivity (FC) feature extraction. Signals were bandpass filtered into four physiologically defined frequency bands: theta (4-7 Hz), alpha (8-13 Hz), beta (14-30 Hz), and gamma (30-70 Hz). Filtering was performed using a second-order Butterworth filter applied in a forward-backward (zero-phase) fashion to prevent phase distortion. Notch filtering at 60 Hz was omitted, as the RNS device is battery-powered and does not introduce mains electrical interference. Stimulation artifacts were identified and removed prior to FC computation. For any stimulation event detected within a recording, the corresponding signal segment was excised along with a 1-second buffer on either side to eliminate residual artefactual activity. Excision was applied simultaneously across all channels at the affected timepoints to preserve cross-channel temporal alignment. Recordings in which residual artifact-free data totaled fewer than 30 seconds after excision were excluded from further analysis. Additional quality control criteria were applied to exclude recordings in which one or more channels lacked data, contained nota-number (NaN) values, or exhibited temporal overlap with another scheduled recording.

FC was quantified using the phase locking value (PLV), a measure of inter-channel phase synchrony 12 . We used PLV as a measure of FC due to its robustness to interictal epileptiform activity (IEA) 14 . Instantaneous phase was extracted from each bandpassfiltered signal using the Hilbert transform and used to compute PLV between channel pairs. To characterize local hippocampal network dynamics, FC was computed exclusively between the two channels on the same lead, i.e., within the left hippocampus (Channels 1-2) and within the right hippocampus (Channels 3-4) independently. This yielded one PLV estimate per hemisphere per frequency band per recording, and two PLV estimates in total per band across both hippocampi. FC was extracted from all retained Scheduled recordings for each participant. This ensured consistent temporal alignment across recordings and facilitated downstream circadian analysis.

Stimulation data preprocessing

Stimulation activity was characterized using the continuous hourly stimulation count log recorded by the RNS device. For each participant, hourly stimulation counts were averaged across all available days for each hour of the day (0-23), yielding a 24-point profile of mean stimulation activity. This profile was then min-max normalized across the 24-hour period within each participant, mapping the minimum mean hourly count to 0 and the maximum to 1, with all intermediate values scaled linearly. This normalization reflects the assumption that neural tissue adapts to the prevailing level of stimulation, such that the minimum delivered stimulation approaches a quiescent baseline, maximum stimulation approaches saturation, and intermediate values scale linearly. All downstream analyses were performed on this normalized average stimulation profile. For longitudinal analyses, the same averaging and normalization procedure was applied within non-overlapping six-month windows anchored to the date of implantation, yielding a time-resolved stimulation profile for each epoch.

Two scalar features were derived from the normalized stimulation profile for each interval of interest. The peak hour of stimulation was defined as the hour of the day at which the normalized average stimulation reached its maximum value (i.e., equal to 1). The stimulation profile area (SPA) was defined as the sum of the normalized average stimulation values over a specified set of hours, quantifying the temporal concentration of stimulation within that interval. SPA was computed for two interval definitions: the full 24-hour period (SPA(24h)), and a specified sub-interval between hours h1 and h2 (SPA(h1-h2)). Higher SPA values indicate stimulation that remains elevated over a broader portion of the day.

Multivariate regression and ML modeling with recursive feature elimination

Multivariate linear regression was employed for four distinct analyses: (i) biomarker discovery, relating network circadian features to seizure reduction; (ii) prediction of longitudinal seizure reduction from circadian and stimulation features; (iii) identification of factors driving changes in circadian peak hour over successive six-month epochs; and (iv) prediction of Kuramoto model coupling constants from circadian cycle and stimulation features. All regression models were implemented using the Python statsmodels library 52.

For each regression target, an initial candidate feature set was assembled and subjected to iterative recursive feature elimination (RFE) within a leave-one-participant-out (LOPO) cross-validation framework. At each iteration, the model was trained across all crossvalidation folds and the number of folds in which each feature was a statistically significant predictor of the dependent variable was recorded. Features that were significant in at least one fold were retained; all others were discarded. This reduced feature set was then used as the input to the next RFE iteration. The process continued until either no features remained (indicating an absence of reliable predictive signal) or all remaining features met the significance criterion in at least one fold. Feature significance was assessed using the training data within each fold to prevent information leakage from the test set. Prior to model fitting within each fold, all features were zscored using the mean and standard deviation computed from the training data only, preventing any leakage of test-set distributional information into the normalization step. LOPO cross-validation was adopted throughout to provide a conservative, clinically relevant estimate of model generalization and to avoid overestimation of effect sizes. Because some participants contributed very few observations (in some cases a single sample), fold-level mean squared error (MSE) estimates were highly variable and unsuitable for direct averaging. To address this, test-set predictions from all folds were pooled across participants and compared jointly against the corresponding ground-truth values. Predictive performance was quantified as the Pearson correlation (r) between pooled predictions and ground truth. Model MSE was similarly computed on the pooled predictions. A baseline model was defined as the participant-agnostic mean predictor, in which the mean of the dependent variable computed from the training data in each fold was used as the prediction for all test observations in that fold. Baseline MSE was computed from the corresponding pooled baseline predictions to ensure a directly comparable reference. Percentage improvement in MSE over baseline was used to report the relative gain achieved by each model.

Logistic regression was employed for binary classification tasks, with feature selection conducted using the same iterative RFE framework described above. At each RFE iteration, features were retained if their unit-normalized absolute regression coefficient exceeded 0.05 in at least one LOPO cross-validation fold, providing a consistent threshold for identifying features with non-negligible discriminative contribution. All features were z-scored using the mean and standard deviation computed from the training data only prior to model fitting within each fold. LOPO cross-validation was applied both during RFE and for final performance evaluation. As with the regression models, test-set predictions were pooled across all folds, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) was used as the primary performance metric.

We note that this validation scheme estimates and evaluates coupling constants on the same set, which departs from conventional train-test separation in machine learning studies, but is not uncommon in Kuramoto literature. We argue that this approach is nonetheless non-trivial and appropriate for four reasons. First, coupling constants are sample-specific by design, as they vary across epochs and participants; a held-out evaluation scheme is therefore not applicable in the conventional sense. Second, the Kuramoto equations are non-linear, such that even small perturbations in coupling constants can produce qualitatively different phase trajectories; achieving low prediction error thus constitutes a meaningful constraint on the solution. Third, using the entire timeseries for parameter estimation is a standard procedure in several previous applications of Kuramoto models to empirical neurophysiological data 53, 54 . Fourth, the coupling constants were obtained from an under-determined system subject to a minimum-norm constraint; achieving low prediction error under these conditions is nontrivial, as it is not guaranteed that biologically arbitrary constraints would yield accurate forward simulations. We further acknowledge that the steady-state approximation does not account for the initial phase configuration of the system, which is known to influence trajectory dynamics in non-linear systems. These are inherent limitations of having access only to the start-and end-point phases of each epoch, rather than a continuous phase timeseries. These limitations should be interpreted as estimation and data limitations rather than fundamental incompatibilities between the Kuramoto framework and the underlying biology.

In silico optimization of stimulation using Kuramoto model

The validated Kuramoto framework was used to perform in silico optimization of stimulation delivery. For a given participant's initial circadian cycle configuration, we asked: what peak hour of stimulation would maximize bilateral alignment of hippocampal circadian cycles over a six-month horizon? Circadian concordance was used as the optimization target, given its established role as a biomarker of ongoing seizure reduction. The peak hour of stimulation was treated as the free parameter, consistent with our empirical finding that stimulation timing influences the long-term dynamics of circadian peak phases.

For each initial configuration of left and right hippocampal circadian cycle peak phases, the Kuramoto model was run forward for 180 days for each of the 24 candidate stimulation peak hours (midnight through 11 PM), in one-hour increments. For each candidate hour, the predicted terminal peak phases of the left and right hippocampal cycles were used to compute circadian concordance as an approximation of the expected seizure reduction under that stimulation setting. The stimulation peak hour yielding the highest circadian concordance was identified as the optimal setting for that initial configuration. To demonstrate the clinical applicability of this approach, all in silico analyses were conducted in a strictly held-out fashion. For each participant, coupling constant prediction models (described below) were evaluated using only the fold in which that participant served as the test set, ensuring that no data from the participant under analysis contributed to any aspect of model training.

Characterization of network circadian cycles

Robustness analysis of circadian cycles as biomarker of seizure reduction

To investigate the robustness of the circadian concordance biomarker, we re-analyzed the data with a stricter inclusion criterion. Only epochs in which at least one of the two alpha band network circadian cycles had a significant LS model fit were retained for the analysis.

With the stricter inclusion criterion, circadian concordance of the alpha band was significantly associated with ongoing seizure reduction (n = 56, r = 0.503, P < 0.001; Fig. S4a). Multivariate models that predicted outcome from circadian features achieved comparable or higher performance when epochs with significant LS model fit cycles were considered, compared to the case when all epochs were used (Fig. S4b). Furthermore, the peak phase of circadian cycles in alpha showed a bimodal distribution even when only significant LS model fit cycles were considered (Fig. S4c) (Kuiper test, n = 228, P = 3.2e-9; cluster centers: 3:18AM and 2:44PM; cluster boundaries: 8:37AM and 8:57PM). Overall, these results support the broader validity of the observation that alignment of network circadian cycles is a biomarker of ongoing seizure reduction, with the bimodal distribution of cycle peaks underpinning the biomarker.

Circadian cycles of power-in-band features

We evaluated how cycling of spectral power-in-band (PIB) compares to cycling of functional connectivity (FC) as a predictor of ongoing seizure reduction. Data was pre-processed using the same pipeline as for the FC values. A single PIB value was computed for each channel and band from a Scheduled recording. Recordings were divided into non-overlapping six-month windows (epochs) from the date of implantation for each participant. Cycle characteristics were measured by fitting Lomb-Scargle models independently to each epoch and band across participants. Only epochs with at least 120 Scheduled recordings were included for downstream analysis.

Similar to network circadian cycles, PIB circadian cycles were prevalent across hippocampi (channels), bands, and participants. PIB circadian cycles were more prevalent than FC circadian cycles (Fig. S5). Cycle amplitudes were largest for theta band and smallest for gamma band (Fig. S5a). Cycles in theta, alpha, and beta bands showed a unimodal distribution with a peak around 3AM (Fig. S5b), which may reflect the sleep-wake cycle. We explored the potential of PIB circadian cycles as a biomarker of ongoing seizure reduction. When combined with stimulation features, network circadian cycles predicted outcomes better than power-based circadian cycles. Thus, circadian cycles are widely prevalent in ECoG data with network circadian cycles being most predictive of ongoing seizure reduction.

Validation of in silico framework

For a given peak hour of virtual stimulation, the Kuramoto model-based in silico framework was used to predict the circadian cycle peaks trajectory over six months. The terminal phase of the circadian cycles at six months was used to obtain the predicted circadian concordance induced to the given virtual stimulation. By exploring all 24 candidate virtual stimulation peak hours across all samples (epochs), we computed the average circadian concordance as a function of stimulation peak hour across the population (Fig. S11b). Concordance was highest when stimulation peaked between 5 AM and 8 AM. The local minima observed near 3 AM and 3 PM reflect the influence of the attractor oscillators at those phases. When stimulation aligns with an attractor, cycles are pulled toward that attractor independently, driving concordance toward either 0 or 1 depending on whether the two cycles are attracted to the same or opposite attractors.
