Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

p=0.95 The exception being slopes of the high classes in MCS and BiB where there was little statistical evidence that these differed; i.e., both are persistently high across time (p=0.95; Figure 1).

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Depression/anxiety symptoms emerge early in life. We examined emotional symptom trajectories, from early childhood, in three UK birth-cohorts spanning successive generations and diverse socio-ethnic contexts.

We used data from the Avon Longitudinal Study of Parents and Children (ALSPAC), Millennium Cohort Study (MCS) and Born in Bradford (BiB) to identify groupbased emotional symptom trajectories from 3-14y (N=11,025; 15,446; 6,711). Participants were born three decades apart (ALSPAC: 1990-2, MCS: 2000-2, BiB: 2007-10). We characterised groups by: sex, ethnicity, maternal depression/anxiety and IMD quintile. In ALSPAC we modelled associations between trajectories and depression/anxiety diagnoses at 24y and 30y.

Results: Participants from ethnic minority backgrounds comprised 4% of ALSPAC, 17% of MCS and 66% of BiB. In all cohorts 49% were female. Each cohort had low-, mid-and highlevel symptom trajectories. High-level trajectories comprised 6-7% of children. In younger cohorts (MCS and BiB), the high-level symptom trajectory started high and persisted while in the older ALSPAC cohort it started low and increased. In MCS and BiB, low-to-medium-level symptoms increased over time, but remained stable in ALSPAC. Female sex and maternal depression/anxiety were associated with increased odds of high-level trajectories (all cohorts). Lower area-level deprivation and belonging to the local ethnic majority group were protective. Mid-and high-level symptom trajectories had higher odds of depression/anxiety diagnoses in early adulthood in ALSPAC.

Conclusions: Childhood emotional symptom trajectory-groups are broadly similar across generations and diverse social contexts. However, children born after 2000, in more diverse contexts, may experience persistent symptoms from a younger age.

Introduction

Depression and anxiety symptoms often emerge early in life. Approximately half of depression cases emerge before age 30 and nearly 40% of anxiety cases emerge before age 14 (Solmi et al., 2022). It is widely reported that prevalence of depression and anxiety in the population is increasing, especially in young people (Hua et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024). In the UK, an increase in primary care reported anxiety and depression has been observed in children as young as six (Cybulski et al., 2021). However, much of the existing research into developmental trajectories of depression and anxiety in young people has focused on adolescence and early adulthood (Shore et al., 2018 , Portogallo et al., 2024).

Evidence from population growth models suggest anxiety/depression are manifesting earlier, lasting longer, and increasing at a faster rate in children born in 2000s vs those born in the 1990s, in the UK (Armitage et al., 2023). However, studies investigating which sub-groups contribute to overall population level growth are lacking. For example, we do not know whether the prevalence of children with persistent, high-level emotional symptoms has changed across generations.

Diverse, contemporary cohorts, using the same assessment method longitudinally are available but have so far been underutilised in comparative cross-cohort trajectory studies.

Especially cohorts in which participants are currently of adolescent age (born around 2010-12). Adolescents of this age grew up amidst important social and economic pressures that have been suggested as possible determinants of mental health trends in young people, such as prevalent smart phone use, rising inequalities, cuts to youth services, educational forms, and the COVID-19 pandemic (McGorry et al., 2025).

Studying long-term trends of early pre-clinical emotional symptoms is critical because clinical anxiety and depression often first manifest as emotional difficulties in childhood. Capturing early onset and longitudinal trajectories can help inform strategies for prevention, intervention, and resource allocation involving health, education, social care and other agencies to improve long-term population-level outcomes. Specifically, identifying groups of children with early-onset, persistent emotional problems in childhood through to adolescence. Prior meta-analysis of longitudinal depressive symptom trajectories in childhood/adolescence shows that persistent emotional symptoms in early life predict later diagnoses of major depressive disorder (MDD) (Portogallo et al., 2024) alongside a range of poor social and physical health outcomes, for example NEET ("Not in education, employment or training") status (Morales-Muñoz et al., 2023; Rahmani & Groot, 2023).

In the current study we aimed to: a) identify a high/persistent emotional symptom trajectory group spanning early-childhood to early-adolescence in three generationally, ethnically, and socioeconomically diverse UK cohorts; b) compare group size/proportions, symptom severity over time and onset across cohorts; and c) characterise trajectory groups by sociodemographic factors, and their association with mental illness diagnosis in earlyadulthood.

Methods

Study design and participants

We used data from three successive, prospective UK birth cohorts born between 1990 and Ethical approval for each cohort was obtained from the relevant Local Research Ethics Committee and informed consent was obtained from participants, full details for each cohort can be found in the Supplementary Materials.

. ALSPAC: enrolled pregnant women living in Avon, UK with expected delivery dates between 1st April 1991 and 31st December 1992. The initial number of pregnancies 14,541 and 13,988 children were alive at age 1y. When children were approximately 7y, the initial sample was bolstered with further eligible cases. The total sample age 7y is 15,447. Of these 14,901 children were alive at age 1y (Boyd et al., 2013; Fraser et al., 2012; Northstone et al., 2019 Northstone et al., , 2025)). The study website contains details of available data through a searchable data dictionary and variable search tool: http://www.bristol.ac.uk/alspac/researchers/our-data/. MCS: enrolled 18,827 babies (age 9 months) from 18,552 families born in England or Wales between 1 st September 2000 and 31 st August 2001, and Scotland or Northern Ireland between 23 rd November 2000 and 11 th January 2002. Children from disadvantaged areas and ethnic minority backgrounds were over-sampled to ensure the sample was nationally representative. MCS data are available via the UK Data Service [SN 2000031] (UK Data Service, 2024).

Measures Emotional symptom measure

Emotional symptoms were measured using the emotional sub-scale of the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ-E; range 0-10). SDQ-E is a widely used measure of child . mental health. Questions include worries/fears, depressed mood and somatic complaints.

SDQ-E has good concurrent and discriminant validity (Goodman, 2001; Lundh et al., 2008; Muris et al., 2003), and performs well in validation studies for detecting anxiety and depression cases across development (Armitage et al., 2023).

SDQ-E was measured at multiple timepoints between age 3-17y. To harmonize timepoints across cohorts we grouped the SDQ-E observations into age five age bands: 3-5, 6-8, 9-11, 12-14, and 15-17 years. In the case of multiple measures for an individual within an age band the first measure was taken. Our primary models used data from 3-14y in all cohorts as data were sparse at 15-17y in BiB.

In ALSPAC and MCS, SDQ-E was parent-reported at all timepoints. In BiB SDQ-E was teacher reported at two waves, parent reported in one wave and self-reported in Age of Wonder. Detailed information regarding measures and reporter type by age bands can be seen in the Supplementary Materials (Tables S1a, 1b), along with sensitivity analyses addressing possible measurement differences between reporter types.

Sociodemographic variables and maternal depression/anxiety

Sociodemographic measures include child sex at birth/early infancy, maternal depression and/or anxiety, ethnic group, and 2010 Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD quintiles). The IMD ranks areas from most to least deprived across seven domains (income, employment, education, health, crime, living environment and housing). Due to data sparsity we dichotomized ethnicity as white/ethnic minority groups. White includes: English, Welsh, Scottish, Northern Irish or British; Irish; any other White background. Ethnic minority groups include: Mixed/multiple, Asian or Asian British, Black, Black British, Caribbean or African, Arab, Other ethnic groups. Data were reported by mothers in questionnaires.

Mental health diagnoses at age 24 and 30 years in ALSPAC.

In ALSPAC we examined associations between trajectory groups and binary ICD-10 diagnosis of depression and presence of generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) at ages 24 and 30y. Cases were derived from the self-administered computerized Clinical Interview Schedule-Revised (CIS-R) (Fairweather et al., 2026).

Statistical analyses

Data was cleaned in STATA v19.0 (StataCorp, 2025). We used Mplus (v8.11) (Muthén, B. O., 2017) to model trajectories.

Trajectory modelling

Our objective was to identify clinically meaningful and interpretable trajectory groups in each cohort, with primary interest in identifying a high/persistent symptom trajectory. Inclusion of BiB as a contemporary, ethnically diverse cohort was key. Emotional symptom trajectories have not been modelled in this cohort before. We used Latent Class Growth Analysis (LCGA) to derive data-driven latent classes from clusters of individuals with similar patterns of change in SDQ-E over time. Parsimony was a priority given the smaller sample in BiB.

Alternative modelling approaches were considered; see Supplementary Materials. Supplementary figure S3b shows that a linear model fits the observed means well. We adhered to the Guidelines for Reporting on Latent Trajectory Studies (van de Schoot et al., 2017) (GRoLTS; checklist in Supplementary Materials).

Class posterior probabilities were used to assign individuals to trajectories. We preserved the relative spacing between timepoints and retained a zero anchor. Identical methods were used across cohorts to ensure we limited variation from sources other than the data.

All models were estimated using maximum likelihood which is robust to non-normality. We used 1000 random starts, 200 final iterations. The best log-likelihood was replicated at least 20 times in all models. We considered class solutions from one to six classes. Final models were selected based on clinical interpretability/meaning of groups, comparison of model fit . statistics, and sample size of the smallest class. We aimed to avoid very small classes (<5% of the overall sample). Fit statistics included: class average posterior probabilities,

Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), sample-adjusted-BIC, entropy and Lo-Mendell-Rubin likelihood-ratio-test (LMR-LRT).

Participants contributed to a model if they had at least one datapoint. Full information maximum likelihood (FIML) was used to fit the model, assuming data are missing at random. See Supplementary Materials for full details of missing data handling and distributions (Supplementary Tables S2a-2c).

Cross cohort comparisons

After estimating trajectories in each cohort individually, we combined data from the three cohorts and formally tested for cross-cohort differences using a categorical variable to define cohort membership. We used the original, cohort-specific derived slope and intercept estimates as starting values for each trajectory group in a combined model and allowed class proportions to differ between cohorts. We performed pairwise Wald tests to test for evidence of differences between slopes and intercepts of the corresponding classes across cohorts (e.g., ALSPAC high vs MCS high). P-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Bonferroni method (Dunn, 2012).

Subgroup characterisation

We used the bias-adjusted-three-step method (Asparouhouv & Muthen, 2014) to characterise latent trajectories by sex, ethnicity, IMD, and maternal depression/anxiety, and to study associations between trajectory class and depression/anxiety diagnoses at ages 24 and 30 in ALSPAC. This method accounts for classification uncertainty while preventing covariates from redefining classes or artificially inflating entropy. Samples for univariable regression models included participants with complete data on the characterising factor and the latent class (hence sample size varied between models).

Sensitivity analyses

We ran supplementary analyses in MCS and ALSPAC using data from age 3-17y (Supplementary Materials, Figure S2). In all cohorts we conducted sensitivity analyses restricting the sample to participants with ≥2 repeated measures (Supplementary Materials, Figure S3a).

Given the multiple reporters in BiB (school, parent and self; Table S1b), we conducted sensitivity analyses to test whether original BiB trajectory groupings were an artefact of reporter type by standardising SDQ-E scores across reporter types in two ways.

First, at each timepoint we calculated mean differences between the observed BiB SDQ-E scores (teacher-or self-reported) and published parent-reported "norms" at ages corresponding to the timepoints in our study (Table S7). These "norms" are based on a large national survey of child and adolescent mental health carried out by the Office for National Statistics (Meltzer et al., 2000).

Second, where there were both parent/teacher or parent/self-reported SDQ-E scores at a single timepoint in BiB, we calculated mean differences between the teacher/self-reports compared to the parent reported means at the same timepoint (Table S8).

Based on these mean differences, we calculated two sets of SDQ-E scores standardised to parent-reported levels (standardised to parent-reported "norms" and standardised to observed BiB parent-reports) by adjusting the non-parent reported scores up or down accordingly. We then re-ran the three-class LCGA models using each set of adjusted SDQ-E scores, and compared the slopes, intercepts and 95%CIs of the classes to those from the original three-class LCGA model (estimated using observed SDQ-E scores)(Supplementary Figure, S1).

Results

Sample characteristics

Baseline samples in our primary analyses comprised 11,025 children from ALSPAC (born 1990-92), 15,446 children from MCS (born 2000-02) and 6711 children from BiB (born 2007-10) with ≥1 parent/teacher reported Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) measure between age 3-14y.

In all cohorts 49% of the baseline sample were female (Table 1). ALSPAC has very few ethnic minority participants (4%) compared to MCS (17%) and BiB (66%). Over two-thirds of BiB participants were in the most deprived IMD quintile, compared to 35% of MCS participants and 14% of ALSPAC participants. Roughly 25% of mothers had depression/anxiety in both ALSPAC and MCS. Nearly half of mothers had depression/anxiety in BiB.

Selection of emotional symptom trajectory groups

A three-class solution was selected. A high-level symptom group was identified in all cohorts by a three-class model. Group sizes became compromised (even in the cohort with the largest sample) when additional classes were added (the four class model in MCS identified a group size <4%; Table S4). Information criteria (AIC, BIC, SABIC) improved with additional classes, although the magnitude of improvement diminished with further classes and class patterns were not clinically relevant to our research aim (Supplementary Material, Tables S3-5).

Similarities and differences between cohorts.

Despite generational and sociodemographic differences between cohorts, we observed a high-level symptom trajectory and similar proportions of trajectory group membership in all cohorts. Between 72-75% of each cohort belonged to a low-level symptom trajectory (scores <2), 18-22% of each cohort belonged to a mid-level symptom trajectory (scores 3-4), and approximately 6-7% belonged to a high-level symptom trajectory (scores ≥5) (Figure 1, Table 1).

Two key differences were observed between cohorts. In the high-level symptom trajectory, symptom level started high and remained persistent across time in the younger cohorts born after 2000 (MCS and BiB) whereas symptom level in this group started low and steeply increased in the oldest cohort (born early 1990s; ALSPAC). In the younger cohorts, born after 2000 (MCS and BiB), either the mid-or low-level symptom trajectories increased over time, unlike ALSPAC where these groups were stable.

In MCS (born 2000) the mid-level trajectory increased and, by age 12-14 years symptom level nearly reached that of the high-level trajectory group. In BiB (the youngest cohort; born 2007-10) the low-level trajectory increased and, over time, overlapped with the mid-level trajectory. However, the high-level trajectory group shows clear separation from the mid-and low-level trajectories (Figure 1, Table S15).

Results of pairwise Wald tests comparing high, mid, and low classes across cohorts support our descriptive findings and trajectory group characterisation (Supplementary Table S6a).

For all but one of the comparisons there was strong evidence that slopes and intercepts differed between cohorts. The exception being slopes of the high classes in MCS and BiB where there was little statistical evidence that these differed; i.e., both are persistently high across time (p=0.95; Figure 1). In contrast, there was strong statistical evidence that the . slope of the high-class in ALSPAC differed to both MCS, and BiB high-class slopes (both comparisons p<0.001).

Sociodemographic correlates of trajectory groups

In all cohorts, females and children of mothers with depression/anxiety had increased odds of being in high-level or mid-level symptom trajectory groups (Figure 2, Tables S14-15).

Ethnicity showed opposing associations with emotional symptom trajectories between cohorts. In MCS, odds of high-level emotional symptoms (vs low-level) were nearly double in the ethnic minority participants vs. white participants (majority group). In BiB this was reversed; belonging to an ethnic minority group (the local majority group in Bradford) was protective against both mid-and high-level symptom trajectory membership (Figure 2, Tables S14-15).

Higher area-level deprivation was strongly linked to high-level emotional symptoms across childhood. In MCS we observed a dose-response pattern. A similar pattern was seen across symptom trajectories: lower deprivation was more protective for children with high-level symptoms compared to low-level symptoms. In ALSPAC, this pattern was mainly seen for the high-level vs low-level symptom group comparison, but not in the mid-level comparison, likely because this cohort is less socioeconomically diverse. In BiB, there was little evidence overall of an association due to small samples and large CIs (Figure 3, Tables S14-15).

In BiB, the high-level trajectory changed when the sample was restricted to participants with ≥2 repeated measures. However the remaining sample was very small at the final timepoint making the estimate difficult to interpret (Supplementary Material, Figure S3a). In BiB, trajectory group patterns were broadly unchanged in sensitivity analyses after adjusting SDQ-E scores to account for differences in reporter type across timepoints (Supplementary Material; Figure S1).

Discussion

We identified and characterised group-based early-childhood trajectories of emotional symptoms in three ethnically and socioeconomically diverse successive UK populationbased cohorts, born two decades apart. Despite generational, ethnic and socioeconomic differences, we identified a high-level symptom group of a similar prevalence (6-7%) in all three cohorts.

Two key differences emerged. In the high-level symptom trajectory, symptom level in childhood starts high and persists across time in the younger cohorts whereas in the oldest . cohort (born in the early 1990s) symptoms start low and steeply increase. In the younger cohorts, born after 2000, mid-or low-level symptom trajectories show an increase in symptom level, and become closer to mid/high groups over time, unlike the oldest cohort (born early 1990s) where these groups are stable. Considering both high-level and increasing symptom trajectories together, a larger proportion of children in the younger, more diverse cohorts belonged to these groups.

These findings support existing evidence that in recent decades emotional symptoms are increasing among children from an early age. Particularly in groups that experience relatively low-level symptoms, while symptom severity in group of children experiencing high-level of symptoms remains stable. Therefore, the overall picture is consistent with an increase in low-to-mid level symptoms in young children in recent decades, aligning with wider data trends in the UK showing a sharp rise in the incidence of depression and anxiety in young people from as young as six years old since 2012 (Cybulski et al., 2021).

Across all cohorts, female sex and maternal depression/anxiety were associated with higher odds of persistent or increasing symptom trajectories, whereas higher SES and belonging to the local ethnic majority were protective factors.

Prevalence of persistent-high symptoms aligns with broader trajectory literature despite differences in age, geography, and psychiatric measure. Group-based trajectory modelling studies identified in a large systematic review (Shore et al., 2018) mostly studied US populations with trajectories starting later (in early/mid adolescence). Studies typically identify similar patterns with <10% prevalence in the persistent group and are mostly of depression trajectories which might explain the slightly higher prevalence in persistence as depression onsets later than anxiety (Solmi et al., 2022). Few studies have modelled early group-based emotional symptom trajectories, capturing pre-clinical symptoms measured by screening questionnaires. Two previous studies which have modelled SDQ-E trajectories in ALSPAC from age 4-17 found 6% in the persistent group, consistent with our findings (Dachew et al., 2023; Tseliou et al., 2024). Studies that have compared SDQ trajectories . across multiple UK cohorts have examined overall population growth rather than investigating group-based trajectories (Armitage et al., 2023; McNicholas et al., 2024). Our findings build upon these, suggesting that that overall population growth may be attributed to symptom increases in children with initially mild to moderate symptoms rather than increased prevalence of children experiencing persistently high emotional symptoms from an early age.

Like our findings, previously identified predictors of trajectories with greater symptom burden include female gender, maternal depression/anxiety, lower income/education and non-white ethnicity (Dachew et al., 2023; Shore et al., 2018; Tseliou et al., 2024). However, research frequently excludes underrepresented populations and ethnic minority groups which are often at highest risk. Our findings build on these by comparing findings in ALSPAC (a largely white, higher SES cohort) with trajectories in two more diverse UK cohorts (MCS and BiB).

In the BiB cohort, belonging to an ethnic minority group was associated with lower odds of persistent high symptoms which contrasts with findings in other cohorts. Bradford, where the BiB cohort is based, has a large community of South Asian heritage. Previous findings in the cohort show South Asian mothers had a lower incidence of common mental health disorders compared to white mothers (Santorelli et al., 2024), our results are consistent with these findings. Protective effects may reflect belonging to the local area ethnic majority, a supportive role of extended family and community networks. However, this observation might also be explained by underreporting of mental health problems in South Asian communities.

Generational differences in social determinants of emotional problems between the cohorts may underpin some of our findings. A review by McGorry et al (McGorry et al., 2025). discusses social trends that might be contributing to rising mental health problems in young people. These include, rising inequalities and economic insecurity, educational reforms, social media/smart phone usage and associated sleep deprivation. Several of these factors disproportionately affect children of lower SES leading to higher emotional problems in this group, which is supported by our findings. In young children effects may be indirect e.g., . mediated by parental or home-environment factors like maternal emotional problems (McGorry et al., 2025).

Our findings reinforce the importance of sociodemographic and early life risk factors in predicting mental health trajectories. The two younger cohorts included in this study were more deprived overall which could explain the earlier onset and the higher proportion belonging to persistent/increasing trajectories. Furthermore, rates of maternal depression/anxiety were higher in the youngest cohort and in MCS rates of severe maternal depression/anxiety were comparable to rates of all/any maternal depression/anxiety in ALSPAC. Family psychiatric history and sociodemographic factors were common features in models identified by a recent systematic review of prediction models for anxiety/depression trajectories (Fairweather et al., 2026).

Increased mental health awareness and reduced stigma might partially explain increases in mild sub-clinical emotional symptoms. In the current study we see a fairly stable proportion of young people belonging to a high-risk, persistent group while the proportion in low/mid symptom groups increase. It has been suggested that reduced stigma and increased reporting/awareness of mental health in both young people and the parents of young people is contributing to this trend. Although beneficial, an unintended consequence of reduced stigma and improved awareness of mental health might be wider recognition/reporting of subclinical or transient emotional symptoms that are overinterpreted as clinical anxiety or depression. While this may lead to artefactual increases it has been hypothesised that this might drive genuine increases in new cases and worsening of existing cases by perpetuating behaviours such as avoidance (Foulkes & Andrews, 2023).

In the wider literature there is evidence of increasing severe CMD cases and symptoms (e.g., self-harm) in young people in the UK (Cybulski et al., 2021) as well as milder/moderate self-reported psychological distress. Identifying increasing and persistent emotional trajectories, early, before symptoms escalate is a useful and novel way to differentiate this . group from children with mild transient symptoms; as highlighted in a recent Lancet Psychiatry Commission on youth mental health (McGorry et al., 2024).

A major strength of our study is replication of findings across three ethnically and socioeconomically diverse populations, including groups frequently underrepresented in mental health research and using a contemporary cohort. We harmonized our modelling approaches across cohorts as far as possible. However, analyses exploring associations between key demographic factors and the persistent trajectory group in the BiB cohort were likely underpowered. Measurement invariance may explain the apparent increase in lowlevel symptoms, reflecting parent/teacher under-reporting earlier and more accurate selfreport later. However, we conducted sensitivity analyses to test this possibility. Broadly trajectory groups were unchanged which was reassuring. The fact that both sex and maternal depression/anxiety associated with high-level vs low-level symptom trajectories in BiB (like the other cohorts) further supports the characterisation of these groups.

High symptom trajectory membership predicted depression/GAD diagnoses in early adulthood (in ALSPAC), strengthening the validity of our trajectory groupings. Anxiety is often under researched compared to depression and our results support further research in this area as we high emotional symptom trajectories in early childhood strongly associates with future GAD diagnoses at age 24 and 30 in ALSPAC. However, we found no evidence that high symptom trajectories predicted depression at age 30. Given the finding at age 24 and strong existing evidence that childhood/adolescent symptoms persist into adulthood (Portogallo et al., 2024), it is likely that this finding reflects selection bias; attrition is a common limitation of longitudinal cohort data and there were substantially fewer depression cases in the high-persistent group at age 30.

In young, contemporary cohorts symptoms are emerging early. Health, education, social care and other services need to be responsive to this emerging trend, which may have longterm implications for young people. Early prediction of children at high-risk of developing persistent symptoms is an opportunity for early intervention and possible improvement of . long-term outcomes. This would support better allocation of resources to children with the highest need. However, ethical implications and risks of early screening for emotional symptoms in young people need consideration. Future research also needs to explore individual causal predictors of early-emerging, persistent trajectories, across diverse groups and generations to understand drivers of early onset.

Conclusion

Our findings show that the prevalence of high-level emotional symptoms in early life remains stable across UK cohorts spanning different generations and diverse ethnic and socioeconomic contexts. However, children in more diverse, contemporary cohorts appear more likely to experience persistently high symptom levels and increases from initially lower symptom trajectories. These findings align with wider data trends in the UK showing a rise in the incidence of depression and anxiety in young people, along with earlier onset in younger generations. Health, education, social care and other services need to be responsive to this emerging trend, which may have important long-term implications for young people.
