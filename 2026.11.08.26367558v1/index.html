Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Background: Unobtrusive recognition of activities of daily living (ADLs) from ambient sensors may support longitudinal assessment of everyday functioning in adults with mild neurocognitive disorder (mild NCD). However, real-world activity recognition is complicated by heterogeneous home layouts, sensor configurations, routines, and class distributions.

Objective: This study evaluated whether a room-aware Transformer that preserves locationspecific sensor structure improves participant-level ADL recognition relative to a global Transformer and a stacked unidirectional long short-term memory (LSTM) network.

Methods: Following pilot feasibility testing with two adult volunteers, we analyzed five homes of adults with mild NCD, comprising 92,765 minute-level observations across 67 annotated home-days. Motion, temperature, illuminance, humidity, and door-contact data were aligned to participant activity logs. The original labels were harmonized into eight categories. Models were developed separately for each home using 10-, 30-, and 60-minute windows. Complete calendar days were assigned to mutually exclusive training, validation, and chronological test sets, and no sequence crossed a day or partition boundary. The same partitions and evaluation procedures were used for all three architectures. Performance was assessed on held-out test days using accuracy, per-class F1 score, and micro-and macro-averaged one-versus-rest area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC).

Results: The room-aware Transformer achieved the highest or tied accuracy in 9 of 15 homewindow comparisons. Its accuracy ranged from 0.27 to 0.57, micro-AUC from 0.57 to 0.82, and macro-AUC from 0.54 to 0.78. Across the 15 settings, mean accuracy was similar for the roomaware and global Transformers and higher than for the LSTM. Mean micro-AUC and macro-AUC were marginally higher for the global Transformer than for the room-aware Transformer. The room-aware model showed its clearest gains in selected contexts, including Homes C and E at 10 minutes and Home A at 60 minutes, but no architecture or temporal window was uniformly optimal. Sleep/Rest was the most consistently recognized activity, whereas low-support activities frequently had low F1 scores despite moderate or high AUC values.

Conclusions: Room-specific feature processing provided context-dependent gains in participantlevel ADL classification, particularly for accuracy in several home-window settings. The marked variation across homes, activities, and temporal windows supports personalized model development, class-sensitive evaluation, and validation in larger and more diverse residential samples before clinical use.

INTRODUCTION

Mild neurocognitive disorder (mild NCD) is characterized by a modest decline in one or more cognitive domains beyond that expected with normal aging, while independence in everyday activities remains generally preserved [1]. Mild NCD substantially overlaps with the mild cognitive impairment (MCI), which is commonly described as an intermediate state between normal cognitive aging and dementia [2]. For some individuals, mild NCD may represent an early point along the Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD) clinical continuum. Although substantial functional dependence is not characteristic of mild NCD, individuals may require greater effort, additional time, compensatory strategies, or accommodations to complete complex daily activities [1]. Therefore, unobtrusive recognition of activities of daily living using ambient sensors may help characterize subtle functional patterns and longitudinal changes within natural home environments.

Early detection and tracking of such functional decline is critical for timely intervention. Moreover, recognition of changes in ADL performance may aid in early diagnosis, as subtle changes in routine motion patterns, sleep-wake cycles, and household behaviors often precede clinically detected deterioration by months or even years [3]. However, traditional assessment methods rely on infrequent clinical visits or caregiver reports, which can be subjective, inconsistent, and unable to capture real-world activity patterns [5]. Low-cost sensors, such as motion, temperature, luminance, and door-contact devices, provide rich temporal signals that may capture ADL performance while preserving privacy and avoiding adherence challenges common to other methods (e.g., cameras, wearable devices) [4].

Indeed, in-home ambient sensors have gained growing attention in biomedical informatics due to their unobtrusive nature, scalability, and ability to capture minute-level activity sequences. Prior work using motion sensors, door-contact sensors, and environmental readings demonstrated potential for early detection of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and functional change among older adults [5] [6]. Similarly, artificial intelligence (AI) models have shown promise for classifying ADLs, but many require dense instrumentation, large training sets, or computationally heavy architectures that limit practical deployment in real homes.

Deep Learning sequence models for ADL recognition may overcome some of these limitations, as they can yield high classification accuracy with lower instrumentation and sample size needs. For example, Liciotti et al reported 66.92 -94.12% accuracy for ADL recognition with LSTM-based models developed from the CASAS datasets [7]. Similarly, across five CASAS homes, Srivatsa and Plötz reported F1-scores of 0.746 to 0.901 for LSTM, 0.755 to 0.904 for transformer, and 0.783 to 0.924 for bidirectional LSTM [8]. Finally, Addepalli et al. proposed a deterministic room-specialized mixture-of-experts architecture that routed activity segments to compact room-specific transformer experts, achieving macro-F1 scores of 0.60-0.88 across five naturally occupied homes [9]. Taken together, these results suggest that deep sequence predictive models can accurately and efficiently recognize ADLs from heterogeneous sensor streams.

Of course, there were important methodological variations in how sensory data was represented spatially and temporally across these studies. Liciotti et al applied unidirectional and bidirectional LSTMs to globally represented CASAS sensor sequences, capturing temporal dependencies without explicitly preserving room-level structure [7]. Srivatsa and Plötz instead modeled sensors as a learned graph based on co-activation relationships, encoding local and global sensor dependencies without organizing features into functional room-specific branches [8]. And the room-specialized mixture of experts used deterministic hard gating to route each activity segment to a single independently trained room expert [9].

Unlike these previous studies, our investigation evaluated ADL classification in five heterogeneous, naturally occupied homes of adults with mild NCD. The proposed room-aware Transformer preserves spatial structure through dedicated room-specific feature projections followed by shared temporal self-attention, and its contribution is examined through comparisons with both a global Transformer and an LSTM baseline. Moreover, the study evaluates 10-, 30-, and 60-minute windows representing recent actions, room-transition patterns, and broader daily routines, respectively, using strictly chronological held-out complete days and both aggregate and class-sensitive performance measures.

Despite these advances, there remains a critical need for efficient, non-invasive ADL recognition systems that can operate reliably in multi-occupant households and accommodate the sparse, noisy nature of real-world sensor data. This study makes three principal contributions: 1.

It introduces a room-aware Transformer that organizes ambient-sensor features by functional location and applies room-specific projections before shared temporal selfattention, thereby preserving spatial structure that global representations may obscure.

2. It evaluates the room-aware Transformer against global Transformer and stacked unidirectional LSTM baselines using 92,765 minute-level observations from five heterogeneous, real-world homes of adults with mild NCD, capturing variation in layouts, sensor configurations, activity distributions, and routines. 3. It examines 10-, 30-, and 60-minute windows representing recent actions, room transitions, and broader daily routines, respectively. The observed variation across homes indicates that temporal-context selection should account for household-specific activity patterns, rather than assume a universally optimal window.

METHODS

Study Design

We used a two-phase design. Phase I was a feasibility and development study conducted in one home with two adult volunteers. Phase II was a real-world model development and evaluation study conducted in five instrumented homes. The two phases used the same general sensing philosophy and sequence-modeling framework but differed in participant population, household configuration and annotation ontology. For this reason, data were analyzed by phase and home, rather than pooled as if all minute-level observations were independent and interchangeable.

Ethical Considerations

The University of Missouri Institutional Review Board reviewed and approved the study procedures (IRB #2101666). All participants, or legally authorized representatives when applicable, and participating caregivers completed the informed consent process before data collection. The sensing system did not use cameras or microphones. Sensor and activity-log data were transmitted through encrypted connections and stored on secure institutional systems, and analyses used study identifiers rather than direct personal identifiers.

Sensing System

For both phases, this study utilized a non-wearable, in-home ambient sensing platform designed to capture continuous patterns of daily activity. The system consisted of multifunction sensors measuring motion, temperature, humidity and luminance, along with door-contact sensors capturing entry and exit events. All devices communicated through a Raspberry Pi 4 equipped with an Aeotec Z-Stick to enable local data collection and wireless connectivity. Sensors were placed in key functional areas of the homes, such as the kitchen, living room, bedroom, and bathroom, to monitor movement signals and environmental changes in light, temperature and humidity relevant to ADLs. The Raspberry Pi, running the OpenHAB homeautomation framework [10], transmitted encrypted data feeds to a secure cloud server via a dedicated Wi-Fi hotspot that can be extracted from API-driven front-end.

All sensor data underwent preprocessing and synchronization procedures prior to training the deep learning models used for ADL classification. The sensing platform, developed by the University of Missouri [11], has been implemented across multiple aging-in-place research sites in Missouri.

Study Sample and Datasets

The study datasets comprised five participant dyads enrolled in the AARG study, labeled as Home A, B, C, D and E each consisting of a participant with Mild Neurocognitive Disorder (NCD) and their informal caregiver. Participants across the five homes ranged in age from 60 to 80 years and had diagnoses of mild NCD between 2022 and 2023 and were enrolled with a clinical diagnosis of mild neurocognitive disorder. Continuous ambient sensor monitoring was conducted following enrollment, and the baseline diagnosis was used to characterize the study population. Dyads were White, non-Hispanic and lived in private homes instrumented with the ambient sensing system. Activity annotations for the participant with mild NCD were used as target labels. Homes were selected based on the availability of continuous ambient sensor streams, coupled with dense activity annotations to support the training and evaluation of deep learning models.

Input Features

The input features used for models training were derived directly from the ambient sensing system deployed in the homes of five participants with mild NCD. The study sensing system contained three types of ambient sensors to capture heterogeneous behavioral and environmental signals. First, multifunction sensors recorded passive infrared motion events together with continuous light and temperature measurements across primary home locations. Second, the multisensor provided the same motion, light, and temperature streams while additionally capturing humidity, and were therefore exclusively placed in main bathrooms to capture humidity fluctuations indicative of showering or other hygiene-related activities. Third, contact door sensors were installed on entry points (e.g., front and garage doors) to capture open-close events associated with household transitions. Accordingly, the collected data were structured into two main types: binary event data (motion and door states, encoded as 0 in events of no-motion or door closure or 1 if motion detected or door open) derived from all three sensor types, and continuous environmental data (temperature, light, and humidity) obtained from the multifunction and multisensor devices.

Motion events were captured in an event-driven manner, whereas temperature, humidity and illuminance were reported either at fixed default intervals (temperature every 15 minutes; illuminance every 60 minutes) or when the device registered a predefined threshold change (≥1 °C for temperature; ~200 lux for illuminance).

Activities of Daily Living

The supervised learning target variable consisted of 13 labeled Activities of Daily Living (ADLs). To ensure clinical alignment with established functional assessment frameworks, these activities were organized into three groups. Basic ADLs were defined according to the Katz Index [12], included four ADLs: Toileting, Hygiene, Eating, and Dressing, which reflect fundamental self-care abilities essential for personal hygiene, nutrition, and autonomy.

Instrumental ADLs (IADLs) followed the Lawton-Brody Scale [13] and comprised five ADLs: Medication, Cooking, Washing Dishes, Transportation (ability to travel outside the home for errands, appointments, or social activities), and Household activities. The remaining four ADLs: Sleep/Rest, Mobility (movement within house limit), Leisure/Social activity, and No Activity were categorized as Recreational/Other activities to avoid conflation with clinically validated ADL and IADL measures. No Activity was operationalized as time intervals in which no activity was logged by the participant, potentially reflecting sedentary or unreported behavior. These 13 ADL labels were derived from the participant activity logs described below and were then aligned to the time-stamped sensor data. Activity annotation for Homes A, B, C, D and E was derived from detailed activity logs completed by the participants and their informal caregivers. Activity logs captured the day date, start time, end time, activity label, location, and additional notes, enabling temporal alignment with ambient sensor streams. Both individuals received structured templates and written instructions developed by the MU research team, and informed consent was obtained prior to data collection. Participants and caregivers documented daily activities three days per week (two weekdays and one weekend day). The raw activity logs were coded and mapped to the 13 core ADLs described in the previous section. Follow-up interviews with the participants and caregivers were conducted to clarify activity patterns, validate interpretations, and refine the final target labels used for model evaluation. Final ADL target labels were aligned to a 24-hour timeline.

The final datasets comprised location-based sensor features and target labels for participants with mild NCD. Data Analysis

Data Preprocessing

To handle the irregular time-interval readings of ambient sensors and enforce a consistent 1-minute temporal granularity, we applied two interpolation strategies. First, for continuous features (temperature, light and humidity), we used AKIMA interpolation. AKIMA [16], a locally adaptive piecewise cubic interpolation method, produced smoother trajectories while preserving the original directional trends and temporal dynamics of the sensor data. Moreover, our own pilot study found AKIMA interpolation also improved deep learning model performance when handling time-series sensors data [17]. Second, for binary event-based sensors (motion and door), missing intervals were handled using zero-filling, reflecting the absence of activity or door closure events. This strategy provides a more realistic representation of inactivity in sparse event streams and avoids biasing sequential models toward false positives (motion detected while there is no motion), ensuring temporal 1-minute consistent gap timeseries across heterogeneous sensor data fed to the deep learning models.

For each home, the minute-level sensor data were partitioned at the complete-calendarday level into mutually exclusive training, validation, and test sets. The most recent days were reserved as the chronological test set, ensuring that all test observations occurred strictly after the development period comprising the training and validation sets. This design approximated the intended prospective application, in which a model developed from previously collected sensor data is used to recognize activities on future, unseen days. Training and validation days were selected exclusively from the earlier development period, with attention to maintaining activity representation and adequate coverage of less frequent classes. All 10-, 30-, and 60-minute sequences were generated separately within their assigned days, and no sequence was permitted to cross a calendar-day or data-partition boundary. This splitting strategy is more rigorous than randomly assigning individual observations or overlapping windows because temporally adjacent samples are highly correlated and can otherwise appear in both training and testing sets, producing optimistically biased performance estimates [18]. The validation set alone guided hyperparameter selection, early stopping, and model selection, whereas the held-out test set was used only for final performance estimation, consistent with established recommendations for prediction-model development and leakage prevention [19]. Robustness was further supported through class-weighted learning, dropout, weight decay, gradient clipping, restoration of the best validation checkpoint, deterministic random seeds, and reporting of per-class F1 and macro-AUC to account for class imbalance. Importantly, identical partitions, preprocessing procedures, sequence lengths, model-selection criteria, and training settings were applied to the LSTM, Global Transformer, and Room-Aware Transformer, thereby improving the fairness and reproducibility of model comparisons. Compared with conventional random or standard k-fold splitting, this design better preserves the temporal structure of continuous in-home sensing data and provides a more realistic estimate of within-home performance on future days, although it should not be interpreted as external validation across unseen homes or participants.

Deep Learning Configurations

The three deep-learning architectures were trained using architecture-specific configurations appropriate to their distinct temporal-learning mechanisms, while shared experimental settings were standardized to facilitate direct comparison [14] [17] [18]. For the stacked unidirectional LSTM, input sequences of 10, 30, and 60 minutes were processed recurrently through stacked LSTM layers with predefined candidate hidden-state dimensions and layer depths. We applied dropout between recurrent layers to reduce overfitting, and the final hidden-state representation was passed to a fully connected layer for multiclass ADL classification. The global Transformer projected all sensor and temporal features jointly through a single embedding layer, added sinusoidal positional encodings, and processed the resulting representations using four encoder layers with four self-attention heads, GELU activation, feedforward dimensions equal to four times the embedding dimension, and dropout of 0.20. In contrast, the room-aware transformer first grouped sensor features by functional-location prefixes. Each group was processed through a dedicated linear projection, layer-normalization, GELU, and dropout branch, while hour, minute, and day-of-week variables were projected separately. The room-specific and temporal embeddings were then concatenated, fused into a 16dimensional representation, combined with sinusoidal positional encodings, and processed through the same four-layer, four-head transformer encoder used by the global architecture before temporal mean pooling and ADL classification. Across all three models, the same withinhome data partitions, upstream preprocessing workflow, sequence lengths, batch-sizes of 64 and 128, class-weighted cross-entropy loss, Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 10³ and weight decay of 10, gradient-norm clipping at 1.0, maximum of 100 epochs, and early stopping based on validation loss with a patience of 10 epochs were applied. The same evaluation metrics were calculated for every architecture, and NumPy and PyTorch random seeds were fixed at 42 to reduce stochastic variation.

Model Performance Assessment

Model performances were evaluated on held-out test days using accuracy, per-class F1score, and micro-and macro-averaged one-versus-rest area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC). Accuracy quantified the overall proportion of correctly classified observations, while the per-class F1-score summarized activity-specific classification performance as the harmonic mean of precision and recall, balancing false-positive and falsenegative errors. ROC-AUC was calculated across the full range of classification thresholds, rather than at a fixed threshold of 0.5, and measured the model's ability to distinguish each ADL from competing classes [21]. Micro-AUC pooled one-versus-rest decisions across all observations and classes; consequently, it primarily reflected discrimination for prevalent activities such as Sleep/Rest, Leisure/Social, and Mobility from less prevalent activities. In contrast, macro-AUC assigned equal weight to every activity-specific AUC, ensuring that less frequent but clinically meaningful ADLs, including Toileting/Continence and Dressing, contributed equally to the overall estimate, regardless of their support.

In summary, high micro-AUC indicated good overall population-level discrimination, whereas high macro-AUC demonstrated that performance was distributed more equitably across common and underrepresented activities. Their joint interpretation prevented performance on dominant classes from obscuring weak recognition of infrequent ADLs, a well-established concern in imbalanced multiclass classification [9]. Per-class F1-score and AUC were additionally reported to identify specific activities for which favorable aggregate performance did not translate into reliable classification.

All models training and evaluation were conducted on a Linux Ubuntu 22.04 workstation running Python 3.10, PyTorch 2.5.1+cu121, and CUDA-enabled deep-learning libraries. The system was equipped with a NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU.

RESULTS

Phase I Feasibility in a Pilot Home

Phase I compared LSTM and global transformer models using data from two adult volunteers (V1 and V2). For V1, both architectures achieved similar average performance, with an accuracy of 0.72 and AUC of 0.94. For V2, the transformer improved average accuracy from 0.51 to 0.59 and AUC from 0.86 to 0.89 relative to the LSTM, with the best transformer configuration reaching an accuracy of 0.64 and AUC of 0.91. These findings established the feasibility of learning participant-specific ADL patterns from low-cost ambient sensor data and motivated Phase II, in which the approach was extended to five real-world homes involving participants with mild NCD. Phase II evaluated both the global feature representation and the proposed room-aware Transformer to accommodate differences in home layouts, monitored rooms, and activity distributions.

DISCUSSION

Principal Findings

This two-phase study evaluated LSTM, global Transformer, and room-aware Transformer architectures for participant-level ADL classification from ambient home sensors. Phase I established feasibility in a pilot home. Phase II compared the three architectures across five homes of adults with mild NCD using identical day-level partitions and 10-, 30-, and 60minute temporal windows. The principal finding was not uniform superiority of one architecture. Instead, the room-aware Transformer provided selective gains in accuracy in several homewindow settings, while the global Transformer produced marginally higher mean micro-AUC and macro-AUC. Performance varied more by home, activity, and temporal context than the aggregate results alone would suggest.

Comparison With Prior Work

The variation in ADL distributions across the five homes suggests that activityrecognition models must accommodate household-specific routines, rather than assume a uniform behavioral profile, consistent with prior research in ADLs recognition across different residential environments and resident populations [24]. The predominance of Sleep/Rest and sparse representation of Dressing and Toileting/Continence may favor recognition of common activities while limiting learning opportunities for underrepresented classes, reinforcing the importance of class-sensitive performance evaluation [25]. These findings motivate home-specific adaptation, supported by evidence that differences in users' activity patterns can reduce recognition performance and that transfer learning can improve adaptation to new users [26]. However, the observed distributions should not be interpreted as evidence of differences in mild NCD severity, because annotation coverage, monitoring duration, and individual routines may also influence activity prevalence.

Across the five homes and three temporal windows, the room-aware Transformer most often achieved the highest accuracy (8 of 15 settings), although its mean accuracy matched that of the global Transformer (0.43 for both). A possible explanation for these selective gains is the room-aware architecture's explicit separation of room-specific feature extraction from temporal modeling. Dedicated projection branches allow each room's sensor features to be transformed independently before fusion, preserving location-specific structure that the conventional LSTM must learn implicitly from jointly presented inputs. Multi-head self-attention subsequently relates observations across the fused sequence, potentially supporting recognition of room transitions and temporally separated activity cues. This design addresses aspects of sensor and environmental heterogeneity identified as important challenges in activity recognition, although its specific contribution requires confirmation through controlled ablation studies [27], [28]. Its AUC results were mixed: it achieved the highest or tied micro-AUC in 7 settings and the highest or tied macro-AUC in 4, but its mean micro-and macro-AUC were slightly lower than those of the global Transformer, indicating that gains in some settings did not translate into uniformly better discrimination across activity classes. Moreover, the LSTM achieved the highest accuracy in Home C at 30 minutes and in Home D at 60 minutes, showing that the simpler recurrent baseline remained competitive in some settings.

The comparison of 10-, 30-, and 60-minute windows highlights an interaction between temporal context, household routines, and model architecture [29] [30]. These associations represent a design rationale rather than fixed activity durations or demonstrated class-specific optima, consistent with evidence that different activities require different amounts of historical sensor context [29]. Window effects did not follow a consistent architectural pattern: the roomaware Transformer achieved its highest accuracy at 10 minutes in Homes C, D, and E, at 30 minutes in Home B, and at 60 minutes in Home A, whereas the LSTM achieved its highest accuracy at 30 minutes in Homes A, C, and E. In principle, self-attention can directly connect temporally separated observations without passing information through successive recurrent states [15], whereas gated recurrence emphasizes local continuity; however, the present results do not show that either mechanism systematically benefited from longer windows. Thus, the findings suggest that window length should be selected on validation data for each household and architecture, rather than a universal advantage of longer sequences, consistent with prior evidence that increasing window size does not necessarily improve recognition [30].

Clinical Implications

Shifting from model performance to downstream impact, accurate ADL classification could have implications for caregiver burden and care coordination across the ADRD continuum [31] [32]. As functional impairment progresses, caregivers experience increasing physical, psychological, social, and economic strain, with caregiver quality of life closely coupled to participant ADL dependence [31]. Anticipating ADL decline enables more timely allocation of supportive services, assistive technologies, and home-based interventions, aligning care resources with expected functional trajectories and potentially mitigating avoidable deterioration [33]. Our results offer only preliminary, model-development evidence for sensor-based activity recognition using deep learning techniques that incorporate room-specific information. Given the modest accuracy observed here, they should not be interpreted as evidence of clinical effectiveness.

Reliable classification of ADLs for aging population, regardless of cognitive diagnoses, could support independent living by transforming unobtrusive home-sensor data into longitudinal indicators of functional status and routine stability [34]. Changes in activities such as mobility, eating, medication use, hygiene, and sleep may reveal emerging functional decline before it becomes apparent during episodic clinical assessments [35] [36]. If validated, and when integrated with appropriate clinical review, ADL classification could enable timely, individualized interventions, support care planning, and help older adults remain safely at home while reducing reliance on continuous direct supervision [36].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the evaluation included only five homes with 9 to 20 annotated days per home, limiting generalizability to other residents, layouts, routines, and sensor configurations. The chronological day-level test design provides a realistic estimate of within-home performance on future days, but it is not external validation in unseen homes or participants. Second, activity labels were based on structured aligned to minute-level intervals. Despite staff review and follow-up clarification, retrospective reporting, uncertain activity boundaries, and incomplete logging may introduce label noise. Third, substantial class imbalance and the absence of some activities from individual test sets limited reliable estimation for infrequent categories. No Activity was particularly ambiguous because it could represent sedentary behavior or an unreported activity. Fourth, each home included a co-resident caregiver, and ambient sensors cannot attribute motion or environmental changes to a specific person; models therefore predicted the participant's logged activity from household-level signals, which may add noise when the participant and caregiver were active in different rooms. Fifth, results came from a single chronological split with 2 to 3 test days per home and a single random seed, without confidence intervals, so small between-model differences should be interpreted cautiously. Finally, cognitive status was not reassessed throughout the analyzed periods, so possible changes in neurocognitive status were not modeled.

Conclusions

This study supports the feasibility of privacy-preserving, participant-level ADL recognition from ambient sensors in homes of adults with mild NCD. The room-aware Transformer achieved favorable accuracy in selected home-window settings, particularly where spatial organization appeared informative, but it did not consistently exceed the global Transformer or LSTM across all metrics. Sleep/Rest was recognized most reliably, whereas infrequent and ambiguous activities remained challenging. The findings support further evaluation of spatially structured models using larger, more diverse samples, repeated uncertainty-aware experiments, and external validation in unseen homes. Clinical use will require evidence that reliable activity recognition translates into meaningful longitudinal assessment and decision support.
