Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Background By 2030, over one million people in the UK are projected to be living with dementia; however, the NHS estimates that only approximately 65% of individuals with dementia currently receive a formal diagnosis. Understanding the geographic and socioeconomic factors contributing to variation in diagnosis is essential for reducing inequalities in dementia care.

We developed a Bayesian spatial modelling framework to separate underlying dementia prevalence from case ascertainment using routinely collected public datasets at Local Authority District (LAD) level across England. Underlying prevalence was modelled using non-modifiable and modifiable risk factors identified by the Lancet Commission, while diagnosis rates were modelled as a function of healthcare provision and sociodemographic factors. Spatial random effects were incorporated to identify residual geographic variation after accounting for observed covariates. We additionally compared case ascertainment with regional variation in dementia subtype coding as a further measure of diagnostic quality.

Findings Substantial geographic variation in dementia diagnosis remained after accounting for differences in underlying disease prevalence. Urbanicity, deprivation, ethnicity, and healthcare provision were associated with variation in case ascertainment, with spatial effects explaining additional regional variation. Diagnostic specificity varied considerably between regions, with areas of lower case ascertainment tending to exhibit higher proportions of undifferentiated dementia diagnoses.

Interpretation By jointly modelling underlying dementia prevalence and case ascertainment, we can distinguish the factors associated with dementia burden from those associated with diagnosis. This enables the identification of areas with low case ascertainment, which when combined with poor diagnostic specificity, provides an evidence base for targeted regional interventions. Potential interventions include strengthening dementia care pathways through enhanced outreach, more equitable access to diagnostic assessment, and locally tailored models of care.

1 Introduction

Dementia prevalence is influenced by both non-modifiable and modifiable risk factors. The main nonmodifiable risk factors are age, sex [1] and genetic susceptibility [2], of which the most significant is age with the incidence of all-cause dementia increasing markedly in later life (reaching 18.2% per year among the oldest age groups) [3]. The most recent Lancet commission on dementia [4] identified a further 14 modifiable risk factors, defined as potentially reversible lifestyle factors. In early life this is: less education; in mid-life: hearing loss, high cholesterol, depression, traumatic brain injury, physical inactivity, diabetes, smoking, hypertension, obesity, excessive alcohol; and in later life: social isolation, air pollution and vision loss.

Dementia is an umbrella term encompassing over 200 different types with heterogeneous clinical presentation [5]. This variation, coupled with challenges in identifying individuals at an appropriate stage of disease, contributes to delayed or missed diagnoses. Consequently, a substantial gap exists between the underlying prevalence of dementia and the number of recorded diagnoses. Underdiagnosis has important implications for patient care as a primary care diagnosis is key to accessing the dementia care pathway, enabling referral to specialist memory assessment services and the development of an individualised care plan [6]. Furthermore, an accurate differential diagnosis is essential for appropriate clinical management, as dementia subtypes differ in their underlying pathology, clinical presentation, prognosis, and treatment requirements.

The discrepancy between underlying dementia prevalence and recorded diagnoses is determined by the rate of case ascertainment. Variation in case ascertainment reflects the influence of interacting social and healthcare determinants; said determinants do not operate through independent causal pathways, making it difficult to disentangle their individual contributions [7]. Socioeconomic deprivation represents an important upstream factor, influencing both healthcare access and the prevalence of dementia-related risk factors. However, its relationship with diagnosis rates is complex and contested. Some studies have reported higher diagnosis rates in more deprived areas, potentially reflecting increased general practice contact through the management of comorbid conditions [8], whereas others have identified lower diagnosis rates, attributed to persistent barriers in accessing healthcare [9, 10]. Lower diagnosis rates have also been associated with single-handed general practices [11], which are disproportionately located in rural and deprived areas [12].

Ethnicity represents a further axis of inequality in dementia diagnosis, with individuals from minoritised communities disproportionately affected by dementia [13], and less likely to receive a formal diagnosis [14]. This disparity is likely driven by multiple interacting mechanisms. First, there are broader, non-disease-specific barriers to healthcare access arising from structural and systemic racism. Dementia-specific barriers, including cultural stigma and differing understandings of cognitive decline, may further discourage, or delay, engagement with diagnostic services. Additionally, in some communities, greater reliance on family-and community-based care for older adults may reduce the perceived need to seek a formal diagnosis.

Accurate measurement of diagnosis rates is a longstanding policy concern [15, 16]. Historically the NHS has used financial incentive schemes to encourage GP practices to record dementia diagnoses more systematically, reflecting ongoing concern about under-coding relative to prescribing data. Strategies related to diagnosis, treatment and care are often made at the level of integrated care board (ICB), or sub ICB (SICB), which are the NHS governing bodies of multiple healthcare trusts. Issues with inequitable dementia diagnosis reflect broader concerns about healthcare disparities in the UK [17]. Identifying areas where dementia may be underdiagnosed could provide valuable evidence for informing NHS policy and targeting healthcare resources. Such insights may also support third-sector organisations in directing services more effectively, including the placement of community support services and regional care hubs.

Existing studies of dementia prevalence use NHS estimates [18, 19], which assume case ascertainment rates operate consistently at a given geographic scale. Current NHS and ONS estimates of regional diagnosis use a numerator of observed cases (general practice-level counts of recorded dementia diagnoses), and a denominator of expected prevalence [20] derived from an ageing cohort study adjusted only for age and sex in each region [21]. This approach does not account for potentially important sources of variation, including modifiable risk factors, confounding factors, or spatial effects. Consequently, estimates of expected dementia prevalence derived from this method may be subject to substantial uncertainty.

The increasing availability of large healthcare datasets has enabled the development of more sophisticated statistical approaches for modelling dementia diagnosis. Previous studies have investigated diagnostic gaps, including machine learning approaches to identify potentially undiagnosed individuals from primary care records [22], and data linkage methods to detect underdiagnosis within general practice records [23]. However, no study to date has modelled regional dementia diagnosis rates in England while accounting for the full range of modifiable risk factors.

To address this gap, we develop a multiplicative model of dementia diagnosis rates using openly available public datasets. We define underlying prevalence as the latent proportion of individuals living with dementia, including both diagnosed and undiagnosed cases. The model separates the contribution of underlying disease prevalence from that of healthcare access and quantifies residual geographic variation after accounting for both. We model spatial variation in both underlying dementia prevalence and diagnosis rates, enabling comparison between areas with differing expected diagnostic burden and healthcare capacity to provide differential diagnoses. This analysis complements recent work by Clark et al. [8], which provides a detailed characterisation of barriers to dementia diagnosis in England, and Giebel et al. [9], which examines geographic inequalities in dementia diagnosis and care. By integrating disease prevalence, healthcare access, modifiable risk factors, and differential diagnosis, this study provides a spatial assessment of potential inequalities in dementia diagnosis across England.

2 Method

We construct a Bayesian multiplicative model of dementia diagnoses jointly estimating two latent quantities: underlying prevalence which is modelled as a function of risk factors, and the case ascertainment rate which is modelled as a function of healthcare access and sociodemographic barriers. All measures are drawn from publicly available data, at the administrative geography of Local Authority Districts (LADs).

2.5 Model fitting

Each model was fitted using Hamiltonian Monte Carlo with the No-U-Turn Sampler (HMC-NUTS) implemented in Stan (via CmdStanPy, [28]), running 4 independent chains in parallel with 1,000 warm-up and 1,000 sampling iterations per chain, yielding 4,000 post-warm-up draws.

Multicollinearity among covariates was evaluated using variance inflation factors (VIF). Due to potential collinearity of variables we also fitted an unconstrained model with the 13 modifiable prevalence variables reduced using principal component analysis (PCA). This was used as a performance ceiling against which the sign-constrained model was evaluated via RMSE. Further details are available in the Supplementary Material.

3 Results

Across the 296 lower-tier local authorities (LADs) in England, the mean recorded dementia prevalence in March 2026 was 8.88 cases per 1,000 population. Prevalence ranged from 1.99 cases per 1,000 in the City of London to 15.95 cases per 1,000 in North Norfolk. As expected, recorded prevalence was strongly correlated with population age structure (𝑟 = 0.79).

3.1 Model performance

Table 2 summarises the performance of the candidate models. The sign-constrained model, in which coefficient directions were informed by prior evidence from the Lancet Commission, achieved a root mean squared error (RMSE) of 0.1199%. To assess the presence of residual spatial structure, two model extensions were considered. Incorporating ICB-level random intercepts produced a modest improvement in model fit (ΔRMSE = 0.0126%, ΔWAIC = 313.1). Extending the prevalence model with an ICAR spatial random effect resulted in the largest improvement (ΔRMSE = 0.1123%, ΔWAIC = 6910.4), indicating substantial residual spatial dependence beyond that explained by the observed covariates.

Given the high degree of collinearity among the explanatory variables, an additional model using principal component analysis (PCA) was evaluated. This yielded only a marginal improvement over the sign-constrained model, suggesting that the informative priors and sign constraints adequately stabilised coefficient estimation despite the presence of multicollinearity.

3.2 Sign-constrained model

In the sign-constrained model, all covariates included in the prevalence component were associated with statistically significant coefficients. Age had the largest estimated effect (𝛽 = 0.17), consistent with its established role as the strongest population-level risk factor for dementia. The second largest effect was observed for sex, with LADs containing a higher proportion of women exhibiting higher estimated prevalence after adjustment for the remaining covariates.

Among the modifiable risk factors, all variables except air pollution were positively associated with dementia prevalence. Air pollution was the only covariate with a negative coefficient, reflecting its inverse association with prevalence after adjustment for the remaining variables. This is likely attributable to its correlation with more densely populated urban areas, which generally have younger population structures.

The largest positive coefficients among the modifiable risk factors were observed for incident hearing loss (+0.045), alcohol-related mortality (+0.044), lower educational attainment (+0.021), relative to: depression, incident vision loss, social isolation, cardiovascular comorbidities, unhealthy lifestyle, and physical inactivity (all ≤ 0.001).

In the sign-constrained model all diagnosis-related covariates had posterior distributions that excluded zero. Population density had the largest positive coefficient (+0.7460). Greater socioeconomic deprivation (proxied by claimant count) and higher proportions of Asian and Black populations were associated with lower case ascertainment, whereas FTE GP availability had a positive, albeit comparatively small, association.

4 Discussion

This work jointly estimates underlying dementia prevalence and case ascertainment using routinely collected administrative data, with the goal of understanding regional variation in diagnosis. By distinguishing latent disease prevalence from case ascertainment, our approach enables a more nuanced analysis than previous studies, which have often used recorded diagnoses as a proxy for prevalence [18, 19]. This distinction helps disentangle regional variation in underlying disease prevalence from differences in regional access to diagnosis. By estimating posterior distributions for all model parameters, the framework quantifies the magnitude and uncertainty of each non-modifiable and modifiable risk factor, as well as factors affecting diagnosis access, enabling assessment of their relative importance. Through combined modelling with rates of unspecified diagnoses, we identify regions where targeted care resources could have the highest impact.

The underlying prevalence model identified age as the dominant predictor followed by sex according with the established literature on risk factors for dementia [4], and hence supporting the epidemiological validity of the modelling framework. Among modifiable risk factors, incident hearing loss showed the strongest association, consistent with evidence identifying hearing loss as both a major risk factor and a potential early marker of neurodegeneration [29, 30]. In contrast, air pollution showed an unexpected negative association with latent dementia prevalence, contrary to the literature [31, 32]. A possible explanation is the influence of spatial differences in case ascertainment, confounding the fact that air pollution is strongly correlated with urbanicity. Although our model accounted for population density, the use of aggregate LAD-level data may limit our ability to disentangle the effects of pollution on underlying disease burden from geographical variation in diagnosis. In particular, higher case ascertainment in urban areas may offset the group level increase in dementia burden associated with air pollution.

The model identified substantial inequalities in dementia case ascertainment, of which population density was the strongest predictor. Higher ascertainment in urban areas is consistent with established rural-urban disparities in access to healthcare [33, 34]. Socioeconomic deprivation was also consistently associated with lower ascertainment, reflecting the role of deprivation as an upstream determinant influencing both modifiable dementia risk factors across life course, and access to healthcare. This reinforces the need for dementia prevention and diagnostic strategies that address wider social determinants of health [17].

Ethnicity was significantly associated with case ascertainment, with areas containing larger Asian and Black populations exhibiting lower diagnosis rates. This finding is consistent with evidence of barriers to dementia diagnosis within minoritised communities, including stigma, differences in healthcare engagement, and greater reliance on informal care networks [14, 35]. These populations have historically been underserved [36], and require greater community engagement to improve access to diagnosis. The consistently larger negative coefficient for Asian ethnicity highlights the heterogeneity in diagnostic inequalities across ethnic minority groups, reinforcing the need for outreach and service provision to be tailored to the needs of specific communities rather than treating ethnic minorities as a homogeneous population [37].

Through the inclusion of the number of GPs, and the multilevel modelling of ICBs, we aimed to quantify the impact of NHS service provision on case ascertainment, to understand the 'postcode lottery of care'. Although a higher number of GPs, and therefore potentially greater availability of primary care, was positively associated with case ascertainment,the magnitude of this association was smaller than we had anticipated [11]. One possible explanation relates to the highly right-skewed distribution of GPs across LADs (see supplementary material), with a small number of areas having substantially higher per-capita GP workforce levels than others. It is plausible that these areas have already been targeted for enhanced healthcare investment, but that any downstream improvement in diagnosis rates has yet to be captured in administrative statistics, a lag that would be expected given the time required for workforce expansion to translate into clinical outcomes. In contrast to the relatively small coefficient for GP count, the multilevel model demonstrated that 34% of residual variation in case ascertainment was attributable to ICB membership. Given that ICBs commission local healthcare services and dementia pathways [6], this finding suggests that regional organisation and delivery of care represent important determinants of diagnostic equity.

The best-fitting model incorporated spatial correlation in underlying dementia prevalence between neighbouring areas, suggesting that regional variation in dementia rates may partly reflect unmeasured determinants of disease risk not fully captured by the model covariates. Further research is needed to identify these factors, which could include differences in particulate matter exposure not captured by the air pollution variable included in our model, which previous studies have linked to dementia [38, 39]. Access to more granular data would enable a more detailed investigation of geographic heterogeneity in both underlying prevalence, and diagnosis rates.

Beyond overall diagnostic coverage, the analysis identified substantial geographic variation in diagnostic specificity. A staggeringly large proportion of dementia diagnoses were recorded without a specified subtype, with marked differences observed between sub-ICBs. This accords with findings of high correlation between deprivation (measured using the index of multiple deprivation) and unspecified dementia diagnoses [10]. This is clinically significant, as dementia subtypes often differ in their underlying pathology, prognosis, and management [40], and accurate differential diagnosis is becoming increasingly important with the emergence of disease-specific therapies [41]. By combining estimates of low case ascertainment with rates of unspecified diagnoses, the model identifies regions where targeted improvements to diagnostic services could have the greatest impact, including Yorkshire, Warwickshire, Central East, Essex, and Kent. We interpret these findings in the context of a model that assumes case ascertainment remains below 100% in all areas, emphasising that improvements to diagnostic services are needed nationally.

Several limitations should be considered. Firstly, although the model incorporates all modifiable risk factors identified by the Lancet Commission [27], their interpretation at the area level requires caution. Aggregate LAD-level measures capture population distributions of risk rather than individual exposure histories and therefore cannot establish individual causal pathways [42]. Furthermore, prevalence and case ascertainment are latent quantities, jointly estimated from the same observed diagnosis data, meaning that estimates of 𝛼 and 𝛽 in the model should be interpreted as geographic patterns rather than definitive causal effects. Secondly, there is the caveat of data availability and quality; several variables were represented using proxy measures, which may introduce unmodelled variation as they do not fully capture the underlying risk factors. Since the analysis relied on public data, there is also uncertainty from differences in data collection methods and temporal alignment between sources.

An important limitation is the need to contextualise diagnosis rates within broader debates about what constitutes a 'good' dementia diagnosis. An ideal diagnosis should be both timely and accurate [6]. However, because dementia symptoms may emerge years after the underlying biological processes begin, there is an inevitable lag between disease onset and diagnosis, as is the case for many long-term conditions [43]. Consequently, a snapshot of recorded diagnoses cannot fully capture the temporal dimensions of diagnostic access, including whether individuals are diagnosed promptly following symptom onset. More granular longitudinal data would enable future research to examine these temporal dynamics and provide a more nuanced understanding of variation in quality of dementia diagnosis across areas.

4.1 Further Work

Beyond the risk factors identified by the Lancet commission, there could be further characteristics affecting underlying disease prevalence. This analysis captures the most commonly reported barriers to diagnosis; however, a recent report by Clark et al. [8] identifies disability and sexuality as additional factors associated with inequitable access to diagnosis. The inclusion of these variables could be explored in future work.

Beyond diagnosis rates, further geographic analysis could examine inequalities in access to post-diagnostic care and support. The quality of dementia care is shaped in part by the availability of, and access to, health, social care and third-sector services [6]. Although our model accounts for variation in GP provision and population density, it does not directly capture geographical accessibility to healthcare providers or downstream support services. This highlights two important avenues for further research. First, spatial analysis could examine how easily people can physically access diagnostic and post-diagnostic services, accounting for factors such as travel distances and service availability [44]. Second, research could investigate the relationship between case ascertainment and access to post-diagnostic care, including health and social care services offered through charities and community groups. Together, these analyses could help establish whether geographical inequalities in diagnosis are accompanied by additional inequalities in access to ongoing care and support, and identify where these intersecting gaps are greatest. This motivates the development of a systematic atlas of dementia care service availability in England, which would allow the geographic patterns identified here to be linked to care access outcomes. This would allow modelling of both availability and accessibility using gravity models [45] and enable more targeted guidance on the location of care services relevant to forthcoming NHS transformations in health and social care service delivery [46].

5 Conclusion

This study presents a scalable Bayesian framework that separates underlying dementia prevalence from case ascertainment, providing a more nuanced understanding of geographic inequalities in diagnosis than analyses based on recorded diagnosis rates alone. Our findings demonstrate substantial geographic variation in dementia diagnosis across England, persisting after accounting for non-modifiable and modifiable risk factors. By identifying areas with low case ascertainment and poor diagnostic specificity, the framework provides an evidence base for more targeted and equitable dementia policy and service planning. The substantial contribution of ICB-level variation further highlights the importance of locally commissioned healthcare pathways in addressing diagnostic inequalities. Ultimately, improving equity in dementia care requires not only increasing diagnosis rates, but also ensuring timely and accurate differential diagnosis to facilitate personalised care and appropriate treatment pathways.
