Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Introduction

Neurological conditions in low-and middle-income countries (LMICs) have become an increasingly significant public health burden. Conditions, such as stroke, dementia, central nervous system infections, cancer, and epilepsy, impose a disproportionate burden in this region, highlighting the need for a greater focus on expanding neurological services (1, 2).

Neuroimaging technologies, such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and computed tomography (CT), play significant roles in the prevention, diagnosis, and interventions of neurological health challenges through characterising (abnormal) structural, functional, and metabolic brain patterns (3, 4). Yet, access to such advanced imaging technologies in LMICs is very limited and remains a key barrier to global brain health equity.

Operating advanced neuroimaging technologies like MRI requires an extensive and consistent power supply, trained personnel, and dedicated infrastructure, including a radiofrequency-shielded room (5, 6). However, such health system infrastructures are limited in low-resource settings, affecting the availability and accessibility of MRI and CT (7, 8).

Moreover, the acquisition of such diagnostic technologies is expensive. For example, a conventional MRI scanner costs between one and half million and four million dollars with ongoing additional expenses for maintenance and hardware replacements (9) (10) (11).

Furthermore, the immobility of MRI scanners can limit accessibility, as the magnet alone weighs between 3000 and 6000 kilograms (10, 12).

To address the challenges of affordability, accessibility, and complex infrastructural requirements associated with conventional diagnostic technologies, a new generation of portable Ultra-Low-Field MRI (ULF-MRI) systems (<0.1T compared to 1.5T or 3T for highfield MRI (HF-MRI)) is now emerging as a compelling alternative. These scanners offer the potential to bring neuroimaging procedures to the point-of-care in emergency units, intensive care units (ICU), and remote hospitals (7) through bypassing the logistical and economic constraints of conventional 1.5 or 3T systems. This is particularly relevant for LMICs where neurological conditions are on the rise due to a high rate of infectious diseases, malnutrition, and other socio-economic factors and within the context of a broader neuroimaging ecosystem gap (13, 14).

Portable ULF-MRI systems differ significantly from HF-MRI devices. They rely, for example, on the use of permanent magnets instead of high-powered superconducting electromagnets, and, as such, have significantly reduced costs associated to power, cooling, and maintenance. This leads to ULF-MRI systems to be smaller, more mobile, and with substantially reduced need for stringent safety measures and electromagnetic shielding.

Additionally, anyone can operate a ULF-MRI scanner after only brief training and local biomedical engineers can perform basic maintenance after minimal preventative training (15, 16). Furthermore, health facilities do not need to change their infrastructure to integrate this technology into their neuroimaging workflow. For example, ULF-MRI systems do not need to be connected to a special power grid, as they can run from a normal wall outlet.

Portable ULF-MRI can acquire key imaging sequences including, T1-weighted (T1W), T2-weighted (T2W), fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), and diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) sequences. These have been used in several studies that reported the clinical applications of ULF-MRI in diverse disease conditions such as stroke (8, 16), multiple sclerosis (17, 18), dementia (19), demyelinating diseases (20), and epilepsy (21, 22). ULF-MRI has also proven effective as a prognostic tool for evaluating optic nerve decompression after endoscopic endonasal surgery (23) and for detecting hypoxic ischemic brain injury (HIBI) after cardiac arrest (24). Beyond its clinical applications, ULF-MRI also expands the frontiers of neuroscience and/or brain health research through reaching underrepresented populations including communities in low-resource settings, paediatrics, and critically ill patients.

In view of the transformational potential of ULF-MRI, it is essential to note some of the technical limitations of this system. For example, studies have mentioned that ULF-MRI has a low signal-to noise ratio (SNR) compared to HF-MRI due to its lower magnetic field. This compromises the quality of images because they are of lower resolution, which could lead to clinicians missing subtle diagnostic details and brain lesions (3, 4, 25). Coarse spatial resolution (~2-3 mm), long scan times, and lack of tissue contrast are also issues that need to be addressed (1). However, advancements in hardware (such as improved magnet and coil designs) and software (e.g. deep learning reconstruction and super-resolution of images) may help address some of these limitations (17, 26).

Researchers and advocacy groups have suggested frameworks to help pave the way for making MRI accessible and affordable in low resource settings, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) (1, 27, 28). One of the recommendations from these frameworks is to accelerate This study therefore evaluates the implementation of ULF-MRI in the Ethiopian healthcare system through a qualitative case study and provides insight into its feasibility and integration within routine clinical practice. Finally, linking diagnostic technologies with health system infrastructure in Africa is essential to develop viable and innovative solutions for strengthening neuroimaging capacity in the region. Accordingly, this study analyses the existing infrastructure, including the healthcare workforce and electrification of healthcare centres, both of which are essential prerequisite for the delivery of advanced imaging services.

Methods and materials

Study design

This study utilized a multi-method research design, combining descriptive quantitative analysis of diagnostic imaging availability and health system infrastructure in Africa with an embedded case study examining the integration of ULF-MRI in a lowresource healthcare setting. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from King's College London (LRS/RGO-24/25-47006).

Material

The Hyperfine Swoop® system is a highly mobile (motorised), cart-based MRI head imager, designed to roll through standard doorways and elevators and to park at the bedside in wards, and intensive care units (Figure 1). Its compact, self-shielded design allows safe deployment in routine clinical spaces without a built-in MRI suite. The scanner's head coil is made of a solid, clear plastic designed to keep the subject's head stable during the scan and can be easily disinfected between subjects. It also contains transmitters and receivers to capture signals to generate the images. Padding is usually placed either side of the head to keep the subjects head still during scanning and minimise motion. The coil is surrounded by a perforated scanning cage with multiple access doors that allow for positioning (Rowand et al., 2025). The system is operated via an iPad-based interface (29). Crucially, it is built around a permanent magnet (0.064 Tesla), avoiding the superconducting electromagnets and cryogenic cooling required by conventional MRI scanners (see Table 1 for more specifications). The mobility of the scanner is powered by an internal battery that is charged while being plugged in via the power cord. The scanner used in the case study was deployed in Ethiopia in 2022, initially for research purpose, and over 300 infants' brain were scanned over a two-year period.

Study setting and participants

The case study component of this research was conducted at Felege Hiwot comprehensive specialized hospital in northwestern Ethiopia, which is one of the main hospitals in Bahir Dar city, located in Amhara Regional State. Felege Hiwot Hospital primarily offers imaging services using CT and X-ray modalities and has recently added the Hyperfine Swoop® portable MRI system to its imaging services. It is the first hospital in Ethiopia to deploy the ULF-MRI for clinical use. However, the hospital does not have a highfield MRI system. The study participants included radiologists, radiology technologists, biomedical engineers, and nurses working at the hospital. They were selected purposively based on their direct experience with and involvement in the implementation of ULF-MRI.

Data collection procedures and analysis

Quantitative data on MRI and CT scanners, healthcare infrastructure indicators (e.g. healthcare facility access to electricity), and health workforce (e.g. physician and biomedical engineers) were collected from the World Health Organization Global Health Observatory database (https://www.who.int/data/gho).These data include the number and density of MRI and CT per million population (full report is available for only 38 African countries), access to electricity in healthcare facilities, and health workforce density per population. The WHO baseline survey was conducted in 2010, with subsequent updates in 2022. WHO initially planned periodic updates (every five years) of the global atlas of medical devices but shifted from fixed reference year surveys to compilation of the latest available country information.

The 2022 data then mean the most recent reported data by each country between 2013 and 2021. Additionally, we used the latest dataset (2022) to get data from the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) countries for comparison purposes.

The qualitative data were collected using a semi-structured interview guide. The interviews focused on healthcare professionals experiences operating the ULF-MRI, the challenges encountered during its implementation, and their perspectives on image quality, technology usability, and workflow integration. In addition, the researcher conducted observations at the hospital, with a particular focus on the infrastructure requirements, the feasibility, and usability of ULF-MRI in a real clinical setting. Two site visits were conducted on May 1, 2025, and August 15, 2025. During these visits, the process of integrating the portable MRI system into the existing imaging infrastructure was observed. The quantitative data were analysed using descriptive statistics such as medians and percentages, in R statistical software. Thematic analysis was used to analyse the qualitative data.

Results

Density of MRI and CT

The results showed that the density of MRI and CT scanners in Africa is low. In 2022, the median MRI density across 38 African countries was 0.115 MRI scanner per million population (interquartile range [IQR]: 0.00-0.28). In addition, the median CT scanner density was 0.42 (IQR: 0.20-1.15) per million population. Only three of the 38 countries reported an MRI scanner density greater than one per million population, while eight countries reported a CT scanner density greater than one scanner per million population (see Table 2). The analysis also showed that, in 2022, at least 12 African countries had no MRI scanners, and three countries had neither MRI nor CT scanners.

Trend analysis of MRI and CT density

Using the 2010 baseline and the latest MRI and CT scanners data in 38 African countries (see Table 2), a trend analysis was conducted to determine whether there have been improvements in the availability of these technologies. The analysis revealed that there was modest growth in MRI units between 2010 and 2022, where it increased from a combined total of 70 to 105 units, representing an increase of approximately 50%. This growth translates into an increase of the median density of MRI scanners from 0.05 per million population to 0.12 per million population. Similarly, a small increase in median CT density per million population was observed from 2010-2020. The median density rose from 0.37 in 2010 to 0.42 per million population in 2020 (see Figure 2). Out of the 15 countries that reported no MRI scanners unit in 2010, 12 of them continued to have no MRI scanners in 2022.

The modest increase in MRI and CT may indicate the gradual expansion of neuroimaging services. However, MRI and CT availability remains extremely low given the population size of Africa and compared with those OECD countries. It is also important to note that most changes were coming from a few countries that added more MRI and CT scanners including Ethiopia, Ghana, and Uganda, while no changes in the absolute number of MRI and CT units were observed in more than two-thirds of the countries.

Health system infrastructure

This study reveals that there is inadequate health system infrastructure in Africa, particularly in indicators such as access to stable electricity and trained health workforce that are imperative to operate advanced diagnostic technologies like MRI and CT. For example, the median density of physicians per 10,000 population was only 1.9 across the African countries, while the median density for OECD countries was 38 physicians per 10,000 population (see Table 3). Similarly, most African countries have very limited number of biomedical engineers. For example, among countries with available data, most reported fewer than 0.05 biomedical engineers per 10,000 population. In contrast, OECD countries had a median density of approximately 0.41 biomedical engineers per 10,000 population, indicating greater availability of technical personnel to support advanced diagnostic technologies. In this study, we also analysed access to electricity in healthcare facilities for over a dozen African countries for which data were available (see Table 4). The results show that in some countries, nearly half of the healthcare facilities do not have access to electricity. For example, about 50%, 39%, 38%, and 31% of healthcare facilities in the Central African Republic, Niger, Chad, and Sierra Leone, respectively, lack access to electricity altogether.

When reliability was considered, only 63%, 54%, and 48% of healthcare facilities in Tanzania, Kenya, and Zimbabwe, respectively had reliable access to electricity. This highlights a major infrastructural barrier to the deployment and operation of advanced neuroimaging technologies like MRI that has high power demand and require consistent power supply.

Case study results

Five experts, including radiologists (n =2), a radiology technologist (n =1), a biomedical engineer (n =1), and a nurse (n =1) with experience using or supporting the ULF-MRI were interviewed at Felege Hiwot Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (FCSH).

Participants were recruited between March 15, 2026 and April 1, 2026, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants before the interviews.

The researcher also conducted on-site observations at the hospital on the resources needed for -and available to -operate the ULF-MRI, the use of the technology, and the clinical workflow processes. The hospital's radiology department operates diagnostic technologies such as CT and EEG; however, it does not have a high-field MRI system.

The transition from research into clinical practice

The portable ULF-MRI system was tested and validated for approximately two years at Felege Hiwot Hospital in Ethiopia. During the two-year research phase, the technology was used to assess infants' brain development, and more than 300 brain scans were conducted. After two years of research, the Hyperfine Swoop® 0.064T MRI system was transitioned into clinical practice, where experts could use it for clinical decision-making.

During the transition of the portable ULF-MRI into clinical practice, several activities, including preventative training, preparation of rooms, and maintenance of the MRI had to be carried out. The MRI engineer of the UNITY project led these activities and the overall transition process. For example, the scanner was operated using its default software version for the duration of the validation research, and it was necessary to update it to the latest software version to benefit from better image quality. Updating the software required up to four hours of uninterrupted power. One of the challenges was frequent power outages at the hospital, which, if it happened during an upgrade, could lead to the malfunction of the ULF-MRI motherboard, as had been reported at other research sites in Pakistan and Bangladesh. If this occurs, the motherboard must be shipped to the US Hyperfine centre for repair. For this reason, a diesel generator had to operate continuously for four hours until the software updating process was completed, resulting in additional fuel costs for the hospital and additional environmental impacts.

Secondly, a group of radiology technologists, biomedical engineers, and a radiologist received three days of in-person preventive maintenance training from the visiting MRI engineer involved in the UNITY project. The purpose of the training was to build local maintenance capacity, enhance troubleshooting skills, and prevent misuse and damage to the machine. The biomedical engineers mentioned that it was their first exposure to an MRI system, but they felt at ease and confident in disassembling and reassembling parts of the ULF-MRI with the guidance of the MRI engineer. One of the biomedical engineers stated: "I have never worked with an MRI machine before. However, I believe that with a bit more training and practice, we will likely be able to perform basic maintenance and replace parts by ourselves. This hands-on training has given me the confidence and foundational knowledge necessary to further enhance my skills as I continue working with the technology."

Although the training plan was intended to involve more participants, including physicians, nurses, and other professionals, only a limited number of radiologists and other professionals participated. This was because the hospital is one of the busiest health facilities in the region, dealing with thousands of patients per day, with a limited number of healthcare professionals available. For example, the hospital only has three senior radiologists, and only one of them was able to complete the entire training program due to their demanding work schedules.

Telephone and electrical lines in the room were also removed to avoid potential electromagnetic interference of the imaging process. Finally, radiologists at Felege Hiwot hospital and experts at UNITY together developed an imaging protocol focusing on core MRI sequences like T1-weighted (T1W), T2-weighted (T2W), Fluid-attenuated Inversion Recovery (FLAIR), and Diffusion-Weighted Imaging (DWI). After that, the ULF-MRI was then ready for the clinical brain imaging of both infants and adults. Phantom scanning was performed for quality assurance to calibrate and ensure image quality. They were also recommended to conduct calibration scan regularly.

Clinical application and acceptability

After completing the training, maintenance, and quality assurance processes, the radiology department began scanning patients who voluntarily agreed to undergo brain imaging for various reasons. Technologists and radiologists who had previously been involved in the two-year research project noted a significant improvement in image quality due to the new software update and the removal of electromagnetic interference in the scanning room.

Reflecting on the image quality one radiologist stated: "I have observed that the image quality has improved with the new software.

These images are diagnostically adequate and even have better quality compared to the low-field MRI images patients bring from private clinics."

Infrastructure and physical environment needed

During the researcher's observations of the scanning process, frequent power outages disrupted the scanning process, and scans had to be repeated several times. The hospital did not have an Uninterruptible Power Supply (UPS) to provide backup power for the ULF-MRI or for most of the other equipment in the radiology department and other hospital units. We observed that several dozen images were incomplete or of limited diagnostic value due to power outages and fluctuations during the scanning process. Rather than only coming for a short visit, patients had to stay for hours to repeat the scan as a result. A nurse who was involved in scanning infants mentioned that keeping parents waiting for hours until the power returned was extremely challenging, making it difficult to achieve daily scanning targets. Our study participants recommended either a built-in battery backup for at least 30 minutes or a separate UPS to address the frequent power interruptions and fluctuations.

The Hyperfine Swoop® ULF-MRI requires a stable internet connection to transfer images from the iPad-controlled console to a computer where radiologists interpret the image. The hospital has a dedicated high-speed broadband connection specifically for the ULF-MRI room. However, due to the popularity of this area's fast internet connection, many hospital staff are also connected to this line, causing the Wi-Fi to be slower for image transfers.

The hospital has allocated a dedicated room with adequate ventilation and the conditions necessary for the safe use of the ULF-MRI scanner. However, electromagnetic interference and noise from the home phone and electrical cables in the room was affecting image quality, requiring the relocation of these cables. The system's power requirement was compatible with the existing electrical infrastructure of the hospital and worked perfectly with the standard wall outlet.

The researcher observed that most of the hospital's pathways, corridors, and room entrances are not suitable for moving the scanner from one room to another. Uneven pathways, non-level flooring, and the location of some clinical wards, such as the pediatric ward, on the first floor without elevator access made it difficult to take advantage of the portability feature of the ULF-MRI.

As part of the maintenance and inspection activities conducted during the transition process, the MRI engineer disassembled the ULF-MRI. We found earplugs and rat droppings deep inside the machine, as well as several electrical cables that had been severed by rats and required repair. In addition, regular cleaning of both the room and the machine itself is necessary to address dust issues. Proper cleaning procedures are necessary to ensure the optimal functioning of the equipment, and cleaners must be trained in the correct use of relevant cleaning tools, processes, and materials. At times, cleaners mistakenly used water to clean areas that only need alcohol-based cleaning, although no damage or malfunction has been reported as a result. Windows may also need to have insulation film or protection film to block dust and wind coming through the window frame gaps and cracks.

Capacity building

As part of the integration of ULF-MRI into the clinical practice of Felege Hiwot Hospital, a brief training session was provided to a small group of participants. However, the participants expressed interest in more advanced training to enhance their technical skills, including integrating AI to improve image quality, and in learning more about the hardware and software details of the ULF-MRI. They believed that these skills' development could provide independence and create a sustainable imaging ecosystem by enabling local professionals to actively participate in the development and innovation of the technology.

One example cited by the radiologists concerned the new CT scanner the hospital had purchased and its maintenance contract with the vendor. In just a year, the CT scanner required high-level maintenance on more than three occasions. It took months to dispatch maintenance technicians from the vendor in Europe, leaving patients and the hospital without CT imaging services for a total of five months. Experts expressed similar concerns that they may still depend heavily on the ULF-MRI vendor if the scanner requires high-level maintenance. They argued that advanced training programs could reduce such dependency and enhance the long-term sustainability of the technology.

Human resources and workflow integration

Felege Hiwot hospital has three radiologists and seven radiology technicians serving an extensive catchment area estimated to be more than five million people. Given the number of people this hospital is serving, having only three radiologist consultants could be considered as severe understaffing.

One of the reported advantages of ULF-MRI is its ease of use and reduced reliance on specialized personnel, who are often in short supply in many African countries. During the research and validation phase of the ULF-MRI, nurses operated the scanner, including performing the entire scanning procedures of over 300 infants, with some occasional support from radiology technicians.

The scanner was then transferred to the hospital's radiology department, where radiology technicians are needed to operate it. Although the intention is for this innovation to be portable within the hospital's emergency rooms, intensive care units (ICU), and paediatric wards (considering the difficulties mentioned above relating to moving the scanner), it is currently placed in the radiology department. Task shifting from radiologists to other physicians and healthcare professionals is not occurring at present. This may occur at a later stage as the adoption process matures and more professionals outside the radiology department become familiar with the technology. However, radiologists also expressed concerns about relinquishing imaging responsibilities and professional authority to nonradiologists.

One of the radiologists stated:

In my opinion, everyone can operate this technology in our hospital. However, interpreting the images should exclusively remain for radiologists, otherwise our training in this discipline may no longer be necessary quite soon, as innovations in imaging including AI applications are threatening our roles."

While some radiologists expressed concerns regarding imaging responsibilities and scope of practice beyond the radiology department, the existing workload suggests that task sharing may become necessary for the efficient use of the technology. This is especially important when considering that the three radiologists and seven radiology technicians were performing hundreds of scans per month using the hospital's existing imaging systems. The adoption of the ULF-MRI into clinical practice would likely increase the workload and may require hiring more radiology staff unless tasks are shared with other physicians.

We also observed that ULF-MRI is not yet well integrated into the hospital's imaging workflow. Many hospital departments still had little awareness of this technology. Besides, as mentioned above, some physicians still had concerns about the quality of images the ULF-MRI produces, and they referred patients to private clinics in the city for scans because there is no conventional MRI in the hospital. Three months after the integration of ULF-MRI, there were still no established patient referral pathways or MRI scan requests from other departments.

Discussion

This study found significant inequities in access to (advanced) neuroimaging services across Africa, driven by not only by the lack of MRI and CT scanners but also by broader weakness of the health system infrastructure. The findings further suggest that portable ULF-MRI may provide a feasible approach to expanding neuroimaging availability in lowresource settings.

As illustrated in this study, the availability of MRI and CT scanners is extremely limited in Africa. Thirty-eight African countries have a combined total of approximately 100 MRI units, compared with 33,000 MRI units across 38 OECD countries. Among the available scanners in Africa, about 39% of them are obsolete but remain in use to meet clinical needs (27). These stark disparities, coupled with ongoing epidemiological and demographic transitions and the growing burden of neurological diseases in Africa (13), are likely to increase the risk of delayed, missed, or incorrect diagnoses of neurological conditions, ultimately adversely affecting health outcomes.

The evidence from this study indicates that the current access gap may not be addressed by simply increasing the number of conventional scanners, as several interconnected constraints remain. Operating these advanced technologies requires multidisciplinary specialized personnel, extensive infrastructure including magnetic and radiofrequency shielding, and reliable high-capacity power supply (12, 16, 30). However, these resources are in short supply. For example, Malawi and Uganda each have fewer than 10 consultant radiologists, serving populations of 21 and 50 million people, respectively, compared with approximately 5,000 consultant radiologists for 70 million people in the United Kingdom (30, 31).

In addition, this study indicated that many healthcare facilities in African countries lack reliable access to electricity. In some countries, such as Central Africa, Chad, Niger, and Sierra Leone, more than one-third of healthcare facilities do not have access to electricity. In line with this, a study in Africa reported that about 57% of MRI facilities experienced downtime at least one to two times per week because of power-related issues (27). These findings suggest that this broader health system weaknesses requires innovative, contextspecific, flexible, and affordable solutions.

Given these constraints, portable ULF-MRI systems may help address some accessibility challenges because they are mobile, less expensive, infrastructure-light, and require substantially less specialised expertise (7, 30). Our case study of the recent integration of this system into clinical practice in an Ethiopian hospital demonstrated a promising approach in the delivery of neuroimaging in resource-limited settings.

In addition, to reduce the workload of available radiologists, more extensive training should be provided to non-radiology healthcare practitioners on the nature and characteristics of ULF-MRI images, scanner operations, and safe and effective patient imaging procedures.

This would support task shifting, particularly in operating the scanner. Other researchers have also suggested that personnel shortages could be addressed through built-in automated abnormality detection methods, including robust internet connectivity in the design of ULF-MRI to enable tele-radiology support from remote radiologists and also remote connection from technicians (30).

In addition to technical and infrastructural considerations, perception and acceptance of the technology by healthcare professionals are imperative for the successful integration of ULF MRI. A lower magnetic field does not necessarily imply reduced clinical utility.

However, some healthcare professionals have such perceptions. The key consideration is whether the images from ULF-MRI have sufficient quality for accurate diagnosis even in a reduced contrast context (1). In line with this, ULF-MRI demonstrated its clinical efficacy in diagnosing various types of neurological conditions including ischemic and haemorrhage stroke, epilepsy, hydrocephalus, multiple sclerosis, and multifocal leukoencephalopathy (16, 17, 21, 35, 36). Hospital-wide sensitization workshops and knowledge sharing activities among experts and users may help improve perceptions and address potential misconceptions.

Despite the benefits of ULF-MRI and its promise of improving accessibility, affordability, and flexibility in brain imaging, it still has important technical limitations worth acknowledging. This imaging system has a high degree of noise and artefacts in images, low spatial resolution, and poor signal-to-noise ratio and contrast, which may affect image quality and interpretation (4, 12, 37, 38). Hence, improving image quality using different mechanisms, such as super-resolution technique, the application of artificial intelligence, and deep learning-based reconstruction of images (26, 39, 40) should be integral to its adoption in standard clinical practice. Furthermore, ULF-MRI is still an evolving technology and needs further validation and testing for many neurological diseases. This limits its current ability to fully replace the roles of HF-MRI in brain imaging, even if ULF-MRI is accessible and affordable.

These findings suggest that efforts to expand neuroimaging services in Africa require not only the availability of diagnostic technologies but also improving the broader healthcare system. Health policymakers and decision-makers may need to invest in workforce development through training and capacity building, as well as in essential infrastructure such as reliable power supply, to sustainably expand access to neuroimaging services. Portable ULF-MRI may complement these efforts by enabling neuroimaging with limited resources and reducing some of the infrastructural barriers often associated with conventional neuroimaging technologies.

Limitations and future directions

The strength of this study lies in its approach to understand the neuroimaging ecosystem in Africa from a holistic perspective by quantifying availability of MRI and CT scanners, analysing health system challenges, and examining the implementation of portable ULF-MRI into clinical practice in a low-resource setting. However, this study has a few limitations worth noting. The first limitation is the relatively short period between clinical implementation of ULF-MRI and the start of data collection for this study, which was only five months. This short time frame may not provide a complete picture of the extent of utilization, staff opinions, and workflow changes. Future evaluations with a longer follow-up period could provide better insight as the implementation matures.

Secondly, this study was conducted in a regional comprehensive specialized hospital that has a radiology set-up equipped with X-Rays and CT scans. Further implementation studies in other healthcare setting levels including in rural healthcare centres without radiological set-up and tertiary hospitals that already have MRI suites would further generate evidence of integration in diverse clinical setting.

Finally, as health systems vary across countries, similar studies conducted in multiple settings would enhance understanding of contextual, organizational, and systemic-level barriers and facilitators influencing the adoption of ULF-MRI.

Future studies should examine the integration and implementation of portable ULF-MRI in multi-county settings across different health system tiers. It is also important to conduct economic evaluation or cost effectiveness analysis, which will provide evidence for decision makers for wider clinical adoption. In addition, further validation of the technology across a wider range of disease conditions and continued technological improvements to address image quality issues are essential.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study showed that the availability of advanced neuroimaging technologies, such as MRI and CT, in Africa remains highly constrained. This limited availability is not only driven by shortage of scanners but also by broader weaknesses in the neuroimaging ecosystem, including a shortage of trained personnel, inadequate health system infrastructure, and affordability issues. ULF-MRI, such as the recently developed 0.064T Hyperfine Swoop® portable system, has the potential to address some of these systemic challenges. The integration of this technology into clinical practice in a hospital in Ethiopia demonstrated the feasibility of deploying ULF-MRI in low-resource settings with limited infrastructural requirements. Capacity building of local experts, particularly in core engineering and technical aspects of the technology, could foster sustainability and selfreliance in maintenance, operation, and further innovation. Further improvements in the technical capacity of the technology, especially in image quality, could enhance its acceptability and scalability. These findings suggest that portable ULF-MRI is a potential pathway to addressing inequalities and expanding neuroimaging access in resource constrained settings.

The integration process only required brief hands-on training for the existing staff, and nurses were able to perform scans, thereby reducing dependence on scarce specialists. In addition, the hospital did not require infrastructural modifications, the installation of special radiofrequency shielded room, or a dedicated power supply, as the ULF-MRI integrated well with the existing systems. Similar findings regarding ease of operation, reduced infrastructure requirements, and cost saving have been reported in studies from high and middle income countries (5, 12, 16, 32, 33). These characteristics may even enable decentralization of neuroimaging services beyond main hospitals and facilitate access to diagnostic care in semiurban and rural areas.

Although ULF-MRI overcomes many of the challenges related to conventional MRI and CT, it is observed that the broader health system constraints and many of the infrastructural and operational challenges discussed earlier re-emerged during the clinical integration process in Ethiopia. For example, in Felege Hiwot Hospital, prolonged power outages lasting even several days, as well as frequent short blackouts, are common. These disruptions may affect scan acquisition and potentially damage the MRI scanner itself. The ULF-MRI should therefore incorporate power backup solutions to protect against power outages, surges, and voltage fluctuations. A recent deployment of solar powered ULF-MRI in a rural maternal healthcare centre in Zimbabwe demonstrates the potential alternative and sustainable solution to overcoming the power issues (34).

The integration process at Felege Hiwot Hospital included a brief training on operating the technology and scanning procedures. However, capacity building beyond scanning, particularly in the core engineering and technical aspects of ULF-MRI is essential.

Developing and empowering local experts in these core engineering and technical skills will foster sustainability and self-reliance in maintenance, operation, and further innovation (1). This could help healthcare facilities avoid long-term proprietary maintenance contract models.
