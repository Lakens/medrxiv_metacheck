To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 8 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Elevated blood pressure (BP) in childhood is associated with cardiovascular risk, but its molecular lipid correlates remain poorly characterized. We investigated the association of plasma lipid species and patterns with concurrent and longitudinal BP phenotypes in school-aged children.

Methods: In the Belgian ENVIRONAGE prospective birth-cohort, 195 children aged 9 to 12 years were examined between July 2021 and September 2024. A total of 1,232 plasma lipid species were quantified using liquid chromatography-electrospray ionization-tandem mass spectrometry. Associations with systolic and diastolic BP percentiles were examined using multivariable linear regression with permutationbased family-wise error rate (FWER) correction. Lipid-set enrichment analysis, with P values from sample permutation to account for correlation among lipid species, evaluated coordinated associations. Significant findings were examined across longitudinal BP patterns in 177 children with BP measured at ages 4-6 and 9-12.

Findings: Each 1-SD increase in triglyceride (TG) 58:9 fatty acyl (FA) 22:4 was associated with 7.36 higher systolic BP percentile points (95% CI=3.91-10.80; 𝑃 FWER =0.03). TG 58:9 FA 22:4 was associated with incident (OR=2.78; 95% CI=1.33-5.79) and persistent (OR=2.14; 95% CI=1.22-3.77), but not resolved elevated BP vs stable-normal BP. Among lipid sets, species with a total carbon number of 38, mainly PC and PE, were inversely enriched with diastolic BP percentile.

Interpretation: Childhood BP was associated with higher levels of a highly polyunsaturated long-chain TG, closely related to current BP elevation and not elevated among children whose BP had normalized.

Introduction

Elevated blood pressure (BP) is a major modifiable contributor to cardiovascular disease worldwide [1] and sustained hypertension already affects about 4% of children and adolescents globally [2]. Childhood BP is associated with subclinical cardiovascular alterations [3], tracks into adulthood [4], and is prospectively associated with cardiovascular events later in life [5, 6]. Its relevance may depend on its course: within childhood, incident and persistent elevated BP were associated with greater left ventricular mass, whereas elevated BP that resolved was not [7]. Identifying molecular signatures of BP and its longitudinal patterns during childhood could help characterize early biological pathways underlying BP regulation [8].

Elevated BP frequently co-occurs with dyslipidemia [9]. Lipidomics, which quantifies hundreds to thousands of individual lipid species, can resolve signals that standard lipid measures cannot. In adults, diacylglycerols (DGs), triacylglycerols (TGs), phosphatidylcholines (PCs), and phosphatidylethanolamines (PEs) have been associated with BP and hypertension, cross-sectionally and longitudinally, although the species and direction of association vary between populations [10] [11] [12] [13] [14] [15] [16]. Pediatric lipidomic-BP studies have implicated glycerophospholipids, sphingolipids and TGs but have largely focused on selected populations, including children with overweight or obesity [17], or on specific dietary [18, 19], environmental [20], or ethnic contexts [21].

Although pediatric longitudinal metabolomic-BP studies exist [22], none has examined whether lipidomic profiles differ by the course of BP over childhood.

We investigated associations of 1,232 plasma lipid species with systolic and diastolic BP in 195 children aged 9-12 years participating in the ENVIRONAGE birth cohort. We additionally tested whether signals clustered by lipid class, total carbon number, and degree of unsaturation. In 177 children with BP measured at both the 4-6-year and 9-12-year childhood follow-up visits, we explored whether BP-associated lipid species and classes differed across stable-normal, incident, persistent, and resolved elevated longitudinal BP patterns.

Methods

The study was reported in accordance with Strengthening the Reporting of

Study population

The ongoing ENVIRonmental influence ON AGEing in early life (ENVIRONAGE) study is a population-based prospective birth cohort established in 2010 in Limburg, Belgium

The analytical platform initially measured 2,017 lipid species. Lipid nomenclature was standardized using the rgoslin R package. Preprocessing was performed in R, version 4.5.0. Lipid species with missing measurements in more than 35% of participants were excluded. This filtering removed 785 lipids, leaving 1,232 lipids for analysis. Among the retained lipids, 8,605 measurements (approximately 3.6% of the lipidomic data matrix) were missing and were imputed using the correlation-based k-nearestneighbour method (kNN-CR), selected on the basis of previous simulation evidence for lipidomic data [24]. Lipid values were subsequently log₂-transformed to reduce right skewness. For lipid-wide association analyses, the log₂-transformed values were standardized to a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one.

Blood pressure assessment

During the follow-up examination at 9-12 years of age, child BP was measured with an automated upper-arm blood-pressure monitor (Omron 705IT; Omron Corporation), equipped with a cuff adapted to the arm size of children. Measurements were performed according to the European Society of Hypertension guideline [25]. After 5 minutes of rest in supine position, a trained observer obtained 5 consecutive readings of the systolic BP and diastolic BP with 1-minute intervals. Mean systolic BP and diastolic BP were based on the mean of the last 3 readings. Mean arterial pressure (MAP) was calculated as diastolic BP plus 40% of pulse pressure (the difference between systolic BP and diastolic BP) [26]. Elevated BP and hypertension stages were defined according to the 2017 American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) guideline [27].

10-

Age-, sex-, and height-specific BP percentiles were calculated for systolic BP and diastolic BP via the online calculator (https://bsolomon.us/app/pediatric-bp/).

For a subset of children (N=177), BP during early childhood was measured during the follow-up visit at 4-6 years of age using the same methodology.

Longitudinal BP patterns

Based on BP status at the first (4-6 years) and second (9-12 years) follow-up, children were classified into four categories: stable normal, defined as normal BP at both visits; incident elevated BP, defined as normal BP at 4-6 years and elevated BP or hypertension at 9-12 years; persistent elevated BP, defined as elevated BP or hypertension at both visits; and resolved elevated BP, defined as elevated BP or hypertension at 4-6 years and normal BP at 9-12 years.

Covariates

For the primary cross-sectional lipidomic analysis of BP at 9-12 years, potential confounders were selected a priori based on hypothesized causal relationships represented in a directed acyclic graph (DAG; Figure S1) and included child age, sex, ethnicity, height, weight, highest parental educational attainment, household smoking, physical activity, screen time, and season of examination. Time of blood collection and plasma storage duration were additionally included as temporal and preanalytical covariates, respectively. Pubertal development, ambient temperature, fish and seafood consumption, cardiometabolic conditions, corticosteroid use, and overweight/obesity were evaluated in sensitivity analyses. Detailed definitions and methods of collection at the visit at 9-12 years are provided in the Supplemental Methods.

All data management and statistical analyses were done using R version 4.5.0.

Participant characteristics were presented as means (standard deviation, SD) or frequency (percentage, %). All statistical tests were two-sided. Analyses were conducted on complete cases for the covariates of each model.

Because sample size was determined by cohort participation, we performed a simulation-based analysis to estimate the minimum detectable effect (MDE) as detailed in the Supplemental Methods.

The analysis workflow is depicted in Figure 1. Primary analyses comprised crosssectional lipidome-wide association analyses of BP at 9-12 years, followed by LSEA; secondary analyses evaluated whether significant species and enriched lipid classes were also associated with longitudinal BP patterns between 4-6 and 9-12 years.

Cross-sectional lipidome-wide association analysis

Cross-sectional lipidome-wide associations with BP at 9-12 years were examined using multivariable linear regression. Separate models were fitted for each individual lipid species and BP outcome. Systolic and diastolic BP percentiles were considered the primary outcomes. Systolic BP, diastolic BP, and MAP, expressed in mmHg, were considered secondary outcomes. Models were adjusted for the confounders identified in the DAG (Figure S1): child sex, age, height, weight, ethnicity, season, parental education, household smoking, physical activity and screen time, along with time of blood collection and plasma storage duration, which were added as temporal and preanalytical covariates. Regression coefficients and their 95% confidence intervals represent the difference in BP percentile points, or in mmHg for the secondary 12outcomes, associated with a 1-standard-deviation higher log₂-transformed lipid level.

To account for multiple testing while preserving correlation among lipid species, empirical permutation-based family-wise error rate (FWER) p-values (𝑃 FWER ) were obtained using a single-step maximum-statistic permutation procedure with 50,000 Freedman-Lane residual permutations, as detailed in Supplemental Methods [28] [29] [30] [31].

Lipidome-wide significance was defined as 𝑃 FWER <0.05. Quantile-quantile plots were used to compare the observed and expected distributions of nominal P values for each lipid-wide analysis. Test-statistic inflation factors (λ) were calculated as the ratio of the median observed 1-degree-of-freedom chi-square statistic to its expected median under the null distribution. The null distribution of the genomic inflation factor λ was obtained from the same Freedman-Lane permutations.

For lipid associations with 𝑃 FWER <0.05, potential departure from linearity and model assumptions were checked as described in the Supplemental Methods. As exploratory secondary analyses, distributions of these lipid species were visualized across pediatric BP categories and associations with BP category were examined using multinomial logistic regression adjusted for the same covariates of the primary model. To assess linear trend, BP category was additionally modelled as an ordinal outcome and the P value for the lipid term was used as the test for trend. Nominal P<0.05 was used for these targeted analyses.

Lipid-set enrichment analysis

LSEA was performed using the lipidr package to determine whether coordinated associations with BP occurred within lipid sets defined by lipid class, total carbon number, or degree of unsaturation. For each primary and secondary BP outcome, lipid 13species were ranked according to the signed t-statistic from the corresponding model.

Positive and negative normalized enrichment scores (NES) indicated lipid sets tending towards positive and inverse associations with BP, respectively. Lipid sets containing fewer than five measured species were not evaluated. Enrichment was assessed using the fast gene-set enrichment algorithm implemented through fgsea. Because lipidr's preranked procedure permutes lipid labels, which assumes independence between species, P values were obtained by permuting BP residuals across children (Freedman-Lane, 50,000 permutations). Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted P was calculated separately for each BP outcome and set type (lipid class, total carbon number, degree of unsaturation), and FDR-adjusted P<0.05 for was considered evidence of lipid-set enrichment.

Sex interaction

Potential effect modification by sex was examined in exploratory analyses by adding a product term between each standardized lipid concentration and sex to the primary model. To account for pubertal development, we also tested the interaction model additionally adjusting for the Pubertal Development Scale (PDS) score.

Longitudinal BP patterns analysis

In the subset of 177 children with BP data available at both childhood follow-up visits, we evaluated whether lipid species meeting the lipidome-wide significance threshold and lipid classes showing significant enrichment in the cross-sectional analyses were also associated with longitudinal BP patterns. Associations of the selected lipid species with BP pattern were assessed using multinomial logistic regression, with stable normal BP as the reference category. To compare the longitudinal groups directly, the 14multinomial models were repeated with resolved elevated BP as the reference category (incident vs resolved; persistent vs resolved), and persistent elevated BP as the reference for incident vs persistent. Models were adjusted using the same covariate sets as the cross-sectional models and nominal P<0.05 was considered significant. Lipidome-wide analyses

For systolic BP percentile, TG 58:9 FA 22:4 was the only lipid species meeting the lipidome-wide FWER threshold. In the primary model (N=186), upon adjustment for child weight, average daily physical activity, average daily screen time, highest parental educational attainment, and household smoking, a 1-SD higher log₂-transformed TG 58:9 FA 22:4 concentration was associated with 7.36-point higher systolic BP percentile (95% CI= 3.91 to 10.80; 𝑃 = 3.98 × 10 -05 ; 𝑃 FWER =0.03) (Figure 3A, Table S2). No lipid species passed the FWER threshold for diastolic BP percentile (Figure 3B), or for systolic, diastolic BP or MAP expressed in mmHg (Figure S4D-F, Table S2). Quantile-quantile plots showed the largest departure from the expected Pvalue distribution for systolic BP percentile (λ=2.45), whereas the distributions for the remaining BP outcomes were closer to expectation (e.g., λ=1.07 for diastolic BP percentile) (Figure S5). The observed value of λ for systolic BP percentile lay at the upper end of its null distribution derived from the Freedman-Lane permutations (median=0.96; 95th percentile=2.31; permutation P=0.04), indicating that the apparent inflation largely reflected inter-lipid correlation, with borderline evidence of a diffuse positive shift (mean t=0.89). Diagnostic analyses did not materially alter the association of TG 58:9 FA 22:4 with systolic BP percentile (Figure S6) and restricted cubic spline analysis provide limited evidence of departure from linearity (P for nonlinearity=0.05, Figure 3C). Each 1-SD higher TG 58:9 FA 22:4 was associated with higher odds of stage 1 hypertension vs normal BP (OR per 1-SD lipid= 3.03; 95% CI= 1.58 to 5.82; P=8.51×10 -04 ); test for linear trend across ordered BP categories supported a gradient across BP categories (OR per 1-SD lipid=2.25; 95% CI= 1.49 to 3.39; P for trend=3.48×10 -05 ) (Figure 3D).

LSEA

In the LSEA, one lipid set was associated with BP: species with a total carbon number of 38 were inversely associated with diastolic BP percentile (NES=-1.81; FDR=0.04), with a leading edge dominated by PC and PE carrying 20-and 22-carbon fatty acyl 18chains (Figure 4). No lipid class or unsaturation set reached significance; PC showed a borderline inverse enrichment with diastolic BP (FDR=0.06). Within the TG class specifically, TG 58:9 FA 22:4 was located within a broader region of predominantly positive associations among long-chain, polyunsaturated TGs (Figure 4D). Complete lipid-set enrichment results and leading-edge species are provided in Table S3.

Sex interaction

We identified evidence of effect modification by sex for the association between PE 20:1 22:0 and diastolic BP percentile (βinteraction=-11.93 BP percentile points; 95% CI, -17.68 to -6.18; nominal P=6.52×10⁻⁵; PFWER=0.044) (Figure S7). The interaction remained similar after additional adjustment for pubertal development score (βinteraction=-11.92; 95% CI, -17.71 to -6.14; nominal P=7.31×10⁻⁵; PFWER=0.049) (Table S4). LSEA did not identified coordinated effect modification by sex across the lipid classes (Figure S8).

Longitudinal BP patterns between 4-6 and 9-12 years and candidate lipid species and classes

Among 173 children with BP at 4-6 and 9-12 years and complete covariates, a 1-SD higher TG 58:9 FA 22:4 was associated with higher odds of incident (OR 2.78, 95% CI 1.33-5.79; P=6.47×10⁻ 3 ) and persistent elevated BP (OR 2.14, 1.22-3.77; P=8.02×10⁻ 3 ) compared with stable-normal BP, but not of resolved elevated BP (OR 0.94, 0.56-1.59; P=0.82). The lipid was higher in incident (OR 2.94, 1.27-6.85; P=0.01) and persistent (OR 2.27, 1.14-4.54; P=0.02) than in resolved elevated BP, and did not differ between incident and persistent elevated BP (OR 1.30, 0.57-2.97; P=0.54) (Figure 3E).

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses did not materially alter the association between TG 58:9 FA 22:4 and systolic BP percentile (β in sensitivities ranging 7.05-8.21 vs 7.36 in the primary model) or longitudinal BP patterns (OR in sensitivities ranging 2.43-4.83 vs 2.78 in primary model for incident elevated BP and 1.90-2.34 vs 2.14 in primary model for persistent elevated BP vs stable-normal BP; and 2.57-4.60 vs 2.94 in primary model for incident elevated BP and 1.81-2.43 vs 2.27 in primary model for persistent elevated BP vs resolved elevated BP), with p-values<0.05 for all model specifications, except for persistent elevated BP vs resolved elevated BP when excluding children with overweight and obesity (Figure S9).

Discussion

In this population-based study of preadolescent children, TG 58:9 FA 22:4 was positively associated with systolic BP percentile after lipidome-wide multiple-testing correction. Longitudinal analyses indicated that TG 58:9 FA 22:4 was higher in children with incident or persistent elevated BP than in those whose earlier elevated BP had resolved or those with stable-normal BP, supporting a closer relationship of this specific lipid with current BP elevation. Although comparable data are lacking in children, adult longitudinal findings show broadly concordant class-level directions. In the Strong Heart Family Study, higher baseline TG species were associated with 20incident hypertension; longitudinal increases in TG species were positively associated with systolic BP change [16]. TG 58:9 FA 22:4 is a long-chain (58 carbons in total), highly polyunsaturated (9 double bonds) TG in which MS/MS identified a 22-carbon FA moiety with four double bonds (FA 22:4), compatible with adrenic acid (22:4n-6) [32]. In our study, it was positively associated primarily with systolic BP, although a similar direction of association was observed across all BP outcomes at nominal P<0.05 and across all model specifications, including additional adjustment for fish and seafood consumption, which argues against habitual fish intake explaining this highly unsaturated TG signal. Among Chinese children from minority groups, hypertension was associated with higher longchain, polyunsaturated TGs sharing either the total composition (TG 58:9) or the FA 22:4 residue, whereas a plasmalogen PE containing FA 22:4 was lower [21]. This suggests that associations of FA 22:4 with BP may depend on the lipid class and metabolic context in which it is incorporated, rather than reflecting a uniform effect. The magnitude of the association is potentially relevant at population level. A 1-SD higher TG 58:9 FA 22:4 corresponded to 7.36 systolic BP percentile, ~ 0.31 SD of the systolic BP percentile distribution or ~3 mmHg (based on the ratio of the SDs of systolic BP percentiles and mmHg in our population). In a pooled analysis of seven cohorts, each 1-unit higher childhood systolic BP z-score was associated with a 34% higher risk of fatal adult cardiovascular events [5]. The BP difference associated with this lipid is therefore of a magnitude that, if persistent, would be relevant for long-term cardiovascular risk.

21-

Despite no evidence of enrichment for TG as a set, TG 58:9 FA 22:4 lay within a region of predominantly positive associations among longer-chain, more polyunsaturated TG species. Pediatric evidence was recently reported in a nested case-control lipidomic study of Chinese children, in which several long-chain, highly unsaturated TGs were elevated in children with higher BP [19]. Potential mechanisms linking TG-rich lipoprotein metabolism to BP include endothelial dysfunction and oxidative stress [33, 34], but this evidence relates to conventional hypertriglyceridemia and cannot be attributed to TG species.

Adiposity is an important potential determinant of both circulating TG profiles and BP.

In children and adolescents, large, weakly unsaturated TG species were elevated in those suffering from overweight or obesity and decreased with greater reductions in BMI SDS during a lifestyle intervention [17]; while TG-related differences associated with hypertension were substantially attenuated after adjustment for BMI z score [19].

In contrast, our lead association (TG 58:9 FA 22:4 with systolic BP percentile) remained relatively stable after weight adjustment and after excluding children with overweight or obesity. This finding, together with the fact that TG 58:9 FA 22:4 is highly unsaturated, suggests that the observed association may reflect a pathway distinct from adiposity-driven TG accumulation; alternatively, adiposity may still contribute through a pathway that weight adjustment does not capture. Children with any elevated-BP pattern, including the resolved group, were more often overweight or obese than stable-normal children (26 to 31% vs 11%; Table S1), so adiposity history could contribute to the resolved-group signals.

22-

Phospholipid species with 38 total carbons, mostly PC and PE carrying 20-and 22carbon chains, were inversely associated with diastolic BP, consistent with the borderline PC pattern. As this set combines several lipid classes and was the only significant set, it should be considered hypothesis-generating.

This study has several strengths, including BP phenotyping via 5 repeated BP standardized measurements; an untargeted, lipid-wide approach across more than 1,200 species in a population-based, not clinically selected or obesity-restricted, pediatric cohort; rigorous correction for multiple testing via a permutation-based empirical family-wise error rate, including lipid-set inference by sample permutation, which accounts for the strong correlation among lipid species that is ignored by the commonly used preranked procedure [35]; extensive sensitivity analyses addressing confounding by adiposity, pubertal timing, seasonality, and comorbidity; and longitudinal BP phenotyping spanning early and later childhood.

This study also has limitations. The estimate for the lead lipid (β=7.36) is below the MDE (8.68), so smaller true effects may have gone undetected and the estimate may be inflated, as is common in discovery analyses. This is a single-cohort study without external validation. BP patterns rest on one oscillometric visit at each age, so some children may be misclassified and regression to the mean may contribute to the resolved group. Lipids were measured once, in preadolescence, and circulating lipid profiles are dynamic [36], so we cannot tell when the differences arose. The cohort is predominantly of European ancestry, limiting generalizability to other populations. Blood sampling was not standardized with respect to fasting status, which may contribute to interindividual variation in lipid species; adjustment for time of blood 23collection may only partially account for this source of variability. We acknowledge that our sex-interaction findings are exploratory and require replication, although plausibility is supported by lipidomic sexual dimorphism in preadolescents [37] and previous evidence that metabolic correlates of BP differ between males and females during adolescence [22].

From a translational perspective, our findings suggest that normalization of BP may coincide with normalization of BP-lipid-associated signatures. Previous life-course studies indicate that cardiovascular target-organ risk is substantially attenuated when elevated childhood BP resolves, although a small residual risk cannot be excluded [7, 38, 39]. However, longitudinal studies with repeated lipidomics and cardiovascular phenotyping are needed to determine whether persistent signatures exist among children whose earlier elevated BP has resolved.

In conclusion, our study found TG 58:9 FA 22:4 associated with systolic BP in preadolescents and was closely associated with current BP elevation.
