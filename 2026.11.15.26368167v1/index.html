To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 11 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Effect Sizes in t-tests and F-tests works

The Effect Size module checks if effect sizes are correctly reported in t-tests and F-tests.

The Effect Size check searches for regular expressions that match typical ways in which effect sizes are reported. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Phantom limb pain (PLP) and residual limb pain (RLP) after amputation impair rehabilitation, prosthetic use, and quality of life. Targeted Muscle Reinnervation (TMR) and Regenerative Peripheral Nerve Interface (RPNI) give transected nerves a physiologic target and may reduce chronic pain.

To synthesize evidence on prophylactic TMR and RPNI at amputation for preventing pain.

Following PRISMA 2020 and a published protocol (PROSPERO CRD42024617299), seven databases were searched to 31 July 2026 for studies of prophylactic TMR or RPNI at amputation reporting PLP, RLP, symptomatic neuroma, or safety outcomes. Pain intensity (Hedges' g) and incidence (risk ratios, RR) were pooled separately for PLP and RLP using random-effects DerSimonian-Laird models. Risk of bias (ROBINS-I, RoB 2) and certainty (GRADE) were assessed.

Twenty-seven studies (30 records, 2,154 participants; 16 comparative) were included. In comparative meta-analyses, TMR/RPNI was associated with lower pain intensity and incidence for PLP (g = -0.68, 95% CI -0.82 to -0.55; RR = 0.56, 0.48-0.65) and RLP (g = -0.68, -0.95 to -0.40; RR = 0.55, 0.42-0.73), or about 300 fewer patients with pain per 1,000 treated. Findings were directionally consistent and stable in sensitivity analyses. No study directly compared TMR with RPNI; indirect subgroup analyses showed no difference. Symptomatic neuroma was less frequent but imprecise (4 studies; RR = 0.35, 0.11-1.09); safety outcomes showed no clear differences. No study had low risk of bias; certainty was low to very low.

Introduction

Limb amputation remains a major and growing source of long-term disability. Approximately 185,000 amputations are performed annually in the United States, and more than 3.6 million people are projected to be living with limb loss there by 2050 (1, 2). Beyond the functional consequences of limb loss, post-amputation pain is among the most disabling sequelae.

Persistent pain can interfere with rehabilitation, prosthetic use, sleep, mobility, work, and quality of life, and it is a common reason for prolonged opioid and neuropathic pain medication use (3, 4). Post-amputation pain is commonly described in two overlapping but clinically distinct domains: phantom limb pain (PLP), perceived in the absent portion of the limb, and residual limb pain (RLP), localized in the stump of the remaining limb. Both are frequent: chronic PLP has been reported in more than 60% of patients, and symptomatic neuroma causing RLP in 4% to 49% of patients across recent systematic reviews (5, 6).

The mechanisms underlying post-amputation pain are multifactorial. Peripheral nerve transection at the time of amputation may contribute particularly to RLP and may also provide ongoing peripheral input relevant to PLP. Central mechanisms, including spinal sensitization and cortical reorganization, can further maintain pain once established (7). Conventional nerve management during amputation, such as traction neurectomy or implantation into soft tissue, attempts to remove the nerve end from mechanical irritation but does not consistently provide a target for regenerating axons. As a result, traditional approaches may leave patients vulnerable to symptomatic neuroma, chronic pain, reduced prosthetic tolerance and subsequent surgeries (8) (9) (10).

Pharmacological management, physical therapy, and mirror therapy have yielded inconsistent results when used to prevent rather than to treat post-amputation pain, and once chronic neuropathic pain is established it responds poorly to any single modality (11, 12). Attention has shifted toward modifying nerve handling at the amputation surgery, when the chance for neuroma formation occurs.

Targeted Muscle Reinnervation (TMR) and Regenerative Peripheral Nerve Interface (RPNI) were developed to address this problem by changing to active nerve management. TMR transfers transected sensory or mixed nerves to expendable motor branches of nearby muscles, allowing axons to reinnervate muscle targets. RPNI wraps the transected nerve end with an autologous free muscle graft, which could become available for reinnervation. Both techniques seek to give regenerating axons a target, reduce disorganized axonal sprouting, ectopic discharge, and symptomatic neuroma formation, and thereby decrease the peripheral input that can contribute to both PLP and RLP (13) (14) (15).

Initially used in selected patients for prosthetic control or for treatment of established neuroma pain, TMR and RPNI are increasingly performed at the time of primary amputation as prophylactic nerve management (16) (17) (18) (19). Observational and comparative cohorts suggest that prophylactic active nerve management may reduce PLP, RLP, and symptomatic neuroma compared with standard amputation techniques (13). A recent multicenter analysis reported lower long-term opioid use and lower opioid dependence after primary TMR, without an increase in stump-related complications (20). However, the available evidence is heterogeneous. Studies vary in amputation level, etiology, timing of intervention, surgical technique, follow-up duration, pain instruments, outcome definitions, and whether pain is reported as incidence, severity, or categorical response.

Previous systematic reviews, including those by De Lange et al., Mauch et al., Yuan et al., and Zimbulis et al., support a protective effect of TMR and RPNI, but leave the prophylactic question incompletely answered (13, (21) (22) (23). Yuan et al. examined primary TMR and RPNI performed at the time of amputation (23), and Zimbulis et al. compared TMR with standard nerve management (13). In these syntheses, however, PLP and RLP are frequently pooled into a single . pain construct, reviews restricted to TMR cannot establish whether RPNI behaves similarly, and the evidence base has grown appreciably since their searches were completed. The present review differs in four respects. PLP and RLP are analyzed as separate domains. Pain incidence and pain intensity are treated as distinct estimands. Both TMR and RPNI are included, which allows the two techniques to be set against one another indirectly by subgroup, something reviews restricted to TMR cannot do. Finally, the certainty of the pooled evidence is rated using GRADE, and sensitivity analyses test how far the findings depend on risk of bias and on outcome-standardization assumptions.

This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to synthesize the available evidence on prophylactic TMR and RPNI performed at the time of limb amputation to prevent neuropathic pain. The primary objective is to compare TMR or RPNI with standard nerve management for PLP and RLP incidence and intensity. Secondary objectives are to summarize effects on symptomatic neuroma and safety outcomes, examine differences by intervention type, assess risk of bias and certainty of evidence, and identify limitations in the current evidence.

Methods

Protocol and Reporting

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with the methodological guidance of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions and reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement (24) (25) (26). The protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024617299) and published before completion of the review (27).

Eligibility Criteria

We included studies evaluating TMR or RPNI performed prophylactically at the time of limb amputation. Eligible populations comprised adults undergoing major upper-or lower-limb amputation for any indication. Studies of delayed or salvage TMR/RPNI for established postamputation pain were excluded unless a prophylactic amputation-time arm could be extracted separately.

Eligible interventions were TMR or RPNI used as active peripheral nerve reconstruction at the time of amputation. Standard care was defined as conventional peripheral nerve management during amputation without TMR or RPNI, including traction neurectomy, ligation, implantation or burial of the nerve into soft tissue, muscle, or bone, myoplasty, or myodesis. We included randomized trials, non-randomized comparative studies, cohort studies, case-control studies, and case series with at least 8 participants. Conference abstracts and brief reports were eligible when they provided extractable clinical outcome data. Case reports, very small case series (n<8), narrative reviews, technical notes without extractable clinical outcomes, and studies not reporting relevant post-amputation pain or safety outcomes were excluded. A comprehensive search was performed in electronic bibliographic databases from inception to 31 July 2026. Databases specified in the protocol included the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), Web of Science, Scopus, PubMed/MEDLINE, CINAHL, Google Scholar, and MedRxiv, the latter two to identify unpublished/grey literature and minimize publication bias. Search terms combined concepts related to limb amputation, TMR, RPNI, peripheral nerve interface, nerve transfer, neuroma, phantom limb pain, residual limb pain, and post-amputation pain. No language restrictions were applied.

Search Strategy and Study Selection

Records were screened independently by two reviewers (JdM, DG) using Covidence. Titles and abstracts were first assessed for potential eligibility, followed by full-text review of potentially relevant articles. Disagreements were resolved by discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. The study selection process was documented according to PRISMA and is shown in the PRISMA flow diagram.

Data Extraction

Data were extracted using a structured data extraction form by three reviewers (DG, JdM, and MR). Each study was extracted independently by two of these reviewers, and disagreements were resolved by consensus or by consultation with the third. Extracted study characteristics included author, year of publication, study design, sample size, follow-up duration, amputation level, amputation etiology, intervention type, surgical timing, and comparator details. Patientlevel aggregate characteristics included age, sex, comorbidities, smoking status, body mass index, vascular disease, diabetes, and other clinically relevant baseline factors when reported.

Outcome data were extracted separately for TMR/RPNI intervention groups and standard care controls. Primary efficacy outcomes were phantom limb pain (PLP) and residual limb pain (RLP), each extracted as both pain intensity and a binary pain outcome whenever possible.

Because the clinical question was prophylaxis, comparative binary analyses were reported as incidence analyses; single-arm summaries were reported as prevalence or pain burden.

Secondary outcomes included symptomatic neuroma, hematoma, infection, wound dehiscence, cardiovascular events, reoperation, and mortality. When required data were reported only in figures, values were extracted from graphical displays and retained with source notes. When several pain instruments or time points were available, standardized extraction rules were used to select the outcome most representative of the relevant post-amputation pain domain.

Risk of Bias Assessment

Risk of bias was assessed according to study design, independently and in duplicate by the same three reviewers, with disagreements resolved by consensus. Randomized trials were assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool (28) and non-randomized comparative studies using ROBINS-I (29). Study records without an eligible standard-care control group were not assigned a formal risk-of-bias judgement and were labelled as not assessed because the planned comparative risk-of-bias framework was not applicable. Domain-level judgements were recorded, together with an overall risk-of-bias judgement categorized as low, moderate, or serious when sufficient information was available. Risk-of-bias judgements were summarized descriptively and used in sensitivity analyses excluding studies judged to be at serious overall risk of bias.

Outcome Standardization and Assumptions

Pain outcomes required standardization because included studies used heterogeneous instruments, time points, and reporting formats. PLP and RLP were analyzed separately. For each pain domain, two outcome types were created: continuous pain intensity and a binary pain outcome. Pain intensity was standardized to a 0-10 metric. Visual Analog Scale (VAS) or Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) scores reported on a 0-10 scale were used directly. PROMIS T-scores were transformed to an approximate 0-10 scale using the formula (T-score -30)/4. When multiple PROMIS domains were available, PROMIS Pain Intensity was preferred, followed by Pain Interference and Pain Behavior. When studies reported pain using ordinal categories only, category midpoints were used to estimate a weighted mean: no pain = 0, mild pain = 2, moderate pain = 5, and severe pain = 8.5.

Means and standard deviations were extracted directly whenever available. Medians were used as approximations of means when means were not reported. Standard deviations were extracted directly when available; when only interquartile ranges were reported, standard deviations were approximated as IQR/1.35. For PROMIS-derived outcomes, standard deviations were rescaled using the same linear transformation as the mean. In selected studies where only binary pain incidence was available but a continuous synthesis required an approximate pain-intensity value, a conservative binary-to-mean transformation was used, assigning a score of 5 to patients with pain and 0 to patients without pain; the corresponding standard deviation was derived from the binomial distribution on this 0-5 contrast. These transformations were recorded at study level and evaluated in sensitivity analyses.

In a minority of studies, the standard deviation used for the standardized pain score, the nopain proportion or both were not directly reported by the source publication and were carried forward from the structured extraction as imputed values. This applied to Alexander 2019, Frantz 2020, Kumar 2024, Raasveld 2024, Reid 2024, Smith 2024, Elmorsi 2025, and Mathieu 2025 (30) (31) (32) (33) (34) (35) (36) (37). Every imputed value was flagged in the study-level source field of the analytic dataset so that its origin remains traceable, and the influence of these assumptions was examined in the sensitivity analyses described below.

The binary pain outcome was expressed as the proportion of patients with pain versus no pain when this was directly reported. When a strict no-pain definition was unavailable but clinically meaningful categories were reported, study-specific transformations were applied and documented. For example, in studies reporting categorical pain severity, no pain or no clinically significant pain was used when this best matched the published data structure. Where a study reported a mean pain score but no usable no-pain category, the proportion was reverseimputed as one minus the mean divided by five, the inverse of the binary-to-mean rule above; this applied only to Smith 2024 (35). All such assumptions were tracked in the dataset.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were performed in Stata version 16 (StataCorp, TX, USA) (38). Comparative metaanalyses were conducted when at least two studies contributed analyzable data for a given outcome. Continuous outcomes were pooled as standardized mean differences using Hedges' g with 95% confidence intervals, as anticipated when studies used different pain instruments.

Binary outcomes were pooled as risk ratios rather than odds ratios: because phantom and residual limb pain are common events, odds ratios diverge from risk ratios, overstate the apparent magnitude of effect, and are frequently misinterpreted as risk ratios. Risk ratios were therefore considered the more appropriate and clinically interpretable measure, and a continuity correction for zero cells was applied as implemented by Stata's meta-analysis commands. Random-effects models using the DerSimonian-Laird method were used throughout because clinical and methodological heterogeneity was expected across studies (39).

Some studies contributed more than one analytic record, either because technique-specific cohorts were reported separately or because different outcomes were reported in different populations. Records from the same study were never entered into the same comparative meta-analysis. In the supplementary single-arm syntheses, two studies contributed two records each (Obinero 2025 and Mahmoud 2024) (40, 41); in both cases the records correspond to disjoint groups of patients, so no participant contributed more than once to any pooled estimate. Where two records from the same study described overlapping populations, participants were counted once in the descriptive totals: the 12-month PLP record of Berberoglu 2025 (47 patients) is a nested subset of its neuroma record (62 patients), and only the larger cohort contributes to the reported participant counts (42).

The main comparative analyses evaluated TMR or RPNI versus standard care for PLP intensity, PLP incidence, RLP intensity, and RLP incidence. Forest plots were generated for all primary meta-analyses. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the Q test, tau-squared, and Isquared. I-squared values were described using the approximate bands suggested in the Cochrane Handbook (24), in which 0% to 40% may not be important, 30% to 60% may represent moderate heterogeneity, 50% to 90% substantial heterogeneity, and 75% to 100% considerable heterogeneity. These ranges overlap by design and were read together with tausquared and the Q test rather than applied as thresholds. Funnel plots and Egger regression tests for small-study effects were generated for the four main comparative pain outcomes, the Egger test being implemented as a regression of the standard normal deviate on precision with a test of the intercept. These tests were interpreted cautiously because each analysis included 11 studies and the evidence was clinically heterogeneous.

No included study compared TMR with RPNI directly. Because each technique was nonetheless compared against standard care, the two were compared indirectly: each of the four main analyses was repeated as a subgroup meta-analysis by intervention type, with a chi-square test for subgroup differences, and the subgroups are displayed in the corresponding forest plots.

Such comparisons are observational at the study level, since the studies contributing each technique differ in population, setting, and era, and few studies evaluated RPNI. They were therefore planned and interpreted as exploratory rather than as a test of one technique against the other.

Secondary and Supplementary Analyses

Single-arm pooled analyses were performed for intervention groups and control groups separately when comparative synthesis was not possible or when summarizing the broader evidence base was informative. For single-arm prevalence analyses, study-level proportions were analyzed with a small continuity adjustment to stabilize estimates in the presence of very high or very low event rates. Single-arm mean pain scores were pooled using study-level standard errors derived from the reported or imputed standard deviations.

Secondary outcomes were synthesized when at least two comparative studies reported the outcome. These comprised symptomatic neuroma and the safety outcomes hematoma, infection, wound dehiscence, cardiovascular events, reoperation, and mortality. All were analyzed as risk ratios using random-effects models.

Sensitivity Analyses

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the findings. First, analyses were repeated after excluding the weakest imputations. For pain intensity, this removed the binary-to-mean transformation applied to studies reporting only pain incidence (Kubiak 2019, Chang 2021, and Chang 2024) (43-45), the incidence-by-severity population-mean approximation (Chang 2024) (45), and acute postoperative VAS values not specific to chronic PLP or RLP (Smith 2024) (35). For pain incidence, this removed studies whose incidence-like endpoint was not a strict no-pain definition, namely those using no or mild pain (Berberoglu 2025) or absence of severe pain (Pejkova 2022) as the no-pain category (42, 46), those whose no-pain proportion was reverse-imputed from a mean score (Smith 2024) (35), and those contributing pain scores only (Mathieu 2025) (37). Second, analyses were repeated excluding studies rated as serious overall risk of bias when risk-of-bias data were available.

Certainty of Evidence

Certainty of evidence for the main outcomes was assessed using the GRADE framework by the same three reviewers (DG, JdM and MR) (47), with the final rating for each outcome agreed by consensus. Because most included studies were observational or non-randomized, certainty ratings started at low certainty unless randomized evidence made a higher starting point appropriate. Downgrading considered risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and potential publication bias. Outcome-scale standardization itself was not treated as a primary reason for downgrading when sensitivity analyses excluding weaker imputations and less directly comparable outcome sources yielded similar conclusions.

Discussion

Principal Findings

This systematic review and meta-analysis suggests that prophylactic active peripheral nerve management with TMR or RPNI at the time of amputation is associated with lower postamputation neuropathic pain compared with standard nerve management. Across comparative studies, TMR/RPNI was associated with reductions in both phantom limb pain (PLP) and residual limb pain (RLP), measured as continuous pain intensity and as binary pain incidence.

The direction of effect was consistent across the four primary pain outcomes: PLP intensity, PLP incidence, RLP intensity and RLP incidence all favored TMR/RPNI.

The magnitude of association was clinically meaningful. PLP intensity was lower in the intervention groups, with a pooled standardized mean difference of approximately two thirds of a standard deviation. PLP incidence was also reduced, with intervention groups showing roughly half the risk observed in standard care groups. Similar findings were observed for RLP, although RLP intensity showed greater between-study heterogeneity.

These relative effects correspond to substantial absolute differences. Applying the pooled risk ratio to the observed control risk of 680 per 1000 gives approximately 379 per 1000 after TMR or RPNI, that is about 301 fewer patients with PLP for every 1000 treated; the corresponding figure for RLP is about 306 fewer per 1000.

The supplementary single-arm syntheses point the same way, with pooled PLP prevalence of 30% in intervention cohorts against 61% in control cohorts, and pooled RLP prevalence of 35% against 62%, although those analyses do not preserve within-study comparisons and carry high heterogeneity.

The consistency of this pattern across individual studies is itself notable. Of the 44 study-level estimates contributing to the four primary analyses, 41 favored TMR or RPNI. For PLP incidence, all 11 contributing studies pointed in the same direction and between-study heterogeneity was nil.

The greater heterogeneity seen for RLP intensity is not unexpected, given variation across studies in amputation level, surgical indication, follow-up duration, pain measurement instruments, and whether reported pain scores reflected chronic RLP specifically or broader postoperative pain.

Interpretation of Findings

The findings are biologically plausible. In standard amputation procedures, transected nerves often lack a physiological target for reinnervation; this predisposes the nerve stump to aberrant regeneration. TMR and RPNI attempt to address this mechanism directly by providing a structured target for reinnervation at the moment when the transected nerve is beginning to heal. The observed reductions in PLP and RLP are consistent with this mechanistic rationale, particularly if early reduction of peripheral ectopic input reduces later peripheral and central drivers of post-amputation pain.

Because much of the evidence included was observational, these findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects. The consistency of the direction of effect supports the hypothesis that active nerve management may reduce post-amputation pain, but residual confounding and differences in patient selection cannot be excluded.

The results also suggest that the benefits of active nerve management may extend beyond symptomatic neuroma alone. PLP and RLP are complex outcomes with both peripheral and central components. By reducing peripheral nociceptive and neuropathic input early after amputation, prophylactic TMR/RPNI may reduce ongoing peripheral drivers of pain and could plausibly influence subsequent sensitization. However, the available evidence does not allow conclusions about mechanism or whether the magnitude of benefit differs across indications, amputation levels, or patient subgroups. Importantly, consistency in the direction of effect does not imply uniformity in its size. The magnitude of benefit varied appreciably across studies, and one prospectively conducted study was exactly neutral for PLP intensity.

RLP outcomes require particularly cautious interpretation. Some studies reported RLP-specific scores, whereas others reported overall pain, acute postoperative pain, or categorical pain outcomes that required conversion before pooling.

Robustness of the Findings

The prespecified sensitivity analyses did not weaken the pooled estimates. Restricting the synthesis to studies not rated at serious overall risk of bias left every outcome essentially unchanged: Hedges' g moved from -0.68 to -0.61 for PLP intensity and from -0.68 to -0.67 for RLP intensity, while the risk ratios moved from 0.56 to 0.57 for PLP incidence and from 0.55 to 0.52 for RLP incidence. Removing the values that depended on the weakest assumptions gave the same picture, with estimates that were unchanged for PLP and somewhat larger for RLP.

This matters for interpretation: if the association were an artifact of poor methodology, it should attenuate when the weaker studies and the weaker assumptions are removed, which it did not.

This robustness speaks to internal validity, and it should not be conflated with the question of reporting bias. Excluding studies at serious risk of bias says nothing about studies that were never published, and the funnel asymmetry observed for PLP incidence is considered below.

What the sensitivity analyses do establish is narrower but still useful: within the evidence that exists, the effect is not being carried by the least reliable studies or by the most aggressive imputations.

Symptomatic Neuroma and Safety Outcomes

Symptomatic neuroma was reported with a clear numerator and denominator by only four comparative studies. The pooled estimate favored TMR/RPNI and was large in relative terms (RR 0.35, 95% CI 0.11 to 1.09), corresponding to roughly 88 fewer symptomatic neuromas per 1000 patients treated, but the confidence interval included no effect. The sparseness of this evidence is striking, because neuroma prevention is the premise of both techniques and was one of the outcomes prioritized in the protocol. The direction of effect is consistent with that premise and with the reductions observed in PLP and RLP, but confirmation will require studies that apply and report a standardized definition of symptomatic neuroma, such as Arnold's criteria (60).

Safety outcomes were also sparsely reported. Available comparative data did not show clear evidence of differences between TMR/RPNI and standard care for hematoma, infection, wound dehiscence, cardiovascular events, reoperation, or mortality. However, confidence intervals were wide, event counts were low, and reporting was inconsistent.

This caution is particularly relevant because prophylactic TMR and RPNI can add operative steps, require specific nerve targets or muscle grafts, and may be technically demanding in complex patients. The available literature suggests that expertise, operative planning, and patient comorbidity may influence both feasibility and outcomes. Reoperation is especially difficult to interpret. Some reoperations may reflect complications of the index amputation, whereas others may represent clinically appropriate revision for pain, neuroma, wound breakdown, prosthetic fitting, or unrelated stump problems. Future studies should distinguish reoperation indications more consistently and report whether secondary procedures were performed for neuroma, pain, wound complications, bone prominence, infection, prosthetic fitting, or planned staged reconstruction.

Methodological Considerations

This review required several outcome transformations because included studies used different instruments and reporting formats. Pain scores were standardized to a 0-10 metric, PROMIS scores were rescaled, ordinal categories were converted using midpoint assumptions, and some binary outcomes were transformed into approximate continuous values. These methods allowed a broader quantitative synthesis but necessarily introduced uncertainty. To address this, transformations were documented at study level, and sensitivity analyses were performed.

The distinction between pain prevalence, pain incidence, and absence of pain was also challenging. Some studies reported no-pain categories, while others reported mild, moderate, and severe pain, or only pain scores. In selected cases, thresholds such as no severe pain were used when another definition was unavailable. These decisions were conservative where possible, but they mean that binary outcomes should be interpreted as standardized pain presence or clinically meaningful pain burden rather than incidence.

The single-arm syntheses carry their own caveats. The proportions and mean scores across cohorts showed high heterogeneity and did not establish comparisons protecting against confounding. They are reported only to describe the observed pain burden.

Limitations

.

Several limitations should be considered. First, most included studies were observational, and several were judged to be at moderate or serious risk of bias. Confounding by indication is likely: patients selected for TMR or RPNI may differ from controls in surgical indication, comorbidity or other characteristics. Second, sample sizes were often small, and some analyses included sparse event counts, leading to imprecision. Third, outcome reporting was heterogeneous. Studies differed in follow-up duration, pain instrument, pain domain definition, and whether PLP and RLP were separated. Some studies reported postoperative pain at early time points, whereas others reported longer-term chronic pain. Fourth, several of the values entering the analyses were derived rather than reported. In eight studies the standard deviation used for the standardized pain score, the no-pain proportion, or both were not available in the source publication and were carried forward as imputed values, and in a smaller number of studies continuous pain values were reconstructed from binary or categorical data. The sensitivity analyses excluding these assumptions did not change the direction or approximate magnitude of the findings, but the pooled estimates nonetheless rest in part on assumptions rather than on directly reported data. Fifth, TMR and RPNI were combined in the main intervention category. Technique-specific estimates are reported as subgroups, but only two to three studies contributed to RPNI, so the indirect comparison between techniques has low power. Sixth, safety outcomes were inconsistently reported and underpowered. Finally, the Egger test was statistically significant for PLP incidence (p = 0.033), raising the possibility of small-study effects or reporting bias for the outcome with the most precise pooled estimate.

With 11 studies this test has limited power, and the certainty of evidence for PLP incidence was nonetheless downgraded on this basis. Two features of that analysis are worth weighing alongside it: all 11 contributing studies favored TMR or RPNI, and between-study heterogeneity was nil. That pattern is more readily explained by smaller studies reporting somewhat larger effects than by a body of null studies missing from the literature, since a reservoir of unpublished null results would be expected to produce disagreement in results direction among the published studies. This interpretation cannot be tested with our data. More generally, assessment of publication bias remains limited by the small number of studies in each meta-analysis and by the observational nature of much of the evidence. There is also a need for adequately powered comparative studies evaluating prophylactic TMR, RPNI and standard care, ideally stratified by comorbidities. Direct comparisons between TMR and RPNI are required, as current evidence does not establish the superiority of one technique or the preference in subgroups. Safety outcomes, operative time, reoperation indications, prosthetic use, functional recovery, quality of life, opioid use, and cost-effectiveness should be reported consistently.

Conclusions

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, prophylactic TMR and RPNI performed at the time of amputation were associated with less post-amputation neuropathic pain than standard peripheral nerve management. Pain intensity was lower by approximately two thirds of a standard deviation for both phantom limb pain (PLP) and residual limb pain (RLP), and the risk of pain was roughly halved (risk ratio 0.56 for PLP and 0.55 for RLP), corresponding to about 300 fewer patients with pain for every 1000 treated.

Three features strengthen this finding. The direction of effect was near-unanimous, with 41 of the 44 study-level estimates favoring active nerve reconstruction. The pooled estimates were essentially unchanged when studies at serious risk of bias were excluded, and again when the values resting on the weakest assumptions were removed. And the effect was consistent across both pain domains and across both ways of measuring them.

Symptomatic neuroma, the mechanism on which both techniques are premised, was reported with usable data by only four comparative studies; the pooled estimate favored TMR/RPNI but was too imprecise. No study compared the two techniques directly. Compared indirectly, through their separate comparisons with standard care, they performed similarly, and no test for subgroup differences approached significance. This is an absence of evidence for a difference rather than evidence of equivalence.

Certainty in these estimates remains low to very low. That rating is driven principally by the observational design of the underlying literature, risk of bias, heterogeneous outcome definitions, and sparse safety and neuroma reporting, even though the direction of effect was consistent. The evidence supports considering TMR or RPNI at the time of amputation as a 685 reasonable strategy in appropriate patients, while the size or distribution of the benefit awaits 686 higher-quality studies using standardized outcomes.

Strengths

This review has several strengths. It was based on a prospectively registered and published protocol, used systematic methods for study selection and data extraction, and synthesized both pain intensity and pain incidence for PLP and RLP separately. The analysis retained studylevel traceability of outcome transformations and incorporated sensitivity analyses to test the robustness of findings to risk of bias and weaker imputations; those analyses left the estimates essentially unchanged, which is more informative than the fact that they were performed. The near-unanimous direction of effect across contributing studies is a further strength of the underlying evidence, independent of the design limitations of individual studies. The inclusion of safety outcomes also provides a broader clinical perspective than pain efficacy alone.Future ResearchFuture studies should use standardized outcome definitions for PLP, RLP, and symptomatic neuroma. Pain outcomes should be reported separately for phantom and residual limb pain, with clear time points and validated instruments. Trials and prospective comparative cohorts should report both incidence and severity, together with standard deviations or other measures of dispersion that allow meta-analysis. Minimum clinically important differences should be prespecified when possible.
