Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

In the UK, declining home ownership and delayed entry into the housing market have reshaped patterns of housing tenure (whether people own or rent their homes) across adulthood. Home ownership may reflect socioeconomic advantage, but may also provide greater housing security, stability and control, with implications for mental health. Using data from the 1970 British Cohort Study (BCS70), this study examines how home ownership trajectories, defined by whether, when and for how long individuals own their homes, are associated with mental health in midlife.

We analysed data from 8,016 BCS70 participants with information on housing tenure histories and mental health outcomes at age 51. Housing tenure was measured repeatedly from ages 26 to 46 and used to define prespecified patterns of home ownership across adulthood (referred to as ownership regimes), including sustained ownership and never owning. Mental health outcomes at age 51 were psychological distress and positive mental wellbeing. G-estimation of a structural nested mean model was used to estimate contrasts between these ownership regimes while accounting for time-varying confounding affected by prior exposure. Models were estimated in lavaan; non-response weights and full information maximum likelihood were used to address selective attrition and missing data.

Results

At age 51, 71% of participants owned their home (23% outright and 48% with a mortgage), while 24% were renting and 5% lived in other arrangements. Sustained home ownership across adulthood was associated with lower psychological distress at age 51 compared with never owning (ATE -0.21, 95% CI -0.47 to -0.03) and higher positive mental wellbeing (ATE 0.56 SD, 95% CI 0.38 to 0.67). In ownership-history regime contrasts, late ownership (first ownership in one's 40s) was associated with lower psychological distress than never owning (ATE -0.15, 95% CI -0.30 to -0.04), while the estimate for early ownership (first ownership in one's 30s) was in the same direction but imprecise (ATE -0.12, 95% CI -0.37 to 0.04). For wellbeing, both late and early ownership were associated with higher scores than never owning (ATE 0.17 SD, 95% CI 0.08 to 0.27 and ATE 0.40 SD, 95% CI 0.24 to 0.51, respectively), with the largest association observed for sustained ownership.

Conclusion

Home ownership across adulthood (age 26 to 46) was associated with better mental health in midlife (age 51) after accounting for time-varying socio-economic and health-related confounding. Findings suggest that both the timing and persistence of ownership may matter, particularly for positive mental wellbeing, with more sustained exposure to ownership associated with more favourable outcomes.

Introduction

Housing is widely recognised as a fundamental social determinant of health (1). Aspects of housing such as affordability, quality and residential stability are important for mental health and may differ according to housing tenure. Housing tenure refers to the legal arrangement under which someone occupies a property (e.g. owning or renting a home) and is increasingly recognised as an upstream determinant of mental health (2) (3) (4) (5). The relationship between housing tenure and mental health is complex and closely linked to income and wealth. Housing tenure is an established indicator of socioeconomic position (SEP), alongside occupational social class, income, wealth and education (6). However, beyond its role as an indicator of SEP, housing tenure may have distinct implications for mental health through differences in perceived control (7), stability (8), housing quality (9) or stigma associated with particular tenure types (5). These dynamics may vary across generations and life stages, reflecting broader changes in housing policy, labour markets, and social inequality. In the UK, for example, owner-occupation expanded substantially over the late 20th century and was relatively common, whereas access to home ownership has become increasingly constrained for more recent generations. As a result, the meaning and implications of housing tenure, including the relative advantages of ownership compared with renting, may differ across cohorts and over time. This makes it important to examine not only whether housing tenure is associated with mental health, but how changes in tenure across adulthood may shape later mental health outcomes, particularly in the context of increasingly unequal access to secure and affordable housing.

Much of the existing literature investigating housing tenure and mental health is cross-sectional, documenting associations at one point in time without clarifying direction or causality (10). In the UK, cross-sectional studies often find that social renters are more likely to report anxiety and depression than owner-occupiers (11, 12). However, longitudinal analyses that yield estimates with a causal interpretation remain relatively scarce. Studies that do explicitly examine how changes in housing tenure affect mental health are "rare but important" (4), with existing research often yielding mixed results.

For example, a recent study using the UK Household Longitudinal Study (13) tracked psychological distress, measured using the General Health Questionnaire, before and after people moved home between 2001 and 2017 using first difference models. They found that moves from renting to home ownership were associated with improvements in mental health. These effects of changing tenure persisted even after adjusting for changes in housing affordability, income, and other factors that vary over time. However, this study only captures short-term changes around the move, and the methods used cannot fully account for time-varying confounders that are affected by earlier tenure. In contrast, analysis of 1991-2009 British Household Panel Survey data compared those who bought their property under Britain's Right to Buy policy, which enabled social renters to become homeowners, with otherwise similar tenants who stayed renting (4). Using a difference-indifferences design, the authors tracked psychological distress, also measured using the General Health Questionnaire, from two years before to one year after the tenure change and adjusted for shifts in marital status, employment, finances and education. The study found that those who bought their homes experienced no significant reduction in psychological distress compared to those who continued renting, concluding that obtaining ownership delivers no short-term mental health benefit beyond buyers' pre-existing socio-economic advantage.

International evidence is similarly inconsistent. A 2012 Australian study using longitudinal data from the HILDA (Household, Income and Labour Dynamics in Australia) survey (3) found that while mental health scores differ significantly across housing tenure, these differences were largely attributable to population characteristics such as income, education, employment status, and age. Once these compositional factors were accounted for, there was little evidence of a causal relationship between housing tenure itself and mental health. By contrast, a recent Finnish birth-cohort study (14) tracking everyone born between 1972 and 1975 and linking housing-register, prescription, and hospital records found that, after controlling for earlier mental health treatment, education, income, and occupation, homeowners were significantly less likely than comparable tenants to require psychotropic medication or specialist care. The benefit remained for people with no prior mental health history and was most pronounced among adults who had grown up in owneroccupied housing or lone-parent families, suggesting a protective effect of homeownership. Length of time in ownership added little, indicating that tenure status itself matters most.

Against a backdrop of declining housing affordability and widening inequalities in access to home ownership in the UK (15), there is a gap in our understanding of the impact of housing tenure on mental health outcomes across the life course. Such knowledge is essential for motivating housing policy changes to address widening health inequalities in the UK. We used data from the 1970 British Cohort Study (BCS70), a birth cohort with prospectively collected information spanning more than five decades. Since housing tenure, mental health, and their shared determinants are measured repeatedly from early life onwards, BCS70 allows tenure histories to be observed across the life course and their effects on mental health to be examined over time. Focusing on this cohort is particularly useful because cohort members formed households during the late 1980s and 1990s, a period marked by recessions, rising inequality and shifts in housing policy that may have shaped opportunities for home ownership and had lasting implications for mental health in midlife (16).

Specifically, this study aims to provide answers to the following research questions: 1.

How do different housing tenure trajectories across adulthood relate to mental health outcomes in midlife? 2. What is the association of sustained exposure to home ownership across adulthood, compared with never owning, on mental health in midlife?

Methods

Data

We used data from the 1970 British Cohort Study (BCS70), a national birth cohort study that follows the lives of individuals born in England, Scotland, and Wales in a single week in 1970 (17). This involved 17,196 babies at baseline, with 11 follow-up sweeps conducted when the cohort members were aged 5, 10, 16, 26, 30, 34, 38, 42, 46 and 51.

At age 51, 12,368 participants were invited to participate (71.9% of original), and 8,016 (64.8%) were interviewed (18). Trained interviewers made initial contact and conducted core interviews, including a cognitive assessment module. Among other factors, BCS70 collects information on participants' health, educational, social, and physical development. Although attrition in BCS70 has been socio-demographically patterned, particularly higher among men and participants from lower socio-economic backgrounds (19), extensive work shows that through application of the survey's inverse-probability non-response weights and full information maximum likelihood estimation, the cohort's baseline representativeness can be fully restored (20-23).

Measures

Housing tenure and home ownership

Housing tenure was assessed at ages 26, 30, 34, 42, 46, and 51 (the age 38 sweep was excluded because it collected a more limited range of measures). Participants reported their housing situation, which was grouped into four categories: owned outright, owned with a mortgage, rented (including both social housing and renting from private landlords), and other arrangements. The 'other' category included living rent-free, squatting, and other non-standard arrangements.

Housing tenure was also converted into a binary exposure indicating home ownership, with participants classified as owners if they owned their home outright or with a mortgage, and as nonowners if they were renting or in other arrangements. Four prespecified longitudinal groups (referred to as ownership-history regimes) were defined to represent patterns of exposure to home ownership across adulthood: never owning, defined as no home ownership at any observed adult exposure sweep; late ownership, defined as first entering home ownership at age 42 or 46 and remaining an owner at subsequent exposure sweeps; early ownership, defined as first entering home ownership at age 30 or 34 and remaining an owner at subsequent exposure sweeps; and always owning, defined as home ownership at every observed adult exposure sweep.

Mental health outcomes

Psychological distress was assessed at each sweep using the Malaise Inventory. Cohort members completed the 9 yes/no items (e.g., "Do you often feel miserable and depressed?"). Affirmative responses are summed to give a total score from 0 to 9, with higher scores reflecting greater distress.

The Malaise Inventory has shown good internal consistency in UK population studies (Cronbach's α ≈ 0.80) and convergent validity against clinically diagnosed depression and anxiety (24, 25). It has also demonstrated measurement invariance over time, with Item Response Theory analyses indicating greater measurement precision at moderate to high levels of psychological distress (26).

Positive mental wellbeing was assessed using the 14-item Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Well-Being Scale (WEMWBS) at ages 42 and 46, and the 7-item Short Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Well-Being Scale (SWEMWBS) at age 51. Because the wellbeing measure differed across sweeps, wellbeing scores were standardised before analysis. The age 51 standardised SWEMWBS score was used as the wellbeing outcome, while standardised wellbeing scores at ages 42 and 46 were included as prior wellbeing covariates. Because wellbeing scores were standardised, estimated effects on wellbeing are expressed in standard deviation units rather than raw scale points. The WEMWBS and SWEMWBS have shown good psychometric properties in UK population samples and are recommended for population-based surveys and cohort studies (27).

Covariates

Covariates were selected based on the hypothesised causal relationships shown in the directed acyclic graph (DAG; Figure 1), to capture pre-exposure and time-varying factors that may confound the relationship between housing tenure and mental health. Time-varying confounders were measured across the adult sweeps where available and included: • Self-rated general health -coded as excellent/very good, good, fair, and poor/very poor.

• Occupational social class -harmonised across sweeps using a five-category occupational class measure: professional/managerial, skilled non-manual, skilled manual, partly skilled, and unskilled.

• Weekly income or earnings -converted to a weekly measure where required and logtransformed for analysis.

• Prior mental health -included where available. For psychological distress models, prior Malaise Inventory scores were included at earlier adult sweeps. For wellbeing models, prior standardised WEMWBS scores were included at ages 42 and 46.

Time-invariant early-life covariates were selected to capture factors preceding adult housing tenure that may influence both subsequent housing trajectories and mental health, informed by the hypothesised causal relationships shown in the DAG (Figure 1) and previous analyses of BCS70 data (28) (29) (30). These included: • Parental social class at birth -measured using Registrar General Social Classes I-V.

• Parental housing tenure at age 10 -measured as the cohort member's family housing tenure in childhood.

• Cognitive ability at age 10 -derived from a principal component analysis of age 10 cognitive test scores, including reading and mathematics measures.

• Internalising symptoms at age 16 -measured using the total Rutter A scale.

Analyses

This study addressed two related research questions. The first examined the association between sustained home ownership across adulthood and mental health in midlife, accounting for timevarying socio-economic and health-related confounding, as well as early-life characteristics. The second examined how different ownership-history regimes across adulthood were associated with mental health outcomes at age 51. G-estimation was used to address both research questions within a causal inference framework, while accounting for time invariant and time-varying confounding. This approach is well suited to longitudinal settings in which both exposures and confounders vary over time, and where confounders may themselves be influenced by prior exposure (31). G-estimation was implemented using a structural nested mean model (SNMM), which estimates contrasts in potential outcomes under alternative exposure histories. Interpretation of these estimates relies on the usual assumptions of consistency, positivity, and no unmeasured confounding conditional on the measured covariate history.

To address the first research question, exposure was defined as home ownership at observed adult sweeps from age 26 to age 46. The estimand (the causal effect of interest) represented the average difference in mental health outcomes at age 51 under a static regime in which all individuals were assumed to own their home at every observed adult exposure sweep, compared with a regime in which none were assumed to own their home at any observed adult exposure sweep, conditional on measured confounders. To address the second research question, prespecified ownership-history regimes were constructed and compared within the same SNMM framework. These represented never owning, late ownership, early ownership, and always owning, as defined above. Each ownership-history regime was compared with never owning to assess whether mental health outcomes at age 51 differed according to the timing and persistence of home ownership across adulthood.

These SNMMs were estimated using the lavaan package (32), following procedures outlined by Loh and Ren (33). Model specification was informed by the DAG displayed in Figure 1. Adjustment included time-varying occupational social class, weekly income or earnings, self-rated general health, and prior mental health where available, as well as early-life characteristics including parental social class at birth, parental housing tenure at age 10, cognitive ability at age 10, and internalising symptoms at age 16.

All models were estimated in lavaan using robust maximum likelihood (MLR), which provides robust standard errors and scaled test statistics to account for departures from normality, including skewness in the Malaise Inventory scores. The analytic sample was restricted to sweep 11 participants with non-missing age-51 outcome data, a valid sweep 11 non-response weight, and at least one observed adult home ownership measure. Sweep 11 non-response weights supplied with the BCS70 data were applied to account for differential participation at age 51. These inverse-probability weights were derived from models predicting response using demographic, socio-economic, health, and behavioural characteristics measured at earlier sweeps (18). Confidence intervals for ownershipregime contrasts were obtained using a nonparametric percentile bootstrap. All analysis was conducted in R version 4.2.2 (34).

Discussion

This study provides new evidence on how home-ownership histories across adulthood are associated with mental health in midlife. Using g-estimation to address measured time-varying socioeconomic and health-related confounding, we found that ownership throughout the observed adult sweeps was associated with lower psychological distress and higher positive mental wellbeing at age 51 compared with never owning. Analyses of prespecified ownership histories indicated that the pattern differed by outcome. For wellbeing, pairwise comparisons supported a graded pattern: always owners had higher estimated wellbeing than early owners, who had higher wellbeing than late owners, while never owners had the lowest estimated wellbeing. For psychological distress, the pattern was less consistent: always owners had slightly lower estimated distress than early owners, but early and late owners did not clearly differ. Taken together, these findings suggest that the timing and persistence of home ownership may be particularly relevant to positive mental wellbeing. They also support conceptualising housing tenure as more than a marker of socioeconomic position: it may additionally capture material assets and aspects of housing security, stability and control. However, these potential pathways were not directly tested and remain hypotheses for future research.

The 1970 cohort occupies an important position in generational changes in housing. Improvements in home ownership seen across earlier post-war generations began to stall among those born in the 1970s, with subsequent cohorts experiencing substantially lower rates of ownership at comparable ages (35). Home ownership may therefore capture more than current socio-economic position. Over time, ownership can allow the accumulation of housing wealth and provide an asset that may offer some protection from financial precarity, while also providing greater residential security and control over the home (36). These potential benefits may become increasingly important as people move through midlife and towards retirement. However, these mechanisms were not directly examined in the present study and should be tested in future research.

These findings are relevant to the concept of Generational Health Drift (37). Evidence from British birth cohort comparisons indicates that more recent generations have experienced poorer health at comparable ages, with particularly consistent differences in psychological distress in adulthood (37). The 1970 cohort has repeatedly been found to report higher psychological distress than earlier-born British cohorts. Our study cannot establish whether differences in home ownership explain these between-cohort differences, but it identifies sustained home ownership as one plausible life-course exposure that could contribute to them and could be examined directly in future cross-cohort analyses.

Our findings extend previous longitudinal research, which has largely focused on short-term changes following entry into home ownership and has produced mixed results (3, 4, 13, 14). In contrast, our results suggest that the timing and persistence of ownership across adulthood may be important, particularly for wellbeing. This supports considering housing tenure as a cumulative life-course exposure, potentially reflecting both sustained housing security and the accumulation of housing wealth over time. In later-born cohorts, delayed entry into ownership and more fragmented housing trajectories (16) may therefore reduce exposure to these potential benefits, providing one possible structural pathway relevant to the Generational Health Drift (37).

Implications

Our findings suggest that the mental health relevance of housing may extend beyond entry into home ownership to the timing, affordability and persistence of ownership across adulthood. Several mechanisms could potentially explain these associations. Sustained ownership may provide greater residential security, stability and control, while enabling the accumulation of housing wealth and reducing exposure to involuntary moves. However, ownership may also create financial strain through mortgage repayments, maintenance costs and changes in interest rates, particularly when housing costs account for a substantial proportion of household income. These mechanisms were not directly examined in this study and should be investigated in future research. Accordingly, our findings should not be interpreted as evidence that promoting home ownership alone will improve mental health. Instead, they emphasise the potential importance of sustained access to stable and affordable housing across tenures, including genuinely affordable housing supply and greater security in the private rented sector.

These findings may also have implications for future generations. If later-born cohorts spend longer in insecure or unaffordable housing, enter home ownership later, and accumulate less housing wealth, this could contribute to the generational differences in mental health described by Generational Health Drift (37). This should be examined directly in future cross-cohort analyses to assess whether differences in housing trajectories account for part of the mental health gap between generations.

Future research should also examine adopt an intersectional approach and investigate whether these associations differ by sex, ethnicity, social class and other markers of socio-economic disadvantage. Access to stable home ownership is itself socially patterned, and understanding who is most affected by changes in housing trajectories will be important for identifying whether housing contributes to inequalities in mental health within as well as between generations.

Strengths and Limitations

This study draws on a large, long-running birth cohort with repeated measures of housing tenure, socio-economic circumstances and mental health across adulthood, allowing us to examine long-term patterns of exposure rather than relying on cross-sectional associations. The application of gestimation within a structural nested mean model allowed us to account for time invariant but also time-varying confounders that may themselves be affected by earlier housing tenure. In addition, examining both psychological distress and positive wellbeing provided complementary perspectives on mental health in midlife.

Several limitations should nevertheless be considered. Although we adjusted for a wide range of early-life and time-varying socio-economic and health-related factors, residual confounding from unmeasured characteristics, such as personality or aspects of financial security not captured in the cohort, cannot be ruled out. Causal interpretation of the SNMM estimates therefore depends on assumptions including no unmeasured confounding, positivity, consistency and correct model specification. However, the richness of BCS70 and availability of repeated measures across adulthood enabled adjustment for a broad range of factors likely to influence both housing tenure and mental health.

Housing tenure was dichotomised into owner and non-owner status to operationalise the timevarying exposure within the binary SNMM framework used in this study and to define interpretable ownership-history regimes across adulthood. This provided a consistent measure across repeated sweeps but necessarily obscured potentially important differences between outright and mortgaged ownership, and between private renting, social renting and other arrangements. The analysis also did not directly capture several mechanisms that may distinguish these housing circumstances, including housing wealth, mortgage or rental burden, affordability, security of tenure and control over the home. These remain plausible pathways linking housing tenure to mental health but were not directly tested in the present study.

Measurement of prior mental health also differed between the two outcomes. Psychological distress was available repeatedly across the adult sweeps, whereas prior wellbeing was available only at ages 42 and 46. Adjustment for pre-existing wellbeing was therefore more limited, although including wellbeing at both sweeps provided some control for differences preceding the age-51 outcome.

Finally, the analysis is restricted to a single cohort born in 1970, so the findings may not generalise directly to generations experiencing different housing markets or policy environments. However, the repeated prospective measurements in BCS70 provide unusually detailed information on housing and mental health across more than two decades of adult life.

Conclusion

Sustained exposure to home ownership across adulthood was associated with better midlife mental health in the 1970 British Cohort Study, with particularly clear evidence of a graded association with wellbeing according to the timing and persistence of ownership. These findings suggest that housing tenure may be relevant to mental health across the life course, potentially through greater residential security and control, the accumulation of housing wealth, or a combination of these processes. Future research should seek to disentangle these mechanisms and examine whether changing housing trajectories contribute to generational differences in mental health. In the context of declining and delayed access to home ownership, improving access to stable, secure and affordable housing should be considered an important part of the public mental health agenda.
