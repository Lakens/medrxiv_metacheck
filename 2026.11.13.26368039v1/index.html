What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Purpose: This study compared inter-articulatory timing patterns of the tongue and jaw across talkers with Parkinson's disease (PD), amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), Huntington's disease (HD), multiple sclerosis (MS), and neurotypical controls (CON) to identify disease-related differences. The study also explored if a talker's tongue body cycle duration, which serve as a proxy for a talker's habitual speech rate, or a talker's reliance on their jaw, as indicated by their jaw movement amplitude, could predict their inter-articulatory timing patterns.

Method: Using three-dimensional electromagnetic articulography, the movements of the posterior tongue and jaw were examined in 24 talkers with PD, 19 talkers with ALS, 18 talkers with HD, 18 talkers with MS, and 34 CON during a sentence repetition task ("Buy Kaia a kite"). The tongue movement during the segment [kai] served as the reference signal (tongue body cycle) for which the phase angle of the jaw reaching the maximum opening position during [kai] was calculated. Tongue body cycle durations were measured as the time between the tongue body movement onset to the tongue body movement offset. The jaw opening amplitude was measured as the relative positional change from the jaw's maximum closing position associated with [k] to the jaw's maximal opening position at the onset of the diphthong [ai].

Results: A significant group effect was found for phase angles. Pairwise comparisons showed that talkers with ALS had significantly larger phase angles than talkers with PD, MS, HD, and CON, indicating a timing pattern where the tongue reached the target [a] earlier relative to the jaw. No significant differences were found.

Introduction

Speech production is a complex goal-directed motor behavior that requires a smooth interaction of multiple speech subsystems (i.e., respiration, phonation, resonance, and articulation). At the articulatory level, motor execution is carefully timed across the tongue, upper and lower lips, and the jaw, to achieve the desired acoustic output [1] [2] [3]. These inter-articulatory timing patterns are thought to play a critical role in the production of intelligible speech [4, 5]. They can also provide insights in the spatiotemporal programming and coordination strategies of the motor control system.

In this study, inter-articulatory timing is understood as a specific, quantifiable aspect of speech motor coordination that describes how distinct speech structures are spatiotemporally aligned. While closely linked to coarticulation, the context-specific physiological overlapping of adjacent speech gestures executed by one articulator, inter-articulatory timing focuses precisely on the coordination between two or more articulators. Inter-articulatory timing patterns can be quantified by relative phasing, expressed as the positional and spatiotemporal state of one articulator relative to another articulator at a specific landmark in terms of a phase angle [6] [7] [8] or temporal latencies, expressed as the absolute time difference between articulatory events (velocity peaks, positional targets) of two articulators [8, 9]. This work focuses on the former, quantifying the relative phasing of the jaw relative to the tongue via phase angles.

Phase angles

In previous studies on neurotypical talkers, phase angles have been used to examine spatiotemporal relations between two articulators. In such analyses, the movement of one articulator (typically the jaw) served as the reference signal, to which lip or tongue tip movements were related during consonant-vowel-consonant sequences such as [bab] or [tat] [10, 11]. The rationale for a target utterance with two stop consonants was that it provided robust discrete kinematic landmarks to define the movement cycles. The calculation of phase angles is based on the concept that articulators are oscillators producing sinusoidal-like movement cycles along a phase plane [6]. Consequently, the relative timing between two articulators can be quantified as phase angles. In this framework, a phase angle of 180 degrees signifies a synchronized timing pattern. Phase angles less than 180 degrees indicate that the reference articulator lags behind the second articulator, while angles greater than 180 degrees suggest that the reference articulator leads the second articulator. This approach provides a nuanced representation of the spatiotemporal relationship between articulators during speech production (Fig. 1).

In their seminal work, Tuller and Kelso [12] examined inter-articulatory timing patterns of the upper and lower lip movements relative to the jaw cycle and reported relatively stable phase angles that were not affected by changes in jaw cycle duration (which were typically used as a proxy for quantifying a change in speaking rate or lexical stress). By contrast, the majority of studies conducted later on suggested that the timing patterns do change when talkers manipulate their lexical stress patterns or speech rate [7] [8] [9] 13, 14]. Specifically, speech demands that resulted in a shortening of the jaw cycle duration (e.g., fast speech, unstressed segments) also decreased the phase angle. Along those lines, speech demands that facilitated longer jaw cycle durations (e.g., slow speech, stressed segments) were associated with an increase in the phase angle, given that movement cycle duration serves as an established proxy measure for speaking rate. However, a great deal of variability was noted across talkers and across different articulator pairs [12, 15]. Specifically, while upper lip timing maintained a relatively stable phase angle range relative to the jaw, tongue tip phase angles exhibited much greater fluctuations [7, 13]. Nevertheless, tongue tip phasing remained sensitive to rate, consistently varying with jaw cycle duration despite a greater underlying variability.

Temporal latencies

Temporal latencies refer to the temporal lag between kinematic trajectory landmarks purely in the time domain, independent of the articulators' positions and velocities.

Previous studies investigated tongue tip -jaw and lower lip -jaw timing patterns during the production of disyllabic utterances in neurotypical talkers [9, 10, 11, 16, 17]. In these studies, temporal latencies were measured as the time elapsed from the onset of the jaw vowel cycle until the initiation of the upward movement (raising) of the tongue tip or lower lip toward the intervocalic consonant [9, 11]. An important finding of these studies was that a shortening of the jaw opening-closing cycle due to speaking rate or lexical stress manipulations systematically reduced the movement onset latency of the second articulator as well. These precise temporal regularities held true across different sets of articulators (tongue-jaw and lip-jaw synergies), which suggests that synergistic timing patterns of vocal tract constriction formations are precisely controlled regardless of where in the vocal tract the constriction occured [10, 16, 17].

Inter-Articulatory Timing Patterns in Talkers with Dysarthria

Only a limited number of kinematic studies are currently available that have examined inter-articulatory timing patterns and the majority of them investigated neurotypical talkers. However, it is commonly assumed that inter-articulatory timing patterns are disrupted in talkers with dysarthria, a neurogenic motor speech disorder that arises when one or more neural components of the speech motor system are affected by a neurological condition. Rather than presenting as a uniform deficit, dysarthria encompasses distinct subtypes depending on the neural components that are affected. In amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), for example, upper and lower motoneurons of bulbar and spinal muscles degenerate, which results in a mixed flaccid-spastic dysarthria. Hallmark symptoms are an abnormally slow speaking rate, imprecise consonants and distorted vowels, and a strained-strangled voice quality [18, 19]. Multiple sclerosis, by contrast, commonly affects the corticobulbar tract and cerebellar motor control circuits, which can result in a mixed dysarthria with spastic and ataxic components. Common perceptual speech characteristics of talkers with MS include a slow speaking rate, irregular articulatory breakdowns, consonant imprecision, and a breathy or harsh voice quality [18, 20]. In other diseases, such as Parkinson's disease (PD) and Huntington's disease (HD) basal ganglia control circuits are commonly affected. PD typically results in hypokinetic dysarthria, which is associated with an accelerated speech rate, short rushes of speech, reduced vocal loudness or loudness decay, a breathy or harsh voice quality, and consonant imprecision [20]. Finally, speech disturbances in Huntington's disease (HD) typically manifest as hyperkinetic dysarthria and are characterized by involuntary movement intrusions, a variable speech rate, consonant imprecision, and irregular articulatory breakdowns.

Only few kinematic research studies are currently available on inter-articulatory timing patterns in neurogenic populations, such as individuals with PD and ALS.

These studies have provided crucial insights into how two neuropathologies can differentially alter inter-articulatory timing patterns [21, 22]. Specifically, Weismer et al. (2003) investigated labial and lingual timing patterns by means of temporal latencies.

They reported that overall timing patterns of the tongue and lips remained largely unaffected in PD; however, individuals with ALS exhibited a significantly wider range and greater disruption of lip and tongue timing patterns [22]. Extending these observations to a spatiotemporal metric, Rong and Heidrick (2021) found that relative phase relationships between the tongue and jaw served as robust markers of bulbar involvement, with relative phasing achieving high diagnostic accuracy in differentiating individuals with ALS from healthy controls [21].

Beyond neurodegenerative conditions, there is only one kinematic study currently available that examined inter-articulatory timing patterns in talkers with dysarthria resulting from traumatic brain injury. That study found no significant group-level differences in inter-articulatory timing patterns between nine talkers with traumatic brain injuries and nine typical talkers [23]. Nevertheless, an inspection of individual performance patterns revealed disrupted inter-articulatory timing patterns in those talkers with traumatic brain injury who displayed the most severe dysarthria symptoms. Given the small sample size, this study offers very limited insights into how inter-articulatory timing patterns vary more broadly across of talkers with dysarthria.

Given the limited kinematic literature on speech motor control in talkers with dysarthria, the current study sought to investigate inter-articulatory timing patterns of the tongue and the jaw in talkers with dysarthria with different neurological conditions [i.e., PD, MS, ALS, HD]. The comparisons across these four disease types are particularly interesting and important because these diseases are associated with different neuropathologies (i.e., differential disruptions to the basal ganglia control circuits in PD versus HD; disruptions to the direct motor pathway in MS and ALS, but additional cerebellar control circuit disruptions in MS). Thus, these four different neurological conditions may give rise to disease-specific deviancies in interarticulatory timing patterns. It is especially important to investigate the interarticulatory timing patterns of the tongue body and jaw in these talkers because tongue and jaw motor performance deficits have been reported in talkers with PD, HD, MS, and ALS [24] [25] [26] [27] [28] [29] [30]. For example, talkers with ALS are thought to produce excessive jaw movements to compensate for impaired tongue muscles during speech production. An overreliance on the jaw may result in more synchronized tongue and jaw movements in these talkers as indicated by phase angles close to 180 degrees. By contrast, talkers with MS, PD, and HD may exhibit less synchronized tongue and jaw movements considering that lesions in cerebellar and basal ganglia motor control circuits have been associated with the disruptions of temporal patterns during speech production [26, 30]. Thus, these talkers may have phase angles that are below or above 180 degrees. However, it is also possible that inter-articulatory timing patterns of talkers with MS and ALS differ from those of talkers with PD due to differences in their speaking rate.

Based on the known speech motor profiles of different dysarthria subtypes, distinct timing disruptions may exist. That is, talkers with PD may exhibit inter-articulatory timing patterns that mimic the timing patterns of healthy controls when asked to speak at an accelerated rate (fast speech), while talkers with MS and ALS may exhibit timing patterns that mimic those of controls when asked to speak at a reduced rate (slow speech). Finally, the speech rate of talkers with HD is commonly described as variable [24], which may disrupt inter-articulatory timing patterns of these talkers in unpredictable ways.

In sum, the aims of the current study were: • Aim 1: To characterize speaking rate of talkers with dysarthria, we determined between-group differences in tongue body cycle durations across clinical groups and controls • Aim 2: To characterize the reliance on the jaw in talkers with dysarthria, we determined between-group differences in jaw amplitudes across clinical groups and controls • Aim 3: To characterize inter-articulatory timing patterns of the tongue and jaw in talkers with dysarthria, we determined between-group differences in phase angles across clinical groups and controls • Aim 4: To determine if differences in speaking rate account for differences in inter-articulatory timing patterns in talkers with dysarthria, we evaluated the association between tongue body cycle durations and phase angles.

• Aim 5: To determine if differences in the reliance on the jaw account for differences in inter-articulatory timing profiles in talkers with dysarthria we evaluated the association between jaw amplitude and phase angle.

Speech Material and Data Collection

Kinematic speech data were recorded with an electromagnetic articulograph (AG 501; Carstens Medizinelektronik GmbH). Small sensors were attached to the articulators to track their movements during speech. That is, one sensor was attached with a small amount of Stomahesive® putty to the gumline of the lower central incisors to track jaw movements. Additional sensors were placed at midline on the vermillion border of the upper and lower lips using dental adhesive (Periacryl 90, GluStitch). Two sensors were placed with dental adhesive on the tongue. One tongue sensor was attached approximately 1 cm posterior to the tongue tip to track its movements. The other tongue sensor was placed approximately 4 cm posterior to the tongue tip to assess tongue body movements. Three additional sensors were placed on plastic goggles (head right, head center, head left) that the participants wore during data collection. These sensors served as reference sensors for head correction [28]. Finally, three sensors were placed on a biteplate, which participants were asked to hold between their front teeth to record a short rest file.

After attaching all sensors, participants were engaged in conversations to provide them the opportunity to familiarize themselves with the presence of the sensors in the oral cavity. Afterwards, the experimental protocol was started. As part of a larger experimental protocol, participants were asked to produce the sentence "Buy Kaia a kite", which was displayed on a computer monitor in front of them. All participants were asked to repeat the sentence ten times at their typical speaking rate. "Kaia" was produced as /'kai. ə/. If a participant did not produce the word "Kaia" correctly, the examiner modeled the correct pronunciation and ask the participant to repeat the task.

Data processing

The raw data were converted into positional data using the CalcPos software (Carstens Medizintechnik GmbH). Then all positional data were head-corrected, down-sampled to 250 Hz, and transposed into a head-based coordinate system using the NormPos software (Carstens Medizintechnik GmbH). Data were filtered with a low-pass filter at 15 Hz and displayed and further processed in SMASH, a MATLAB-based software program [34]. The data analysis focused on the first, stressed syllable of the word "Kaia", consisting of a stop consonant and a diphthong (/'kai. /). For the purpose of this study, only the tongue body and jaw movement in the vertical dimension (i.e., y-dimension) were considered.

Within the movement sequence of the tongue body during the syllable [kai], the onset of the jaw raising movement during the diphthong [ai] can be determined as an angle on the tongue body's phase plane. The segment [kai] was of particular interest in this study because, although the tongue body and jaw are anatomically coupled, the tongue and the jaw can independently reach the maximum vocal tract opening at the onset of the diphthong different times and, consequently, initiate the movements towards the maximum vocal tract closing at the offset of the diphthong at different times. Furthermore, the inter-articulatory timing patterns of the jaw and tongue body were also of interest because the velar place of articulation has been shown to be affected at the early stages of articulatory performance decline in talkers with neurodegenerative diseases [35].

We did not decouple the jaw from the tongue in the current study. An estimation of independent tongue movement based on linear subtraction of the jaw movement would have resulted in an overestimation of the jaw contribution due to the jaw sensor's location being more anterior from the center of the jaw rotation than the posterior tongue sensor's location [36]. Consequently, the phase angles reported here describe the relationship of the jaw relative to the tongue body+jaw composite movement rather than to an independently derived tongue body movement. While individual differences in the intrinsic contribution of the jaw to tongue movement could not be fully captured without decoupling, pilot trajectory inspections confirmed consistent asynchrony between jaw and tongue positional minima. Thus, the phase angles in this study represent only the relative phasing of the jaw in relation to the tongue body+jaw movement during the syllable [kai].

Contrary to most previous studies in which the jaw cycle was used as a reference, in the current study the reference for the phase angle calculation was the tongue body cycle. For the selected target word, the jaw could not be used as a reference because its movement did not offer a reliable landmark for the cycle offset. Instead, the vertical movement of the tongue body during the sequence [kai] offered reliable kinematic landmarks. The local maxima in the tongue movement were associated with the constriction of [k] and the maximum closing at the offset of the diphthong [ai] and the local minimum was associated with the maximum vocal tract opening at the onset of the diphthong [ai]. However, for two talkers with ALS and one talker with HD, the tongue body movement of all ten sentence repetitions did not contain the second local maximum. Another talker with HD could only produce the second local maximum in two of ten sentence repetitions. When the second local maximum was missing, the diphthong was either severely distorted or replaced by the vowel [a] and a glottal stop. In these cases, phase angles could not be calculated.

Using SMASH [34], the vertical tongue movement and the vertical jaw movement were trimmed using the local maxima the tongue body associated with [k] and the offset of the diphthong [ai] as the onset and offset of the tongue body cycle. These local maxima were algorithmically determined in SMASH based on the zero crossing of the velocity signal of the vertical tongue movement (see also Fig 2). In instances where multiple local maxima were observed, the last local maximum before the opening movement was selected for the onset and the first local maximum after the closing movement was selected for the offset. The trimmed trajectories of the tongue and jaw were saved as a new file. A custom-written Matlab script was then used to calculate the velocity signal of the trimmed tongue body movements. To ensure precise temporal alignment when extracting the tongue velocity at the time of maximum jaw opening, the MATLAB script employed central differencing via the gradient function to avoid time-shift artifacts associated with the standard diff function that is used in SMASH. velocity signals during the sentence "Buy Kaia a kite" based on a female neurotypical talker. The black circles mark the positional minimum during the maximum opening of the tongue and jaw at the diphthong onset. After spatial normalization of the tongue position and velocity signal, the normalized tongue body position and velocity value at the time of the jaw reaching the positional minimum (marked with red circles) was extracted to calculate the phase angle.

To compute phase angles, position and velocity signals were mapped onto a phase plane. Because position (mm) and velocity (mm/s) rely on different physical units, both variables were normalized to the numerical interval [-1, +1] within each movement cycle prior to applying the inverse tangent function, following the classic procedure described by Kelso et al [6]. Thus, the spatial normalization of the tongue body positions was calculated for the complete tongue body cycle while the velocity was normalized on the half-cycle in which the jaw reached the positional minimum at the maximum jaw opening [6]. As a result, the normalized tongue body cycle ranged in values from -1 to 1 with the positional minimum of the tongue at the onset of the diphthong [ai] nearing the minimum of -1 and the onset and offset of the tongue body cycle nearing the maximum of 1 (see Fig 3A). The normalized velocity ranged from 0 to -1 when the jaw reached the maximum opening position before the tongue and from 0 to 1 when the jaw reached the maximum opening position after the tongue (see Fig 3A). The same Matlab script that calculated the tongue velocity signal was used to spatially normalize the trimmed tongue body movement and tongue velocity signal as described in [6], extract the point of maximum jaw opening at the onset of the diphthong [ai], and calculate the resulting phase angle of the jaw relative to the tongue body (Equation 1).

A unit cycle is shown in Fig 3B which is centered at P (0,0) and has a radius of 1 defined by normalized position and velocity values. As can be seen in Fig 3B, the tongue position is plotted on the vertical axis while the tongue movement velocity is plotted on the horizontal axis [6]. The points P (velocity, position) describe the movement of the tongue body along the phase plane within the unit cycle. The direction of movement in the current study is counterclockwise. Thus, the tongue body lowering from [k] to the maximum opening position at the onset of the diphthong /ai/ is reflected in the movement from A to C via B (Fig 3B). The velocity is zero at A and C, as the tongue body reaches its highest point P (0,1) and its lowest point P (0,-1), respectively. At B, the tongue body reaches its local velocity maximum as the tongue body reaches its midpoint of the opening phase towards maximum tongue lowering. The tongue body raising from the onset to the offset of the diphthong [ai] is described by the movement from point C to point A via point D. At D, the midpoint of the tongue raising movement, the velocity reaches its local maximum again (Fig 3B).

Statistical analysis

For each participant, ten repetitions were analyzed unless there were not ten repetitions available due to accidental speech errors (e.g., mispronouncing the target word), miscounting of trials, or missing positional landmarks. In addition to the already mentioned missing second local maximum of the tongue body, there were four instances where the jaw did not produce a local minimum during the tongue body cycle. In these cases, the jaw was continuously raised during the tongue body cycle. These trials were also excluded. Missing or excluded productions were evenly distributed across the cohort rather than clustered in a small number of participants.

In total, 1,066 out of 1,100 productions (96.9%) were submitted to the statistical analysis. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Version 32.0.

To select the appropriate statistical tests to assess group effects on tongue body cycle durations, jaw opening amplitudes, and phase angles, data distributions were evaluated for normality, skewness and influential outliers using visual inspections (i.e., histograms and box plots). We found that the distributions for the jaw opening amplitudes were positively skewed. Therefore, we used a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) with gamma distributions and log link to test for between-group differences in jaw opening amplitudes. In the GLLM we used the residual method to determine the degrees of freedom and a robust estimation to handle violations of the model assumptions.

Phase angle distributions and tongue body cycle durations were not skewed but included some extreme values. Extreme values were defined as values that were more than three times of the interquartile range away from the edge of the first or third quartile (e.g., [37, 38]). We removed these extreme values. Specifically, for tongue body cycle durations, we removed one value of one talker with HD and one value for one talker with PD. For phase angles, we removed a total of three values in the control group, six values in the HD group, and one value in the MS group. We then submitted the tongue body cycle durations and phase angles to separate linear mixed models (LMMs). In each model (GLMM, LMM), group was submitted as a fixed factor and repeated trials were submitted as a random factor. If the group effect was found to be significant at p < .05, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were completed and the critical alpha level was adjusted for multiple comparisons using Bonferroni corrections.

Because sex was confounded with dysarthria severity in the four clinical groups, sex could not be tested as a covariate in the full models. However, when assessing a potential sex effects in typical talkers, we did not find any significant sex effects on tongue body cycle durations [F(1, 334) = 1.806, p = .180], jaw opening amplitudes [F(1,334) = 1.512, p = .220], and phase angles [F(1,31.976) = 0.567, p =.457].

Similarly, because age was confounded by disease type and dysarthria severity in the current study, age could also not be tested as a covariate in the full models.

However, we assessed potential age effects on tongue body cycle durations, jaw opening amplitudes, and phase angles by submitting each control talker's age and their mean tongue body cycle duration, jaw opening amplitude, or phase angle to a bivariate correlation analysis. None of the bivariate correlational analyses yielded a signification correlation [duration x age: r(34) = .163, p = .357; jaw amplitude x age: r(34) = .055, p = .756; phase angle x age: r(34) = .093, p = .601].

To determine if phase angles varied systematically with tongue body cycle duration or jaw amplitude, bivariate correlation analyses were conducted across all talkers as well as talkers within each group. For the correlation analyses, the normality of the parameters was assessed for the entire sample as well as for each group using the Shapiro-Wilk test. The Shapiro-Wilk test yielded significant findings for durations, jaw opening amplitudes, and phase angles when all talkers were considered (p < .046) as well as for jaw opening amplitudes of talkers with MS, HD, and typical talkers (p < .001), and for phase angles of CON (p = .025). Therefore, a Spearman correlation was conducted when considering all talkers, when correlating jaw opening amplitudes with phase angles in talkers with PD, MS, HD, and CON, and when correlating duration with phase angles in CON. Otherwise, Pearson's correlations were conducted.

No significant correlations were observed within the MS group [rS(18) = .044, p = .861], HD group [r S (17) =.098, p = .708], ALS group [r P (17) = .072, p = .782], and the PD group [r P (24) = .200, p = .349].

Discussion

This study investigated inter-articulatory timing patterns of the tongue body and jaw in talkers with different neurological conditions (i.e., PD, HD, MS, and ALS). First, we tested for between-group differences in tongue body cycle durations and jaw opening amplitudes. Findings for between-group differences in tongue body cycle duration aligned with previous reports of acoustic studies on speaking rate abnormalities in talkers with PD, ALS, MS and CON [e.g., [39] [40] [41]. That is, talkers with ALS and MS had significantly longer tongue body cycle durations indicating that their speaking rates were slower than those of talkers with PD and CON. Findings of comparable tongue body cycle durations in talkers with HD and CON differed from previous reports of prolonged segment durations and slowed speaking rates in talkers with HD [37, 38]; however, they aligned with reports of a dysarthria subgroups of talkers with HD with an unaffected speaking rate [36]. Furthermore, findings for jaw opening amplitudes were congruent with previous kinematic studies [42]. That is, talkers with ALS had significantly larger jaw opening amplitudes than talkers with PD and CON and tended to be larger than those of talkers with MS and HD.

With regards to inter-articulatory timing patterns, we examined phase angles and expected them to be smaller or larger than 180 degrees in talkers with HD, MS, and PD due to less synchronized movements given that their neuropathologies involve impairment of the basal ganglia or cerebellar control circuits. In addition, phase angles of talkers with ALS were expected to be close to 180 degrees given their potential overreliance on the jaw and limited independent tongue movements during speech. However, our findings did not support this hypothesis. We found that phase angles of talkers with ALS were significantly greater than those of talkers with MS, PD, HD, and typical talkers. Although talkers with ALS produced abnormally large jaw movements, they did not exhibit more synchronized tongue and jaw movements than talkers with MS, PD, HD, or typical talkers. Specifically, their phase angles were on average 8.4 degrees above 180 degrees while talkers with HD, MS, PD and typical talkers were on average between 5.3 degrees (talkers with HD) and 8.3 degrees (talkers with MS) below 180 degrees. A weak positive correlation was observed between jaw opening amplitudes and phase angles when considering all talkers and when considering only typical talkers. This finding suggests that the size of the jaw movement cannot be the main driver of the between-group differences in phase angles, especially because the weak positive association between jaw opening amplitudes and phase angles observed across all talkers was predominantly driven by the articulatory performance of typical talkers, not by talkers with ALS.

Alternatively, we examined if phase angles vary with speech rate abnormalities across these clinical groups given previous findings of speech rate effects on phase angles in typical talkers. That is, talkers with MS and ALS, and potentially also talkers with HD, were expected to differ in their phase angles from talkers with PD and typical talkers due to their speech rate differences. However, our findings did not support this hypothesis. Only talkers with ALS but not talkers with MS differed in their phase angles from those of talkers with PD and typical talkers. No significant association between cycle duration and phase angles were observed when considering all talkers or when considering only talkers within the same group. Our findings highlight an important distinction between habitual speaking rate differences and experimentally elicited rate manipulations. While previous studies demonstrated that cued changes in speaking rate (e.g., instructing a speaker to speak faster or slower) can systematically alter phase angles, our results indicate that habitual cycle durations do not exert the same driving effect. Specifically, despite significant differences in habitual tongue body cycle durations across clinical and control groups, no significant associations between cycle duration and phase angles were observed.

This suggests that a speaker's intrinsic, habitual rate, whether typical or slowed due to pathology, does not inherently constrain inter-articulatory timing in the same manner as a targeted rate adjustment paradigm.

Disease-specific inter-articulatory timing pattern in talkers with ALS

Talkers with ALS had significantly greater phase angles than talkers with HD, MS, PD, and typical talkers. On average, phase angles of talkers with ALS were greater than 180 degrees, which indicates that the tongue body commonly reached the positional minimum at the diphthong onset before the jaw. In fact, only five of the 17 talkers with ALS (29%) did not follow this pattern and had phase angles smaller than 180 degrees. By contrast, the average phase angles of typical talkers, talkers with MS, PD, and HD were smaller than 180 degrees, which indicates that the jaw commonly reached the target before the tongue body. More specifically, only ten of the 34 CON (29%), two of the 18 talkers with MS (11%), five of the 24 talkers with PD (21%), and six of the 17 talkers with HD (35%) did not follow this pattern and had phase angles that were greater than 180 degrees. Thus, with regards to the order in which the articulators reached the target, the inter-articulatory timing patterns were fairly robust within each group.

It is important to point out that because the current study used the tongue body as the reference articulator and not the jaw, phase angles should be smaller at longer tongue body cycle durations to align with previous findings on cued slow speech effects [13]. That is because phase angles below 180 degrees indicate that the jaw leads the tongue when the tongue body is the reference (current study) whereas phase angles above 180 degree indicate that the jaw leads the tongue when the jaw is the reference sensor (previous studies). Therefore, the observed phase angles of most talkers with ALS in the current study do not align with the inter-articulatory timing pattern previously observed for cued slow speech in typical talkers.

The discrepancies between the findings for phase angles under cued slow speech in typical talkers and those for phase angles of talkers with ALS who spoke at their habitual albeit abnormally slow rate suggest that the absolute movement duration, or the absolute speech rate, may not determine a talker's inter-articulatory timing pattern. This notion is supposed by our correlational analysis of tongue body cycle durations and phase angles, which did not yield a significant finding. Thus, it may rather be the speech task (i.e., the execution of a cued slow speech rate) that determines the inter-articulatory timing pattern. Indeed, previous studies revealed speech rate effects on phase angles in typical talkers [8, 13, 15]. However, in these studies, talkers were cued to increase or reduce their speaking rate. In the current study, talkers were asked to speak at their habitual rate, which resulted a wide range of tongue body cycle durations across talkers due to their dysarthria. Especially talkers with MS and ALS had significantly longer tongue body cycle durations than CON, talkers with PD, and talkers with HD. However, the absolute tongue body cycle duration, which indirectly characterized a talker's speech rate, was not predictive of their inter-articulatory timing pattern. To better understand the unique interarticulatory timing pattern of talkers with ALS, future studies could examine if speaking rate modifications elicit changes in the phase angles of talkers with ALS.

Such insights would help determine if talkers with ALS are constrained to their specific inter-articulatory timing pattern due to their disease or if they are able to modify their inter-articulatory timing patterns in response to various speech task demands.

Similar inter-articulatory timing patterns across talkers with MS, PD, and typical talkers

No significant differences in phase angles were found between typical talkers, talkers with MS, HD, and PD. The lack of significant differences in inter-articulatory timing patterns between typical talkers and talkers with PD and MS contradicts findings of disrupted timing patterns of limb motor control studies [43] [44] [45], whereas the lack of group-level differences in HD may be attributable to high individual variability which will be discussed later on. The findings of the current study may suggest that the speech motor system is spared from the negative impact of the basal ganglia and cerebellar neuropathologies while the limb motor system is affected. However, it is also possible that most of the talkers with dysarthria in the current study were too mild in their dysarthria severity to observe disrupted timing patterns within the speech motor system. For example, Bartle and colleagues [23] reported that only the most severe talkers with dysarthria showed disrupted tongue-jaw timing patterns.

Therefore, we compared the data of the talkers with moderate and severe dysarthria to those with mild dysarthria within each group; however, no systematic pattern could be detected. The four panels of Fig 8 illustrate the lack of a relationship between SIT scores and phase angles. However, given the limited number of talkers with moderate and severe dysarthria in each clinical group, future studies are needed to investigate timing patterns of limb and speech movements side by side and include more individuals with moderate and severe dysarthria to better understand the impact of basal ganglia and cerebellar pathologies on timing patterns across the speech and limb motor system. group.

Highly variable inter-articulatory timing patterns in talkers with HD

Although not evident in the group level plots, we would like to point out differences in inter-articulatory timing patterns at the individual performance level. Specifically, we noted that phase angles varied greatly across repetitions within each talker with HD.

For example, the phase angle's coefficient of variation across trials was on average 11.4 degrees for talkers with HD whereas the coefficient of variation for talkers with PD, MS, ALS and typical talkers was on average much lower (3.9 degrees in ALS, 6.4 degrees in MS, 5.8 degrees in PD, and 7.5 degrees in CON). This observation suggests that although phase angles of talkers with HD did not significantly differ from the other clinical groups and typical talkers, the basal ganglia pathology may negatively impact the ability to produce a consistent inter-articulatory timing pattern in talkers with HD.

Quantifying inter-articulatory timing patterns with phase angles

In the current study, we chose to characterize inter-articulatory timing patterns by measuring phase angles. Phase angles are expressed relative to 360 degrees regardless of the duration of the cycle and are heavily influenced by the shape of the movement trajectory towards and away from the target position (i.e., maximum opening position). However, it should be noted that phase angles do not provide the same insights as the latency measure (i.e., temporal lag). Although a greater latency is typically associated with a phase angle that is much smaller or larger than 180 degrees, a specific phase angle can have slightly different latencies and a specific latency can have slightly different phase angles. That is because the shape of the movement trajectory of the articulator that serves as the reference signal impacts the phase angle more than the latency itself. For example, if the shape of the tongue movement trajectory is characterized by a plateau around the maximum opening position at the onset of the diphthong [ai], phase angles will be closer to 180 degrees even if there is a relatively small lag between the jaw and tongue. By contrast, if the movement trajectory of the tongue is characterized by more "peakedness" towards and away from the maximum opening position, relatively small lags between the tongue and jaw can produce phase angles that are disproportionally smaller or larger than 180 degrees.

Due to these concerns, we informally tested the strength of association between latencies and phase angles by extracting the lag durations between the tongue and the jaw reaching the maximum opening position at the onset of the diphthong [ai] together with the phase angles across all repetitions of all talkers. Each talker's mean latencies and phase angles were submitted to Pearson's correlational analysis. We found a strong positive correlation of r(110) = .88, p < .001 between latencies and phase angles across talkers and similarly strong correlations within each group [PD: r(24) = .94, p < .001, HD: r(17) = .85, p < .001; MS: r(18) = .88, p < .001; ALS: r(17) = .86, p < .001; CON: r(34) = .92, p < .001]. Thus, the reported phase angles in the current study can provide good insights in the extent to which tongue and jaw movements are synchronized.

Study limitations and future directions

Previous studies investigated inter-articulatory timing patterns by expressing the timing of the articulator of interest (tongue, upper lip, lower lip) with respect to the jaw cycle . In the current study, the tongue cycle was taken as reference to which the timing of the jaw reaching the maximal opening of the jaw at the onset of the diphthong /ai/ was expressed, which limits the comparability to previous studies.

However, with cycle durations of typical talkers in the current study being comparable to those of a slow speech condition in previous studies, the phase angles of our typical talkers were well-aligned with those of previous studies when accounting for the different reference articulator [8, 13]. That is, the average phase angle for our control group was about 172 degrees indicating that the jaw led the tongue body. The average phase angle of ten talkers in Masapolo and Nittrouer's study [8] was about 200 degrees, also indicating that the jaw led the tongue tip in typical talkers.

Mathematically, phase relations measure relative spatiotemporal coupling regardless of which articulator is assigned as the reference frame. Therefore, we believe that our approach of using the tongue body as the reference was well-suited to study interarticulatory timing patterns, specifically to explore the jaw-tongue coupling patterns.

However, future studies should investigate inter-articulatory timing patterns with the traditional approach of using the jaw as the reference signal to replicate and further extend current study findings on talkers with dysarthria.

Furthermore, a major difference in the experimental design between the current and previous studies was that the target utterance did not contain two stop consonants, such as [tad, bab, bak, tak], but [kai]. However, the offset of the diphthong [ai] at the end of the tongue body cycle also required a vocal tract constriction that was consistently executed by the tongue body. Although, the jaw was consistently lowered to produce the maximum opening position at the onset of the diphthong [ai], it was not consistently raised during the diphthong. However, the jaw was always elevated after reaching the maximum opening position at the onset of the diphthong [ai] and eventually supported the constriction of [k] in "kite". Nevertheless, due to the presence of reliable landmarks for the tongue body and jaw to reach the maximum opening position and initiate the upward movement towards vocal tract constriction at the offset of the diphthong [ai], we argue that the sequence [kai] was also well-suited to study inter-articulatory timing patterns.

Another major difference between the current study and previous studies is that we could not decouple the jaw from the tongue. Decoupling jaw movements from the posterior tongue is challenging because the jaw movements are recorded more anteriorly in the vocal tract where, due to jaw rotation, the movement amplitudes of the jaw are larger than at the location of the posterior tongue. A linear subtraction of jaw movement from the posterior tongue, which is an approach commonly used to decouple the lower lip or the tongue tip from the jaw, would not yield accurate data of independent posterior tongue movement [36]. Nevertheless, the timing of the tongue and jaw reaching the maximum opening position at the onset of the diphthong [ai] was asynchronous in most cases (983 out of 1066 productions, 92%) indicating that independent tongue movements are reflected in the tongue movement trajectory.

However, the shape of the tongue movement could be partially affected by the jaw in the current study and this could impact the specific value of the phase angle.

However, it would not affect whether a phase angle was smaller or larger than 180 degrees because the order of the articulators reaching the target and not the specific tongue position and velocity determines whether a phase angle is smaller or larger than 180 degrees (i.e., if the jaw is reaching the target before the tongue, the phase angle is smaller than 180 degrees if the tongue body is the reference signal).

Therefore, the overall finding of the current study, which suggest that talkers with ALS differ in their inter-articulatory timing patterns from talkers with PD, MS, HD, and typical talkers was primarily driven by the order of the articulators reaching the maximum opening position at the onset of the diphthong [ai]. Nevertheless, it is important to develop a decoupling algorithm that can accurately determine independent posterior tongue movements for future studies to replicate the current findings.

In the current study, we did not control for medication status in talkers with HD and surgical intervention (DBS) in talkers with PD. However, we did not observe any systematic differences in phase angles due to the medication status in talkers with HD. One talker with HD on medication produced the largest phase angles of the HD group while two other talkers with HD on medication had very small phase angles and the remaining three talkers with HD on medication fell somewhere in the middle of the group's distribution. Similarly, two of the three talkers with PD who had undergone DBS treatment had phase angles comparable to those of talkers with PD who did not have DBS. The third talker with DBS produced the largest phase angles of all talkers with PD. Thus, no systematic pattern could be detected that suggest medication status or DBS could be considered confounding factors.

Finally, future studies are needed to examine inter-articulatory timing patterns across a wide range of phonetic contexts to determine if current findings generalize or if they are stimulus-specific. Future studies should also include the tongue tip and jaw as well as the upper lip and lower lip or jaw as articulators of interest to determine if inter-articulatory timing patterns reveal similar disease-related group differences.

Conclusion

This study investigated temporal, spatial and spatiotemporal alterations between tongue body and jaw movements across several diseases. While tongue body cycle durations were significantly prolonged in ALS and MS, jaw amplitudes were enlarged specifically in ALS. Inter-articulatory phase angles differentiated ALS from all other cohorts. Talkers with ALS exhibited significantly larger phase angles compared to PD, HD, and MS groups, whose phase angles remained comparable to typical talkers. This highlights distinct spatiotemporal timing profiles in ALS that evolve largely independently of speech rate or jaw amplitude variations.
