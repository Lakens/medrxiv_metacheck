Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Background

There is limited research on persistent or recurrent depression into young adulthood. We examined the prevalence of recurrent/persistent depression in young adults, and associations with childhood trauma/adversity and systemic inflammation, and if inflammatory markers mediated associations between childhood trauma and subsequent depression.

Methods

ALSPAC participants with ICD-10 depression assessments at ages 18, 24 and 30 were classified as never depressed, transiently depressed (one episode), or persistently/recurrently depressed (≥2 episodes). Trauma/adversity from birth to age 16/17 was characterised by type, timing and burden. Interleukin-6 (IL-6) and C-reactive protein (CRP) were measured at ages 9, 16, 18 and 24. Analyses used multinomial logistic regression, linear regression and causal mediation models.

Results

Among 2,014 participants, 78.9% were never depressed, 15.0% had transient depression and 6.1% had persistent/recurrent depression. Trauma/adversity was more strongly associated with persistent/recurrent depression (RRR 3.17, 95% CI 1.98-5.06; FDR-P=6.2×10⁻⁶) than transient depression, with a dose-response pattern and stronger associations for threat adversities. Middle-tolate childhood (11-17 yrs) emerged as a sensitive period for persistent/recurrent (RRR 2.85, 95% CI 1.47-5.52) and transient depression (RRR 1.85, 95% CI 1.25-2.73). Trauma/adversity was associated with modestly higher IL-6 at age 24 (all FDR-adjusted P<0.05); CRP associations did not survive multipletesting correction. Neither marker mediated trauma/adversity associations with persistent/recurrent depression.

Conclusion

Persistent/recurrent depression affects approximately 1 in 20 young adults. Childhood trauma/adversity, particularly during middle-to-late childhood, was associated with persistent/recurrent depression, but measured inflammatory proteins did not mediate this association. Further work is needed to understand biological mechanisms underlying the embedding early life psychosocial stress.

2 Introduction

Depression commonly emerges in early life and often follows a chronic, relapsing-remitting course. Approximately 35% of mood disorders begin before age 25 (Solmi et al., 2022), with depression typically emerging earlier (Bromet et al., 2011). Persistent, recurrent depression during adolescence to early adulthood imposes significant personal and clinical burden, including impaired social, educational, and occupational functioning (Thapar et al., 2022). However, the prevalence of persistent/recurrent depression in young adults during the second and third decades of life remain poorly characterised. It is also unclear how established psychosocial and biological risk factors, such as childhood trauma/adversity and inflammation (Mac Giollabhui et al., 2025; Nelson et al., 2017; Watson et al., 2025), for depression relate to persistent/recurrent depression into adulthood.

Childhood trauma/adversity, often assessed using adverse childhood experience (ACE) measures (Felitti et al., 1998), has a strong, dose-response relationship with the risk and severity of depression throughout life (Flouri et al., 2020; Iob et al., 2022; LeMoult et al., 2020; Nanni et al., 2012; Tan & Mao, 2023). Beyond cumulative exposure, the nature and developmental timing of adversity may influence subsequent depression. Threat-related adversities have shown stronger associations with elevated depressive symptom trajectories than other ACEs (Iob et al., 2022) and maltreatment has been associated with a more persistent/recurrent course of depression with poorer treatment outcomes (Nanni et al., 2012). However, the extent to trauma/adversity type and timing are differentially associated with transient or persistent/recurrent depression into adulthood remains unclear.

Altered systemic inflammation may link ACEs with the development and persistence of depression. A meta-analysis of longitudinal studies associated higher circulating interleukin-6 (IL-6) and C-reactive protein (CRP) concentrations with subsequent depressive symptoms, although associations were small (Mac Giollabhui et al., 2021). In the Avon Longitudinal Study of Parents and Children (ALSPAC), higher childhood IL-6 was associated with depression at age 18 (Khandaker et al., 2014), while higher IL-6 at age 9 was associated with persistent depressive symptoms between ages 10 and 19, but not adolescentonset symptoms (Khandaker et al., 2018). These findings suggest that inflammation may precede depression and be particularly relevant to persistent symptoms, supporting investigation of its potential mediating role between ACEs and depression.

Evidence linking childhood trauma/adversity to systemic inflammation is mixed, with associations varying by adversity type, sex, inflammatory marker and age at assessment. Lacey et al. reported that parental separation or divorce was associated with higher IL-6, whereas several other adversities were associated with lower IL-6, with further differences according to the sex of the child (Lacey et al., 2020). Crick et al. found no evidence of associations between cumulative or individual ACEs and inflammatory marker glycoprotein acetyls (GlycA) from ages 8 to 24, but observed a small association between cumulative ACE exposure and GlycA among mothers at age 49 (Crick et al., 2022). Similarly, older adults reporting three or more ACEs retrospectively had chronically elevated CRP compared with those reporting none (Iob et al., 2020). These findings suggest that inflammatory correlates of adversity may differ across the life course, although their biological significance remains uncertain. Further investigation is therefore needed to establish whether adversity-related inflammation helps explain transient or persistent/recurrent depression across young adulthood.

Despite evidence linking childhood adversity to depression, and separate evidence implicating inflammation in depression risk and further separate evidence linking trauma/adversity to inflammatory disturbances, relatively little is known about how trauma exposure, inflammatory biomarkers, and depression course are related within the same longitudinal framework into early adulthood. Inflammation may mediate the relationship between trauma/adversity and later depression, but current evidence is limited and focuses on IL-6 in childhood (up to age 10) (Flouri et al., 2020). Repeated assessments extending into the third decade of life provide an opportunity to distinguish depression identified at a single assessment from depression present at multiple assessments, and to examine whether these patterns share early-life risk factors. Understanding these associations could inform efforts to prevent enduring depression and clarify potential biological pathways linking childhood adversity with later mental health.

Using data from ALSPAC, a population-based prospective birth cohort, we have examined the longitudinal course of depression in young adults based on ICD-10 diagnosis of depression at ages 18, 24 and 30 years identifying those with transient (one episode) and persistent/recurrent (two or more episodes) depression. We have examined the associations of transient and persistent/recurrent depression with: i) childhood trauma/adversity including the impact of specific trauma types, cumulative exposure, and developmental timing of trauma exposure; and ii) systemic inflammatory marker levels in blood from childhood through to adulthood including any mediating effects of inflammation on the trauma-depression relationship.

3 Methods

3.1 Data Source

This study uses data from the ALSPAC cohort. Pregnant women resident in Avon, UK with expected dates of delivery between 1st April 1991 and 31 st December 1992 were invited to take part in ALSPAC. The initial number of pregnancies enrolled was 14,541. Of these initial pregnancies, 13,988 children were alive at 1 year of age. When the children were approximately 7 years old, an attempt was made to expand the initial sample by recruiting eligible participants who had not originally enrolled. The total sample available for analyses using data collected after age 7 increased to 15,447 pregnancies, resulting in 15,658 foetuses. Of these, 14,901 children were alive at 1 year of age (Boyd et al., 2013; Fraser et al., 2013; Northstone et al., 2019 Northstone et al., , 2026)). Study data were collected and managed using REDCap electronic data capture tools hosted at the University of Bristol (Harris et al., 2009). REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) is a secure, web-based software platform designed to support data capture for research studies. Please note that the study website contains details of all the data that is available through a fully searchable data dictionary and variable search tool (http://www.bristol.ac.uk/alspac/researchers/our-data/ ). Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the ALSPAC Ethics and Law Committee and the Local Research Ethics Committees. Informed consent for the use of all data collected was obtained from participants following the recommendations of the ALSPAC Ethics and Law Committee at the time. Participants can contact the study team at any time to retrospectively withdraw consent for their data to be used. Study participation is voluntary and during all data collection sweeps, information was provided on the intended use of data.

3.2 Assessment of trauma and adverse childhood experiences

Childhood trauma/adversity was assessed using two sets of variables available in ALSPAC: trauma variables and adverse childhood experience (ACE) variables. Using both sets enabled us to include a broader range of specific adversities while also examining exposure during distinct developmental periods. Where exposures overlapped, trauma variables were used because they provided periodspecific measures. All included variables are described in Supplementary Table 1.

Croft et al. derived trauma variables from 121 questions across parent-completed questionnaires and participant self-reports (Croft et al., 2019). These captured any trauma and specific trauma types from birth to age 17 and within early childhood (0-5 yrs), middle childhood (5-11 yrs), and adolescence (11-17 yrs). Types included physical, sexual, and emotional abuse; emotional neglect; domestic violence; and bullying.

Houtepen et al. derived ACE variables from 541 questionnaire items covering ages 0-16 years (Houtepen et al., 2018). These captured ten classic ACE categories: sexual, physical, and emotional abuse; emotional neglect; parental substance misuse; parental mental illness or suicide attempt; violence between parents; parental separation; bullying; and parental criminal conviction. Data before age eight were predominantly parent-reported; participants also provided self-reports thereafter.

3.3 Assessment of systemic inflammatory markers

Levels of CRP were measured in blood samples collected at ages 9, 16, 18 and 24 years, and IL-6 levels were measured in blood samples collected at ages 9 and 24 (see Supplementary Methods 1 for further information). CRP concentrations (mg/L) were measured using ELISA and standardised to Z-scores prior to analysis. IL-6 (pg/mL) was also measured via ELISA and transformed at all age points, other than at age 24 (where no ELISA IL-6 variable is available) where levels were measured via Olink assay and expressed as NPX values on a log2 scale. At each assessment, CRP values >10 mg/L were set to missing for that timepoint only, before z-transformation, to reduce the influence of elevations potentially reflecting acute infection or other inflammatory conditions (Pearson et al., 2003).

3.4 Assessment of depression and definition of transient, and persistent/recurrent depression

Depression was assessed at ages 18, 24, and 30 years using the clinical interview schedule revised (CIS-R) (Lewis et al., 1992). CIS-R is a computerised, self-administered, semi-structured interview which assesses symptoms of common mental disorders occurring in the past two weeks and provides diagnosis of depression and other common mental disorders according to ICD-10 criteria. The CIS-R has been extensively validated and used for largescale epidemiological research and RCTs such as the NHS England Adult Psychiatric Morbidity Survey (Brugha et al., 1999; McManus et al., 2016).

Longitudinal depression groups were defined using ICD-10 depression diagnoses at ages 18, 24, and 30 years. Participants were classified as never depressed (no diagnosis at any assessment), transiently depressed (a diagnosis at one assessment only), or persistently/recurrently depressed (a diagnosis at two or more assessments).

The main analysis included participants with depression status available at all three assessments and those whose longitudinal classification could be determined despite missing data. Specifically, participants with depression at two assessments and missing status at the third were classified as persistently/recurrently depressed, as the missing value could not alter their classification (see Supplementary Methods 2).

ICD-10 depression diagnoses at ages 18, 24, and 30 years were also examined as separate binary outcomes, with participants classified as depressed or not depressed at each assessment.

3.5 Assessment of potential confounders

Potential confounders included sex, ethnicity, maternal age at delivery, maternal education and maternal social class. Biomarker analyses were additionally adjusted for blood collection variables (month of collection, time of collection and infection status). See Supplementary Methods 3 for further information.

3.6 Statistical Analysis

Analyses were conducted in RStudio version 4.6.0. Analysis code is available at GitHub in public repository (https://github.com/shepherdfreya/immunometabolic_depression).

Missing covariate data were imputed under a missing at random assumption using multiple imputation by chained equations, generating 20 imputed datasets (list of auxiliary methods used in Supplementary Table 1). Auxiliary sociodemographic, biological, and timing-related variables were included as predictors but were not imputed. Further details of the imputation procedure are provided in Supplementary Methods.

Results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) or relative risk ratios (RRRs) with 95% confidence intervals and p-values. RRRs represent the relative change in risk associated with each one-point increase in the predictor score. For analyses of depression trajectory groups, the Never group was used as the reference category, with estimates presented for Transient versus Never and Persistent/Recurrent versus Never. Models were fitted within each imputed dataset and pooled using Rubin's rules. P-values were adjusted for multiple testing using false discovery rate (FDR) correction within each analysis, model, and outcome contrast.

3.6.1 Associations of adversity/trauma with depression and inflammatory markers

Regression analyses examined associations of childhood ACEs and trauma with depression and circulating inflammatory markers. Multinomial logistic regression modelled longitudinal depression groups (reference: never depressed), binary logistic regression modelled depression at individual ages, and linear regression modelled CRP at ages 9, 16, 18 and 24 and IL-6 at ages 9 and 24 years.

Exposures included any trauma, cumulative ACE and trauma scores, individual adversity types across childhood, and trauma within developmental periods (0-5, 5-11 and 11-17 yrs).

Models were unadjusted and adjusted for sex, child ethnicity, maternal age at delivery, maternal education and maternal social class. Inflammatory marker models additionally adjusted for sample month, sample time and infection before the visit.

Inflammatory markers were measured between ages 9 and 24, whereas depression trajectories covered ages 18, 24 and 30. To preserve temporal ordering, biomarker-depression analyses used the nearest subsequent binary depression assessment.

3.6.2 Mediation analysis

We tested whether IL-6 or CRP at age 24 mediated associations between childhood adversity/trauma and ICD-10 depression at age 30. Exposure measures were selected based on associations with IL-6 that survived FDR correction; corresponding CRP mediation pathways were examined as exploratory analyses.

Causal mediation analyses were conducted using the parametric g-formula implemented in CMAverse. Models adjusted for child sex and ethnicity, maternal age at delivery, maternal education, maternal occupational social class, and blood collection month and time at age 24. BMI at age 24 was included as an intermediate confounder.

We estimated total effects, direct and indirect effects, and the corresponding proportion mediated. Models were fitted separately within each of 20 imputed datasets, with 1,000 bootstrap replications within each dataset.

3.6.3 Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses repeated biomarker analyses including participants with CRP concentrations greater than 10 mg/L. Analyses were also repeated using complete observed covariate data only, excluding imputed covariate values. Finally, analyses of longitudinal depression groups were repeated among participants with depression status available at all three assessments, excluding those classified as persistently/recurrently depressed based on two observed diagnoses and one missing assessment. See Supplementary Methods 5 for further description.

4 Results

4.1 Prevalence of persistent/recurrent depression diagnosis at ages 18, 24 and 30 years and associations with sociodemographic factors

Among all participants with valid ICD-10 depression status at each assessment, 355 of 4,513 (7.9%) met criteria for depression at age 18, compared with 340 of 3,852 (8.8%) at age 24 and 324 of 3,614 (9.0%) at age 30 (Supplementary Table 3). These age-specific samples included participants regardless of whether depression status was available at other assessments.

The longitudinal analysis included 2,014 participants: 1,966 with depression status available at all three assessments and 48 with depression at two assessments and missing status at the third, allowing unambiguous classification as persistently/recurrently depressed. Within this longitudinal sample, 1,590 (78.9%) were classified as never depressed, 302 (15.0%) as transiently depressed, and 122 (6.1%) as persistently/recurrently depressed (Figure 1).

Several sociodemographic characteristics differed across depression groups, including sex, maternal education, marital status and housing type (Supplementary Table 4). Persistent/recurrent depression was associated with female sex and mothers with lower education achievement, divorced or never married, living in council rented accommodation.

4.2 Associations of persistent/recurrent depression with childhood adversity/trauma

Results are summarised in Figure 2 and Supplementary Table 7. Overall, childhood trauma/adversity was consistently associated with increased risk of subsequent depression, with stronger associations observed for persistent/recurrent depression than transient depression.

Individuals exposed to any childhood trauma/adversity had over three times the risk of persistent/recurrent depression compared with those reporting no trauma (RRR 3.17, 95% CI 1.98-5.06, FDR-P = 6.2 × 10⁻⁶), while the association with transient depression was more modest (RRR 1.65, 95% CI 1.26-2.16, FDR-P = 2.46 × 10⁻ 3 ).

Among individual trauma/adversity types, threat related types had the strongest associations with depression. Physical cruelty (RRR 2.91, 95% CI 1.93-4.37, FDR-P = 2.25 × 10⁻⁶), sexual abuse (RRR 2.86, 95% CI 1.75-4.67, FDR-P = 9.07 × 10⁻ 5 ), physical abuse (RRR 2.45, 95% CI 1.53-3.92, FDR-P = 4.29 × 10⁻ 4 ) and emotional cruelty (RRR 2.37, 95% CI 1.55-3.59, FDR-P = 1.49 × 10⁻ 5 ) were all associated with a substantially increased risk of persistent/recurrent depression. These associations were consistently stronger than those observed for transient depression, although sexual abuse, physical abuse and physical cruelty also remained significantly associated with transient depression.

Continuous measures of cumulative trauma/adversity (ACE score and trauma count) showed evidence of dose-response in their associations with depression. Each additional reported trauma increased the risk of persistent/recurrent depression by 65% (RRR 1.65, 95% CI 1.43-1.91, FDR-P = 2.24 x 10⁻ 10 ), while each additional ACE score points increased risk by 44% (RRR 1.44, 95% CI 1.24-1.68, FDR-P = 6.2 x 10 -6 ).

Associations were also evident for transient depression, although effect sizes were consistently smaller. Adjustment for sex, ethnicity, maternal age, maternal education, and maternal social class had little impact on the observed associations, with adjusted and unadjusted models yielding very similar results.

4.3 Age of trauma exposure identifies middle and late childhood as sensitive periods for persistent/recurrent depression in adulthood

To investigate whether there is a sensitive period during which trauma is more strongly associated with subsequent depression, trauma exposure was examined across three developmental periods: 0-5, 5-11 and 11-17 yrs, using both binary and continuous measures (Figure 3, Supplementary Table 8). Overall, trauma exposure during middle childhood (5-11 yrs) and adolescence (11-17 yrs) was associated with later depression, whereas there was little evidence of an association for exposure during early childhood (0-5 yrs).

Both any trauma exposure and trauma count during middle childhood (5-11 yrs) were associated with persistent/recurrent depression (RRR = 2.49, 95% CI 1.27-4.90, FDR-P = 0.0119; and RRR = 1.34, 95% CI 1.07-1.67, FDR-P = 0.0151, respectively), with little evidence of associations with transient depression.

Trauma exposure in late childhood/adolescence (11-17 yrs) emerged as the period most strongly associated with later depression. Participants exposed to any trauma during this period had almost three times the risk of persistent/recurrent depression (RRR = 2.85, 95% CI 1.47-5.52, FDR-P = 0.00572), and nearly two-fold higher risk of the transient depression (RRR = 1.85, 95% CI 1.25-2.73, FDR-P = 0.00609), compared to the never depressed group. Similarly, each additional traumatic event experienced between 11-17 yrs was associated with an 83% increase in the risk of persistent/recurrent depression (RRR = 1.83, 95% CI 1.45-2.32, FDR-P = 1.42 × 10⁻⁶) and a 46% increase in the risk of transient depression (RRR = 1.46, 95% CI 1.23-1.74, FDR-P = 5.8 × 10⁻ 6 ).

To determine whether these findings simply reflected the temporal proximity between trauma exposure (11-17 yrs) and depression measurement (the first CIS-R assessment was at 18 yrs), we next examined the associations between trauma experienced between 11-17 yrs and depression at ages 18, 24 and 30 yrs (Figure 4). These results revealed that trauma occurring between 11-17 yrs had similar effects on depression at ages 18, 24 and 30 yrs. Using the continuous trauma count measure, we found that with each additional traumatic event was associated with increased odds of depression at age 18 (OR = 1.59, 95% CI 1.30-1.94, FDR-P = 2.32 x 10 -6 ), age 24 (OR = 1.84, 95% CI 1.52-2.22, FDR-P =1.23 x 10 -9 ), and age 30 (OR = 1.26, 95% CI 1.02-1.56, FDR-P = 4.64 x 10 -2 ). These findings suggest that the stronger associations observed for trauma during adolescence are unlikely to be explained solely by the temporal proximity of trauma exposure to the first depression assessment.

4.4 Childhood adversity/trauma is associated with increased circulating IL-6 levels

Childhood adversity/trauma was associated with higher IL-6 concentrations at age 24 after adjustment for potential confounders, although effect sizes were generally modest. Associations were observed for ACE score (β = 0.027, 95% CI 0.002-0.052, FDR-P = 0.036), trauma count (β = 0.050, 95% CI 0.025-0.076, FDR-P = 4.55 × 10⁻⁴), and any trauma (β = 0.100, 95% CI 0.040-0.160, FDR-P = 0.002) (see Figure 5, Supplementary Table 9).

Timing-specific analyses showed associations with higher IL-6 at age 24 for trauma exposure during middle childhood (5-11 yrs) and adolescence (11-17 yrs). Associations were observed for trauma count during both periods (5-11 years: β = 0.061, 95% CI 0.026-0.096, FDR-P = 7.24 × 10⁻⁴; 11-17 years: β = 0.076, 95% CI 0.033-0.118, FDR-P = 7.25 × 10⁻⁴) and for any trauma (5-11 years: β = 0.093, 95% CI 0.032-0.155, FDR-P = 0.004; 11-17 years: β = 0.108, 95% CI 0.046-0.171, FDR-P = 0.002). Results were similar across models adjusting for biomarker collection covariates and sociodemographic characteristics.

There was little evidence that trauma exposure during early childhood (0-5 yrs), assessed as either trauma count or any trauma, was associated with IL-6 at age 24. Associations between trauma exposure and CRP at age 24 did not survive correction for multiple testing (see Supplementary Table 9 and Supplementary Results).

4.5 IL-6 or CRP levels do not mediate the trauma-depression association

Overall, across extensive analyses, there was little evidence that IL-6 or CRP mediated associations between childhood adversity/trauma and ICD-10 depression at age 30 (Supplementary Table 10).

Across models examining overall trauma exposure, cumulative trauma and exposure during specific childhood periods, estimated proportions mediated ranged from -1.4% to -0.1% for CRP and from 4.4% to 13.2% for IL-6, although confidence intervals were wide and all included zero. Indirect-effect estimates were close to the null (1.00-1.03), with all 95% confidence intervals including 1.

4.6 Sensitivity analysis results

Sensitivity analyses generally supported the main findings. Including participants with CRP concentrations >10 mg/L and excluding participants with incomplete depression assessments produced small changes in effect estimates. Analyses restricted to participants with observed covariate data also yielded similar estimates, although some associations crossed the FDR-adjusted threshold. Full results are provided in Supplementary Results and Supplementary Tables 11a-c.

5 Discussion

Persistent or recurrent depression affects approximately 1 in 20 young adults across ages 18, 24 and 30 years. Our findings showed that childhood trauma was consistently associated with depression in young adulthood, with stronger associations for persistent/recurrent depression than for transient depression.

Threat-related adversities showed the strongest association with persistent/recurrent depression, and cumulative trauma showed a clear dose-response relationship, suggesting that both the type and burden of childhood adversity are important risk factors for depression into adulthood. Although trauma was associated with higher systemic inflammatory marker levels in later childhood (11-17 yrs), these did not appear to be a major explanatory pathway linking trauma exposure to depression. Incorporating newly available age-30 depression assessments allowed us to extend the study of depression course beyond early adulthood.

Extensive evidence links childhood trauma/adversity with later depression (Flouri et al., 2020; Iob et al., 2022; LeMoult et al., 2020; Nanni et al., 2012; Tan & Mao, 2023). Our findings suggest stronger associations with persistent/recurrent than transient depression, building on evidence that childhood adversity is associated not only with depression onset but also with its chronicity, recurrence and poorer treatment outcomes (Nanni et al., 2012).

Our findings suggest that middle childhood (5-11 yrs) and adolescence (11-17 yrs) may be sensitive periods for adversity-related depression risk. This aligns with evidence linking ACEs at ages 11-17 with depression in early adulthood (Tran et al., 2026). Previous ALSPAC research also linked moderate or high adversity exposure with greater depression risk and severity regardless of timing (Tracy et al., 2019), highlighting the importance of considering both developmental timing and overall adversity burden. However, apparent timing effects may reflect recency or accumulated exposure rather than distinct sensitive periods (Dunn et al., 2018).

We therefore examined whether the adolescent association reflected proximity to the first depression assessment at age 18. Trauma between 11-17 yrs was associated with depression at ages 18 and 24, and trauma count remained associated with depression at age 30, suggesting longer-term relevance beyond proximity to the first outcome assessment, although associations did weaken over time. Notably, a similar timing pattern was observed for inflammation, with trauma exposure in mid to late childhood also showing stronger associations with IL-6 at age 24. Associations between childhood trauma/adversity and CRP were largely attenuated to the null, consistent with findings from previous studies (Crick et al., 2022; Flouri et al., 2020).

Our work highlights that trauma exposure, particularly threat-related adversity and cumulative trauma burden, may be more strongly associated with recurrent patterns of depression than with isolated depressive episodes. The strong associations observed for threat-related adversities are in line with dimensional models of childhood adversity, particularly the threat-deprivation framework proposed by McLaughlin and colleagues, which suggests that experiences involving threat, such as physical, sexual, or emotional abuse, may be especially relevant to socioemotional processing and later psychopathology (McLaughlin et al., 2014; Sheridan & McLaughlin, 2014). Identifying trauma types that are more strongly associated with later mental health may help inform more targeted approaches to prevention and intervention.

Our work adds to existing reports showing that while childhood trauma is associated with higher levels of systemic inflammatory markers such as IL-6 and CRP, these do not fully mediate the traumadepression relationship (Flouri et al., 2020; Iob et al., 2022). There are several possible explanations for this finding. Raised inflammatory markers do not reflect/capture the aspect of immune response "shaped" or "programmed" by experiencing adversity/trauma. It is also possible that raised inflammatory marker levels are not key mediating pathway linking trauma to depression, but other mechanisms are involved. Hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis functioning, which regulates cortisol and stress responsivity (Herman et al., 2016), has been suggested as a possible mediator. Childhood adversity has been proposed to increase depression risk through allostatic overload of the HPA axis (Wilkinson & Goodyer, 2011), and broader evidence suggests that early adversity can become biologically embedded through interacting changes in stress physiology, immune function, neural development, and epigenetic regulation (Takahashi & Shelton, 2025; Vargovic et al., 2026; Zhou & Ryan, 2023). These processes are highly relevant to depression, as dysregulated cortisol signalling, altered immune activity, and stress sensitisation may increase vulnerability to low mood, anhedonia, fatigue, cognitive symptoms, and impaired emotion regulation (Rousson et al., 2020). Over time, such biological changes may lower resilience to later stressors, helping to explain why childhood trauma had greater association with persistent/recurrent depression than with transient depression. Therefore, the absence of IL-6/CRP mediation in the present study does not rule out biological mechanisms but suggests that a single circulating inflammatory marker may be insufficient to capture the relevant pathways.

Strengths of this study includes the use of ALSPAC, a rich longitudinal cohort spanning childhood, adolescence, and young adulthood. The inclusion of newly available CIS-R data at age 30 allowed depression trajectories to be extended beyond early adulthood, distinguishing persistent/recurrent from transient depression over a longer period than previous ALSPAC studies. The detailed characterisation of childhood adversity allowed examination of cumulative trauma burden, specific trauma types, and developmental timing. The availability of inflammatory biomarker data across childhood allowed trauma-related differences in systemic inflammation to be assessed across development. Multiple imputation, incorporating a range of auxiliary variables, was used to reduce bias from missing data; imputed findings were broadly consistent with complete-case sensitivity analyses, although completecase estimates were less precise.

Limitations include attrition over time, limited geographic and ethnic diversity, and potential biases arising from self-reported or parent-reported data. Notably, trauma exposure was higher among participants with missing depression repeat assessment data than among those in either observed depression group, suggesting that attrition may have led to underestimation, rather than overestimation, of the association between childhood trauma and later depression.

Prospective assessment of childhood adversity is a key strength. However, most ALSPAC adversity measures before age 8 were parent-reported. Although both parent-and child-reported trauma predict later mental health outcomes (Croft et al., 2019), caregivers may underreport maltreatment (Dunn et al., 2026). While reporting source may have introduced measurement error, the observed associations are unlikely to be entirely attributable to one informant type. Possible underreporting, particularly in early childhood, may have led to exposure misclassification and attenuation of associations with later depression.

In conclusion, our study shows the extent of persistent or recurrent depression between late adolescence and young adulthood between ages 18 and 30 years, which affect approx. 1 in 20 individuals. It also identifies abuse/maltreatment, especially threat adversities experienced during middle and late childhood as potentially important risk factors for persistent depression in young adults. This work also highlights research gaps by highlighting the need for identifying biological mechanisms linking early-life adversity and adult depression.
