Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 29 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Background: Iron deficiency is common in women of reproductive age, frequently occurs without anemia, and is often accompanied by fatigue and hair shedding. Lactoferrin (LF), an iron-binding glycoprotein, may support iron absorption without the hepcidin response provoked by higher-dose iron, but a human-equivalent LF (heqLF; effera ® ) produced by precision fermentation has not been tested in a randomized trial.

Objective: To examine the effects of two doses of heqLF combined with low-dose iron on iron status, inflammation, hair shedding, and participant-reported wellness in women with low ferritin.

In this randomized, double-blind, parallel-group, decentralized pilot study, 31 women aged 18-64 years with ferritin <35 ng/mL were randomized to 100 or 50 mg/d of heqLF, each with 5 mg/d of iron as ferrous bisglycinate, for 6 weeks; 27 were included in the modified intention-to-treat analysis. Iron, hematological, and inflammatory biomarkers were measured at Weeks 0, 2, 4, and 6, and hair shedding (self-administered modified hair pull test) and participant-reported outcomes were assessed weekly.

Results: Ferritin, the primary endpoint, did not change in either group (100 mg: +0.9%; 50 mg: +11.3%; both P ≥ 0.34). With both groups combined, total iron-binding capacity decreased by 17 µg/dL, unsaturated iron-binding capacity by 34 µg/dL, and transferrin by 13 mg/dL (all adjusted P ≤ 0.028), whereas increases in serum iron and transferrin saturation were not statistically significant in the primary analysis. High-sensitivity C-reactive protein and hemoglobin did not change. Hair pull counts decreased by 62% with 100 mg (adjusted P = 0.017) and by 39% with 50 mg (adjusted P = 0.039). Participant-reported wellness and hair scores improved in both groups, and skin scores improved with 100 mg only. No outcome differed between doses.

In women with low ferritin, 6 weeks of heqLF with low-dose iron was accompanied by lower iron-binding capacity and transferrin, consistent with improved iron availability, as well as reduced hair shedding and improved wellness, without a change in ferritin. Because the study had no placebo or iron-only control arm, these exploratory findings warrant confirmation in a larger placebo-controlled trial.

1 Introduction

Iron deficiency (ID) is arguably the most prevalent micronutrient deficiency worldwide and disproportionately affects women of reproductive age as a consequence of menstrual blood loss, pregnancy-related demands, and frequently inadequate dietary iron intake (1, 2). ID develops along a continuum: depletion of storage iron, reflected by a low serum ferritin concentration, precedes a decline in circulating iron (lower serum iron and transferrin saturation), iron-deficient erythropoiesis and, ultimately, iron-deficiency anemia (IDA) (1). Even in the absence of anemia, ID can impair work capacity, cognitive function, and quality of life, and it is commonly accompanied by fatigue and changes in hair, skin, and nail quality (3) (4) (5) (6). Because anemia is a late manifestation, a substantial proportion of women with depleted iron stores remain unrecognized when screening relies on hemoglobin alone (3). In a nationally representative analysis of US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data, 38.6% of females aged 12-21 years had ID (ferritin <25 µg/L), and in 83.6% of affected individuals ID occurred without anemia (7).

Non-anemic ID is increasingly recognized as a clinically meaningful condition rather than a benign laboratory finding (3, 4). In randomized controlled trials, iron supplementation reduced fatigue in non-anemic women with low ferritin (8, 9), and a systematic review of randomized trials in non-anemic iron-deficient adults found that iron supplementation reduced subjective fatigue, although objective measures of physical capacity did not improve (10). Restoration of iron status has also been associated with improved attention, memory, and learning in young women (5). These observations are particularly relevant to physically active women. Iron is central to oxygen transport and oxidative metabolism, and ID is commonly diagnosed in athletes, particularly females and endurance athletes (11). Exercise-related mechanisms, including hemolysis, gastrointestinal and sweat iron losses, and transient post-exercise elevations in hepcidin driven by interleukin-6 (IL-6), may compound menstrual losses and low energy availability (12, 13). In iron-deficient non-anemic endurance athletes, iron treatment improves iron status and aerobic capacity (14).

Hair shedding is among the most frequently reported, yet least systematically studied, consequences of low iron status. Hair follicle matrix cells are among the most rapidly dividing cells in the body and depend on iron-containing enzymes, including ribonucleotide reductase, the rate-limiting enzyme of DNA synthesis, providing a biological rationale for an association between iron depletion and telogen hair shedding (6). Several case-control studies have reported lower ferritin concentrations in women with diffuse non-scarring hair loss (15) (16) (17) (18). The evidence is not uniform, however; in a controlled study of 381 women with female pattern hair loss or chronic telogen effluvium, ID was common but not more prevalent than in controls (19), and others have found no association between ferritin concentrations above 10 µg/L and hair loss activity (20). Interventional data remain limited (6, 21). Iron-independent pathways may also be relevant: lactoferrin (LF), an iron-binding glycoprotein described below, promoted hair growth in mice and increased the proliferation of dermal papilla cells, which regulate the hair growth cycle, through ERK/Akt and Wnt signaling pathways (22), although these findings derive from animal and cell culture models using topical or parenteral administration at supraphysiological concentrations. The potential roles of LF in hair and skin health have recently been reviewed (23). Clinically, active shedding is commonly evaluated using the hair pull test, a simple, standardized assessment (24); adaptation of this test for self-administration may permit repeated monitoring of hair shedding in remote or decentralized trials.

Oral iron salts remain first-line therapy for ID but have important limitations. Systemic iron balance is governed by the hepatic peptide hormone hepcidin, which circulates to bind the cellular iron exporter ferroportin on target tissues, which occludes ferroportin and induces its internalization and degradation, thereby restricting export of absorbed dietary iron from enterocytes, recycled iron from macrophages, and tissue stores in the liver (25) (26) (27). Hepcidin primarily responds to iron loading as a negative feedback. In iron-depleted women, a single oral iron dose of ≥60 mg increases serum hepcidin for approximately 24 h, reducing fractional absorption from doses given later the same or next day; consequently, alternate-day dosing maximizes fractional absorption (28, 29). Inflammation also signals for hepcidin. As such, sources of inflammation including infection, strenuous exercise, and excess adiposity also elevate hepcidin and limits the effectiveness of supplemental iron (26, 30). Oral iron salts also frequently cause gastrointestinal side effects, which undermine adherence (31), and unabsorbed iron reaching the colon may shift the gut microbiota toward potentially pathogenic taxa and promote intestinal inflammation (32, 33). Collectively, these constraints have motivated interest in adjuncts that improve iron absorption rather than simply increasing the amount of iron delivered.

LF is an ~80 kDa iron-binding glycoprotein of the transferrin family that reversibly binds two ferric ions with high affinity and is abundant in human milk, exocrine secretions such as saliva, tears, and bile, and neutrophil secondary granules (34, 35). Several mechanisms may allow LF to support iron homeostasis. First, LF is recognized by a specific intestinal LF receptor (intelectin-1) and internalized via clathrin-mediated endocytosis independently of its iron saturation, providing a route for receptor-mediated uptake (36, 37). Second, LF exerts anti-inflammatory activity relevant to iron regulation: in human monocytes/macrophages, LF prevented the lipopolysaccharide-induced decrease in ferroportin by reducing IL-6 secretion (38), and clinical studies have linked LF-associated improvements in iron status to reductions in circulating IL-6 (39), although these effects have been reported mainly in specific subpopulations, and a large recent trial found no meaningful change in inflammatory markers with bLF (40). These effects may mitigate inflammatory inhibition of dietary iron aborption and tissue iron export to enhance fractional iron absorption and support iron availability from storage. . Third, LF possesses broader immunomodulatory properties that may be relevant in individuals with low-grade inflammation (41).

Clinical studies, predominantly using bovine LF (bLF), support these mechanisms. In pregnant women with ID and IDA, oral bLF increased red blood cell count, hemoglobin, serum iron, and ferritin while decreasing serum IL-6, whereas ferrous sulfate increased IL-6 and failed to improve hematological parameters (42); similar benefits were observed in non-pregnant women of childbearing age receiving bLF 100 mg twice daily (42). A meta-analysis of four randomized trials in 600 pregnant women found that bLF was as effective as ferrous sulfate in improving hematological parameters, with significantly fewer gastrointestinal side effects (43), and a broader meta-analysis reported greater improvements in serum iron, ferritin, and hemoglobin and lower IL-6 with LF than with ferrous sulfate (44). Notably, in a study of 16 female long-distance runners, 8 weeks of LF plus iron maintained ferritin, serum iron, and red blood cell count during summer training, whereas these indices declined with the same iron dose (5 mg ferric pyrophosphate) alone (45), suggesting that LF may complement conventional iron supplementation in active women. Not all trials have been positive, however. In a randomized trial of 555 Bangladeshi women with IDA, bLF at 200 or 400 mg/d for 12 weeks, given without iron, was inferior to ferrous sulfate (60 mg/d elemental iron) for improving hemoglobin and produced only small increases in ferritin, although constipation and black stools were less frequent (40); the authors suggested that LF may be more useful as an adjunct to iron than as a substitute.

Human and bovine LF share only ~69% amino acid sequence identity and differ in glycosylation (46). Although bLF can be taken up by the human intestinal LF receptor (35), human LF is its native ligand (47). Until recently, however, human LF could not be produced at a scale suitable for supplementation. Precision fermentation has enabled the production of a human-equivalent LF (heqLF; effera ® , Helaina Inc.) produced by the yeast Komagataella phaffii, which has an amino acid sequence identical to that of native human milk LF and equivalent secondary and tertiary structures (48). The safety of heqLF has been evaluated following an expert panel-guided roadmap (49), which included an allergenicity assessment (50), a 4-week immunotoxicity study in rats (51), an in vitro digestion study under physiologically relevant conditions (52), and a randomized, double-blind, controlled trial in which 66 healthy adults received heqLF at 0.34 or 3.4 g/d or bLF at 3.4 g/d for 28 days to evaluate alloimmunization potential (53). On the basis of these data, heqLF meets the generally recognized as safe (GRAS) standard. Emerging clinical data also suggest benefits for iron status: in a recent case series of two active women with persistently low iron and poor response to, or intolerance of, standard oral iron, 100 mg/d of heqLF for 6-20 weeks was followed by notable increases in ferritin, with one participant also showing marked increases in serum iron and transferrin saturation, alongside improved energy and exercise capacity and no gastrointestinal complaints (54). However, no randomized controlled trial has examined heqLF administered with iron in women with low ferritin, nor its effects on commonly reported outcomes such as fatigue, mood, and hair shedding.

Therefore, the purpose of this randomized, double-blind, parallel-group pilot study was to examine the effects of two doses of heqLF (50 mg/d or 100 mg/d, each with 5 mg/d iron), administered for 6 weeks, on (1) biomarkers of iron status (ferritin, serum iron, transferrin, total and unsaturated iron-binding capacity, and transferrin saturation) and hematological indices; (2) systemic inflammation (high-sensitivity C-reactive protein); (3) hair shedding, self-assessed using a modified hair pull test; and (4) participant-reported wellness (energy, fatigue, and mood) and perceived hair and skin health in adult women with low ferritin concentrations. We hypothesized that both doses would improve biomarkers of iron status, reduce hair shedding, and improve participant-reported wellness over 6 weeks, and that these effects would be greater with the higher LF dose.

2 Materials and methods

2.1 Study design

This pilot, proof-of-concept study used a randomized, double-blind, parallel-group design to examine the effects of two doses of heqLF administered with iron on indices of iron status, inflammation, hair shedding, and subjective measures of wellness in adult women with low ferritin concentrations. Eligible participants were women aged 18-64 years with a documented ferritin concentration <35 ng/mL within the previous 6 months and a body mass index (BMI) between 18.5 and 32.5 kg/m². The ferritin criterion used in the present study (<35 ng/mL) lies slightly above the physiologically based threshold for iron-deficient erythropoiesis (55) and therefore captures women ranging from those at risk of functional iron deficiency to those with absolute iron deficiency by the WHO criterion (56). Individuals were excluded if they had gastrointestinal disorders that could affect study outcomes, select chronic medical conditions, acute illness or infection within the previous 30 days, or recent changes in medications that could confound study outcomes, or if they were pregnant or lactating. Participants were supplemented for 6 weeks, with blood sampling, hair pull tests, and questionnaires at baseline (Week 0) and at regular intervals through Week 6 (Section 2.3; Figure 1A). No placebo or iron-only control arm was included.

All participants provided informed consent in accordance with a protocol approved by the BRANY Institutional Review Board (Protocol Version 2; approved May 8, 2026). The study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov ([NCT07953972]).

2.2 Supplementation protocol

Participants were randomly assigned to receive either 100 mg/d of heqLF (effera ® , Helaina Inc.) plus 5 mg/d of iron or 50 mg/d of heqLF plus 5 mg/d of iron for 6 weeks. Iron was provided as ferrous bisglycinate chelate (Ferrochel®, Albion Laboratories; Iron 5 mg Bio-Max Series, Kirkman Group, Lake Oswego, OR, USA). Both participants and investigators were blinded to dose allocation. Compliance was assessed by pill count and expressed as the percentage of prescribed doses consumed; participants consuming ≥80% of prescribed doses were considered compliant, and participants without a reported pill count were not excluded as non-compliant.

2.3 Study procedures

The study was conducted in a decentralized manner: questionnaires and study communications were completed electronically, and fasted venous blood samples were collected by trained mobile phlebotomy professionals. Participants completed baseline demographic and health assessments, including weekly hours of resistance training and cardiovascular exercise, selfreported duration of low iron, and current symptoms. The screening ferritin value used to determine eligibility was the documented result obtained within the 6 months before enrollment. The modified hair pull test and the participant-reported outcome questionnaire were completed at baseline (Week 0) and weekly through Week 6.

Venous blood samples were collected at Weeks 0, 2, 4, and 6 and analyzed by a commercial clinical laboratory (Labcorp) for ferritin, serum iron, transferrin, total iron-binding capacity (TIBC), transferrin saturation (TSAT), complete blood count, and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP). Iron indices and hs-CRP were measured in serum, and the complete blood count in EDTA whole blood. Serum iron and TSAT were measured in two panels: an iron and TIBC panel (Fe panel), in which TSAT was calculated as (serum iron ÷ TIBC) × 100; and a transferrin panel (Tf panel), in which TSAT was calculated as (serum iron ÷ Tf) × 70.9. Unsaturated iron-binding capacity (UIBC) was derived from the Fe panel as TIBC -serum iron and is therefore not independent of these two measures. Fe panel values are reported in Table 3; Tf panel values are reported in Table 4.

2.4 Modified hair pull test

Hair shedding was evaluated using a researcher-adapted modified hair pull assessment based on the standard clinical hair pull test (24). Participants received standardized written and illustrated instructions and were instructed to grasp a small bundle of hair, approximately the diameter of a marker, from the side of the scalp and apply gentle, steady traction. Extracted hairs were counted and recorded by the participant; lower counts indicate less shedding. The hair shedding endpoint was the change in hair count from Week 0 to Week 6.

2.5 Participant-reported outcomes

Participant-reported outcomes were assessed using a researcher-developed 10-item questionnaire of 5-point Likert-scale items grouped into three domains: wellness (Q1, energy; Q2, fatigue; Q3, mood), hair (Q4, shedding; Q5, vibrancy; Q6, perceived new growth; Q7, overall hair quality), and skin (Q8, hydration; Q9, clarity; Q10, overall skin satisfaction). Participants rated their current status for each item. The fatigue and shedding items were reverse-scored so that higher scores indicated more favorable outcomes for every item, and each domain score was calculated as the mean of its items. The full questionnaire is provided in Supplementary Material S1.

2.6 Statistical analysis

Analysis population. Four analysis populations were defined: intention-to-treat (ITT; all 31 randomized participants analyzed as assigned, 29 with laboratory data), modified ITT (mITT), complete-pairs, and per-protocol. Primary analyses were conducted in the mITT population, instead of the ITT population prespecified in the statistical analysis plan, because of unexpected participant-level deviations, including withdrawal, non-use of the study product, and incomplete blood data (Section 3.1; Figure 1B). One participant who consumed heqLF without iron was retained. Complete-pairs, and per-protocol analyses served as sensitivity analyses.

Complete-pairs analyses of blood biomarkers were conducted within the mITT population and included participants with Week 0 and Week 6 measurements for each marker, without imputation. The blood biomarker per-protocol population required ≥80% compliance, blood draws at Weeks 0 and 6, and no more than one of four draws missed; draws missed because of laboratory error were excused. The modified hair pull test and subjective questionnaire perprotocol populations required ≥80% compliance and completion of both assessments at Weeks 0 and 6. Analyses of these outcomes were restricted to participants with available paired Week 0 and Week 6 data. Missing data were not imputed.

Baseline characteristics. Participants' baseline characteristics were compared between groups using Welch's unpaired two-sided t-tests for continuous variables and Fisher's exact tests for categorical variables. P-values were not adjusted.

Blood biomarkers.

Blood biomarkers were analyzed using linear mixed model (LMM) with group, visit (Weeks 0, 2, 4, and 6), and the group × visit interaction as fixed effects, fit by restricted maximum likelihood (REML) with Kenward-Roger degrees of freedom; the residual covariance structure was selected by Akaike's information criterion and after evaluation of residual Q-Q plots and plots of residual-versus-predicted values.

Ferritin, hs-CRP, and white blood cell count were log-transformed before analysis and are reported as geometric means, with changes expressed as percentages. Ferritin was also adjusted for inflammation using the Biomarkers Reflecting Inflammation and Nutritional Determinants of Anemia (BRINDA) regression approach (57): ln(ferritin adj ) = ln(ferritin) -β[ln(CRP) -ln(0.16)]

The reference hs-CRP concentration was 0.16 mg/L, based on the BRINDA internal dataset for women of reproductive age (57). The coefficient (β) was estimated separately for each analysis population using a LMM regressing ln(ferritin) on ln(hs-CRP) across visits, with a participantlevel random intercept to account for repeated measures (β = 0.153 in the mITT population).

Ferritin was adjusted only when hs-CRP was ≥0.16 mg/L; no adjustment was applied when hs-CRP was below this threshold or missing. Because AGP was not measured, adjustment was limited to CRP rather than both CRP and AGP as recommended by BRINDA. Adjusted ferritin values were log-transformed for analysis. hs-CRP values below the limit of detection (LOD) were replaced with LOD/√2.

Within-group changes from Week 0 to Week 6 and between-group differences in these changes were estimated from model contrasts, and pooled changes across both dose groups were estimated from models without a group term. Ferritin was the primary endpoint. P-values were Bonferroni-adjusted for the three post-baseline time points (×3); as a stricter sensitivity analysis, P-values were also adjusted across the 13 blood markers analyzed (×13). Adjusted P-values were capped at 1. LMM with group, week, and group × week as fixed effects and a random intercept for participant (REML; Type III F-tests with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom) were fit using all available observations. In the mITT population, the non-parametric approach was primary for the wellness and hair domains, and the LMM was primary for the skin domain. Both approaches are reported for all domains.

3 Results

3.1 Participant characteristics

Thirty-one women were randomized (100 mg, n = 16; 50 mg, n = 15; Figure 1B). Four were excluded from the mITT population: in the 100 mg group, one did not take the study product, and one had incomplete blood data; in the 50 mg group, one withdrew and one did not take the study product. This left 27 participants (100 mg, n = 14; 50 mg, n = 13). One participant in the 50 mg group who received heqLF without iron was retained. Complete-pairs blood biomarker analyses included 16-23 participants per marker. Per-protocol populations included 18 participants for blood biomarker analyses (100 mg, n = 8; 50 mg, n = 10) and 17 for the hair pull test and questionnaire (100 mg, n = 8; 50 mg, n = 9). Per-protocol results for blood biomarkers and the modified hair pull test are presented in Tables 4 and 5, respectively, and per-protocol results for questionnaire data are presented in Supplementary Table S2.

One internally inconsistent Week 6 iron panel, drawn at an outside laboratory after a missed study draw, was excluded. Two Week 6 ferritin values (429 and 730 ng/mL) were identified as potential outliers based on their magnitude relative to preceding measurements and the absence of evidence of inflammation. The value of 429 ng/mL was excluded from the analysis, while the value of 730 ng/mL was adjusted to 73 ng/mL based on a suspected decimal-place transcription error. One participant was also excluded from hair pull analyses because of implausible counts.

Baseline characteristics of the mITT population are presented in Table 1. Participants were 36.9 ± 9.0 years of age, had a BMI of 25.6 ± 4.6 kg/m², and reported 2.2 ± 2.2 h/wk of resistance training and 4.6 ± 3.3 h/wk of cardiovascular exercise (typical weekly hours, self-reported at baseline). Most participants (73.1%) reported low iron status for more than 1 year prior to enrolling. Mean screening ferritin was 14.0 ± 9.0 ng/mL; 92.6% of participants had screening ferritin <30 ng/mL and 63.0% had values <15 ng/mL. Mean ferritin at Week 0 was higher (31.8 ± 17.6 ng/mL), and 46.2% of participants remained below 30 ng/mL. Three of 24 participants with hemoglobin data (12.5%) were anemic (hemoglobin <12.0 g/dL), and all three met the WHO-based definition of iron-deficiency anemia (hemoglobin <12.0 g/dL with ferritin <15 ng/mL) (56). No baseline characteristic differed significantly between groups (all P ≥ 0.14). Symptom data were available for 25 participants: all reported fatigue, and most reported brain fog (88%) and hair shedding or thinning (84%) at baseline (Table 2).

3.2 Iron status, hematological, and inflammatory biomarkers

Changes in blood biomarkers in the mITT population are presented in Table 3. Ferritin did not change significantly from week 0 -week 6 in either group (100 mg: +0.9% [95% CI -18.4, +24.8], P = 0.93; 50 mg: +11.3% [-11.1, +39.4], P = 0.34) or with both groups combined (+5.7% [-9.1, +22.9], P = 0.46), and results were similar after BRINDA adjustment for hs-CRP (AGP not measured).

TIBC and transferrin decreased from week 0 -week 6 in both groups, but only TIBC in the 100 mg group remained significant after adjustment. In the 100 mg group, TIBC significantly decreased by 19 µg/dL (95% CI [-32, -6]; P = 0.005; adjusted P = 0.014) and transferrin by 15 mg/dL (95% CI [-28, -2]; P = 0.020; adjusted P = 0.061). In the 50 mg group, TIBC decreased by 15 µg/dL (95% CI [-29, 0]; P = 0.045; adjusted P = 0.14). Serum iron (100 mg: +13 µg/dL; 50 mg: +15 µg/dL) and TSAT (+4.6 and +5.2 percentage points, respectively) increased in both groups, but these changes were not statistically significant. No between-group differences were detected (all P ≥ 0.41).

From Week 0 to Week 6, pooled TIBC decreased by 17 µg/dL (95% CI [-27, -8]; P < 0.001), UIBC by 34 µg/dL (95% CI [-55, -14]; P = 0.001), and transferrin by 13 mg/dL (95% CI [-22, -3]; P = 0.009). All three decreases remained statistically significant after adjustment (adjusted P = 0.002, 0.004, and 0.028, respectively). Pooled serum iron increased by 14 µg/dL (95% CI [-4, +32]) and TSAT increased by 5.0 percentage points (95% CI [-0.2, +10.1]); neither change was statistically significant (all P > 0.05). Hemoglobin, hematocrit, hs-CRP, and white blood cell count also showed no significant changes in either group (all P > 0.05).

Findings were broadly consistent across analysis populations (Table 4). In the complete-pairs population, pooled TSAT (Fe panel) increased by 6.7% (95% CI [+1.5%, +11.9%]; P = 0.015; adjusted P = 0.044), and UIBC decreased by 37 µg/dL (95% CI [-55, -18]; adjusted P = 0.002); both remained significant after adjustment. Serum iron increased by 22 µg/dL (95% CI [+4, +40]; P = 0.017), but this change was not significant after adjustment (adjusted P = 0.052). In the per-protocol population, TSAT (Fe panel) increased by 7.3% (95% CI [+1.5%, +13.0%]; adjusted P = 0.042), while TIBC decreased by 13 µg/dL (95% CI [-23, -4]; adjusted P = 0.025) and UIBC decreased by 38 µg/dL (95% CI [-60, -16]; adjusted P = 0.004). Serum iron increased by 22 µg/dL (95% CI [+2, +42]; P = 0.028), but this change was not significant after adjustment (adjusted P = 0.085). In the ITT population, only the pooled decrease in UIBC remained significant after adjustment (-33 µg/dL; 95% CI [-48, -18]; adjusted P < 0.001); the decrease in TIBC did not reach statistical significance after adjustment (adjusted P = 0.051). Ferritin did not change significantly, and no marker differed between doses in any population. Replacing the implausible Week 6 ferritin value with 73 ng/mL rather than excluding it did not change these conclusions.

3.3 Hair shedding

Results of the modified hair pull test are presented in Table 5. Counts at both Week 0 and Week 6 were available for 11 participants in each group. In the 100 mg group, the mean count significantly decreased from 3.64 (median 3) to 1.36 (median 1) hairs, a reduction of 2.27 hairs (-62%; dz = -0.73; P = 0.009; adjusted P = 0.017), indicating a large effect. In the 50 mg group, the mean count significantly decreased from 4.45 (median 6) to 2.73 (median 2) hairs, a reduction of 1.73 hairs (-39%; dz = -0.96; P = 0.019; adjusted P = 0.039), indicating a large effect.

Friedman tests indicated significant change across weeks in both groups (100 mg, P = 0.007; 50 mg, P = 0.020). The reduction did not differ between groups (Mann-Whitney P = 0.95), and the ANCOVA sensitivity analysis showed no significant group difference in Week 6 counts after adjustment for baseline (50 mg -100 mg: +1.08 hairs [95% CI -0.52, +2.68]; P = 0.17). In the per-protocol population, reductions were of similar magnitude (100 mg: -2.75 hairs, -69%; 50 mg: -1.78 hairs, -36%) but did not reach significance after adjustment (adjusted P = 0.058 and 0.097, respectively).

3.4 Participant-reported outcomes

Questionnaire results for the mITT population are presented in Table 6. Scores in all three domains changed significantly across Weeks 0-6 within both groups (Friedman P ≤ 0.011; Kendall's W = 0.39-0.85). In the 100 mg group, wellness scores increased from 2.36 at Week 0 to 3.90 at Week 6 (adjusted P = 0.015), hair scores from 1.96 to 3.77 (adjusted P = 0.015), and skin scores from 2.28 to 3.72 (adjusted P = 0.023). In the 50 mg group, wellness scores increased from 2.44 to 3.72 (adjusted P = 0.023; significant at Weeks 3, 4, and 6) and hair scores from 2.21 to 3.33 (adjusted P = 0.035; significant at Week 6 only), whereas the increase in skin scores (2.92 to 3.72) was not significant (adjusted P = 0.145). Domain scores did not differ between groups at any week (Mann-Whitney adjusted P ≥ 0.34). In the mixed-model analyses, the effect of time was significant for all domains (P < 0.001), and the group × time interaction was significant for the skin domain (the primary analysis for this domain; P = 0.009) and the hair domain (P = 0.039), but not for wellness (P = 0.17), indicating that the trajectories of perceived hair and skin health over time differed between doses.

At the item level, energy improved significantly by Week 6 in both groups (100 mg: 2.08 to 4.23, adjusted P = 0.013; 50 mg: 1.75 to 3.58, adjusted P = 0.031), as did perceived new hair growth (100 mg: 1.69 to 4.08; 50 mg: 1.92 to 3.25; both adjusted P = 0.046). In the 100 mg group, fatigue (adjusted P = 0.046), mood (P = 0.025), hair vibrancy (P = 0.020), skin clarity (P = 0.017), overall skin satisfaction (P = 0.028), and skin hydration (P = 0.050) improved significantly. Changes in self-perceived shedding and overall hair quality were not statistically significant (both P > 0.05). In the 50 mg group, the remaining items were not significant by weeks (all P > 0.05). No item differed significantly between groups at any week (all adjusted P > 0.05).

In the per-protocol population, the direction and magnitude of change were similar, but Week 6 improvements in domain scores were not significant after adjustment (adjusted P = 0.054-0.309). Friedman tests remained significant for all domains within both groups (P ≤ 0.007) except skin in the 50 mg group (P = 0.051), and the group × time interaction was significant for the hair (P < 0.001) and skin (P = 0.049) domains, for which the mixed model was the primary analysis in this population. Full per-protocol questionnaire results are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

4 Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first randomized, double-blind study of heqLF administered with iron in women with low ferritin. Over 6 weeks, both doses (50 and 100 mg/d, each with 5 mg/d iron) were accompanied by decreases in TIBC and transferrin, with smaller increases in serum iron and TSAT, whereas ferritin, the primary endpoint, did not change. Hair pull counts decreased by 39-62%, participant-reported wellness and perceived hair health improved in both groups, and perceived skin health improved significantly only with 100 mg. Responses were similar with both doses. The consistency of the iron transport findings across analysis populations, together with parallel improvements in hair shedding and participant-reported outcomes, provides an encouraging signal that warrants confirmation in a controlled trial. Below, we interpret these findings in the context of previous studies of iron and LF supplementation.

4.1 Iron transport markers and ferritin

Hepatic transferrin synthesis is upregulated when iron stores are depleted, so transferrin and TIBC are elevated in iron deficiency and fall as iron supply to tissues improves (1, 2). The pooled decreases observed here in TIBC (-17 µg/dL), transferrin (-13 mg/dL), and UIBC (-34 µg/dL) are therefore consistent with improved iron availability. These changes were directionally consistent across all four analysis populations, and the decrease in UIBC remained significant after adjustment in each. Increases in TSAT (+6.7 to +7.3 percentage points) and serum iron (+22 µg/dL) were nominally significant in the complete-pairs and per-protocol analyses but not in the mITT or ITT analyses, suggesting that missing visits attenuated these signals. Because transferrin has a circulating half-life of roughly 8 days, transport markers can respond within weeks, whereas a measurable rise in ferritin requires sustained net iron retention. A response in transport markers without a change in ferritin is therefore plausible over a 6-week period. UIBC is derived from TIBC and serum iron and does not provide independent evidence. The stable ferritin concentrations are best understood by comparison with iron trials in similar populations. In non-anemic menstruating women with fatigue and ferritin <50 µg/L, 80 mg/d of elemental iron for 12 weeks raised ferritin by 11.4 µg/L and hemoglobin by 0.32 g/dL relative to placebo (8). The present study provided 16-fold less iron for half as long. Stable-isotope studies in iron-depleted women show that fractional absorption is highest at low doses and declines once doses reach ≥60 mg, because such doses increase hepcidin for about 24 h; absolute absorption, however, still rises with dose, from 6.7 mg at a 40 mg dose to 18.1 mg at 240 mg (28, 29). Even with efficient absorption, a 5 mg dose is therefore likely to supply no more than 1-2 mg of absorbed iron per day, which is similar in magnitude to the basal requirement of menstruating women (2) and leaves little surplus for rebuilding stores within 6 weeks. The well-absorbed iron form may have partly offset this: ferrous bisglycinate improves hemoglobin more than other iron salts in pregnant women and caused fewer gastrointestinal adverse events (58). In addition, mean ferritin at Week 0 (31.8 ng/mL) was more than twice the mean screening value (14.0 ng/mL), and nearly half of participants had Week 0 ferritin ≥30 ng/mL, which narrowed the scope for improvement. This gap may reflect regression to the mean, iron intake between screening and enrollment, or inter-laboratory differences, because screening values came from participants' own records. Inflammation is unlikely to have masked a ferritin response: hs-CRP was low and stable, and BRINDA-adjusted ferritin followed the same pattern (57).

4.2 Comparison with previous lactoferrin studies

Most clinical evidence for LF comes from bLF in pregnant women with ID or IDA. In those studies, bLF increased hemoglobin, serum iron, and ferritin and decreased IL-6, whereas ferrous sulfate increased IL-6 (39, 42). Meta-analyses have concluded that bLF is at least as effective as ferrous sulfate for hematological recovery in pregnancy, with fewer gastrointestinal side effects (43), and that it produces larger gains in ferritin despite lower fractional iron absorption (44). Several features distinguish those studies from the present one. Participants in previous bLF studies were generally anemic or more severely iron-deficient, LF was typically given at 200 mg/d (100 mg twice daily) or more, and responses were often driven by correction of inflammation-related iron sequestration (38, 39). In contrast, our participants were largely nonanemic but experiencing absolute iron deficiency with low baseline hs-CRP (mean 2.1 mg/L) that did not change, so other mechanisms, such as receptor-mediated uptake of iron-bound LF (36, 37), may be more relevant in this population.

A recent large trial helps clarify the role of LF. In 555 Bangladeshi women with IDA, bLF at 200 or 400 mg/d for 12 weeks, given without iron, was inferior to 60 mg/d ferrous sulfate for hemoglobin and increased ferritin by only about 3 µg/L, compared with about 41 µg/L with ferrous sulfate; CRP and α1-acid glycoprotein did not differ between groups (40). Notably, hepcidin rose only minimally with bLF (0.5-1.0 ng/mL) compared with about 16 ng/mL with ferrous sulfate (40). These findings suggest that LF is best positioned as a complement to iron rather than a replacement, and the absence of a hepcidin surge makes it an attractive adjunct to optimize fractional iron absorption with low-dose iron, the approach tested here. Because ferrous sulfate also raised ferritin far more, its larger hepcidin response may partly reflect greater iron repletion; the proposed benefit of LF therefore remains enhanced fractional iron absorption, which was not measured here. This combination is also supported by a study of 16 female longdistance runners, in which ferritin, serum iron, and red blood cell count declined during 8 weeks of summer training with iron alone but were maintained with LF plus iron (45). The present study is consistent with these findings and extends them to free-living women with low ferritin and to a heqLF, which is digested more like human milk LF than bLF is (52) and is the native ligand of the intestinal LF receptor (36, 47). However, without an iron-only control arm and with no change in ferritin, the present study cannot isolate an effect of LF.

In the case series that motivated the present study, 100 mg/d heqLF was followed by marked increases in ferritin in two active women with persistently low iron over 6-20 weeks (54), suggesting that longer exposure may allow effects on iron transport to translate into replenished stores. Contrary to our hypothesis, changes were similar with 50 and 100 mg/d, indicating that the lower dose may be sufficient for effects on iron transport; larger samples will be needed to define the dose-response relationship, as between-group confidence intervals were wide (for ferritin, -19% to +50%). Both doses were also far below the 0.34 and 3.4 g/d evaluated in a 28day safety trial in healthy adults (53).

4.3 Hair shedding

Hair pull counts decreased by 2.3 hairs (-62%) with 100 mg and 1.7 hairs (-39%) with 50 mg, with moderate-to-large within-group effect sizes (dz -0.73 and -0.96), and 10 of 11 and 8 of 11 participants, respectively, shed fewer hairs at Week 6. Evidence linking iron status to hair shedding is mixed: case-control studies have reported lower ferritin in women with diffuse nonscarring hair loss (15) (16) (17) (18), but larger controlled studies found no excess of ID or no association above a ferritin of 10 µg/L (19, 20), and interventional evidence is sparse (6, 21). Because ferritin did not change, the reduction in shedding may involve mechanisms beyond iron stores. An iron-independent action of LF is biologically plausible: LF promoted hair growth in mice and stimulated dermal papilla cell proliferation through ERK/Akt and Wnt signaling (22), although whether orally ingested LF or its peptides reach the follicle remains to be determined. A recent review identified no registered human trials of LF for hair outcomes (23), so the present data are, to our knowledge, the first clinical observations of hair shedding during LF supplementation.

Reductions of similar magnitude were observed in the per-protocol population. As in any uncontrolled comparison, seasonal variation, regression to the mean, and growing familiarity with the self-administered test may also have contributed, so confirming these encouraging findings with clinician-performed pull tests (24) or phototrichograms in a placebo-controlled trial will be an important next step.

4.4 Participant-reported outcomes

Energy improved in both groups by 1.7-2.0 points on a 5-point scale, and the 100 mg group also reported improvements in fatigue and mood. These changes are directionally consistent with placebo-controlled iron trials in non-anemic women. In those trials, fatigue fell by 47.7% with iron vs. 28.8% with placebo over 12 weeks (8) and by 29% vs. 13% over 4 weeks, with benefit limited to women with ferritin ≤50 µg/L (9). A systematic review likewise found that iron reduced subjective fatigue in non-anemic iron-deficient adults without improving objective physical capacity (10). Because placebo groups in these trials also improved, and our questionnaire was researcher-developed, expectation effects may have contributed, and validated instruments with a placebo comparator will help quantify the specific contribution of heqLF.

The improvements in perceived skin health with 100 mg are consistent with clinical evidence that oral LF reduces inflammatory acne lesions, which may reflect its antimicrobial and antiinflammatory properties (23). In the mixed models, the significant group × time interactions for the hair and skin domains suggest a steeper trajectory with 100 mg, a pattern worth examining prospectively, although groups did not differ at any individual week.

4.5 Analysis populations and multiplicity

Results were examined in several analysis populations, and the rationale warrants explanation. The ITT population as designated as primary, but it included two participants who confirmed they never took the study product and one who withdrew, which would bias estimates toward the null. We therefore present the mITT population, which excludes only participants who did not receive the intervention or contributed no usable blood data, and report the ITT, complete-pairs, and per-protocol analyses as sensitivity analyses so that the robustness of each finding can be judged. The decrease in UIBC remained significant after adjustment in every population. The decrease in TIBC was nominally significant in every population and remained significant after adjustment in two. The increases in serum iron and TSAT were nominally significant only in the complete-pairs and per-protocol analyses, indicating that they depend on how missing data are handled. For the hair pull test and questionnaire, the within-group tests require paired observations, so the mITT analyses of these outcomes are equivalent to complete-pairs analyses; ITT analyses were not performed for these outcomes, which is a limitation.

We applied conservative Bonferroni corrections throughout. Within dose groups, the decreases in UIBC in both groups, in transferrin with 100 mg, and in TIBC with 50 mg were nominally significant but not after Bonferroni adjustment, and in the per-protocol population the hair pull and questionnaire changes were not significant after adjustment. This is expected in a pilot study where 11-14 per group contributed to the main analyses, so these results are best interpreted as estimates of effect size to inform the design of a larger trial.

4.6 Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study include randomized, double-blind allocation to two doses; a decentralized design with mobile phlebotomy that enabled recruitment of community-dwelling women; repeated blood sampling and weekly participant-reported assessments; inflammation-adjusted ferritin; prespecified normality-based analysis rules; and multiple sensitivity analyses.

As a pilot study, this trial also has limitations. Without a placebo or iron-only arm, the specific contribution of heqLF cannot yet be isolated. The sample was small and the intervention short. Some blood data were missing because of laboratory errors and missed draws, and screening ferritin values came from participants' own records. Hair shedding and participant-reported outcomes were assessed with self-administered instruments that have not yet been validated, and hepcidin and soluble transferrin receptor were not measured. Missing data were not imputed for the hair pull analysis, reducing the sample size for perprotocol analyses. We chose not to impute missing values for several reasons: (1) we did not have a theoretical or prespecified rationale for selecting predictors for the imputation model; (2) including all available variables without a clear rationale increase the risk of overfitting; (3) the small sample size limited our confidence that the imputed values would reliably represent the actual outcome measures; and (4) given the exploratory, hypothesis-generating nature of the perprotocol analyses, we considered the potential uncertainty introduced by imputation less justifiable than analyzing the available paired data.

4.7 Future directions

An adequately powered trial with placebo and iron-only comparator arms, lasting at least 12 weeks, is needed to isolate the effect of heqLF. Such a trial should include hepcidin and soluble transferrin receptor to test the proposed mechanisms, validated fatigue and quality-of-life instruments, and objective measures of hair shedding. Enrolling women with confirmed low ferritin at baseline, rather than at screening, would also increase the scope for detecting changes in iron stores.

5 Conclusions

In women with low ferritin, 6 weeks of heqLF at 50 or 100 mg/d with 5 mg/d iron was accompanied by reductions in iron-binding capacity and transferrin (i.e., improved iron availability), fewer hairs shed on a self-administered pull test, and improvements in participantreported wellness and perceived hair health, without a change in ferritin or differences between doses. These encouraging findings, obtained with lower LF doses and a shorter duration than in previous LF trials, support a larger placebo-controlled trial of heqLF as an adjunct to low-dose iron.
