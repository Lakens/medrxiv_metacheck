How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. Abstract

Sepsis is a leading cause of hospital mortality, yet timely recognition is hampered by nonspecific presentations and label noise in code-based case definitions. We developed STRIDE, a machine-learning framework for sepsis detection across seven hospitals with a scalable approach to label quality. We used a large language model applied to discharge summaries to refine encounter-level labels, with 349 physician-adjudicated encounters informing prompt development and serving as a clinical reference for evaluation. We compared 8-, 24-, and 48-hour observation windows and benchmarked against SOFA, SIRS, and Epic. Among 352,415 eligible encounters, STRIDE-8 achieved ROC-AUCs of 0.949 in derivation testing and 0.871 in physician-adjudicated evaluation. Removing SIRS-related predictors reduced these values to 0.853 and 0.743. Llama reclassified 12.8% of initially positive derivation encounters as nonseptic. These results support further investigation of short-window physiological screening combined with retrospective label review and evaluation against independent clinical reference outcomes. 1.

Introduction

Sepsis is a life-threatening medical condition that arises when the body's response to an infection becomes dysregulated, resulting in widespread inflammation, tissue damage, and potential organ dysfunction [1]. It remains one of the leading causes of mortality in hospital settings, where delayed diagnosis can significantly impact patient outcomes. Each year, approximately 1.7 million adults in the United States develop sepsis, and around 270,000 of them ultimately die as a result [2]. Early intervention is crucial for survival, but the nonspecific and evolving presentation of sepsis makes reliable recognition difficult, often leading to delayed recognition and treatment initiation [3].

Traditional screening tools, such as the Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome (SIRS) criteria [4] and the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score [5], together with guideline-based early recognition and management strategies [6], have long served as foundational methods for sepsis identification. SIRS offers high sensitivity by flagging a broad range of inflammatory responses. However, it is not specific to sepsis: many patients who meet SIRS criteria have no infection, so the criteria have poor specificity and a high false-positive rate, which can contribute to alert fatigue [7]. In contrast, SOFA, though more specific, relies on lab values and organ dysfunction markers that manifest later in the disease course, limiting its utility for early detection and itself is an organ dysfunction score which is not necessarily diagnostic of sepsis. More recently, commercial systems such as the Epic Sepsis Model have been implemented in electronic health record systems, promising automated and real-time risk alerts. However, studies have raised concerns about the transparency and generalizability of proprietary models like Epic, which in some settings have shown substantially suboptimal performance compared to the one reported by its developer [8].

These established screening instruments illustrate the core tension between sensitivity and specificity. High false positive rates in many early warning systems can increase unnecessary clinical evaluations, contribute to resource strain, and promote clinician alert fatigue [8, 9]. Conversely, making the alerting criteria more conservative to suppress false positives comes at the cost of more false negatives, risking missed cases and delayed intervention when timely treatment is essential [10]. This trade-off is especially important because sepsis becomes easier to detect as it progresses, as organ dysfunction and clear clinical signs appear, but the effectiveness of treatment diminishes with each hour of delay [3, 11].

A less visible but equally consequential obstacle lies in the labels used to build and benchmark these tools. Studies show that coded sepsis rates have risen sharply without matching clinical evidence [12] [13] [14]. Rhee et al. 2015 compared data from two academic hospitals between 2003 and 2012 and found a dramatic increase in sepsis International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes (up to +706% for severe sepsis) while objective markers like positive blood cultures, shock, or elevated lactate remained stable or declined [12]. This illustrates how coding for sepsis has become more inclusive, extending the label to patients with less definitive evidence of severe infection. Rhee et al. (2014) illustrated that sepsis-coded hospitalizations increased while hospitalizations coded for common infections remained stable or declined. They also noted declining reported sepsis mortality, suggesting that changes in coding practices may partly explain these trends [15]. In addition, evolving definitions of sepsis have caused inconsistencies in documentation that contribute to overdiagnosis [13, 14]. Any model trained on billing codes therefore risks learning to reproduce documentation behavior rather than physiology.

Machine learning has nonetheless advanced sepsis recognition considerably, and much of this work has explicitly moved away from billing codes toward clinical definitions. COMPOSER issues alerts while abstaining on cases it deems out-of-distribution, reducing false alarms [16]; the Risk of Sepsis score, trained on objective clinical-surveillance criteria rather than diagnostic codes, reports AUCs of 0.93-0.97 [17]; and the Sepsis ImmunoScore, validated against a physician-adjudicated clinical reference standard, became the first FDA-authorized AI tool for sepsis, with AUCs of 0.85 in derivation and 0.81 in external validation [18].

Effective feature engineering and temporal modeling have proven critical for handling high-dimensional, noisy, and irregularly sampled electronic health record (EHR) data: gradient-boosted trees over dynamic vital-sign features yield robust predictions [19], while recurrent neural network-based models such as DeepAISE capture evolving risk through time [20]. More recent work has extended this line of research with deep-learning and probabilistic approaches, including validation across multiple sites and international settings [21] [22] [23] [24].

Yet important limitations persist even in these more advanced approaches. First, label quality remains a fundamental constraint: whether labels come from billing codes or from clinical consensus definitions, they are generated automatically from structured proxies (suspicion of infection inferred from culture and antibiotic timing, organ dysfunction inferred from score changes) and are rarely validated against the clinical record at scale. While some approaches have incorporated unstructured clinical notes to improve sepsis identification [25, 26] or used physician adjudication to validate labels [18], these strategies are rarely combined and seldom applied at the scale of a full derivation cohort, leaving residual label noise that propagates into both training and evaluation. Second, many models commit to a single fixed observation window, leaving open how much patient history is actually needed to characterize a patient's current state at the point of care. Together, these gaps motivate the framework we present here.

Language Model-Based Label Refinement

To enhance label precision across the large derivation cohort, we integrated a language-modelbased label refinement step (Fig. 3). We applied Llama 3.1-8B Instruct [27] to analyze discharge summaries and classify septic encounters as truly septic or non-septic. In the 349 physicianadjudicated encounters used to develop the prompt, Llama agreed with physician labels in 314 cases (90.0%; 95% CI 86.4-92.7%). Sensitivity was 93.8% and specificity was 62.8% (Supplementary Table S12). Of the encounters Llama retained, physicians had confirmed sepsis in 94.7% (287/303), compared with 87.7% (306/349) of all sampled encounters. We then applied this model across the septic-labeled encounters in the derivation cohort. As a result, 8,395 encounters (12.8%) were reclassified by Llama as non-septic. Patients with any reclassified encounter were assigned only to training, so the test sets in the main analysis contained no reclassified encounters; Supplementary Table S13 evaluates alternative allocations. Further details about the use of Llama are provided in the Methods. Demographics and length of stay were analyzed per encounter. Septic encounters involved older individuals (mean age 67.1 years, SD 16.8) compared to non-septic SIRS+ (62.6 years, SD 19.7) and SIRS-groups (63.3 years, SD 19.5). Men represented 52.5% of septic, 48.4% of SIRS+, and 45.8% of SIRS-encounters. Prolonged hospitalizations (≥9 days) occurred in 31.9% of septic encounters versus 21.4% for non-septic SIRS+ and 11.0% for non-septic SIRS-. Only 9.6% of septic encounters resulted in discharge within 0-2 days (vs. 31.9% in non-septic SIRS-). The physician-adjudicated cohort mirrored these trends. Predictive Model Performance

We evaluated three gradient-boosted (XGBoost) models, hereafter STRIDE-8, STRIDE-24, and STRIDE-48, that differ in how much preceding patient history they use to classify the patient's current sepsis state. STRIDE-8 uses only the most recent 8-hour interval, a single static snapshot with no prior context, whereas STRIDE-24 and STRIDE-48 incorporate the three and six consecutive 8-hour intervals leading up to the detection point, respectively, allowing them to capture progressively longer-term physiological trends (full specification in Methods, Table 7).

Using these windows, we assessed both discrimination and calibration of the models on the testing set of the derivation cohort. STRIDE-8 and STRIDE-24 achieved mean ROC-AUCs of 0.949, followed by STRIDE-48 at 0.945; corresponding ROC-AUCs in the physician-adjudicated cohort were 0.871, 0.870 and 0.861, respectively (Table 2).

To assess performance across different populations, we further evaluated all models across multiple encounter subpopulations of the derivation cohort, including an only septic encounter cohort, and populations where non-septic encounters either met or did not meet SIRS criteria.

Discussion

We developed and evaluated STRIDE, a machine-learning framework for dynamic sepsis detection using vital signs and laboratory measurements from seven Hartford HealthCare hospitals.

We first refined encounter labels using discharge summaries and then trained the detection models on structured clinical measurements. Three observations emerge from the analyses. First, the label used for training is itself a design decision with measurable consequences. Second, recent physiology carries most of the information needed to characterize a patient's current status. Third, the performance of any model evaluated against an operational definition is bounded by what that definition encodes.

Broad operational definitions and administrative codes are known to overflag sepsis [29], while unstructured clinical notes can help refine case identification [25, 26, 30]. Our adjudication illustrated the first point directly, as specialists rejected sepsis in roughly one in eight sampled encounters that carried both a sepsis code and the operational clinical criteria. A language model applied to discharge narratives makes it possible to audit labels across an entire derivation cohort, a scale that manual review cannot reach. We used physician-reviewed encounters to develop the Llama prompt and then applied it to discharge summaries in the derivation cohort.

In the prompt-development cases, physicians confirmed sepsis more often among encounters Llama retained than among coded encounters overall (94.7% versus 87.7%), but they also confirmed sepsis in 19 of 46 encounters that Llama classified as non-septic (Supplementary Table S12). Label refinement is therefore a trade-off on the labels themselves rather than a correction, and the balance chosen should be stated explicitly. The prompt also asked whether an infection was documented, not whether a sepsis definition was met. It therefore targets sepsis codes assigned to patients without a documented infection, rather than misjudged severity or organ dysfunction. STRIDE performed similarly in the adjudicated cohort however the reclassified encounters were allocated (Supplementary Table S13). In this study, the main contribution of refinement therefore lies in the composition and auditability of the training labels. Establishing whether it improves detection requires a reference standard independent of both the diagnosis codes and the prompt.

In the eligible encounters, all of which had at least 48 hours of recorded data, longer input windows added little to discrimination. Part of this follows from the task. Because each label describes the current interval, the most recent measurements are the most directly informative, and earlier trajectories add little to characterizing present status. Longer histories may matter more for forecasting onset, which this study did not address. For contemporaneous classification, a short window has practical advantages. It requires less preceding data, is less exposed to gaps in the record, and can begin classifying once 8 hours of data are available rather than 24 or 48. To capture the variability of frequently measured variables within each interval, the model summarizes every 8-hour period by its minimum, maximum and mean. This design preserves fluctuations that a single reading could miss, as in other temporal representations of clinical time series [31, 32].

The most influential predictors were SIRS components, which is expected because SIRS forms part of the outcome definition. This pattern also aligns with systematic reviews showing that dynamic vital signs and laboratory values are the most influential features in machine learning models for sepsis prediction [33].

Two analyses clarify what this overlap means. Without the SIRS measurements, STRIDE still discriminated better than Epic and SOFA in both cohorts, drawing on variables such as albumin, oxygen saturation and age (Table 5). The model therefore uses information beyond the SIRS criteria. Among intervals already meeting SIRS criteria, however, discrimination fell for every method, and the remaining variation in the label depended on sepsis coding and the presence of culture or imaging orders. These reflect clinical practice as well as the patient's condition, and the lower discrimination is consistent with the difficulty of recovering order-dependent labels from physiological measurements. This illustrates a broader point. Operational definitions built from orders and codes encode clinician behavior, and models trained and evaluated against them partly learn that behavior. Under such labels, the ceiling on physiology-based detection is set by the label as much as by the model. The comparison with established tools should be read in light of what each measures. SIRS performs well against this outcome largely because it is part of it. SOFA quantifies organ dysfunction, which typically appears later and is not specific to infection. The Epic Sepsis Model was developed under its vendor's own outcome definition and alerting configuration, which we could not reproduce [8]. Lower discrimination for these tools against a contemporaneous, SIRSbased label does not show how they would perform against other clinical definitions. Rather, the comparison shows how much of the operational target structured physiology can recover beyond simple rules. Interval classifications are not alerts. Most positive classifications followed an earlier positive interval in the same encounter, so the burden on clinicians would depend less on per-interval specificity than on the alerting policy built around the model, including how repeated positives are consolidated, when alerts are suppressed after acknowledgement, and which patients are monitored. Assessing STRIDE as a clinical tool requires specifying such a policy and evaluating it prospectively. This study did not assess whether STRIDE shortens time to treatment or improves patient outcomes. Several limitations should be considered. Our data come from a single health system, and although it spans seven hospitals, these share an electronic health record and common clinical practices, so external validation across independent systems is needed before broader deployment. The operational sepsis definition combined SIRS, clinical orders used as a proxy for infection investigation, and sepsis diagnosis codes. The order criterion included all entries in the imaging table, extending beyond studies ordered specifically for suspected infection. Physicians established whether sepsis occurred during an encounter but did not assign independent onset times. Diagnosis codes were also visible to them, and paired ratings were not retained. The adjudicated cohort contained only initially sepsis-positive encounters and also informed prompt development, so it is neither an independent test of Llama nor a basis for estimating performance among patients without a sepsis code. Performance was examined by hospital, calendar year and demographic group, but not by ward or illness severity, and calibration was not assessed within subgroups.

Label refinement also remains imperfect. Refinement was applied only to the positive class, so sepsis missed by coding remains among the negatives. The septic cohort was identified from billing codes and infection-related criteria, whose accuracy depends on documentation quality, and the language model relies on clinical notes that are themselves subjective and written after the hospitalization. These narratives influenced only the training labels and were not available to STRIDE as predictors. Future work could incorporate additional automated tools or manual chart review to strengthen labels in both classes.

Finally, incomplete and irregularly sampled data required imputation and aggregation, which may introduce bias, particularly because interpolation can draw on later observations.

Omitting predictor interpolation reduced but did not remove discrimination (Supplementary Table S10). Prospective use would nonetheless require a fully time-causal preprocessing pipeline and an independently timed clinical reference. The eligibility criteria also excluded short stays and encounters with substantial missingness, and excluded sepsis-coded encounters had higher in-hospital mortality than included ones, so STRIDE's performance in these patients is unknown (Supplementary Table S15). Some informative variables, most notably procalcitonin, were not routinely measured and could not be included [34, 35].

Our results support combining retrospective review of coded sepsis labels with short-window physiological detection, and indicate that the reference outcome, rather than the model, is the main constraint on evaluating this approach. Independent, time-specific clinical adjudication that includes coded-negative encounters would allow both stages to be evaluated without overlap between predictors and outcome definition, and would make it possible to measure when STRIDE classifications occur relative to clinical recognition. Together with prospective assessment of a defined alerting policy, it is the most important next step in evaluating the clinical use of STRIDE.

Methods

Exclusion Criteria

We began by identifying hospital encounters labeled as septic or non-septic using diagnosis codes. The EHR data covered January 2019 to July 2025 across seven Hartford HealthCare hospitals and included diagnosis codes, clinical orders, demographic characteristics, vital signs and laboratory results. To ensure high data quality and temporal continuity for modeling, several exclusion criteria were applied. Encounters were eligible regardless of age, so the cohort includes some newborn encounters with neonatal sepsis codes (Supplementary Table S16); adult SIRS thresholds were applied to all encounters. The encounter inclusion diagram for both septic and non-septic encounters can be seen in Fig. 2. Encounters with more than 40% missing feature values after initial interpolation were excluded, as high levels of missingness compromise the reliability of downstream analyses (see Table S2 of the Supplementary Information). We also excluded encounters with fewer than 48 hours of recorded data, so all three models could be evaluated on a common study population. After these preprocessing steps, we applied our previously defined operational sepsis criteria and the septic encounters that did not meet this definition were excluded. Finally, we required that the data for each encounter span consecutive calendar days; if gaps in the time series were present, the encounters were split and any resulting segment shorter than two days was excluded. Characteristics of included and excluded encounters are compared in Supplementary Table S15. Reporting was guided by TRIPOD+AI [36].

Sepsis Definition and Label Refinement: Operational Definition of Sepsis

For the purposes of this study, we define sepsis using criteria established by the 2001 International Sepsis Definitions Conference [37], which operationalized sepsis as the presence of systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) in the context of suspected or confirmed infection. Specifically, a patient is considered septic during any 8-hour interval in which all of the following conditions are met: 1.

SIRS criteria are satisfied [4]. This includes meeting at least two of the standard SIRS thresholds (See Section 3 of the Supplementary Information for further details).

2. At least one of the following records was assigned to the interval as an operational indicator of suspected infection: (a) A culture order (e.g., blood, urine, sputum) (b) An imaging-table record (e.g., radiograph or CT; the table also includes non-radiological studies).

3. The hospital encounter is associated with a sepsis-related diagnosis code.

This operational definition uses the 2001 consensus criteria [37] and combines physiological evidence of systemic inflammation with clinical investigation for a possible infection. Culture orders were assigned to intervals using their recorded time, while imaging records were assigned to all three intervals of the order date. The imaging criterion included all entries in the imaging table, regardless of body region or modality (Supplementary Section 5). Labels were generated using recorded and interpolated measurements before multivariable imputation, and each positive label referred to the current 8-hour interval.

Sepsis Definition and Label Refinement: Llama 3.1-8B Instruct

Sepsis diagnosis codes in administrative data are known to have variable accuracy, often leading to false positives due to overcoding, retrospective documentation practices, or cases where sepsis is suspected but ultimately ruled out [15]. In our initial derivation cohort, we observed that a subset of encounters labeled as septic based on diagnosis codes and operational clinical criteria did not exhibit clinical features consistent with sepsis, raising concerns about label quality and potential noise in model training.

To address this issue, we implemented a label refinement strategy using the Llama 3.1-8B Instruct language model [27], which enabled integration of unstructured clinical narrative into the labeling process (Fig. 3). As described in the Physician-adjudicated Cohort Overview section, we used physician-adjudicated encounters reviewed by Infectious Disease specialists to develop the Llama prompt and provide clinical reference labels for STRIDE evaluation.

We developed the prompt through an iterative refinement process, adjusting it based on agreement with the physician-adjudicated labels. The clinical reviews were completed before prompt development, and the same cases were used to assess agreement with Llama; they were not an independent test of the prompt. To focus the analysis on the most clinically relevant text, the Llama model was provided with the "Hospital Course" section from each discharge summary (or the full summary when that section could not be identified) and prompted to return a binary (yes/no) classification regarding documented evidence of infection or sepsis.

This approach offered a consistent and scalable interpretation of clinical text to complement structured data.

We then applied the finalized prompt to the remaining initially positive derivation encounters. Encounters that the model reclassified as non-septic were relabeled accordingly. Patients with reclassified encounters were assigned to training in the primary model evaluation. Initially non-septic encounters were not reassessed. Discharge narratives were used for label refinement and were not included as STRIDE predictors. Further technical details about the Llama model and prompt structure are available in Section 8 of the Supplementary Information.

Sepsis Definition and Label Refinement: Derivation Cohort Overview

The derivation cohort was analyzed in three mutually exclusive groups: septic, non-septic SIRS-positive and non-septic SIRS-negative. Initial classification depended on sepsis diagnosis codes and the operational clinical criteria, followed by Llama refinement of initially positive encounters.

The first group consists of 57,301 encounters with a sepsis diagnosis code and the operational criteria that retained a positive label after Llama refinement. These are considered septic and constitute the positive class for model training and evaluation. The second group comprises encounters that did not receive a sepsis diagnosis code but did meet the SIRS and infection-investigation components of the clinical sepsis criteria. It also includes the encounters reclassified by Llama. These encounters were classified as non-septic either because they lacked a sepsis diagnosis code or because they were reclassified by Llama following retrospective review of their discharge summaries. The third group contains encounters that neither carried a sepsis diagnosis code nor met the clinical sepsis criteria at any point during hospitalization.

Together, the second and third groups form the negative class used for model training and evaluation. The final two non-septic groups can be partitioned between the SIRS-positive and the SIRS-negative encounters.

This structure ensures that the model is trained on a broad spectrum of clinical presentations, including coded sepsis, cases with sepsis-like features but no clinical diagnosis, and encounters without the specified clinical criteria. This allowed performance to be assessed using both SIRS-positive and SIRS-negative non-septic encounters.

Sepsis Definition and Label Refinement: Physician-adjudicated Cohort Overview

Each of the physician-adjudicated cohort encounters represents a unique patient randomly selected from the initially sepsis-positive eligible encounters and set aside from the derivation cohort. Specifically, these encounters had a hospital sepsis diagnosis code assigned and met the clinical sepsis criteria at least once during hospitalization. Each case was manually reviewed on a case-by-case basis by Infectious Disease specialists from Hartford HealthCare. The infectious disease experts reviewed the clinical record, including diagnoses, medical history and discharge documentation, and assigned a single encounter-level sepsis label. Original diagnosis codes were visible, but paired independent ratings were not retained, so inter-rater agreement could not be calculated. The review did not determine the time of sepsis onset. As a result of this rigorous adjudication process, 306 encounters were confirmed as truly septic, while 43 were reclassified as non-septic, reflecting the clinical ambiguity that often surrounds sepsis diagnosis. In confirmed encounters, 8-hour labels followed the operational clinical criteria; in rejected encounters, all intervals were considered non-septic.

Feature Engineering and Modeling Approach: Feature Selection

Data were collected using the EHR database of the Hartford HealthCare hospital system. The features included in this study were selected in collaboration with Infectious Disease specialists at Hartford HealthCare to ensure clinical relevance to the detection of sepsis. The final dataset comprised 25 clinical and demographic variables: 6 demographic variables, 6 vital signs, and 13 laboratory-based clinical measurements. The 19 time-varying variables were each summarized by three statistics, giving 63 model inputs per 8-hour interval once the six demographic attributes were added. Demographic variables, age, sex, race, multiracial identification, ethnicity and smoking status, were treated as static for each hospital encounter. Time-varying features were extracted from structured EHR and selected to reflect physiologic domains known to be disrupted in sepsis, including inflammation and organ dysfunction. Vital signs are central to clinical scoring systems such as SIRS and SOFA, while the laboratory features reflect renal and hepatic function, inflammatory response and metabolic status. These measurements are routinely collected in inpatient settings and have demonstrated relevance to sepsis pathophysiology and clinical outcomes [38] [39] [40] [41]. All features were selected for their clinical relevance, consistency across patient records, and utility in sepsis risk stratification. The interval timestamp was retained as a non-clinical temporal indexing variable associated with each 8-hour interval, used to preserve temporal ordering when constructing the observation windows; it was not a predictor, nor were patient identifiers or medical-history variables.

Feature Engineering and Modeling Approach: Data Restructuring and Sliding Window

To prepare the data for modeling, time-varying features were aggregated at the observation level using fixed 8-hour intervals across each patient encounter. As a result, each day consists of three 8-hour intervals. Within each interval, we computed the mean, minimum, and maximum values for every continuous feature, resulting in a structured time-series representation of the patient's physiological state. This approach enables the model to capture clinically meaningful trends in vital signs and laboratory results. After initial interpolation, each segment was evaluated for sepsis using our operational sepsis definition. A binary label was assigned to each interval: 1 if the patient met sepsis criteria during that interval, and 0 otherwise. After linear interpolation, IterativeImputer was used to impute any remaining missing values [42]. For each split, an imputer fitted to pooled training observations from both classes was applied without refitting to test and adjudicated patients. Fitting used at most 200,000 sampled training rows, ten iterations and a split-specific random seed. A separate training-fitted imputer completed the additional measurements required to calculate SOFA. The interval labels had already been assigned and were not changed by this multivariable imputation. More details about the missing data imputation process can be found in Section 6 of the Supplementary Information.

To handle the temporal nature of the data, we applied a sliding window technique to transform the time-series data into a format suitable for supervised learning. This approach captures the temporal evolution of the variables by creating fixed-size windows of data that move sequentially across the time series with a specified shift interval. Each window moved forward by one 8-hour interval. For our analysis, we utilized three window configurations: 1.

A window size of 6, where each window encompasses six consecutive 8-hour intervals, corresponding to a total duration of 48 hours.

2. A window size of 3, where each window encompasses three consecutive 8-hour intervals, corresponding to a total duration of 24 hours.

3. A window size of 1, where each window contains data from a single 8-hour interval.

For each multi-interval window, the target label was derived exclusively from the final interval in the sequence, enabling the model to learn from preceding physiological patterns to classify the patient's current sepsis state. The single-interval model does not incorporate historical information; the label corresponds directly to the single 8-hour interval being analyzed.

This design allows the models to leverage different depths of historical context while maintaining a consistent goal: identifying the patient's sepsis status in the present. An overview of these configurations is presented in Table 7, and a detailed explanation of the sliding window approach is shown in Fig. 6 and Section 9 of the Supplementary Information. The 8-, 24-and 48-hour models used 63, 189 and 378 inputs, respectively.

Feature Engineering and Modeling Approach: Model Development

We trained XGBoost binary classification models to classify the presence of sepsis, with the outcome labeled as 1 if the patient had sepsis and 0 otherwise [43]. XGBoost performed similarly to Random Forest with an 8-hour window and slightly better with longer windows (Supplementary Section 12). Model training was performed using five repeated patient-level model variability and generalization. To contextualize our results, we compared the predictive performance of our models to established clinical benchmarks, including the SOFA score, the Epic score and the SIRS.

SIRS was calculated from temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate and white blood cell count using the same recorded and interpolated measurements used to assign the interval labels. In each partition, the dataset was randomly split into training and testing sets, with approximately 70% of eligible patients assigned to the training set and the remaining patients to the test set. To maintain independence and prevent data leakage, all encounters from the same patient were assigned exclusively to either the training or testing subset within each split. Finally, model performance was evaluated on the physician-adjudicated cohort, which remained separate from STRIDE training.

For each model, StandardScaler was fitted on the training features and applied unchanged to evaluation patients. The same specification was used across splits, without using test or physician-adjudicated labels to select hyperparameters. No hyperparameter search was performed, and the Random Forest comparison used the same values (Supplementary Section 12).

Performance Evaluation: Model Evaluation

We evaluated model performance across three observation windows: 48 hours, 24 hours, and 8 hours, each ending at the interval being classified. Key metrics included the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC) and average precision from the precisionrecall curve, which were chosen to capture both overall discrimination and performance in the presence of class imbalance. Calibration curves were generated for each time window to assess the agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcome frequencies, providing insight into the reliability of the model's risk estimates. To evaluate model performance at a clinically relevant operating point, we identified the probability threshold that achieved a target sensitivity of 80% using derivation test patients. Each derivation test set was divided into two groups of patients: a threshold was selected on one group and evaluated on the other, and then the groups were exchanged. The threshold selected from the full derivation test set was applied unchanged to the physician-adjudicated cohort. For the integer-valued SOFA score, the highest threshold achieving at least the target sensitivity was selected. SIRS was evaluated at its prespecified fixed rule. All performance metrics were reported as the mean and the corresponding 95% confidence intervals, calculated from 200 patient-level bootstrap resamples per split, pooled across the five splits.
