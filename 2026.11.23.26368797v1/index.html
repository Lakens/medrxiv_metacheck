Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

P-Value Text
p < 0.05 When the Friedman test indicated a statistically significant difference among the models (p < 0.05), post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using the two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 2 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. Abstract

Tabular foundation models (TFMs) are increasingly used for structured prediction, yet their suitability for healthcare applications remains uncertain because predictive performance alone does not establish fairness, probabilistic reliability, or robustness. We developed a multidimensional benchmark to compare four TFMs: TabFM, TabPFN, TabDPT, and TabICL, against Logistic Regression and XGBoost across a curated, standardized suite of ten WHO-aligned healthcare classification datasets across diverse disease domains. Additionally, we harmonized clinical schemas, standardized protected sex attributes and outcome targets, and released both balanced-scale and natural-prevalence variants of the datasets to establish a benchmarking resource in healthcare machine learning. All models were evaluated using identical five-fold stratified cross-validation partitions and standardized preprocessing. Performance was assessed using 20 metrics spanning predictive discrimination, sex-based fairness, probability calibration, and robustness to Gaussian feature perturbations. A composite Clinical Model Evaluation Score (CMES) was used to summarize performance across these dimensions. Google TabFM achieved the highest mean CMES (0.8811), followed closely by TabDPT (0.8796), TabICL (0.8775), TabPFN (0.8756), XGBoost (0.8650), and Logistic Regression (0.8549). The Friedman test indicated significant differences among models (χ 2 F (5) = 24.914, p = 1.45 × 10 -4 ; Kendall's W = 0.498). However, post-hoc comparisons revealed no statistically significant differences among the four TFMs after Holm correction. TFMs generally demonstrated stronger discrimination and probabilistic calibration than conventional baselines, with TabFM achieving the highest mean Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (0.9327) and the lowest expected calibration error (0.0674). Conversely, Logistic Regression exhibited the greatest stability under feature perturbations, while fairness performance varied substantially across datasets and models. These findings highlight the potential of TFMs for small-data healthcare prediction while demonstrating that their advantages are dimension-dependent. Multidimensional, dataset-specific evaluation and external validation remain essential before clinical implementation. All analysis codes, original datasets, and multiple variants of benchmark datasets are publicly available in a GitHub repository to ensure reproducibility and future research.

1 Introduction

Tabular foundation models (TFMs) represent an emerging paradigm for structured data prediction, leveraging patterns learned across large pretraining corpora to generalize to new tasks with minimal or no dataset-specific training. Early progress was catalyzed by TabPFN, which demonstrated strong predictive performance on small tabular datasets through transformer-based tabular in-context learning (ICL) [1].

Building on this foundation, recent TFMs have extended tabular ICL through diverse architectural designs and pretraining strategies, including TabFM [2], TabICL [3], and TabDPT [4]. However, as these models gain attention for high-stakes applications such as digital health, strong accuracy on general-purpose tabular benchmarks does not by itself establish how they perform on clinical data or whether their probability estimates and failure modes are suitable for healthcare decision-making.

Many studies evaluated Machine Learning (ML) models on healthcare data, focusing on model's discriminative power [5, 6]. However, evaluating machine learning models for healthcare requires assessing reliability beyond a single aggregate accuracy score. Predicted probabilities must be well calibrated so that estimated risk aligns with observed outcome frequencies, because poor calibration can distort clinical risk stratification and deteriorate across shifting patient populations [7, 8]. Fairness also requires explicit scrutiny because measurement disparities and historical clinical workflows can induce unequal error rates or selection rates across demographic subgroups such as biological sex [9, 10]. Furthermore, deployment settings routinely introduce recording variability and feature noise, meaning models should remain stable under input perturbations rather than exhibiting abrupt probability shifts or flipped classifications [11]. Contemporary clinical AI governance frameworks therefore emphasize multidimensional evaluation encompassing predictive discrimination, uncertainty calibration, algorithmic fairness, and robustness [12]. Conducting such an evaluation rigorously requires not only harmonized metrics, but also a disease-oriented dataset suite anchored in real global public health priorities.

The World Health Organization (WHO) Global Health Estimates provide an epidemiological basis for selecting conditions that reflect major global mortality and morbidity burdens. According to WHO estimates for 2023, five of the ten leading causes of death worldwide are ischaemic heart disease, stroke, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), Alzheimer disease and other dementias, and diabetes mellitus [13]. Including clinical classification tasks for these five conditions anchors our benchmark in the leading drivers of global mortality, alongside cardiac arrhythmia, which is closely connected to ischaemic heart disease because myocardial ischaemia induces electrical instability and lethal ventricular arrhythmias that frequently precipitate sudden cardiac death [14]. To broaden coverage beyond leading causes of death to WHO global elimination and chronic morbidity priorities, the suite also incorporates hepatitis and chronic liver disease, reflecting WHO's 2030 viral hepatitis elimination agenda in which chronic hepatitis B and C infections serve as major drivers of chronic liver disease, cirrhosis, and liver cancer. Finally, autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and thyroid disease represent high-burden neurodevelopmental and endocrine conditions characterized by pronounced, opposing sex asymmetries: ASD affects approximately 1 in 127 people globally with substantially higher prevalence and diagnostic rates in males than in females [15, 16], whereas thyroid disorders rank among the most prevalent endocrine conditions worldwide with markedly higher incidence and prevalence in women than in men [17]. Together, these ten tasks span distinct organ systems, clinical modalities, and demographic risk profiles.

Evaluating TFMs across this multi-disease suite makes it possible to examine whether general-purpose tabular ICL models capture clinically meaningful patterns consistently across heterogeneous healthcare domains. Because these datasets encompass demographic variables, clinical symptoms, physiological measurements, and biochemical markers, comparing models across all ten tasks reveals whether strong performance remains consistent beyond isolated disease areas. At the same time, high performance on one evaluation dimension does not imply adequacy on another: a model with high discrimination may still produce miscalibrated probabilities, exhibit disparity between male and female patients, or react sensitively to minor input noise. Moreover, apparent associations or strong cross-validation scores within curated datasets should not be interpreted as evidence of causal mechanisms or autonomous diagnostic readiness.

Rather, a multidimensional benchmark clarifies specific operational strengths and trade-offs across candidate TFMs before any downstream clinical adaptation is considered.

Previous evaluations of TFMs have focused primarily on broad multi-domain leaderboards and headline 3/28 predictive accuracy or AUROC, leaving open how leading tabular ICL architectures compare under unified clinical trustworthiness criteria. Comparing TabFM [2], TabPFN [1], TabDPT [4], and TabICL [3] independently across disparate preprocessing pipelines risks conflating model behavior with differences in data splitting, imputation, or metric definitions. A fair head-to-head comparison therefore requires evaluating all four models on identical stratified cross-validation partitions, applying missing-value imputation strictly within each training fold to prevent data leakage, and enforcing uniform positive-class definitions, protected-group mappings, and decision thresholds. Standardizing these experimental controls ensures that observed differences across fairness, discrimination, calibration, and robustness reflect intrinsic model properties rather than evaluation artifacts.

A rigorous multidimensional benchmark should evaluate complementary metric families that answer distinct clinical and statistical questions. Predictive discrimination metrics, including the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) measure how effectively a model separates positive and negative outcomes. Uncertainty calibration metrics like Expected Calibration Error (ECE), negative log-likelihood (NLL), Brier score, calibration slope, and calibration intercept assess whether predicted positive probabilities correspond to observed event frequencies and remain free from systematic over-or under-confidence [7]. Group fairness metrics, such as Disparate Impact (DI), Equal Opportunity Difference (EOD), Demographic Parity Difference (DPD), Equalized Odds Difference (EqOD), and Predictive Parity Difference (PPD) quantify prediction equity between male and female subgroups [10]. Finally, robustness metrics under controlled Gaussian feature perturbations, signed degradations in AUROC, accuracy, and F1-score, alongside prediction flip rate and mean absolute probability change (MAPC) capture how reliably individual predictions and overall performance hold under measurement noise [12]. Assessing all 20 metrics jointly provides a comprehensive profile of model trustworthiness rather than relying on a single leaderboard ranking.

Against this background, benchmarking TabFM, TabPFN, TabDPT, and TabICL across ten WHO-aligned healthcare classification datasets establishes empirical evidence on the relative trade-offs of modern tabular ICL models in clinical settings. The objective is not to replace domain-specific clinical validation or establish readiness for autonomous medical deployment, which would require external validation in prospective target populations [7, 8]. Instead, a standardized internal cross-validation benchmark provides a reproducible baseline identifying which TFMs balance predictive discrimination, sex-based fairness, probabilistic calibration, and noise resilience across diverse healthcare tasks, and where specific vulnerabilities warrant targeted mitigation.

Accordingly, this study investigates how TabFM, TabPFN, TabDPT, and TabICL compare across diverse tabular healthcare classification datasets by addressing six research questions:

1.1 Related Work

For tabular prediction tasks, gradient-boosted decision trees (GBDTs) long served as the default baseline over deep neural architectures. In a large-scale empirical comparison across 176 classification datasets, McElfresh et al. demonstrated that neither GBDTs nor task-specific neural networks dominated universally, showing instead that dataset metafeatures and tuning budgets heavily governed model rankings [18]. To eliminate per-dataset gradient optimization altogether, Hollmann et al. introduced TabPFN, a transformer pretrained on millions of synthetic causal datasets to approximate Bayesian posterior predictive distributions via tabular in-context ICL in a single forward pass [19]. While this initial formulation was constrained to small, purely numerical tables without missing entries, a subsequent iteration (TabPFNv2) expanded the synthetic structural causal prior to accommodate missing values, categorical variables, and heterogeneous feature distributions [1], with later scaling efforts extending context capacity and features [20].

Concurrently, competing TFMs have explored distinct pretraining paradigms and scalable attention mechanisms. Qu et al. proposed TabICL, employing a two-stage column-then-row attention architecture that embeds rows independently before performing cross-row ICL on large datasets [3], and later introduced TabICLv2 with scalable softmax attention and diversified synthetic data generation [21]. Departing from purely synthetic priors, Ma et al. developed TabDPT, which combines retrieval-augmented in-context inference with self-supervised pretraining on real-world tabular corpora [4]. Most recently, Google Research introduced TabFM to strengthen zero-shot generalization across heterogeneous tabular schemas [2]. To standardize comparisons across these rapidly evolving architectures, Erickson et al. established the TabArena living benchmark over 51 curated datasets, showing that modern tabular ICL models now match or surpass heavily tuned GBDT ensembles on independent and identically distributed (IID) tasks [22]. However, a critical synthesis by Kurashkin et al. cautioned that benchmark gains on general independent and identically distributed (IID) leaderboards do not resolve open questions regarding conditional probability coverage, distribution-shift resilience, and fairness [23], underscoring the need to evaluate TFMs within domain-specific, high-stakes settings such as digital healthcare.

1.1.1 Tabular Foundation Models in Clinical and Healthcare Domain

Transitioning tabular prediction models into healthcare introduces stricter methodological and clinical requirements than general-purpose benchmarking. The TRIPOD+AI consensus statement updated clinical prediction reporting standards to cover ML alongside regression, emphasizing probability calibration, and subgroup evaluation [24]. Similarly, the FUTURE-AI guideline formalized six core principles: fairness, universality, traceability, usability, robustness, and explainability, as prerequisites for trustworthy medical AI [12]. Against these standards, recent empirical studies have begun testing TFMs on specialized medical tasks. Zhang et al. evaluated TabPFN against five conventional ML algorithms in a retrospective cohort of 986 neonates for hyperbilirubinemia risk stratification, reporting strong discrimination, Brier scores, and net clinical benefit across both internal cross-validation and temporal holdout cohorts [25]. In musculoskeletal screening, Liang and Wang combined anatomical pose features with TabPFN and Shapley-based explanations to classify forward head posture across 1,577 adolescents [26]. Beyond physiological screening, Shawosh et al. evaluated TabPFN for explainable health insurance claim prediction, highlighting its utility in reducing dataset-specific hyperparameter tuning overhead [27].

Despite encouraging early results, the existing healthcare literature on TFMs exhibits two notable limitations. First, applied clinical evaluations to date have focused almost exclusively on TabPFN as a standalone representative of tabular ICL, comparing it against classical baselines such as XGBoost or logistic regression rather than against competing TFMs like TabFM, TabDPT, or TabICL. Second, prior medical evaluations typically examine a single clinical endpoint or institutional cohort, making it difficult to assess whether observed strengths generalize across diverse disease categories, sample sizes, and feature modalities. Consequently, there remains little comparative evidence on how distinct TFMs perform across a standardized, multi-disease public health benchmark.

1.1.2 Trustworthiness Dimensions: Calibration, Fairness, and Perturbation Robustness

Beyond predictive discrimination, clinical usefulness depends on probabilistic calibration, demographic fairness, and stability under noisy inputs [28]. Van Calster et al. emphasized that discrimination alone cannot guarantee safe risk estimation, as miscalibrated probabilities directly distort clinical decision thresholds [7], while Youssef et al. argued that calibration drift under shifting clinical environments necessitates recurring local validation [8]. Several recent studies have begun examining TFM calibration explicitly. Helli et al. demonstrated that standard TabPFN can experience degraded accuracy and ECE under temporal distribution shifts, motivating a drift-resilient causal prior to preserve calibrated predictions [29]. Extending this inquiry to multimodal embeddings, Zhang et al. reported that TabPFN consistently lowered NLL and ECE relative to classical classification heads across 22,820 evaluation episodes, though its relative accuracy advantage narrowed under high feature dimensionality and scarce labeled contexts [30]. Whether these calibration properties hold uniformly across TabFM, TabDPT, and TabICL on clinical tabular data, when assessed with logistic calibration slope and intercept alongside ECE, NLL, and Brier score remains largely unexamined.

Algorithmic fairness represents a parallel concern in healthcare AI, where biased sampling and unequal access to care can yield disparate error rates across protected attributes such as biological sex [10, 31, 32].

Addressing causal bias in prior-data fitted networks, Robertson et al. introduced FairPFN, demonstrating that standard synthetic-prior pretraining can propagate observational correlations tied to protected attributes unless counterfactual fairness is explicitly encoded during pretraining [33]. Most directly relevant to multi-model TFM comparison, Kenfack et al. conducted the first systematic investigation of group fairness across TabPFNv2, TabICL, and TabDPT, revealing that all three models exhibit measurable demographic parity, equalized odds, and equal opportunity gaps under standard context selection unless uncertainty-based or correlation-removal interventions are applied [34]. However, their benchmark evaluated general tabular datasets rather than clinical disease tasks, and did not include Google TabFM or pair fairness audits with calibration and noise-robustness profiling.

Finally, real-world clinical measurements are routinely affected by sensor noise, transcription errors, and physiological variability, making perturbation robustness essential for reliable deployment. Djilani et al. investigated test-time adversarial robustness across TabPFN and TabICL on finance, cybersecurity, and healthcare datasets, showing that small, structured perturbations to test inputs with a fixed training context can significantly degrade accuracy and flip predictions [35]. Yet while targeted adversarial attacks establish worst-case vulnerability, less is known about how competing TFMs, including TabFM and TabDPT respond to standardized, non-adversarial Gaussian measurement noise across clinical covariates when protected demographic attributes remain unperturbed.

1.1.3 Synthesis and Research Gap

Synthesizing recent advances from 2023 to 2026 reveals a clear disconnect between rapid architectural progress in TFMs and the multidimensional evidence required for clinical AI adoption. General-domain benchmarks such as TabArena establish strong predictive accuracy across TFMs [22], yet critical reviews note persistent gaps in uncertainty coverage, fairness, and shift robustness [23]. Meanwhile, emerging clinical studies test TabPFN in isolated single-disease cohorts [25, 26], and specialized trustworthiness studies examine fairness [34], distribution-shift calibration [29], or test-time perturbations [35] in isolation across non-standardized datasets. No prior study has conducted a controlled, head-to-head comparison of TabFM, TabPFN, TabDPT, and TabICL across a WHO-aligned suite of healthcare classification datasets a harmonized 20-metric framework spanning fairness, predictive discrimination, calibration, and Gaussian noise robustness. The present benchmark directly addresses this gap.

2 Materials and Methods

We establish a standardized, reproducible benchmarking pipeline across ten diverse healthcare classification tasks to systematically evaluate the reliability of emerging TFMs in clinical settings. Our experimental protocol compares four TFMs and two traditional ML models under identical data partitions, preprocessing 6/28 rules, and evaluation criteria spanning four complementary dimensions: predictive discrimination, group fairness, uncertainty calibration, and robustness to input perturbations.

2.1 Benchmark Datasets

The benchmark suite comprises ten publicly available tabular healthcare classification datasets selected to represent WHO-prioritized global mortality and chronic morbidity conditions across diverse clinical domains, organ systems, and demographic risk profiles. The study is designed to comparatively evaluate TFMs and conventional ML classifiers under a constrained sample-size regime, with particular emphasis on predictive performance, fairness, calibration, and robustness across heterogeneous healthcare classification tasks.

Table 1 summarizes the clinical domains, class distributions, and sample sizes of the selected datasets. To establish a small-scale evaluation setting and limit dataset-size disparities, datasets exceeding 700 observations were subjected to random majority-class downsampling, restricting the maximum sample size to 700 observations. This constraint is motivated by the small-data setting in which TFMs are particularly relevant, allowing their comparative performance to be examined against traditional ML approaches without relying on large task-specific datasets [3, 36]. This choice of 700 samples is consistent with previous empirical studies suggesting that ML models often require several hundred to over a thousand observations to achieve stable predictive performance [37, 38]. Accordingly, this benchmark investigates comparative model behavior under constrained data availability rather than establishing clinical generalizability, population-level risk estimation, or deployment readiness. The original dataset sources, processed datasets, and analysis code are made publicly available through the study's GitHub repository to facilitate reproducibility [39]. Additionally, three supplementary datasets are made available through the GitHub repository, including a dataset with original sample size and natural class distribution, providing researchers with access to unaltered data for further investigation, alternative benchmarking configurations, and future comparative studies beyond the constraints of the present experimental setting [39]. We accessed the datasets for research purpose in October 2026, and had no access to information that could identify individual participants during or after data collection.

2.2 Data Preprocessing and Partitioning

All ten datasets undergo a uniform preparation pipeline prior to model evaluation in order to ensure cross-model comparability and eliminate preprocessing discrepancies.

1. Standardized Protected Attribute and Target Encoding: Across all datasets, the protected attribute (sex) is standardized as a binary variable with Female = 0 and Male = 1, and positioned as the first feature column. All classification tasks are formulated as binary prediction problems where the target variable (outcome), positioned as the final column, maps the negative class to 0 and the positive clinical class to 1. For datasets originally containing multi-class outcomes (e.g., Thyroid and Heart Disease UCI), all non-positive categories are consolidated into the negative class (0), while ensuring that intermediate risk categories are not improperly grouped into the negative class.

7/28

2. Categorical Feature Encoding: Non-numerical categorical attributes are mapped to integer representations via explicit manual label replacement (Python .replace()) rather than one-hot encoding. This design preserves the original tabular schema, prevents dimensionality inflation, and retains feature-level interpretability.

3. Class Balancing: Class distributions across all datasets are approximately balanced in some datasets due to random downsampling. For some datasets with sample size already lower than 700, their natural class ratio is preserved. All the balanced and original imbalanced dataset versions are archived in the study's GitHub repository for reproducibility and future research.

4. Cross-Validation and Leak-Free Imputation:

Each dataset is evaluated using 5-fold stratified cross-validation, preserving the approximate class distribution within every fold. Identical train/test fold assignments are enforced across all evaluated models. Missing values are retained in the prepared datasets and imputed independently within each cross-validation fold using k-nearest neighbors (KNN) imputation fit solely on the training fold, and subsequently applied to the corresponding test fold, thereby preventing data leakage.

2.3 Tabular Foundation Models

We benchmark four representative TFMs using their fixed, reproducible release versions: TabFM (v1.0.0) by Google, TabPFN (v2.0) by Prior Labs, TabDPT (v1.3) by Layer 6 AI, and TabICL (v2) by Inria.

For each fold, every model is provided the identical training partition as context (training data) and outputs both continuous positive-class probabilities and discrete binary predictions on the held-out test partition.

Binary predictions are generated using a fixed classification threshold of τ = 0.50 across all models as well as both clean and noisy test evaluations. Threshold tuning on the test set is strictly prohibited.

2.4 Baseline Models

We include two classical ML baselines: Logistic Regression (abbreviated as LogReg in several figures and tables) and XGBoost, to contextualize the performance of TFMs against established clinical modeling approaches. Both baselines are evaluated across the identical 5-fold stratified cross-validation as the foundation AI models. For Logistic Regression, input features are standardized prior to model fitting with the Stochastic Average Gradient Augmented (SAGA) optimizer. XGBoost is trained directly on the imputed features using the logistic loss objective.

Hyperparameter tuning is performed independently within each outer training fold via nested Bayesian optimization with a Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE). For each model and outer fold, the optimizer maximizes the mean AUROC across shuffled inner stratified cross-validation splits of the outer training fold. KNN imputation (and feature standardization for Logistic Regression) is fit independently on each inner training split to strictly prevent data leakage during training. 2.6 Composite Metrics and Statistical Analysis

A composite evaluation is constructed using eight metrics selected to capture complementary dimensions of model performance relevant to the objectives of this study: DI, DPD, AUROC, Sensitivity, Brier Score, ECE, AUROC Degradation, and F1 Degradation. This selection prioritizes predictive discrimination, positive-class detection, fairness in group-level outcomes, probabilistic accuracy and calibration, and robustness to input perturbations. Specifically, AUROC and Sensitivity characterize discriminative ability and positive-class detection, respectively, while DI and DPD capture disparities in selection rates across biological sex subgroups. The Brier Score and ECE jointly assess the quality of probabilistic predictions, reflecting both prediction error and calibration reliability. AUROC Degradation and F1 Degradation further quantify the extent to which discriminative performance deteriorates under feature perturbations, providing 10/28 complementary perspectives on model robustness. These metrics are therefore selected to align the composite evaluation with the central research objectives, rather than to exhaustively represent all 20 metrics. The remaining metrics are retained for individual analyses to provide more granular assessments of specific performance characteristics but are not incorporated into the composite score to avoid broadening its scope beyond the dimensions of primary interest. The composite scores were calculated using the mean values of the selected evaluation metrics across the five-fold cross-validation procedure.

2.6.2 Non-parametric Statistical Analysis

3.8 Discussion

593

The results indicate that the tabular foundation AI models offered a consistent advantage in discrimination 594 and probabilistic reliability over the conventional baselines, but only a limited advantage in fairness and a 595 disadvantage in robustness. Google TabFM was the strongest model overall, achieving the highest mean 596 AUROC, sensitivity, and NPV and the lowest ECE, NLL, and Brier score (Table 5), together with the best 597 mean CMES and rank (Table 7). The advantage was most apparent in Liver and Arrhythmia, where the 598 AUROC of Logistic Regression was markedly lower (Figure 3 (A)), and in the sensitivity of the models, 599 where Logistic Regression missed a substantially larger proportion of positive cases (Figure 3 (B)).

23/28

The robustness findings qualify this picture. The models with the best discrimination and calibration were also the most affected by feature noise, and the degradation was concentrated in the Thyroid and Liver datasets (Figure 6 (C)). The near-perfect robustness of Logistic Regression should be interpreted accordingly: its low flip rate and probability change (Figure 6 (B)) partly reflect a lower-capacity model that already performs worse on clean data (Figure 6 (A)), and the robustness composite rewards stability irrespective of the baseline performance level. After noise, the advantage of the foundation models in AUROC over Logistic Regression shrank to approximately 0.01-0.02, and XGBoost fell below Logistic Regression. TabDPT combined a competitive discrimination with the smallest degradation, flip rate, and MAPC among the foundation models, which suggests that stability is not an inherent trade-off of the foundation-model class.

The fairness results highlight that DI was largely dataset-driven. Five datasets (Heart, Arrhythmia, COPD, Autism, and Hepatitis) fell below the 0.80 threshold for virtually all models (Figure 5 (A)), and no model was best across all fairness metrics (Figure 5 (B)). The relationship between discrimination and DI was weak (Figure 5 (C)), and the fairness composite scores were nearly identical across models (Figure 2 (A)). These findings suggest that adopting a foundation model neither improves nor worsens algorithmic disparities in a systematic way, and that fairness auditing and, where necessary, mitigation remain necessary regardless of the model class.

The CMES condensed these competing profiles into a single score, but the resulting differences were modest (mean CMES between 0.855 and 0.881) and were partly compensatory, as the weakness of the foundation models in robustness was offset by their strengths in calibration and predictive performance. The statistical analysis supports a moderately strong conclusion: the omnibus test was significant (Table 6), and TabFM, TabICL, and TabDPT were each significantly better than Logistic Regression, with TabDPT also significantly better than XGBoost (Table 8). However, the foundation models were statistically indistinguishable from each other, and TabPFN could not be separated from either the best foundation models or the baselines (Figure 7). The ranking within the top group should therefore not be over-interpreted, particularly because the best model changed from one dataset to another (Figure 1 (C) and Figure 7 (C)).

3.9 Limitations and Future Work

This study has several limitations. First, majority-class downsampling was applied to datasets exceeding 700 observations to establish a constrained small-data benchmarking setting. However, this altered the natural class distributions and disease prevalence, potentially affecting predictive values, calibration, fairness, and model behavior under real-world conditions. Both the balanced datasets used in this study and their original imbalanced versions are available in our GitHub repository. Future research should extend this evaluation to the full datasets with their natural class distributions to assess model performance under realistic prevalence and varying degrees of class imbalance.

Second, the use of ten public datasets and internal five-fold cross-validation may limit the generalizability of our findings to other populations and clinical settings. External, temporal, and prospective validation using larger and more diverse clinical cohorts is therefore warranted to establish the reliability of these models beyond the evaluated datasets.

Third, fairness evaluation was restricted to binary sex-based comparisons, while robustness assessment relied on Gaussian feature perturbations. These analyses do not capture broader demographic disparities, intersectional inequities, or other clinically relevant sources of data variability. Future studies should incorporate intersectional fairness analyses and more realistic perturbation and distribution-shift scenarios, alongside investigations of potential fairness and robustness mitigation strategies.

Finally, the CMES depends on the selected metrics and their weighting, potentially obscuring dimension-specific trade-offs. Future work should examine alternative weighting schemes and incorporate clinical utility and decision-analytic outcomes to determine whether benchmark performance translates into meaningful clinical benefit.

This study presents a multidimensional benchmark of four tabular foundation AI models, TabFM, TabPFN, TabDPT, and TabICL, against Logistic Regression and XGBoost across ten WHO-aligned healthcare classification datasets. By evaluating predictive discrimination, sex-based fairness, probabilistic calibration, and robustness under controlled Gaussian feature perturbations within a standardized cross-validation framework, this study provides a comparative assessment of the trustworthiness-related properties of contemporary tabular models in healthcare. Overall, the foundation models demonstrated favorable predictive and probabilistic performance relative to the conventional baselines. TabFM achieved the highest mean CMES, followed closely by TabDPT, TabICL, and TabPFN. It also attained the highest mean AUROC and sensitivity, together with the lowest ECE, NLL, and Brier score, suggesting comparatively strong discrimination and probability reliability across the evaluated datasets. Nevertheless, the statistically significant omnibus comparison did not translate into significant pairwise differences among the four foundation models after multiple-comparison correction, indicating that their overall performance differences were relatively small and dataset-dependent. In conclusion, TFMs offer promising predictive and calibration advantages for healthcare classification under constrained data conditions, but these advantages are not universal across trustworthiness dimensions. Their responsible use requires context-specific evaluation, transparent reporting, and validation beyond benchmark performance. The proposed framework provides a reproducible foundation for such evaluations and supports more evidence-informed selection of tabular models for future digital health research.
