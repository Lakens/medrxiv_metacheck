What we found

We found 8 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: The inhibition of NOD-, LRR-and pyrin domain-containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome is an emerging therapeutic strategy for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD). Although early clinical trials demonstrate reductions in inflammatory biomarkers, whether these translate into cardiovascular benefit remains uncertain. We investigated the potential efficacy and safety of NLRP3 inhibition using human genetics.

Methods: We developed a cis-genetic instrument for NLRP3 downregulation using Creactive protein (CRP) associated variants (P<5×10⁻⁸) within the NLRP3 locus (±100 kb of NLRP3 transcription start site) from 575,531 participants. Mendelian Randomization and colocalization analyses assessed the effect of the genetic instrument against gene expression of NLRP3 and other inflammatory mediators in immune cells, downstream inflammatory biomarkers, and pharmacodynamic responses to the NLRP3 inhibitor ruvonoflast. Two-sample Mendelian randomization assessed cardiovascular, cardiometabolic, and infectious outcomes, followed by phenome-wide analyses of 1,387 clinical outcomes.

Results: Using 14 variants in the NLRP3 locus, we constructed a genetic instrument that corresponded to a 19% difference in circulating CRP between the extreme percentiles of its distribution. Colocalisation evidence suggested the effects on CRP to be driven by reduced NLRP3 expression in naïve monocytes (PP.H4=98.7%). In accordance with the expected biological role of NLRP3, genetically proxied NLRP3 downregulation by the constructed instrument was associated with lower IL-18 (β=-0.11; 95%CI, -0.14 to -0.08) and IL-6 (β=-0.15; 95%CI, -0.17 to -0.12) and broadly similar pharmacodynamic effects to the NLRP3 inhibitor ruvonoflast across 8 biomarkers. Genetically proxied NLRP3 inhibition was associated with lower risk of recurrent pericarditis (OR per 19% decrease in CRP, 0.68, 95%CI, 0.58-0.80), but slightly elevated risk of coronary artery disease (OR, 1.06; 95%CI, 1.03-1.08) and peripheral arterial disease (OR, 1.10; 95%CI, 1.04-1.15) and no effect on ischaemic stroke (OR, 0.98; 95%CI, 0.94-1.02). The instrument was also associated with higher carotid-intima-media thickness and a worse cardiometabolic profile with increasing effects on blood pressure, waist-to-hip ratio, LDL cholesterol, and triglycerides. We found no associations with increased risk for prespecified infectious disease outcomes.

Conclusions: A genetic proxy for NLRP3 inflammasome downregulation reproduced key molecular effects of pharmacological NLRP3 inhibition but was not associated with reduced ASCVD risk.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of death worldwide 1 . The projected increase in its burden over the coming decades highlights the need for novel preventive strategies 2, 3 . Targeting vascular inflammation has emerged as one therapeutic avenue for CVD prevention 4 . Among inflammatory pathways, the interleukin (IL)-1β-IL-6 axis received particular attention, supported by converging experimental, genetic, and clinical evidence. Proof of concept for this pathway was established in the CANTOS trial, in which inhibition of IL-1β with canakinumab reduced major cardiovascular events [5] [6] [7] . Canakinumab was not developed further for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), but attention shifted toward upstream and downstream molecules. Downstream of IL-1β, three monoclonal antibodies targeting IL-6 [8] [9] [10] successfully completed phase 2 trials. However, ziltivekimab, one of these agents, failed to demonstrate cardiovascular benefit in the phase 3 ZEUS trial in patients with ASCVD and chronic kidney disease 11.

Upstream of both IL-1β and IL-6 lies the NOD-, LRR-and pyrin domain-containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, a central intracellular sensor of cellular stress that is activated by diverse danger signals, including cholesterol crystals within atherosclerotic plaques 12 . NLRP3 activation triggers the maturation and release of several pro-inflammatory cytokines, most notably IL-1β and IL-18. By acting upstream of the IL-1β-IL-6 axis, NLRP3 inhibition offers the potential to suppress multiple downstream inflammatory pathways simultaneously. Although clinical development is less advanced than for IL-6 inhibitors, several NLRP3 inhibitors are progressing rapidly. Recently, the oral small-molecule inhibitor ruvonoflast reduced high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP), IL-6, and fibrinogen concentrations in patients with high hsCRP levels at elevated cardiovascular risk 13 . Similar reductions in hsCRP have also been reported for additional oral NLRP3 inhibitors (BGE-102, parunoflast and MRT-8102) in early-stage trials of patients with an unfavorable cardiometabolic profile [14] [15] [16] . Whether these biomarker improvements translate into reductions in cardiovascular events, however, remains unknown.

Human genetic studies played a central role in prioritising IL-6 signaling as a therapeutic target. Naturally occurring variants in the genes encoding IL-6 17 or its receptor [18] [19] [20] [21] [22] [23] [24] [25] [26] that attenuate downstream signaling are consistently associated with lower lifetime risk of CVD, providing genetic support for pharmacological IL-6 inhibition. By contrast, there have been only limited efforts to identify genetic proxies of NLRP3 inhibition that could be used to evaluate this emerging therapeutic strategy. One previous study reported that a common intronic NLRP3 variant increased NLRP3 expression and cardiovascular mortality 27 , but these findings were not replicated in subsequent genome-wide association studies (GWAS) of coronary artery disease 28 or other cardiovascular phenotypes 29, 30.

Importantly, inhibition of NLRP3 may not simply recapitulate the effects of inhibiting downstream cytokines such as IL-1β or IL-6. Experimental studies suggest that Nlrp3 haploinsufficiency in mice induces compensatory overexpression of Nlrp1, potentially accelerating inflammation and aging over time 31 . In addition, targeting different levels of the inflammatory cascade may have distinct safety profiles. For example, IL-1β inhibition with canakinumab was associated with an increased risk of fatal infections in CANTOS [5] [6] [7] , highlighting the need to better understand the efficacy and safety implications of targeting NLRP3 itself.

To address these gaps, we developed a genetic instrument proxying NLRP3 inflammasome downregulation by leveraging variants in the locus of the gene encoding NLRP3 and used it to predict the effects of pharmacological NLRP3 inhibition on cardiometabolic and safety outcomes. We first validated the instrument against the expected biological effects of NLRP3 inhibition and biomarker changes observed in the recently completed phase 1b trial of the oral NLRP3 inhibitor ruvonoflast. We then evaluated its associations with cardiovascular and metabolic diseases, as well as infectious disease outcomes.

Methods

Study Design

We performed a drug-target Mendelian randomization (MR) study 32 to evaluate the effects of genetically proxied NLRP3 inflammasome perturbation on downstream inflammatory biomarkers, cardiometabolic outcomes, and infectious diseases. Genetic instruments comprised C-reactive protein (CRP)-associated cis-acting variants within the NLRP3 locus. Circulating CRP was selected as the biomarker for instrument construction because it represents a distal, clinically validated marker of NLRP3-IL-1β-IL-6 pathway activity and is an established pharmacodynamic readout in clinical trials of NLRP3 inhibition 13 . The study design is summarized in Figure 1.

Data Sources

Genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary statistics for circulating CRP were obtained from Said et al 33 , comprising 575,531 predominantly European-ancestry participants. Summary statistics for inflammatory biomarkers, cardiovascular and cardiometabolic outcomes, infectious diseases, quantitative cardiovascular traits, and circulating proteins were obtained from publicly available GWASs conducted predominantly in individuals of European ancestry. Full details of the contributing GWASs, including sample size, ancestry and accession identifiers, are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Construction of the NLRP3 Genetic Instrument

Genetic proxies for NLRP3 perturbation were identified as CRP-associated variants within the NLRP3 cis-region (GRCh37; chromosome 1:247,579,458-247,612,410). Variants within ±100 kb of NLRP3 were selected at genome-wide significance (P<5×10⁻⁸) and clumped for linkage disequilibrium (LD) at r²<0.1 using the European subset of the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 reference panel 34 to construct the primary instrument. Robustness to the choice of cis-window was assessed using a ±10-kb window. A more stringent LD-clumping threshold (r² < 0.01) was additionally applied to assess the robustness of estimates to residual linkage disequilibrium among genetic instruments. The proportion of variance in circulating CRP explained by each selected genetic variant (R²) was calculated 35, 36 , and the instrument strength was evaluated using F statistics [35] [36] [37].

Genetic Score Construction and Effect Scaling

To express the effect of genetically proxied NLRP3 inhibition on a clinically interpretable scale, MR estimates were rescaled to the difference in circulating CRP observed between the extremes of the distribution of a weighted genetic score comprising the selected genetic variants. This was performed in the UK Biobank (N = 464,264) and methodology was described elsewhere. 17, 38 . Scaling was applied only to the final MR estimates. Scaled estimates were subsequently re-oriented to represent the effect of genetically proxied NLRP3 downregulation.

Functional Validation

Instrument variants were additionally annotated using the Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) 39 against the NLRP3 MANE Select transcript to characterise their predicted functional consequences. The functional validity of the NLRP3 instrument was evaluated using complementary expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) resources, including the eQTL Catalogue 40 and eQTLGen 41 . Nominally significant (two-sided P<0.05) eQTL associations were used to descriptively characterise the tissue-and cell-specific regulatory landscape of the NLRP3 locus. Given the predominant role of the NLRP3 inflammasome in myeloid cells, particularly monocytes and macrophages 42, 43 , colocalization analyses were then performed to assess whether NLRP3 eQTL signals in relevant myeloid lineages, including naïve monocytes 44 , and circulating CRP shared a causal variant.

Pharmacodynamic Validation

To evaluate whether genetically proxied NLRP3 perturbation reproduced the expected downstream inflammatory effects of NLRP3 inhibition, we examined associations of the primary instrument with circulating IL-6 (N=76,592) and IL-18 (N=76,646), using the largest independent proteomic platform per biomarker as the primary analysis, across multiple independent proteomic platforms [45] [46] [47] [48] [49] [50] . IL-18 is directly matured following NLRP3-mediated caspase-1 activation, whereas IL-6 represents a downstream mediator of IL-1β signaling, thereby capturing complementary components of the NLRP3 inflammatory pathway 43.

Although IL-1β is itself a canonical downstream product of NLRP3 inflammasome activation, its low circulating abundance due to short half-life 51 presents challenges for reliable quantification in large-scale proteomic datasets. As such, it was not available in the primary proteomic dataset 45 . To further assess concordance with pharmacological NLRP3 inhibition, MR-derived biomarker effects were compared with pharmacodynamic responses reported in a randomized phase 1b trial of the NLRP3 inhibitor ruvonoflast 13.

Comparisons included IL-6, IL-18, fibrinogen, lipoprotein(a), total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, and triglycerides [45] [46] [47] [48] [49] 52 (Supplementary Table S1). Genetic estimates, expressed in outcome-specific standard deviation (SD) units, were selected and compared with the corresponding pharmacological effects of ruvonoflast. The effects of ruvonoflast were calculated as placebo-adjusted percentage changes in mean biomarker levels from baseline to day 28. Concordance between genetic and pharmacological effects were assessed using Spearman rank correlation.

Positive and Negative Control Analyses

Recurrent pericarditis (4,894 cases/1,457,822 controls) 53 was selected as a positivecontrol outcome given the established efficacy of IL-1 inhibition in this disease 54, 55 and the role of NLRP3 upstream of IL-1β activation 56 . Left-handedness (194,198 cases/1,534,836 controls) 57 was selected as a negative-control outcome.

Cardiometabolic Analyses

Cardiovascular and cardiometabolic outcomes were prespecified to evaluate the potential clinical effects of genetically proxied NLRP3 inhibition. Primary outcomes comprised coronary artery disease (CAD; 181,522 cases/984,168 controls) 28 , ischaemic stroke (62,100 cases/1,234,808 controls) 30 , heart failure (139,533 cases/1,568,809 controls) 29 , peripheral arterial disease (PAD; 31,307 cases/211,753 controls) 58 , atrial fibrillation (228,926 cases/1,611,415 controls) 59 , carotid plaque (21,540 cases/26,894 controls) 60 , and type 2 diabetes mellitus (428,452 cases/2,107,149 controls) 61 . Secondary exploratory outcomes were selected to characterise effects on intermediate cardiovascular and cardiometabolic phenotypes and comprised measures of subclinical atherosclerosis (coronary artery calcification 62 , carotid intima-media thickness 60 ), haemodynamic traits (systolic and diastolic blood pressure and pulse pressure 63 ); adiposity (body mass index and waist-to-hip ratio adjusted for BMI 64 ); glycaemic traits (HbA1c, fasting glucose, and fasting insulin 65 ); lipid traits (HDL cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, non-HDL cholesterol, total cholesterol, and triglycerides 52 ); and renal traits (blood urea nitrogen [BUN], estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR], cystatin C-based eGFR 66 and urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio 67 ) (Supplementary Table S1).

Infectious Disease Safety Analyses

We evaluated 13 prespecified infectious disease outcomes. Hospital-treated infections comprised any infection (123,508 cases/577,141 controls), pneumonia (55,241/645,408), skin and soft tissue infection (29,247/671,402), urinary tract infection (21,958/464,526) and Candida infection (7,286/690,977). Sepsis outcomes comprised sepsis requiring hospital admission (18,931/663,531), sepsis in individuals aged <75 years (11,568/462,869), critical sepsis (1,380/429,985) and 28-day mortality following critical sepsis (347/431,081). Viral infection outcomes comprised COVID-19 (122,616/2,475,240), COVID-19 requiring hospitalisation (32,519/2,062,805), and very severe respiratory COVID-19 (13,769/1,072,442), each compared with the population, and influenza (5,133/429,900). Data were drawn from UK Biobank and FinnGen 68, 69 , the COVID-19 Host Genetics Initiative 70 , and the Million Veteran Program 71 (Supplementary Table S1). Where the same phenotype was available from multiple sources, the GWAS with the largest sample size was selected for the primary analysis.

Statistical analysis

Two-sample MR analyses were performed using harmonised exposure and outcome summary statistics. Fixed-effects inverse-variance weighted (IVW) MR was prespecified as the primary estimator 72 . Robustness was assessed using multiplicative random-effects IVW, weighted median 73 , MR-Egger regression 74 , MR-PRESSO 75 , and leave-one-out analyses. The Cochran's Q test was used to assess between-variant heterogeneity 76 . Following MR estimation, effect estimates were re-oriented to represent genetically proxied NLRP3 downregulation and rescaled to the corresponding genetically predicted reduction in circulating CRP, as described above.

Regional colocalisation analyses were performed using the approximate Bayes factor implementation of coloc, with prior probabilities of association with trait 1 (p₁=1×10⁻⁴), trait 2 (p₂=1×10⁻⁴), and both traits through a shared causal variant (p₁₂=1×10⁻⁵) 77 . Analyses were conducted within a ±100-kb window around the NLRP3 locus. Colocalisation was assessed using the posterior probability of a shared causal variant (PP.H4) and the conditional posterior probability of a shared causal variant given association with both traits (PP.H4/[PP.H3 + PP.H4]). For each measure, a posterior probability >0.80 was considered strong evidence of colocalisation, whereas >0.50 was considered suggestive evidence. Posterior probabilities for all hypotheses (PP.H0-PP.H4) are reported in the Supplement.

Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied separately within the prespecified biological validation outcomes, binary cardiometabolic outcomes, continuous cardiometabolic traits, infectious disease safety outcomes, and phenomewide outcomes 78 . MR sensitivity analyses were considered assessments of robustness rather than independent hypothesis tests and were therefore interpreted according to the consistency of effect estimates across methods rather than subjected to additional multiple-testing correction. Concordance between MR-derived biomarker effects and pharmacodynamic responses to ruvonoflast was treated as a validation analysis and was not included within the multiple-testing correction framework. The positive-and negativecontrol analyses represented separate prespecified hypotheses, were not included in any FDR family, and were evaluated at a nominal (p<0.05) significance threshold.

PheWAS Follow-up

A phenome-wide follow-up analysis was undertaken to systematically evaluate the clinical consequences of genetically proxied NLRP3 downregulation across binary outcomes from FinnGen Release 12 (500,348 individuals of Finnish ancestry) 79 and its public metaanalyses with UK Biobank 80 and with the Million Veteran Program and UK Biobank 71 . Outcomes with fewer than 1,000 cases were excluded. Where an outcome was available from more than one of these sources, the source with the largest sample size was used, giving 1,387 binary outcomes (624 from FinnGen, 440 from the FinnGen-UK Biobank metaanalysis, and 326 from the FinnGen-Million Veteran Program-UK Biobank meta-analysis). Associations were estimated using IVW MR, using a fixed-effects model where there was no evidence of heterogeneity between variant-specific estimates (Cochran's Q P≥0.05) and a multiplicative random-effects model where there was (Cochran's Q P<0.05), or the Wald ratio where a single variant was available. Multiple testing was controlled using the false discovery rate (FDR) across all 1,387 outcomes (FDR-adjusted P<0.05). FDR-significant associations were further evaluated using weighted median and MR-Egger sensitivity analyses. Associations were considered robust when they demonstrated: (1) concordant direction of effect and P<0.05 using the weighted median estimator; (2) no evidence of directional horizontal pleiotropy by the MR-Egger intercept (P>0.05); and (3) where heterogeneity was present (Cochran Q P<0.05), concordant effect directions across IVW, weighted median, and MR-Egger estimates, with the MR-Egger estimate differing by no more than 20% from the IVW estimate.

Results

Instrument construction and biological validation

The primary instrument for genetically proxied NLRP3 downregulation comprised 14 CRPlowering variants within the NLRP3 locus (chromosome 1q44; Supplementary Table S2).

Restricting the cis-window from ±100 kb to ±10 kb yielded an 11-variant instrument, which was used for sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Table S2). A stricter r² threshold of < 0.01 yielded 5 variants in both ±100kb and ±10kb with 2 variants overlapping (Supplementary Table S2). Across all cis-window and LD-clumping specifications, individual variant F statistics ranged from 34 to 408 (Supplementary Table S2), indicating a low likelihood of weak-instrument bias. The 14-and 11-variant instruments explained 0.23% and 0.20% of the variance in serum CRP, respectively (Supplementary Table S2). In the UK Biobank, median circulating CRP was 19% (95% CI, 14%-23%) lower among individuals in the lowest versus highest percentile of the weighted 14-variant genetic score (Figure 2A; Supplementary Table S3). A concordant 19% reduction (95% CI, 14%-23%) was observed between the corresponding extremes of the restricted 11-variant score distribution (Supplementary Table S3).

None of the 14 instrument variants were located within the coding sequence of NLRP3 (Supplementary Table S4). However, the variants demonstrated cis-regulatory associations with NLRP3 expression across whole blood, immune-cell populations, and nonimmune tissues (Supplementary Figure 1; Supplementary Table S5). Ten of the 14 variants were genome-wide significant cis-eQTLs for NLRP3 expression in whole blood. In the eQTL Catalogue, genome-wide significant cell-specific associations were predominantly observed in the myeloid lineage (Supplementary Table S5). Consistent with the predominant activity of the NLRP3 inflammasome in monocytes and macrophages 42, 43 , colocalisation in naïve monocytes demonstrated strong evidence that NLRP3 expression and circulating CRP shared a causal variant (PP.H4=98.7%; Figure 2B, Supplementary Table S6).

Downstream inflammatory pathway validation

In line with the known biological effects of NLRP3 inflammasome 43 , genetically proxied NLRP3 downregulation was associated with lower circulating IL-18 (β=-0.11; 95% CI, -0.14 to -0.08; P<0.0001) and IL-6 concentrations (β=-0.15; 95%CI, -0.17 to -0.12; P<0.0001) (Figure 3A; Supplementary Table S7). Both associations remained significant after correction for multiple testing and were directionally consistent across independent proteomic platforms (Supplementary Table S7), weighted-median analyses, and analyses using the restricted 11-variant instrument (Supplementary Tables S7 and S8), with no evidence of directional horizontal pleiotropy (Supplementary Table S7). Estimates were robust to leave-one-out analysis (Supplementary Table S9). Colocalisation provided strong evidence that the NLRP3-region association shared a causal variant with both IL-18 (PP.H4=99.1%) and IL-6 (PP.H4=99.6%; Figure 3B; Supplementary Table S6), further supporting that the instrument recapitulates the known role of NLRP3 in regulating downstream cytokine signaling, including IL-18 signaling and IL-1β-induced IL-6 production.

Furthermore, the biomarker effects of genetically proxied NLRP3 downregulation were broadly consistent in direction with the pharmacodynamic response to ruvonoflast, a small molecule NLRP3 inhibitor, as tested in a randomized, double-blind phase 1b trial 13 , with similar findings across the 14-and 11-variant instruments (Figure 4A and 4B; Supplementary Tables S8 and S10). Except for IL-18 that was reduced by our instrument but paradoxically not by ruvonoflast, there were broadly consistent effects across 7 biomarkers, including IL-6, lipoprotein(a), fibrinogen, triglycerides, total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, and HDL cholesterol (Figure 4C). Although, rank correlation was nonsignificant (ρ = 0.64; P = 0.094). Together, these results supported the validity of the genetic instrument as a proxy of NLRP3 inhibition.

Cardiovascular and cardiometabolic outcomes

Next, we evaluated associations of genetically proxied NLRP3 downregulation with clinical cardiovascular and cardiometabolic outcomes relevant to therapeutic development efforts for NLRP3 inhibitors. Consistent with the established efficacy of pharmacological IL-1β inhibition in pericarditis 55, 87, 88 , genetically proxied NLRP3 downregulation was associated with a lower risk of recurrent pericarditis (OR per 19% reduction in CRP, 0.68; 95% CI, 0.58-0.80; Figure 5A; Supplementary Table S11). However, among the primary clinical outcomes, there was no evidence of reduced risk of coronary artery disease (OR, 1.06; 95% CI, 1.03-1.08), peripheral arterial disease (OR, 1.10; 95% CI, 1.04-1.15), or type 2 diabetes (OR, 1.03; 95% CI, 1.01-1.04) with effects pointing to the opposite direction (Figure 5A; Supplementary Table S11). There was also no association with heart failure (OR, 1.00; 95% CI, 0.97-1.02) or ischaemic stroke (OR, 0.98; 95% CI, 0.94-1.02). Effect estimates were directionally consistent in weighted-median analyses, and leave-one-out analyses (Supplementary Tables S11 and S12), whereas MR-Egger estimates were attenuated and less precise, with no evidence of directional horizontal pleiotropy (MR-Egger intercept P>0.05 for each outcome; Supplementary Tables S11). For outcomes that were significant in the primary analysis, findings were similar using the restricted 11variant instrument (Supplementary Table S8). Colocalisation provided little evidence that the NLRP3-region signal shared a causal variant with recurrent pericarditis (PP.H4=2%) or the primary cardiometabolic outcomes (Supplementary Table S6). There was no association with the negative-control outcome of left-handedness (P=0.54; Supplementary Table S11).

Among secondary exploratory outcomes, genetically proxied NLRP3 downregulation was associated with higher carotid intima-media thickness (β=0.01; 95% CI, 0.004-0.013; Figure 5B; Supplementary Table S11), higher waist-to-hip ratio adjusted for BMI (β=0.13; 95% CI, 0.017-0.038), and higher levels of systolic and diastolic blood pressure, pulse pressure, HDL, LDL, total and non-HDL cholesterol, and triglycerides (Supplementary Table S11). Colocalisation provided evidence of a shared causal variant between the NLRP3-region signal for CRP and total cholesterol (PP.H4=92.4%). Evidence for colocalization with systolic blood pressure did not meet the threshold (PP.H4=49.6%), while no evidence of colocalisation was observed for diastolic blood pressure, carotid intima-media thickness, or HDL cholesterol (Supplementary Table S6).

Infectious disease outcomes

Given the increased incidence of serious infections observed with IL-1β inhibition in CANTOS [5] [6] [7] , we evaluated associations of the constructed instrument with infectious disease outcomes. Genetically proxied NLRP3 downregulation was associated with lower odds of pneumonia requiring hospital admission (OR, 0.93; 95% CI, 0.90-0.96; P=8.5×10⁻⁵), the only prespecified infectious outcome that remained significant after FDR correction (Supplementary Figure 2; Supplementary Table S13). There was no evidence of heterogeneity (Cochran Q P=0.88) or directional horizontal pleiotropy (MR-Egger intercept P=0.75; Supplementary Table S13), and the direction of association was consistent in weighted-median (OR, 0.95; P=0.028), MR-PRESSO analyses (OR, 0.93; P<0.001), leave-one-out analyses and the 11-variant instrument (Supplementary Tables S8, S13 and S14). However, colocalisation provided little evidence that the NLRP3-region and pneumonia association signals shared a causal variant (PP.H4=0.6%; Supplementary Table S6). We observed no associations with higher risk of any other of the tested infectious disease outcomes.

Phenome-Wide Analysis

In a phenome-wide associations study, we explored associations between genetically proxied NLRP3 inhibition and 1,387 binary clinical outcomes with more than 1,000 cases. After correcting for multiple comparisons (false discovery rate (FDR)-corrected P < 0.05) and excluding outcomes that lacked consistency across sensitivity MR methods, we found 94 outcomes to be significantly associated with genetically proxied NLRP3 downregulation (Supplementary Table S15; Figure 6). Genetically proxied NLRP3 downregulation was associated with higher odds of the following clinical outcomes: (1) aortic aneurysm (abdominal aortic aneurysm and aortic aneurysm, broader definition), (2) peripheral vascular disease, (3) dyslipidemia (for example, statin use, hypercholesterolemia and disorders of lipoprotein metabolism), (4) diabetes mellitus, (5) non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, (6) hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, (7) dementia, (8) glaucoma and ( 9) musculoskeletal disease (for example, rotator cuff syndrome, Dupuytren's contracture and lateral epicondylitis). However, genetically proxied NLRP3 inhibition was associated with lower odds of: (1) tonsillar disease and upper and lower respiratory tract infections (for example, chronic diseases of tonsils and adenoids, tonsillectomy, acute upper respiratory infections and pneumonia), (2) appendicitis, (3) macular degeneration, (4) heart failure, (5) non-small cell lung cancer, (6) intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy, (7) reactive arthropathies, (8) erythema nodosum and lichen simplex, and ( 9) asthma (Figure 6 and Supplementary Table S15). These signals highlight potential repurposing opportunities or safety concerns that might warrant further investigation.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a cis-genetic instrument for NLRP3 downregulation that reproduced the expected molecular consequences of NLRP3 inhibition but did not support a protective effect on ASCVD. Instrument validity was supported by effects on NLRP3 expression, strong colocalisation between NLRP3 expression and circulating CRP, reductions in downstream IL-18 and IL-6, and concordance with the pharmacodynamic profile of the NLRP3 inhibitor ruvonoflast. Genetically proxied NLRP3 downregulation was also associated with a lower risk of recurrent pericarditis, consistent with the established therapeutic effect of IL-1β pathway inhibition. Despite this biological validation, NLRP3 downregulation was not associated with protection against any major ASCVD phenotypes, with modestly higher odds of CAD and PAD alongside several adverse cardiometabolic traits. We found no associations with increased risk of any infectious disease endpoints. Finally, phenome-wide analyses largely reinforced the absence of a protective cardiovascular signal and identified additional associations of potential relevance to safety and therapeutic repurposing.

These findings have implications for the evolving therapeutic landscape of cardiovascular inflammation. CANTOS established proof of concept that targeting inflammation through IL-1β inhibition can reduce cardiovascular events [5] [6] [7] , while subsequent genetic and pharmacological evidence has supported the downstream IL-6 pathway as a therapeutic target [8] [9] [10] 17 . However, the absence of cardiovascular efficacy in ZEUS and discontinuation of the HERMES and ATHENA programmes have introduced uncertainty regarding the extent to which suppression of this pathway translates into clinical cardiovascular benefit 11, 89, 90 . NLRP3 represents a more proximal target, regulating inflammasome-dependent maturation of IL-1β and IL-18, and several small-molecule inhibitors are in clinical development 91 . Ruvonoflast reduced hsCRP and downstream inflammatory biomarkers, but whether these pharmacodynamic effects translate into fewer cardiovascular events remains unknown 13 . Our observation that genetically proxied NLRP3 downregulation reproduced key features of this pharmacodynamic profile without reducing ASCVD risk suggests that biological target engagement and suppression of circulating inflammatory biomarkers alone may be insufficient to predict cardiovascular efficacy.

The divergence between the molecular and cardiovascular effects of NLRP3 downregulation may partly reflect its position within the inflammatory cascade. Unlike IL-6, which directly stimulates hepatic CRP production, NLRP3 lies further upstream, with its effects propagated through inflammasome-dependent maturation of IL-1β and subsequent IL-6 signalling 92 . Consistent with this greater biological distance, the NLRP3 genetic score produced an approximately 19% difference in circulating CRP, whereas the IL6 and IL6R instruments developed by Zhang et al. produced 24% and 30% differences, respectively, and were associated with lower risks of CAD, PAD, and large artery atherosclerotic stroke 17 . These estimates are not directly comparable across instruments, but they illustrate that similar reductions in a distal inflammatory biomarker need not imply equivalent perturbation of the underlying pathway or equivalent cardiovascular effects.

Genetically proxied NLRP3 inhibition was associated with small increases in circulating lipid markers, blood pressure and waist-hip ratio measurements, as well as slightly elevated risk of type 2 diabetes. This raises the possibility that an adverse cardiometabolic profile may offset benefits from immunomodulation. However, these associations were not reproduced during 28 days of pharmacological NLRP3 inhibition with ruvonoflast. Despite hsCRP suppression, reductions in body weight, total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol and triglycerides were comparable between the intervention and placebo groups.

Similarly, a Phase 1b study of dapansutrile, oral NLRP3 inhibitor, in patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (n = 24) identified small reductions in blood pressure measurements after approximately 12 days 93 . Both studies are limited by their short treatment durations, small samples and in the ruvonoflast trial, concurrent calorie restriction. The divergence between genetic and pharmacological findings may reflect metabolic consequences of lifelong NLRP3 downregulation that are not reproduced by inhibition initiated in adulthood. Such effects could potentially contribute to the absence of genetically predicted ASCVD protection without necessarily excluding cardiovascular benefit from pharmacological NLRP3 inhibition. Moreover, our instrument was associated with increased risk of type 2 diabetes, despite being associated with minimal, albeit significant, decreases in HbA1c levels in a population without diabetes. This discrepancy may reflect genetic effects on type 2 diabetes risk through pathways not adequately captured by fasting glycaemic parameters or may be attributable to horizontal pleiotropy.

Lifelong genetic perturbation may also differ importantly from pharmacological NLRP3 inhibition. Chronic downregulation could induce compensatory responses within the innate immune network that are not reproduced by shorter-term therapeutic inhibition. Experimental evidence in mice that Nlrp3 haploinsufficiency is accompanied by upregulation of Nlrp1 provides one potential example of such pathway compensation 31 . Although these mechanisms cannot be established from the present genetic analyses, they provide a plausible explanation for why lifelong genetically proxied NLRP3 downregulation could reproduce expected downstream biomarker effects without conferring the anticipated cardiovascular benefit. Conversely, this distinction means that our findings should not be interpreted as excluding cardiovascular benefit from pharmacological NLRP3 inhibition. The recent Lp(a)HORIZON trial provides a parallel example of potential discordance between genetic and pharmacological evidence, where substantial pharmacological Lp(a) lowering failed to reduce cardiovascular events despite robust genetic evidence supporting a causal role for Lp(a) in ASCVD 94 , highlighting the limitations of extrapolating lifelong genetic effects to therapeutic interventions initiated later in life.

We found no evidence that genetically proxied NLRP3 downregulation increased susceptibility to prespecified infectious outcomes. This contrasts with the increased risk of serious or fatal infection observed with downstream IL-1β inhibition in CANTOS [5] [6] [7] and raises the possibility that targeting NLRP3 may have distinct effects on host defence compared with broader suppression of IL-1β signalling. Notably, genetic NLRP3 downregulation was associated with lower odds of pneumonia requiring hospital admission, with directionally consistent estimates across sensitivity analyses. However, the limited colocalisation support suggests that this association may not be attributable to the same causal variant underlying genetically proxied NLRP3 downregulation and therefore warrants cautious interpretation. More broadly, genetic analyses of acute infection are inherently influenced by pathogen exposure, healthcare-seeking behaviour, and case ascertainment and may not capture the consequences of short-term pharmacological inhibition during an infectious challenge. These findings therefore provide some reassurance against a large increase in infection susceptibility but should not be interpreted as establishing the infectious safety of pharmacological NLRP3 inhibition, which will require prospective evaluation in adequately powered clinical trials.

Finally, phenome-wide analyses identified potential safety signals and therapeutic opportunities beyond the prespecified outcomes. Associations with aortic aneurysm, peripheral vascular disease, diabetes, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, dementia, glaucoma, and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy warrant targeted investigation during NLRP3 inhibitor development, while inverse associations with heart failure and non-small-cell lung cancer may suggest opportunities for therapeutic repurposing. These findings are hypothesis-generating and require independent replication and mechanistic or pharmacological validation. The inverse association with heart failure is noteworthy, particularly given the absence of evidence for cardiovascular benefit across the major atherosclerotic outcomes. Although the primary heart failure analysis was null, the larger PheWAS analysis yielded an effect estimate in the same protective direction, suggesting that the discrepancy may partly reflect differences in statistical power. This signal is supported by limited early clinical evidence. A pooled analysis of dapansutrile in patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (n = 24) demonstrated an approximately 2% improvement in left ventricular ejection fraction after 12 days 93 . However, treatment was not associated with reductions in hsCRP or NT-proBNP, and the small sample size, short treatment duration, and early-phase design preclude conclusions regarding clinical efficacy 93 . Collectively, the genetic and preliminary pharmacological findings raise the possibility that the effects of NLRP3 inhibition may differ between heart failure and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, but whether pharmacological NLRP3 inhibition improves cardiac function or clinical outcomes in HFrEF remains uncertain.

Several limitations should also be considered. Mendelian randomization estimates the consequences of relatively small, lifelong genetically determined differences in pathway activity and cannot directly reproduce the magnitude, timing, reversibility, tissue specificity, or treatment context of pharmacological NLRP3 inhibition 32 . Consequently, the absence of a genetically predicted cardiovascular benefit does not establish that pharmacological NLRP3 inhibition cannot reduce cardiovascular events. Although the instrument showed strong biological and pharmacodynamic validation, CRP is a distal readout of NLRP3 activity, and the selected variants were noncoding, potentially influencing the expression of neighbouring genes. Horizontal pleiotropy therefore cannot be excluded; however, nearby genes have no established roles in inflammasome signaling and there was significant colocalization between effects on CRP and NLRP3 expression in monocytes, reducing the biological plausibility of alternative mechanisms 95 . The limited colocalisation observed for several clinical outcomes further warrants caution when attributing these associations specifically to NLRP3 perturbation. Most contributing GWASs were conducted predominantly in individuals of European ancestry, limiting generalisability to other ancestral populations. Finally, phenome-wide analyses are inherently exploratory and, despite multiple-testing correction and sensitivity analyses, require independent replication before individual associations can be considered established therapeutic or safety effects.

In conclusion, we developed a genetic instrument for NLRP3 downregulation that recapitulated the established biology of the NLRP3 inflammasome and key molecular effects of pharmacological NLRP3 inhibition. Still, genetically proxied NLRP3 downregulation was not associated with reduced ASCVD risk or a more favorable cardiometabolic risk profile.
