Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Methods

Study Design

We performed a two-stage, cross-platform biomarker study. Stage 1 mined CNVs from 1,086 primary breast tumors and 942 matched peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) controls in the TCGA-BRCA cohort to derive a breast-cancer-specific CNV panel. In the subsequent phase, patient-level phylogenetic reconstruction of a multi-subtype single-cell RNA-seq cohort (n=50) confirmed the panel's features as early oncogenic events conserved across molecular subtypes.

Stage 2 prospectively validated the panel in two independent clinical series: a cohort of 57 women with nipple discharge samples and an independent multicenter set of 183 plasma cfDNA samples. All participants were diagnosed through standard clinical and histopathological evaluation. Breast cancer cases were further classified into clinical stages according to the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system [62] and into molecular subtypes based on hormone receptor (HR) and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) status determined by immunohistochemistry. In the first clinical series, all specimens underwent LP-WGS. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) was then used to analyze matched tumor tissue from five malignant cases to trace the cellular origin of nippledischarge-derived CNVs.

Recurrent CNV identification

We downloaded copy-number profiles for 1,086 primary breast cancer tissues and 942 matched PBMC controls from the TCGA-BRCA cohort [63] via the National Cancer Institute Genomic Data Commons portal [64]. Recurrent amplifications and deletions were identified using Genomic Identification of Significant Targets in Cancer 2.0 (GISTIC 2.0, v2.0.23) [25] under default parameter settings (confidence level=0.95, q-value threshold=0.1, amplification threshold=0.1, deletion threshold=-0.1, and arm-level peeling enabled). Output gene-level tables and region summaries were retained for downstream filtering.

Determination of CNV frequency cutoff

To balance sensitivity and panel size for clinical cost-effectiveness, we modeled the relationship between CNV prevalence and diagnostic sensitivity in the TCGA-BRCA cohort. Starting with a 10% prevalence threshold, we increased the cutoff in 5% increments, recorded the number of retained loci, and computed the corresponding breast cancer detection sensitivity in the same cohort. A 50% prevalence threshold yielded a compact feature set (20 loci) while maintaining ≥98% diagnostic sensitivity, and was therefore selected as the final cutoff.

In a subset of 5 patients with pathologically confirmed malignancy, matched primary tumor tissue and peripheral blood samples were also obtained, enabling comparative multi-omics analysis. The primary tumor tissue samples underwent scRNA-seq, and the extracted cfDNA underwent whole-genome sequencing (WGS).

Single-cell RNA sequencing

Library construction and sequencing. Fresh primary breast cancer tissues were surgically harvested and immediately washed in ice-cold PBS (SH30256.01, Hyclone, Logan, UT, USA), then dissociated using the SeekMate Tissue Dissociation Reagent Kit A Pro (K01801-30, SeekGene, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. DNase Ⅰ (9003-98-9, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) treatment was applied as needed based on homogenate viscosity. After erythrocyte removal (R1010, Solarbio, Beijing, China), cell count and viability were assessed using a Fluorescence Cell Analyzer (Rigel S2, Countstar, Shanghai, China) with AO/PI reagent, followed by optional debris and dead cell removal (130-109-398/130-090-101, Miltenyi, Bergisch Gladbach, NRW, Germany). Fresh cells were washed twice in RPMI1640 (11875119, Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) and resuspended at 1×10 cells/ml in RPMI1640 with 2% FBS (10100147C, Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA). Single-cell RNA-seq libraries were prepared using the SeekOne® DD Single Cell 3' library preparation kit (K00202, SeekGene, Beijing, China). Cells were mixed with reverse transcription reagent and loaded into the SeekOne® chip S3, after which Barcoded Hydrogel Beads and partitioning oil were added. After emulsion droplet generation, reverse transcription was performed at 42°C for 90 minutes, followed by inactivation at 85°C for 5 minutes. cDNA was purified, amplified, cleaned, fragmented, endrepaired, A-tailed, and ligated to sequencing adaptors. Indexed polymerase chain reaction amplified the 3' polyA regions containing Cell Barcodes and Unique Molecular Indices.

Libraries were cleaned with VAHTS DNA Clean Beads (N411-01, Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, JS, China), quantified using Qubit (Q33226, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and Bio-Fragment Analyzer (Qsep400, Bioptic, Hsinchu, TW, China), and sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq 6000 with PE150 read length. The scRNA-seq reads from 5 samples were aligned to the human reference genome GRCh38 using the Cell Ranger (v7.1.0) [71].

Data processing, batch correction, and clustering.

Subsequent single-cell data analysis was conducted using the Seurat package (v4.4.0) [72]. Quality control filtering was performed with the following parameters: cells expressing between 100 and 7,000 genes were retained, and those with mitochondrial gene content exceeding 10% were excluded. Following filtering, the gene expression matrices were normalized and scaled using Seurat's "NormalizeData" and "ScaleData" functions. To mitigate batch effects across patient samples, the Harmony package (v1.2.3) was used for data integration [70]. A total of 30,709 genes were retained for downstream analysis, encompassing 48,996 cells from primary breast cancer samples. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted on the batch-corrected expression matrix, using highly variable genes identified by the Seurat "FindVariableGenes" algorithm. Dimensionality reduction was performed via the "RunPCA" function. Cell clusters were subsequently identified by applying the "FindClusters" function to the most informative principal components.

Cell type annotation by canonical markers.

The marker genes for each cell type were listed as follows: CD3D and CD3E for T cells (CD4, CD8A, and FOXP3 for further grouping); JCHAIN, MZB1, and IGKC for plasma cells; NKG7, GNLY, and NCAM1 for natural killer cells; LYZ, CD14, and CD68 for myeloid cells; TPSAB1, TPSB2, and CPA3 for mast cells; EPCAM, CDH1, and KRT8 for epithelial cells; PECAM1, VWF, and CD34 for endothelial cells; HLA-DRA and CD83 for dendritic cells; PDGFRA, PDGFRB, and COL1A1 for fibroblasts; MS4A1 and CD79A for B cells [73, 74].

CNV inference.

The inferCNV package (v1.19.1) [30] was used to infer large-scale CNVs in epithelial subclusters. Downsampled T cells and myeloid cells served as reference populations to normalize gene expression and identify CNV events. The analysis was performed within a single patient using the hidden Markov model. The "subcluster" mode was used to determine clonal CNV changes.

Phylogenetic tree construction and ancestral CNV state inference

Phylogenetic trees for individual patients were reconstructed from the inferCNV data using maximum parsimony implemented in the phangorn package (v2.12.1) [36] in R. Ancestral CNV states for each gene were then inferred using the ancestral.pars function from the phangorn package with the maximum parsimony reconstruction (MPR) algorithm. The algorithm identified the most parsimonious state assignments and computed their associated likelihoods at every internal node. An ancestral state was considered confidently resolved when its maximum parsimony likelihood exceeded 0.5, the conventional threshold for binary classification in parsimony-based reconstruction.

Calculation of Cohen's d effect sizes

To quantify the functional differences associated with each CNV feature, all epithelial cells from the combined cohort were stratified into two groups based on whether the CNV feature was present or absent. Gene Ontology Biological Process (GOBP) pathway scores were calculated for each cell using the Seurat AddModuleScore function with default parameters. Cohen's d effect sizes were then computed for each GOBP pathway using the effsize package (v0.8.1) [37] in R, with positive values indicating higher pathway activity in CNV feature-positive cells and negative values indicating lower activity. Statistical significance was assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with Benjamini-Hochberg correction for multiple comparisons.

Nipple discharge sample processing

Nipple discharge samples were manually extruded, and approximately 0.3-0.5 mL of fluid was collected using a 1-mL syringe. The collected fluid was immediately transferred to 3 mL of preservation solution and stored at 0-4 °C. All samples were processed for library construction and sequencing within 48 hours of collection.

The ndDNA was extracted using the Amp Genomic DNA Kit (TIANGEN Biotech, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. Sequencing libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) with a mean input of 251.9 ng genomic DNA per sample, followed by LP-WGS. The DNA fragments were ligated with 8bp barcoded sequencing adapters and amplified by PCR. Purified libraries were subjected to massively parallel sequencing on an Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Approximately 4Gb of raw sequencing data per sample were filtered and aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh37) using BWA-MEM (v0.7.17) [75]. Genomic coverage was quantified with SAMtools mpileup (v1.15) [76]. Coverage values for each 200-Kb genomic bin were calculated and normalized for GC content. An external normal control dataset from healthy individuals was used as a reference baseline for subsequent normalization. A Z-score approach was applied to identify regional CNVs. The thresholds (Zscore > 0.4 for amplification and < -0.8 for deletion) were established based on our preliminary data analysis.

Plasma cfDNA sample processing

A total of 10 mL of peripheral blood was collected from 188 patients and placed in Streck cfDNA blood collection tubes (Streck, Omaha, NE, USA). Of these, 5 were from the nipple discharge cohort and 183 from the plasma cfDNA cohort. Plasma separation was performed using a two-step centrifugation protocol: initial centrifugation at 1,600 × g for 15 minutes at 4°C to isolate plasma from cellular components, followed by a second centrifugation at 16,000 × g for 10 minutes at 4°C to remove residual platelets and debris. The purified plasma was carefully aliquoted and stored at -80°C until analysis. cfDNA was extracted from plasma samples using the QIAamp Circulating Nucleic Acid Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer's specifications. The integrity and concentration of the extracted cfDNA were assessed. Library preparation was performed using the Fast Library Prep Kit v2.0 (iGeneTech, Beijing, China) for end repair and adapter ligation. A total of 750 ng of the pre-library was subjected to hybridization capture with the TargetSeq® Pan-Cancer Panel and the TargetSeq One® Hyb & Wash Kit v2.0 (iGeneTech, Beijing, China).

The captured libraries were sequenced on the HiSeq X Ten platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) using PE150. Raw sequencing data were processed with Fastp for quality control and filtering. Clean reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh37) using BWA-MEM (v0.7.17) [75], and CNV calling was performed using CNVnator (v0.4.1) [77]. The thresholds (segment copy-number altered > 0.2 for amplification and < -0.2 for deletion) were established based on our preliminary data analysis.

Reference genome conversion

To enable comprehensive multi-omics and multi-database analysis, this study converted genomic coordinates between GRCh37 and GRCh38 using the "liftOver" function from the rtracklayer package (version 1.62.0).

CNV profile visualization and significance testing

CNV profiles per sample were visualized using the cnSpec function from the GenVisR package (v1.34.0) [78]. The alternation frequencies of the nipple discharge cohort and the plasma cfDNA cohort were displayed using the cnFreq function. The alternative significance of these two cohorts was assessed using the two-proportion Z-test, adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) Correction, and visualized with the ggplot2 package (v3.5.1) [79].

Functional annotation of epithelial subclusters

Epithelial cells were unsupervised re-clustered and combined with GOBP functional enrichment scoring [80] to identify functionally distinct subclusters. The analysis was conducted using the "AddModuleScore" function from the Seurat package, which calculates pathway activity scores for each cell based on the average expression of genes within predefined GOBP gene sets. We focused on biological processes relevant to epithelial cell function, including cellular development, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, adhesion, cellular response, OP, and immune response pathways. Pathway scores were computed for each epithelial subcluster, and significantly enriched GOBP terms (adjusted p-value < 0.05) were identified with BH correction for multiple comparisons. The resulting pathway activity profiles were visualized using heatmaps to illustrate the functional heterogeneity among epithelial subclusters.

Fidelity Rate and Fidelity Score calculation

The Fidelity Rate is defined as the proportion of predefined CNV features that are jointly detected in both the ndDNA and the subcluster's inferCNV profile, relative to the total number of features identified in either source.

Survival Analysis

Survival analysis was conducted using the survival (v3.8-3) and survminer (v0.5.0) packages in R. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from diagnosis to death from any cause.

Disease-free survival (DFS) was defined as the time from diagnosis to disease recurrence or death from any cause. Patients who were alive or lost to follow-up were censored at the last follow-up date. Kaplan-Meier (K-M) survival curves were generated using the survfit function from the survival package, with the formula specified as Surv(time, status) ~ group_variable, where time denotes survival time and status indicates event status (1 = event,0 = censored).

Survival curves were visualized using the ggsurvplot function from the survminer package. Logrank tests were performed using the survdiff function to compare survival distributions between groups. All statistical tests were two-sided, and p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (v4.4.3). Between-group comparisons were made using two-tailed Student's t-tests (for two groups) or one-way/two-way ANOVA (for three or more groups). For non-normally distributed data, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was employed.

Correlations were analyzed using Spearman's method. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value < 0.05. The BH method was used to calculate adjusted p-values for multiple comparisons. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for the CNV features diagnostic model in the nipple discharge cohort and the plasma cfDNA cohort were generated using the pROC package in R.

Identification of recurrent CNV features for breast cancer

To identify recurrent somatic CNV features in breast cancer, we analyzed whole-genome CNV data from 1,086 primary breast tumors and 942 matched PBMC controls in the TCGA-BRCA cohort. Using the GISTIC 2.0 analytical pipeline [25], we identified 158 recurrent somatic CNV features (87 amplifications and 71 deletions) specifically associated with breast carcinogenesis (Supplementary Fig. S1A). Functional mechanisms and clinical prognostic value of the epithelial cell Fidelity Score

To further establish a molecular metric reflecting tumor cell shedding propensity, we defined the Fidelity Score (Supplementary Table S5). It is calculated from the top 30 differentially expressed genes between groups with high or low Fidelity Rate (Fig. 6A). Our comprehensive characterization of the Fidelity Score incorporated three key dimensions: its association with genomic, functional, and spatial states, its relationship with cell-cell interactions, and its clinical relevance.

The Fidelity Score correlated positively with the CNV score, confirming that high genomic instability may lead to a stronger shedding propensity (Fig. 6B). We next investigated the molecular pathways, including estrogen and HER2 signaling, and found that the high-fidelity group was characterized by significantly enriched estrogen receptor (ER) signaling and attenuated HER2 signaling (Fig. 6C). Consistent with this, the Fidelity Score correlated with high ER signaling and low HER2 signaling, and each functional epithelial subcluster carried its own pathway signature (Fig. 6D). This correlation was further validated in the PDO cohort, with distinct clustering observed across molecular subtypes (Supplementary Fig. S6A). These results suggested that nipple discharge may reveal expression levels of key biomarkers and could therefore help guide treatment decisions.

Cell-cell interaction analysis indicated that the high-fidelity group engaged in stronger interactions with immune cells, suggesting a potential role for the immune microenvironment in regulating the fidelity phenotype (Fig. 6E). Ligand-receptor analysis suggested that the downregulated immune interactions in the low-fidelity group were primarily mediated by the MIF-(CD74+CD44) and MIF-(CD74+CXCR4) axes (Supplementary Fig. S6B). Spatial transcriptomics validation further confirmed heterogeneity in the Fidelity Score across distinct histological regions (Fig. 6F). Moreover, the high-fidelity epithelial group was found to be spatially closer to cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), CD8 T cells, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), and endothelial cells than to the low-fidelity group, consistent with the cell-cell interaction analysis (Fig. 6G). Single-cell spatial transcriptomics on a slice with welldefined ductal architecture revealed high-fidelity epithelial subclusters localized near the duct lumen (Supplementary Fig. S6C).

In the METABRIC and TCGA-BRCA cohorts, the patient-level Fidelity Score was significantly higher in Luminal (HR-positive) breast cancers than in TNBC and HRnegative/HER2-positive subtypes, suggesting a strong association between shedding propensity and Luminal-type breast cancer. This association was also validated in the PDO cohort (Supplementary Fig. S6D). In the METABRIC and TCGA-BRCA cohorts, the highest Fidelity Score was observed in clinical stage I, indicating that high shedding propensity is an early event in tumor development (Fig. 6H; Supplementary Fig. S6E). Most importantly, the Fidelity Score is a robust prognostic indicator. Patients with high Fidelity Scores had significantly better OS than those with low scores in both the METABRIC and TCGA-BRCA cohorts (p < 0.0001 and p = 0.00032, respectively). This prognostic advantage persisted specifically within Luminal patients across both cohorts (Fig. 6I; Supplementary Fig. S6F).

Discussion

Early diagnosis of cancer is crucial for improving patient prognosis [38], particularly in breast cancer, the most common malignancy in women worldwide [39, 40]. Clinical symptoms remain the foundational element of cancer diagnosis. Nipple discharge is experienced by over 80% of women during their reproductive years [41]. Nevertheless, as one of the principal presenting complaints in breast surgery, its molecular diagnostic potential remains largely unexplored.

Therefore, developing early diagnostic methods with high sensitivity and specificity has been a major priority. CNVs are recognized as key early drivers in breast cancer tumorigenesis [42, 43] and serve as promising biomarkers for early-stage detection. Here, we derived a 20-feature CNV panel by analyzing CNV data from TCGA-BRCA (n=1,086). Large-scale validation on GSE87048 (n = 100), GSE118527 (n = 401), METABRIC (n = 2,087), and MSK-CHORD (n = 5,751) confirmed that these CNVs are recurrent, breast-cancer-enriched genomic features.

Several implicated genes have been reported as high-frequency driver genes (e.g., the amplified oncogene MYC on AMP10 and the deleted tumor suppressor gene WWOX on DEL05) [43] [44] [45] [46].

Integrative single-cell analysis of 50 breast cancer patients across molecular subtypes provided mechanistic insight into this 20-feature CNV panel. Phylogenetic reconstruction showed that 19/20 CNV features were present at early evolutionary nodes in 70% of patients, and 4 features were recurrent in >30% of cases across all molecular subtypes. Notably, the cooccurring AMP07~12 cluster on chromosomes 8q and 20q was functionally linked to metabolic reprogramming, suggesting that these early-arising amplifications confer bioenergetic advantages that are conserved across molecular subtypes. These findings establish that the CNV features comprising our diagnostic panel represent recurrent, early oncogenic drivers rather than late passenger events.

Significant progress has been made in the use of blood-based liquid biopsies for cancer [11, 47, 48]. Notably, CNV has been employed as an effective biomarker in multiple cancer studies [49, 50]. However, detecting CNV in plasma cfDNA still faces several challenges, including low early-stage ctDNA abundance [51, 52], release influenced by clinical and biological characteristics [53, 54], and clonal hematopoiesis-associated false positives [52, 55]. In contrast, non-blood liquid biopsy specimens, anatomically adjacent to the tumor, are less affected by dilution effects and clonal hematopoiesis [12]. Low-depth detection of CNV biomarkers has already been successfully implemented in non-blood fluids such as urine (for bladder cancer) and cerebrospinal fluid (for central nervous system tumors) [14, 56].

Here, we report a key finding: CNV profiling of nipple discharge yields superior diagnostic performance compared with plasma cfDNA. In the prospective nipple discharge cohort (39 malignant, 18 benign), the panel achieved an AUC of 0.863, with 94.4% specificity and 96.7% PPV, correctly identifying 29/39 cancers while yielding only 1 false-positive. Meanwhile, CNV signals were virtually undetectable in matched plasma cfDNA despite a 10-fold higher sequencing depth. This comparison highlights ndDNA as a locally enriched, tumor-specific DNA source that serves as a high-confidence "rule-in" test to reduce unnecessary invasive diagnostic procedures. Moreover, detection of ndDNA CNVs indicates tumor invasion into the mammary ductal system, suggesting its potential utility in guiding surgical margins.

Compared with blood-based liquid biopsy, our ndDNA strategy offers distinct practical and biological advantages. In GRAIL's Circulating Cell-free Genome Atlas study of 4,077 participants, a targeted methylation panel at 100× depth achieved 99.5% specificity; however, overall detection sensitivity was 51.5%, and only 16.8% for stage I cancer [57, 58]. The subsequent PATHFINDER prospective study of 6,621 participants confirmed these metrics but still demonstrated low sensitivity for stage I disease [59]. Our model achieved a 70.0% positive rate (14/20) in stage 0 (n = 12) and stage I (n = 8) patients within the nipple discharge cohort, indicating strong potential for early detection. Remarkably, while DELFI's blood-based fragmentomics approach reported a sensitivity of only 57% for breast cancer at 1-2× depth under a comparable 98% specificity threshold [60], our ndDNA framework preserved a substantially higher sensitivity of 74.4% at an ultra-low sequencing depth of ~1× (requiring only 4 Gb of data).

This sharp contrast underscores the biological superiority of tumor-proximal biofluids, which capture concentrated, high-fidelity diagnostic signals that are otherwise heavily diluted by the systemic circulation. Furthermore, blood-based assays typically require 10-20 mL of blood, whereas we obtain equivalent genomic information from only 0.3-0.5 mL of nipple discharge, improving patient adherence and enabling non-invasive self-collection. Prospective integration of ndDNA with imaging should quantify further reductions in invasive downstream procedures.

To elucidate the biological mechanisms underlying this diagnostic efficacy, we used singlecell multi-omics data to trace CNV features to the malignant epithelium, uncovering unequal contributions of different tumor subclusters to ndDNA. We identified a "high-fidelity" group of four epithelial subclusters (Cytoskeleton, Mesenchymal, Mammary Gland, Stress) that faithfully release their CNV features into nipple discharge. Spatial transcriptomic analysis indicated that high-fidelity tumor cells were located closer to the mammary ducts. Further functional analysis revealed a significant correlation between high shedding propensity and high HR signaling and low HER2 signaling. Meanwhile, Luminal cases in TCGA also exhibited higher Fidelity Scores, indicating that ndDNA may be more reliable in patients with Luminal breast cancer. Furthermore, the high-fidelity cells displayed significantly stronger interactions with CD8 T cells and TAMs, suggesting that immune-microenvironment cross-talk may facilitate DNA shedding-a hypothesis warranting future validation.

Clinically, high Fidelity Scores strongly predicted better outcomes, suggesting that abundant CNV shedding is a marker of a less aggressive, well-differentiated Luminal tumor. Because the score is derived from standard RNA-seq of resected or biopsied tissue, it could be incorporated into existing molecular pathology workflows for early risk stratification. Prospective studies are needed to determine whether high-fidelity tumors also show better response to endocrine or immune-based therapies.

Our study has several limitations. First, the sample size of the nipple discharge cohort, while sufficient to demonstrate robust diagnostic accuracy in our first-in-field analysis, will need to be expanded in future multicenter studies to validate and refine the performance of the CNV panel.

Because only about 4% of breast cancer patients present with nipple discharge [61], we generalized our findings by validating the CNV panel in two large, independent data sets (TCGA and METABRIC). Nevertheless, even larger prospective cohorts are still required to confirm all observations.

Conclusions

This work introduces nipple discharge as a highly effective liquid biopsy source for breast cancer diagnosis through CNV profiling. By integrating bulk genomic analyses with single-cell and spatial resolution, we validated a sensitive diagnostic tool, provided a deep biological rationale for its efficacy, and linked diagnostic signal strength to specific tumor cell subpopulations and their underlying biology. These findings pave the way for the clinical translation of nipple discharge-based testing and offer new insights into tumor cell shedding as a biologically and prognostically meaningful phenomenon.
