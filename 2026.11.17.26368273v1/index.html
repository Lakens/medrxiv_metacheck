Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Introduction Spatial transcriptomics can resolve compartment-specific molecular programs in kidney disease, but its cost and technical requirements limit its use in clinical diagnostics and in large biopsy archives. Predicting spatial gene expression from routine histology with artificial intelligence could extend molecular characterization to existing clinically annotated kidney tissue collections.

We adapted DeepSpot-M, a pretrained multimodal foundation model that predicts spatial gene expression from histology, using paired hematoxylin and eosin images and Visium spatial transcriptomics from 209 Kidney Precision Medicine Project (KPMP) participants. We evaluated virtual profiles in held-out human biopsies, associated them with histopathologic and biochemical injury markers in 567 rats with defined nephrotoxin exposures from a public toxicogenomics database, and generated a virtual atlas from 1 534 histology slides of 476 additional KPMP participants.

Results Kidney-specific adaptation of DeepSpot-M doubled the mean Pearson correlation of each model's 1 000 best-predicted genes (0.43 versus 0.22) and exceeded recent foundation models.

Virtual profiles preserved the direction of all 13 disease-marker differences in measured expression, including tubular injury, maladaptive repair, gluconeogenesis, and fibrosis-associated programs.

All 11 measured associations between molecular programs and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) were also preserved. Without rat-specific training, a virtual injury score distinguished moderate or severe histopathologic lesions (area under the curve, 0.85) and at least twofold blood urea nitrogen elevation (0.82). In our atlas of 5.46 million virtual spots, higher proximal tubule program scores were associated with higher eGFR (partial 𝑟, 0.32) and less interstitial fibrosis (-0.57), and program-pathology associations agreed with those of measured Visium profiles in independent participants (Spearman 𝜌, 0.64).

Conclusions Kidney-adapted virtual spatial transcriptomics links histology-derived molecular programs to experimental renal injury and human clinical and histologic phenotypes. Our approach enables spatial molecular analyses in routine biopsy histology, which could help decipher diseaseassociated pathways in existing biopsy archives.

Translational Statement

Spatial molecular profiling of kidney biopsies is informative but too costly for routine use, whereas hematoxylin and eosin sections exist for nearly every biopsy. We show that a model adapted to paired kidney histology and spatial transcriptomics infers programs of tubular injury, metabolic differentiation, and fibrosis from routine histology, and that these programs track kidney function and pathology across 476 participants. Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) and chronic kidney disease (CKD) shift the balance between differentiated nephron function, epithelial repair, and interstitial remodeling. Single-cell and spatial transcriptomic studies, among them those of the Kidney Precision Medicine Project (KPMP), resolved injury and repair within specific nephron segments and tissue neighborhoods. [1] [2] [3] In mice and humans, proximal tubular cells either regained differentiated functions after injury or persisted in altered states with inflammatory and profibrotic programs, 2, 4 and spatial studies located these injured epithelial populations alongside immune cells, fibroblasts, and myofibroblasts. [5] [6] [7] [8] Such unresolved injury states were associated with functional decline in the human kidney, 3 indicating that gene expression programs carry information beyond the morphological appearance of the tissue. Among these programs, HAVCR1 (kidney injury molecule-1) marked acutely injured proximal tubules, VCAM1 marked tubular cells that failed to repair, 2, 4 and MMP7, a Wnt/𝛽-catenin target, linked epithelial injury to experimental fibrosis. 9 In contrast, reduced expression of the gluconeogenic genes PC, PCK1, FBP1, and G6PC1 marked loss of differentiated proximal tubular function in CKD. 10 Jointly characterizing these partly opposing programs within the same tissue can help distinguish preserved tubular function from injury, inflammation, and fibrosis.

However, the high cost, low throughput, and specialized tissue requirements of spatial transcriptomic assays confine them to research centers and preclude their use in large kidney cohorts and routine diagnostics. As a consequence, few or no molecular data exist for patients with rarer kidney diseases. By contrast, routine hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained sections are widely available in biopsy archives and are often linked to pathology and clinical information, but they provide no direct measurement of gene expression. Computational pathology has automated the segmentation of renal structures, the detection and grading of lesions, and the mapping of immune cells on these images, [11] [12] [13] [14] [15] [16] [17] [18] [19] yet these approaches quantify tissue morphology without measuring the underlying molecular programs.

Predicting gene expression directly from histology, termed virtual spatial transcriptomics, moves this approach from assigning predefined labels to inferring spatial molecular profiles across the tissue. [20] [21] [22] [23] Recent foundation models, deep learning models pretrained on large spatial transcriptomics collections, predicted thousands of genes from H&E images, [24] [25] [26] and such virtual profiles uncovered molecular biomarkers in routine breast cancer pathology. 27 These models, however, were developed predominantly in oncology. It therefore remains unresolved whether virtual spatial transcriptomics in the kidney merely reproduces morphology-correlated gene expression or truly recovers renal disease programs and their links to kidney injury, function, and pathology.

To address this gap, we adapted DeepSpot-M, a multimodal foundation model that predicts gene expression from histology, 24 to paired kidney images and spatial transcriptomic measurements of KPMP (Figure 1). DeepSpot-M represents each gene by its protein sequence. 28, 29 Therefore, the same model can be applied across species without species-specific training. In held-out human biopsies, we compared virtual with measured gene expression, including disease-associated molecular differences and associations with estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). In public data from rats with defined nephrotoxic exposures, we related virtual injury programs to the site and severity of histopathologic injury and to blood urea nitrogen (BUN), thus testing the model against injury of known origin. 30 Finally, we applied the model to 1 534 archived slides of 476 independent KPMP participants to build a virtual spatial transcriptomics atlas that links diagnoses, histopathology, and kidney function to molecular programs. Our aim was to evaluate routine histology as a proxy for spatial molecular profiling.

Methods

Study population and data

We analyzed paired H&E images and 10x Genomics Visium profiles from 209 KPMP participants, with one frozen specimen per participant embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound (Table 1; Supplementary Table S1). 1, 31 Participants were grouped by KPMP enrollment category into AKI (𝑛 = 62), CKD (𝑛 = 95), diabetes mellitus resilient (DM-R, 𝑛 = 24), defined by KPMP as long-standing diabetes without clinically overt kidney disease, and healthy reference tissue (𝑛 = 28).

Clinical information came from the KPMP open-access participant table (Supplementary Methods).

KPMP calculated baseline eGFR with the CKD-EPI creatinine equation, for AKI from creatinine values before the injury episode. Therefore, analyses of eGFR excluded participants with AKI.

All participants provided informed consent under KPMP protocols approved by the participating institutions' review boards.

Model and kidney adaptation

DeepSpot-M combines histologic features from the Midnight image encoder 32 with ProtT5 gene embeddings derived from protein sequences 28, 29 through a cross-attention decoder and was developed on 1.14 million spot profiles from 16 cancer spatial transcriptomics datasets without KPMP data. 24 For kidney adaptation, the image encoder and gene embeddings remained frozen, and the model was trained on H&E tiles centered on the 55 µm Visium spots (65 µm field of view) paired with measured spot profiles (counts per 10 000, log1p transformed; 19 338 genes; Supplementary Methods). We compared the model adapted on pooled kidney biopsies (DeepSpot-M Kidney), models adapted for each enrollment group together with the reference biopsies (DeepSpot-M Kidney (group-specific)), and the pretrained model without kidney adaptation (DeepSpot-M (zero-shot)). For the rat study and the atlas, DeepSpot-M Kidney was adapted on all 209 paired biopsies and applied to whole sections on a lattice of non-overlapping 56 µm tiles, termed virtual spots, each profiled from the 65 µm image crop centered on it.

Study design and accuracy

Five group-stratified splits at the participant level each used 147 biopsies for adaptation and held out the other 62 for evaluation, and 171 of 209 participants (82%) were held out at least once (Supplementary Figure S1). To reduce the effect of gene dropout, 33, 34 the reference expression of each spot averaged its measured expression with that of its five nearest neighbors in expression space within the same biopsy, 24 and individual-spot expression served as a sensitivity analysis.

Accuracy was the Pearson correlation (𝑟) between virtual and reference expression across the spots of a held-out biopsy, averaged over the held-out biopsies of a group. Top-𝐾 accuracy, the mean 𝑟 of each model's 𝐾 best-predicted genes ranked by held-out accuracy, is reported as mean±standard deviation (SD) over splits together with a chance level after permuting spots within each held-out biopsy. Gene sets of nephron compartments and tissue programs (Supplementary Table S2) were summarized by the mean 𝑟 of their genes.

Each model family was evaluated before and after fine-tuning on the training biopsies of each split, with the published training objectives for the recent foundation models STPath 25 and SQUALL 26 and the kidney adaptation above for DeepSpot-M (Supplementary Methods). As released, STPath and SQUALL had seen 21 KPMP specimens in pretraining, 2 19 of which were among the 171 held-out participants. These 19 participants were excluded from all top-𝐾 comparisons.

Disease groups and kidney function

We compared AKI and CKD with DM-R biopsies for prespecified injury and maladaptive repair markers (VCAM1, HAVCR1, MMP7, CDH6, and CDKN1A), gluconeogenic enzymes (PC, PCK1, FBP1, and G6PC1), and a fibroblast program score. 2, 10 Pseudo-bulk expression, the mean virtual or measured expression of each gene over the spots of a biopsy, was 𝑧-scored within each split across the held-out AKI, CKD, and DM-R biopsies, and group differences in mean 𝑧-score were averaged over splits. A measured difference of at least 0.5 SD of these 𝑧-scores was counted as reproduced when the virtual difference had the same sign. Tissue program scores were the mean 𝑧-score of program genes from published KPMP and kidney atlas studies (Supplementary Table S2), 2, 3, [35] [36] [37] [38] computed only with models that had not seen the participant and averaged over splits. Program scores were related to eGFR by Spearman correlation in participants with CKD, DM-R, or reference tissue (𝑛 = 111), and adjudicated diagnoses were compared within AKI (acute interstitial nephritis versus acute tubular injury) and CKD (diabetic versus hypertensive kidney disease) by the difference in mean program score. A measured association whose 95% confidence interval (CI; 10 000 bootstrap resamples of biopsies) excluded zero counted as reproduced when the CI of the virtual association excluded zero on the same side.

Rat toxicology study

We analyzed kidney H&E sections, histopathologic grades, BUN, and kidney expression arrays of 567 male Sprague-Dawley rats in the public Open TG-GATEs database, 30, 39, 40 which received one of six nephrotoxic compounds (cisplatin, carboplatin, gentamicin, 2-bromoethylamine, puromycin aminonucleoside, and doxorubicin) at three doses, or vehicle, for up to eight exposure durations (Supplementary Methods). DeepSpot-M Kidney queried 20 368 rat genes through their ProtT5 embeddings from the STRING database, 29, 41 without rat-specific training or mapping to human orthologs. An injury program (Lcn2, Clu, Spp1, Timp1, Cd44, and Sox9) and a proximal tubule program (Lrp², Slc34a1, and Slc5a2) were scored in the cortex, outer medulla, and inner medulla with papilla, delimited by depth borders calibrated on the control sections. Program scores averaged, over their genes, the difference in regional mean virtual expression from each rat's three time-matched controls divided by the pooled control SD, with proximal tubule loss as the negative proximal tubule score. The region of expected injury of each compound was specified from the literature before the analysis. Expression arrays from separate kidney fragments of the same rats were normalized by robust multi-array average 42 and scored in the same way. Rat data came from a published study whose protocols had been approved by the responsible animal ethics committees. 30

KPMP virtual spatial transcriptomics atlas

For the atlas, DeepSpot-M Kidney profiled an independent KPMP histology archive without participant overlap, comprising 1 534 slides from 476 participants, of which 968 were formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) and 566 frozen (Supplementary Methods).

Program scores were computed from the virtual pseudo-bulk of each slide relative to expressionmatched control genes and averaged per participant and tissue preparation. Associations with baseline eGFR (participants without AKI), binned albuminuria, proteinuria, and glycated hemoglobin, and pathologist descriptors of the KPMP core (255 participants) were estimated by rank partial correlation adjusted for site, sex, age, and scanner (and cortex fraction for descriptors), separately for FFPE and frozen slides. Partial correlations computed in the same way from measured pseudo-bulk profiles of the 209 Visium participants served as a reference. Tissue niches were identified on FFPE slides of the Aperio GT450 scanner (583 slides from 293 participants) by Leiden clustering of the virtual expression of program and highly variable genes after Harmony integration across instruments, 43 annotated by their virtual marker genes and representative H&E tiles, and compared with cell types measured by Xenium in the same participants (Supplementary Methods). To extend Xenium panels, DeepSpot-M Kidney was further adapted to the measured panel genes of single cells in 49 KPMP Xenium sections. The panel genes on chromosomes 1, 3, 5, 7, and 9 were withheld from adaptation, together with their paralogs on other chromosomes, to prevent leakage through the similar protein sequence embeddings of paralogs. 24 Agreement of the withheld genes with measured expression was compared before and after adaptation by a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

Statistical analysis

In rats with lesions, the region of expected injury was compared with the other two regions, and each region with the matched controls, by two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, and trends with the duration of dosing were assessed by Spearman correlation. The Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) was computed over the three compound-specific comparisons of the expected region and controlled at 0.05 over the atlas associations. Areas under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUCs) were computed with 95% CIs from stratified bootstrap resampling. Two-sided P < 0.05 was considered significant. Analyses of disease groups and kidney function in the Visium cohort were descriptive and not corrected for multiple comparisons.

Results

Participant characteristics

In the 209 participants with paired histology and Visium profiles, median baseline eGFR ranged from 48 ml/min per 1.73 m 2 in CKD to 109 ml/min per 1.73 m 2 in DM-R (Table 1).

Accuracy of virtual spatial expression in held-out participants

Kidney-specific adaptation improved the agreement between virtual and measured Visium expression in held-out participants, with a mean Pearson correlation of 0.43 for the 1 000 best-predicted genes of DeepSpot-M Kidney, twice that of DeepSpot-M (zero-shot) (0.22). DeepSpot-M Kidney also outperformed the recent foundation models STPath and SQUALL in every disease group, both as released (0.35 and 0.11) and after fine-tuning on the same training biopsies (0.38 and 0.18; Figure 2a). The gain held against individual-spot expression (0.20 versus 0.08; Supplementary Table S3), and the ranking of the models was unchanged with Spearman correlation and for genes ranked by spatial autocorrelation or variability in the training biopsies instead of held-out accuracy, except that fine-tuned STPath was more accurate for the 100 most variable genes (Supplementary Tables S4-S6). DeepSpot-M Kidney performed as well as the group-specific models overall and better on AKI biopsies (Supplementary Figure S2a), and accuracy averaged over all genes improved in 168 of 171 held-out biopsies (Supplementary Figure S3). Furthermore, each doubling of training biopsies increased the gain in accuracy over DeepSpot-M (zero-shot) by 10 (top-100 genes) to 21 percentage points (all genes), without saturation at 147 biopsies (Supplementary Figure S4).

Across renal compartments and tissue programs, accuracy was highest for epithelial and fibrosis-associated programs, including proximal tubule, podocyte, vessel, and fibroblast programs (mean 𝑟, 0.36-0.55), followed by injury and repair (0.30) and immune programs (0.19-0.21; Figure 2b; per-gene accuracy in Supplementary Table S7). When held-out spots were assigned to their highest-scoring program, canonical cell type markers outside the program gene sets peaked in their own compartment for 33 of 41 genes in virtual and 36 in measured expression (Supplementary Figure S5). Spatial maps of MMP7 in AKI, PCK1 in CKD and reference tissue, and FBP1 in DM-R agreed with measured Visium patterns (𝑟 = 0.68-0.83; Figure 2c).

Disease-group differences and kidney function associations in virtual profiles

In measured pseudo-bulk expression, prespecified injury and repair markers and the fibroblast program score were higher in AKI and CKD than in DM-R biopsies, whereas gluconeogenic genes were lower, except PC in AKI (Figure 2d). Virtual profiles from DeepSpot-M Kidney preserved the direction of all 13 measured differences of at least 0.5 SD (filled points in Figure 2d), and virtual and measured differences were strongly correlated (𝑟 = 0.97, compared with 0.80 for DeepSpot-M (zero-shot)). DeepSpot-M Kidney also reproduced their size (median ratio of virtual to measured, 0.93), whereas the group-specific models, each applied to biopsies of its own enrollment group, enlarged them (1.54; Supplementary Figure S2b). Therefore, a single model trained across disease groups derives group differences from the tissue alone and can be applied to biopsies of unknown diagnosis, as in the atlas. Deconvolution with the KPMP single-cell atlas 2 showed lower proximal tubule and higher adaptive proximal tubule and fibroblast fractions in AKI and CKD than in DM-R biopsies in both measured and virtual profiles (Supplementary Figure S6). Among participants with CKD, DM-R, or reference tissue, the virtual fibroblast program score correlated with eGFR similarly to the measured score (Spearman 𝜌, -0.51 [95% CI, -0.65 to -0.35] versus -0.49 [95% CI, -0.63 to -0.32]; Supplementary Figure S7). Overall, virtual profiles reproduced all 11 program-eGFR associations whose measured CI excluded zero and all eight such differences between diabetic and hypertensive kidney disease. In contrast, the three differences between acute interstitial nephritis and acute tubular injury kept their direction without meeting this criterion.

Virtual molecular programs in experimental nephrotoxicity

To relate virtual programs to the site and severity of injury of known origin, which human biopsies rarely provide, we applied DeepSpot-M Kidney without rat-specific training to archived kidney sections of 567 rats that had received nephrotoxic compounds under controlled conditions, from the public Open TG-GATEs database (Figure 1d). In rats given cisplatin, virtual injury exceeded that of time-matched controls in the cortex and outer medulla, which contain the proximal straight tubules that cisplatin injures, 44 but not in the inner medulla (P < 0.001, P = 0.006, and P = 0.17), and was higher in the outer medulla, the region of expected injury, than in the other two regions (P = 0.02; FDR, 0.051). Likewise, injury after 2-bromoethylamine was higher in its region of expected injury, the inner medulla (P = 0.04; FDR, 0.054), 45 whereas proximal tubule loss after gentamicin was not higher in the cortex (P = 0.33; FDR, 0.33; Figure 3a and b). 46 Scores in the region of expected injury increased with the duration of daily dosing for cisplatin injury and for proximal tubule loss after puromycin aminonucleoside and gentamicin (Spearman 𝜌, 0.62, 0.86, and 0.67), whereas after 4 days they did not exceed those of matched controls (Figure 3c; Supplementary Figure S8).

Virtual scores were also associated with independently assessed kidney injury, as the wholesection injury score increased with lesion grade and distinguished moderate or severe lesions from no lesion (AUC, 0.85) and at least twofold BUN elevation relative to matched controls (AUC, 0.82; Figure 3d and e). In animals with lesions, the virtual injury program correlated with the injury program measured by expression arrays in separate kidney fragments (Spearman 𝜌, 0.55 [95% CI, 0.36 to 0.69]), whereas the proximal tubule loss program did not (𝜌, 0.24 [95% CI, -0.05 to 0.50]), and neither program correlated in animals without lesions (Supplementary Figure S9).

Tissue niches, clinicopathologic associations, and Xenium validation of the virtual atlas

The atlas comprised 5.46 million virtual spots with expression profiles of 19 338 genes (Figure 4a; Supplementary Figure S10) and was linked to baseline eGFR in 440 participants and to pathologist descriptors in 255. To our knowledge, it is the largest histology-derived virtual spatial transcriptomics atlas of non-neoplastic human kidney disease reported to date.

Clustering of virtual expression from 583 FFPE slides of one scanner model identified seven tissue niches and one image-quality niche (Figure 4b and c; Supplementary Figure S11a). Their top virtual marker genes matched the histology, for example, SLC5A12 and SLC4A4 in proximal tubule and MS4A1 in lymphoid aggregates resembling tertiary lymphoid structures (Figure 4c). Across 102 participants, proximal tubule regions decreased whereas injured tubule, fibrotic, and lymphoid aggregate regions increased with interstitial fibrosis (Spearman 𝜌, -0.63, 0.47, 0.71, and 0.58; Supplementary Figure S11b).

At the participant level (Figure 4d), higher proximal tubule program scores were associated with higher eGFR (partial 𝑟, 0.32), less interstitial fibrosis (-0.57), and less tubular atrophy (-0.56). In the 231 FFPE participants with interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy scores, the association with eGFR persisted after additional adjustment for both and for diagnosis (partial 𝑟, 0.18). In cross-validation, adding the virtual program scores to demographic and technical covariates, interstitial fibrosis, and tubular atrophy increased the explained variance of eGFR from 0.35 to 0.41 (Supplementary Table S8). Overall, 64 of 72 program-phenotype associations reached FDR < 0.05 in FFPE tissue, and 54 of these had the same direction in frozen sections from the same participants.

Atlas program-pathology associations agreed with those derived from measured Visium profiles of independent participants across 864 descriptor-program pairs (Spearman 𝜌, 0.64), and strongly for interstitial fibrosis, tubular atrophy, and mononuclear infiltrate (𝜌, 0.82-0.87; Figure 4e). Beyond predefined programs, exploratory gene-level analyses identified 1 534 genes associated with eGFR at FDR < 0.05 in both tissue preparations with the same direction, including the proximal tubule transporter genes SLC4A4 and SLC3A1, which were higher at higher eGFR, and the extracellular matrix genes COL14A1 and OGN, which were lower (Supplementary Figure S11c).

In the 22 participants with both a Xenium section and niche-annotated FFPE slides, atlas niche shares followed the measured Xenium cell shares (Spearman 𝜌, 0.65 for the proximal tubule niches and 0.59 for thick ascending limb and collecting duct; Figure 4f; Supplementary Figure S12a and b).

On the Xenium sections themselves, the share of spots in the virtual injured tubule niche followed the measured share of injured proximal tubule cells (Spearman 𝜌, 0.53; Supplementary Figure S12c and d).

Finally, after adaptation of DeepSpot-M Kidney to the remaining panel genes in single cells of all 49 KPMP Xenium sections, the 233 withheld panel genes agreed more closely with their measured expression (median Pearson 𝑟 per section, 0.11 before and 0.21 after adaptation), with improvement in all 34 sections from participants outside the Visium training (P < 0.001; Figure 4g) and gains in every enrollment group, on both Xenium panels, and in the 56 µm spots of the atlas (Supplementary Table S9). Virtual expression of 19 338 genes for the cells of all 49 Xenium sections, extending their 480-gene panels, is provided as a resource (Data Sharing Statement).

Discussion

In this study, kidney adaptation of DeepSpot-M yielded virtual molecular profiles from standard histology that reproduced nephron compartments, disease-group differences, and associations with kidney function in held-out participants. Beyond the paired cohort, virtual programs tracked graded injury and BUN elevation in rats under controlled nephrotoxic exposures, which retrospective biopsy collections cannot provide, and broadly reproduced the clinicopathologic relationships of measured spatial transcriptomics across 476 independent participants. Recent histology-based methods predict targeted gene panels at single-cell resolution, 47, 48 localize bulk RNA expression within healthy tissues, 49 or predict expression across organs without retraining, 25, 26 and were developed predominantly on cancer or healthy tissue. In contrast, DeepSpot-M Kidney was adapted to diseased kidney tissue and remained more accurate than STPath and SQUALL, also after their fine-tuning on the same training biopsies. Moreover, most of these methods, like earlier predictors trained on single cohorts, 20, 22, 50 predict a fixed set of genes represented during training, whereas DeepSpot-M queries each gene by an embedding of its protein sequence. 24 This flexibility allowed the kidneyadapted model to extend Xenium panels to unmeasured genes and to profile rat kidneys without species-specific training or mapping to human orthologs, so that virtual spatial transcriptomics also applies to preclinical toxicology studies. Furthermore, accuracy was still increasing at 147 training biopsies, indicating that larger paired cohorts could further improve virtual spatial transcriptomics of the kidney.

The central finding is an axis of proximal tubular differentiation, along which virtual profiles reproduced lower gluconeogenic enzyme expression and higher injury and maladaptive repair markers in AKI and CKD than in DM-R. Across the atlas, higher proximal tubule program scores accompanied higher eGFR and less interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy. Together, these findings link the loss of differentiated proximal tubular expression, including gluconeogenic enzymes, to tubular injury, fibrosis, and lower kidney function. 4, 10, 51 Studies of urinary EGF and renal gluconeogenesis described differentiated tubular expression as a marker of tubular health, 10, 35 and here this axis was read from routine histology. In parallel, injured tubule, fibrotic, and lymphoid aggregate niches expanded with interstitial fibrosis.

The main translational opportunity lies in existing biopsy archives, in which H&E slides are linked to eGFR trajectories, kidney failure, and death. Measured spatial transcriptomics is impractical at this scale, whereas virtual profiling needs only digitized archival H&E slides. Such archives would allow testing whether the molecular state of the tissue predicts outcomes beyond interstitial fibrosis, tubular atrophy, and eGFR, 35 and searching for molecular subgroups within morphologically defined diagnoses, such as diabetic kidney disease. 38 Moreover, virtual proximal tubule scores were associated with eGFR beyond interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy, suggesting that virtual profiles might capture a loss of differentiated tubular expression in tissue that still appears preserved, which matters when a needle biopsy samples relatively preserved cortex. Diagnostic applications, such as complementing chronicity scores or the automated Banff lesion scores now emerging in transplant pathology, 52 remain speculative and will require prospective validation in several centers. This study has several limitations, most notably the variable accuracy of virtual profiles across genes and programs. Immune and injury programs were reproduced least accurately, particularly genes that Visium rarely detected, such as HAVCR1 (Figure 2b; Supplementary Table S7), so the inflammatory findings above are supportive rather than definitive. Deconvolution also indicated that virtual profiles underestimate adaptive tubular states in AKI (Supplementary Figure S6), and because the measured fractions are themselves estimates, we interpret virtual composition only in relative terms. Moreover, virtual profiles are derived from the same histology that pathologists grade, so part of their agreement with histopathologic descriptors is expected. In contrast, kidney function and, in rats, BUN and tissue expression provide independent evidence. Each spot also combines tissue composition with cellular expression, so a lower program score cannot distinguish the loss of a cell population from altered expression in surviving cells. Single-cell virtual profiling may help separate the two. 23 Furthermore, both the paired cohort and the atlas come from a single consortium that excludes glomerular diseases and transplants and relies on needle biopsies with little medulla, so the findings may not transfer to other institutions, diagnoses, or medullary programs. Rodent models reflect human kidney disease only partially, 53 all rats were male, and small dose groups and arrays from separate kidney fragments restrict the rat results to validation under controlled exposure rather than prediction of human drug toxicity. In the atlas, FFPE tissue was validated against Xenium through niche shares and withheld panel genes rather than against Visium (Figure 4f and g), the associations are cross-sectional, and the gene-level eGFR associations remain exploratory. Therefore, cross-site molecular validation and longitudinal studies are needed before use beyond exploratory tissue characterization.

In conclusion, kidney-adapted virtual spatial transcriptomics opens existing, clinically annotated biopsy archives to spatial molecular analysis, complementing measured spatial transcriptomics in the study of disease-associated tissue programs. Linked to adjudicated diagnoses, histopathology, and kidney function, the virtual atlas provides a resource for generating and testing hypotheses on the tissue basis of kidney disease, selecting patients for targeted molecular profiling, and integrating spatial molecular readouts into translational studies.
