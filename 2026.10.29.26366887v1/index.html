Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 31 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

Patterns are designed to capture:

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Impact and implications:

Preclinical studies have identified TMPRSS6 inhibition as a potential therapeutic strategy for metabolic liver disease, but its effects in humans and potential safety consequences remain uncertain. Using human genetic evidence, we show that genetically proxied TMPRSS6 inhibition is associated with lower hepatic iron content but not hepatic steatosis, while identifying increased coronary artery disease risk as a potential cardiovascular safety liability. These findings are relevant to researchers and drug developers pursuing TMPRSS6-directed therapies, as they suggest that clinical benefit may be more likely in patients with excess hepatic iron and highlight the importance of cardiovascular risk assessment and lipid monitoring during clinical development. As genetic proxies reflect lifelong perturbation rather than pharmacological treatment, prospective clinical studies are required to determine whether these hepatic and cardiovascular effects are reproduced with therapeutic TMPRSS6 inhibition.

Introduction

Liver disease accounts for over two million deaths annually, representing 4% of all deaths worldwide (1). Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is the most prevalent chronic liver disease globally, affecting an estimated 25-38% of adults with projections anticipating continued increases in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH)-related cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, and liver-related mortality through 2030 (2). Despite this burden, disease-modifying therapies that reliably halt or reverse steatohepatitis and fibrosis remain limited, highlighting the need to identify and validate novel therapeutic targets. TMPRSS6 (Transmembrane Protease Serine 6), also known as matriptase-2, is a type II membrane-bound serine protease predominantly expressed in hepatocytes, that plays a critical role in systemic iron homeostasis (3, 4). TMPRSS6 cleaves the hemojuvelin coreceptor, thereby attenuating bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) signaling and limiting hepcidin transcription (5). In preclinical MASLD models, hepatocyte-directed downregulation of Tmprss6 using a GalNAc-conjugated antisense approach reduced steatohepatitis and attenuated fibrosis (6). TMPRSS6 inhibition is currently in drug development pipelines for iron overload disorders, with early-phase clinical studies providing initial evidence of tolerability (7). However, whether the hepatic benefits observed in preclinical models translate to humans, and whether sustained TMPRSS6 inhibition may have unintended systemic consequences, remains uncertain.

Genetic studies have identified the TMPRSS6 locus, and specifically the missense variant rs855791, as a robust determinant of iron homeostasis, with genome-wide significant associations with serum iron, hemoglobin levels, transferrin saturation, and erythrocyte mean cell volume (8, 9). Subsequent large-scale genome-wide association studies (GWAS) using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-derived liver phenotypes have identified associations between variation at the TMPRSS6 locus and hepatic iron content, with fine-mapping implicating rs855791 as the likely causal variant (10, 11). These findings provide a genetic basis for investigating the consequences of reduced TMPRSS6 activity in humans. However, whether genetically proxied TMPRSS6 inhibition influences hepatic steatosis, fibroinflammatory activity, or clinical liver disease, and whether it carries broader cardiometabolic consequences, remains unclear.

Human genetics offers a powerful tool to prioritize therapeutic targets (12), but its application to some liver outcomes, such as hepatic fat, has been limited by challenges in accurately characterizing liver phenotypes (13, 14). Large biobank GWAS have relied on both MRIderived imaging phenotypes and diagnostic code-based case definitions, and the latter are susceptible to underdiagnosis and misclassification (13). MRI-derived liver phenotypes, including proton density fat fraction (PDFF) as a quantitative measure of hepatic steatosis, and corrected T1 (cT1) as a non-invasive marker of fibroinflammatory activity, provide complementary quantitative measures of hepatic pathology (15) (16) (17). Leveraging these phenotypes alongside genetic approaches, such as Mendelian randomization (MR), enables . the mechanistic evaluation of the causal pathways underlying hepatic disease and the identification of robust therapeutic targets.

MR is a powerful method that enables the identification of causal effects in humans (18). MR leverages the naturally randomized allocation of genetic variants among the population as instrumental variables, analogous to treatment allocation in a randomized controlled trial. Under certain assumptions, this approach estimates the causal effects of exposures on outcomes, overcoming the limitations of traditional observational studies such as residual confounding and reverse causality (18).

Here, we aimed to evaluate the potential hepatic benefits and systemic safety implications of TMPRSS6 inhibition using human genetic evidence. We leveraged a functional genetic variant as a proxy for reduced TMPRSS6 activity to investigate its associations with MRIderived hepatic phenotypes, clinical liver disease, and cardiometabolic outcomes. We further integrated biomarker-wide, proteome-wide, and phenome-wide analyses to characterize potential downstream mechanisms and safety liabilities.

Materials and methods

Study overview

The study design is summarized in Fig. 1. We first identified a biologically plausible genetic instrument to proxy reduced TMPRSS6 activity. Using a two-sample Mendelian randomization framework, we then assessed the associations of genetically proxied TMPRSS6 inhibition with primary hepatic outcomes and secondary cardiometabolic outcomes. To characterize the broader systemic effects of genetically proxied TMPRSS6 inhibition, we performed a phenome-wide scan to identify potential secondary indications and safety liabilities, and biomarker-wide and proteome-wide scans to explore downstream biological pathways and potential markers of target engagement. All analyses were conducted and reported in accordance with the STROBE-MR guidelines (19).

Instrumental variable selection

To proxy the pharmacological inhibition of TMPRSS6, we used rs855791 (p.Ala736Val), a common functional missense variant in TMPRSS6 that reduces matriptase-2 serine protease activity, weighted by its effect on circulating hepcidin levels (4). Reduced matriptase-2 activity attenuates suppression of the BMP-SMAD signalling, resulting in increased hepcidin expression. The hepcidin-increasing allele was therefore used to approximate the direction of the downstream biological effects expected from pharmacological TMPRSS6 inhibition.

Outcome variables and data sources

Details on the GWAS data sources used in our analyses are shown in Table 1. GWAS selection was primarily based on sample size and the availability of relevant phenotypes. When available, we used data on associations estimated in European ancestry populations rather than multi-ancestry populations to minimize population stratification.

We considered serum iron levels (20) as a positive control outcome, to assess whether the selected variant captured the expected downstream effects of TMPRSS6 perturbation on systemic iron homeostasis. As primary outcomes, we investigated markers of hepatic disease, including liver iron (21), cT1 (21), PDFF (21), liver volume (22), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT).

To complement the quantitative MRI phenotypes, we additionally considered clinically defined nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) (23), cirrhosis (24) and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) (25). These clinical liver outcomes were analyzed as a separate outcome group.

As secondary outcomes, we considered cardiometabolic traits and disease endpoints, including body mass index (BMI) (26), waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) (26), visceral adipose tissue (VAT) (22), abdominal subcutaneous adipose tissue (ASAT) (22), type 2 diabetes (T2D) (27), coronary artery disease (CAD) (28), aortic stenosis (29), any ischemic stroke (30), heart failure (31) and atrial fibrillation (32).

Phenome-wide analyses included 2,272 disease outcomes derived from FinnGen release 9 (24). Proteome-wide analysis included relative concentrations of 2,940 plasma proteins, measured using the Olink platform (33). Biomarker-wide analysis included a range of hepatic, renal, hematological biomarkers described in detail in Table 1.

Tissue-specific TMPRSS6 expression

Informed by the outcome-level MR results, we next examined whether the observed disease associations were accompanied by evidence of shared genetic signals with tissue-specific TMPRSS6 expression, using expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) data from the Genotype Tissue Expression project (GTEx v7), which profiles gene expression across 48 human tissues. We included biologically relevant tissues, including liver and arterial tissues (coronary, tibial, and aortic arteries).

Statistical Analysis

Mendelian randomization analyses

Mendelian randomization rests on three assumptions: the selected genetic instruments must be strongly associated with the exposure, be independent of confounders of the exposureoutcome relationship, and influence the outcome only through the exposure of interest (34). MR effect estimates were obtained using the Wald ratio estimator. To reflect TMPRSS6 inhibition, MR estimates reflect the change in outcome per standard deviation (SD) increase in genetically proxied circulating hepcidin levels. MR estimates are expressed as beta coefficients with 95% confidence intervals for continuous outcomes and odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals for binary outcomes. To account for multiple testing, we applied Bonferroni correction separately for primary outcomes (P < 0.007, 7 outcomes), secondary outcomes (P < 0.0045, 10 outcomes), and the biomarker-wide analysis (P < 0.0024, 21 outcomes). For hypothesis-generating analyses (proteome-wide and phenomewide scans), we used a false-discovery rate (FDR) approach: associations with q < 0.05 were . considered significant, and those with unadjusted P < 0.05 but q ≥ 0.05 were considered suggestive associations (35).

Colocalization analyses

For primary, secondary, biomarker-wide, or expression outcome where MR analysis indicated a nominally significant result, we performed colocalization as a sensitivity analysis to verify that the two traits were driven by the same variant and shared a common biological mechanism. (36). Colocalization was tested across the TMPRSS6 gene region (chr22:37,462,936-37,505,753 ± 100 kb, GRCh37) using all SNPs available in both datasets for a given pair. This approach estimates posterior probabilities (PP) across five competing hypotheses: H0 (no causal variants in the region), H1 (a causal variant for trait 1 only), H2 (a causal variant for trait 2 only), H3 (separate causal variants for each trait), and H4 (a single shared causal variant for both traits, consistent with a true causal relationship). The prior probability of any given SNP being causal for a single trait within a region was set to 10⁻⁴. Evidence for colocalization was classified based on PP.H4 as follows: strong (PP.H4 ≥ 0.80), moderate (0.50 ≤ PP.H4 < 0.80), weak (0.20 ≤ PP.H4 < 0.50), or no colocalization (PP.H4 < 0.20). We additionally computed conditional colocalization [PP.H4/(PP.H4+PP.H4)] to quantify the relative support for a shared versus distinct causal variant, conditioned on at least one causal variant being present for each trait in the region.

Mediation analysis

To explore pathways that might underlie the associations between genetically proxied TMPRSS6 inhibition and clinical endpoints identified in primary and secondary analyses, we conducted a two-step (network) MR mediation analysis. Candidate mediators were biomarkers that met two criteria. First, they were associated with genetically proxied TMPRSS6 inhibition in the biomarker-wide MR. Second, they showed at least suggestive evidence of a shared regional genetic signal with the relevant clinical endpoint at the TMPRSS6 locus in colocalization analyses. The effect of genetically proxied TMPRSS6 inhibition on each mediator (β1) and on each clinical endpoint (total effect, βTE) was obtained from the primary Wald ratio analyses. We estimated the effect of each mediator on the clinical endpoint (β2) using univariable MR with genome-wide instruments for the mediator. Instruments were variants associated with the mediator at P < 5 × 10⁻⁸, clumped at r² < 0.001 within a 10,000 kb window. We excluded variants within ±1 Mb of TMPRSS6, to reduce potential overlap with the target-specific genetic signal used to estimate the exposuremediator and exposure-outcome associations. The inverse-variance weighted method was the primary estimator. The indirect effect through each mediator was calculated as β1 × β2, and the proportion mediated as β1 × β2 / βTE. The proportion mediated was calculated on the linear scale for continuous outcomes and the log-odds scale for binary outcomes. The 95% confidence intervals were derived using the delta method. Given the assumptions underlying two-step MR mediation and the use of a single variant to proxy TMPRSS6 inhibition, these analyses were considered exploratory.

Pathway enrichment analysis.

To identify biological pathways enriched among proteins associated with genetically proxied hepcidin, we performed over-representation analysis using clusterProfiler (v4) (37). Proteins reaching FDR significance (q < 0.05) in the proteome-wide MR analysis were tested against Gene Ontology (Biological Process), KEGG, and Reactome pathway databases. The background set was restricted to all 2,940 proteins measured on the Olink panel, rather than the whole genome, to account for the platform's non-random protein coverage. Enrichment pvalues were calculated using a hypergeometric test and corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg method; pathways with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Software

All analyses were performed in R version 4.3.2. Mendelian randomization analyses were performed using the 'MendelianRandomization' package, and colocalization analyses were performed using the 'coloc' package. Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using clusterProfiler, with gene annotation from org.Hs.eg.db and pathway databases queried via KEGG and Reactome (ReactomePA, reactome.db).

Results

The TMPRSS6 rs855791 A allele reduces matriptase-2 activity, thereby attenuating suppression of hepcidin expression (β = 0.042, SE = 0.005, P = 1.34 × 10⁻¹⁷; effect allele frequency = 0.436). The selected genetic instrument demonstrated a strong association with circulating hepcidin, with an F statistic of 72. MR estimates for primary and secondary outcomes are shown in Fig. 2. As a positive control outcome, serum iron was strongly inversely associated with genetically proxied TMPRSS6 inhibition (β= -3.65, 95% CI -3.86 to -3.44, P<0.001), consistent with the expected effects of reduced TMPRSS6 activity on iron homeostasis.

Among the primary hepatic outcomes, genetically proxied TMPRSS6 inhibition was associated with lower liver iron content (β= -2.50, 95% CI -2.84 to -2.16, P<0.001) and higher cT1 (β=1.59, 95% CI 1.22 to 1.96, P<0.001). There was no evidence of an association with PDFF (β= -0.24, 95% CI -0.57 to 0.09, P=0.15) or liver volume (β= -0.03, 95% CI -0.36 to 0.30, P=0.86). Among hepatic enzymes, genetically proxied TMPRSS6 inhibition was associated with lower ALP levels (β=-0.02, 95% CI -0.03 to -0.01, P<0.001), but there was no evidence of an association with ALT (β=-0.01, 95% CI -0.03 to 0.01, P=0.28) or GGT (β=0.01, 95% CI= -0.02 to 0.03, P=0.68).

For clinically defined liver outcomes (Supplementary Fig. 1), genetically proxied TMPRSS6 inhibition was not associated with NAFLD (OR=1.07, 95% CI 0.78 to 1.46, P=0.69), cirrhosis (OR=0.72, 95% CI 0.15 to 3.42, P=0.68) or hepatocellular carcinoma (OR=0.09, 95% CI 0.00 to 1.96, P=0.12). However, the wide confidence intervals for cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma, precluded definitive conclusions regarding potential protective effects.

For secondary outcomes, there was suggestive evidence of an association between genetically proxied TMPRSS6 inhibition and lower ASAT (β=-0.41, 95% CI= -0.75 to -0.06, P=0.02). There was no evidence of an association with VAT (β=-0.09, 95% CI= -0.41 to 0.22, P=0.56) nor type 2 diabetes (OR=0.88, 95% CI= 0.70 to 1.11, P=0.29). Genetically proxied TMPRSS6 inhibition was associated with higher risk of several atherosclerotic cardiovascular outcomes, including CAD (OR=1.59, 95% CI= 1.26 to 2.01, P<0.001) and aortic stenosis (OR=1.42, 95% CI= 1.09 to 1.85, P=0.009), with comparable magnitudes of effect. No associations meeting the multiple-testing threshold were observed for ischemic stroke, heart failure or atrial fibrillation.

Biomarker-wide Mendelian randomization

Biomarker-wide MR estimates are shown in Fig 3. Among glucose metabolism traits, genetically proxied TMPRSS6 inhibition was associated with higher HbA1c (β = 0.45, 95% CI 0.39 to 0.51; P = 2.1 × 10⁻⁴⁷), while fasting glucose and fasting insulin showed no association. Among lipid traits, genetically proxied TMPRSS6 inhibition was associated with higher LDL cholesterol (β = 0.22, 95% CI 0.16 to 0.29; P = 2.0 × 10⁻¹¹) and lower triglycerides (β = -0.17, 95% CI -0.24 to -0.11; P = 1.5 × 10⁻⁷). Systolic blood pressure was nominally associated with genetically proxied TMPRSS6 inhibition (β = 1.75 mmHg, 95% CI 0.61 to 2.89; P = 0.003), but did not meet the prespecified Bonferroni threshold (P < 0.0024). No association was observed with diastolic blood pressure diastolic blood pressure (β = -0.52 mmHg, 95% CI -1.24 to 0.19; P = 0.150).

Among renal markers, genetically proxied TMPRSS6 inhibition was associated with lower blood urea nitrogen (β = -0.06, 95% CI -0.09 to -0.02; P = 0.001) and higher eGFR estimated using creatinine (β = 0.08, 95% CI 0.03 to 0.14; P = 0.002). eGFR estimated using cystatin C (β = 0.03, 95% CI 0.00 to 0.05; P = 0.028) reached nominal but not Bonferroni significance, while urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio showed no association (P = 0.179).

Among inflammatory biomarkers, genetically proxied TMPRSS6 inhibition was associated with lower C-reactive protein (β = -0.17, 95% CI -0.26 to -0.08; P = 3.9 × 10⁻⁴).

Among haematological traits, genetically proxied TMPRSS6 inhibition was associated with lower neutrophil count (β = -0.23, 95% CI -0.33 to -0.14; P = 9.5 × 10⁻⁷), monocyte count (β = -0.20, 95% CI -0.30 to -0.11; P = 9.4 × 10⁻⁶), and white blood cell count (β = -0.21, 95% CI -0.30 to -0.12; P = 3.5 × 10⁻⁶), and with higher red blood cell count (β = 0.50, 95% CI 0.41 to 0.59; P = 2.1 × 10⁻²⁷) and platelet count (β = 0.67, 95% CI 0.58 to 0.76; P = 4.2 × 10⁻⁴⁹). Eosinophil, lymphocyte, and basophil counts showed no significant association.

Colocalization and mechanistic characterization

Cross-trait colocalization results are shown for primary, secondary, and biomarker-wide traits in Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 3. Colocalization analysis between liver iron content and cT1 at the TMPRSS6 gene region yielded a PP.H4=99.7%, indicating strong evidence that the associations with liver iron and cT1 share a causal genetic signal.

Colocalization analysis also supported shared regional genetic signals involving hepcidin, HbA1c, platelet count, WBC, neutrophil count, and monocyte count, with PP.H4 ≥ 0.99 for all pairwise combinations examined. CAD risk showed moderate evidence of colocalization with circulating hepcidin at the TMPRSS6 locus (PP.H4 = 0.56; conditional PP = 0.85), consistent with, but not establishing, a shared causal genetic signal (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Table 3). Despite comparable effect sizes and directions in the MR analysis, CAD and calcific aortic stenosis did not show evidence of colocalization (PP.H4 = 0.01; PP.H3 <0.01) (Fig. 4B, Supplementary Table 3).

CAD risk also showed weak-to-moderate support for colocalization with HbA1c, platelet count, and LDL-c (PP.H4 = 0.48-0.57), but showed limited support for colocalization with CRP (PP.H4 <0.01) or systolic blood pressure (PP.H4 <0.01). Total cholesterol and LDL-c showed strong evidence of colocalization with hepcidin and HbA1c (PP.H4 = 0.996-0.999), supporting shared regional genetic signals across iron-related, glycaemic, and lipid traits.

We next tested whether CAD risk could be explained by tissue-specific TMPRSS6 expression, using cis-eQTLs from three arterial tissues (aorta, coronary artery, and tibial artery). No evidence of colocalization was observed (all PP.H4 < 0.001; Supplementary Table . 3a ).

However, none of the artery eQTL datasets contained genome-wide significant cis-eQTLs at the TMPRSS6 locus.

Two-step mediation analysis

We included LDL-C and platelet count in the mediation analysis because both biomarkers were associated with genetically proxied TMPRSS6 inhibition and showed at least weak to moderate evidence of colocalization with CAD at the TMPRSS6 locus. Higher genetically predicted LDL-C was associated with greater CAD risk (OR 1.55 per SD, 95% CI 1.46 to 1.64, P = 6.4 × 10⁻⁴⁶), whereas genetically predicted platelet count was not (OR 0.99 per SD, 95% CI 0.95 to 1.02, P = 0.53) (Fig. 5). Results were consistent across the weighted median and MR-Egger estimators, with no evidence of directional pleiotropy (MR-Egger intercept P = 0.23 for LDL-C and P = 0.55 for platelet count). In exploratory two-step MR mediation analysis, LDL-C accounted for an estimated 21.2% (95% CI 8.5%-33.9%) of the association between genetically proxied TMPRSS6 inhibition and CAD. In contrast, there was no evidence of mediation through platelet count (-1.7%, 95% CI -6.9% to 3.6%). These estimates should be interpreted in the context of the assumptions underlying genetic mediation analysis.

Proteome-wide Mendelian randomization

Of the 2,940 tested plasma proteins, 30 were associated with genetically predicted TMPRSS6 inhibition (Fig 6, Supplementary Table 4). The strongest associations were with irontransport and erythropoiesis-related proteins: TFRC (β = 3.27; P = 9.9 × 10 -134 ), AHSP (β = -1.82; P = 6.6 × 10 -39 ), EPO (β = 1.82; P = 3.5 × 10 -35 ), MELTF (β = -1.43; P = 7.0 × 10 -28 ), and CSF2RB (β = -1.35; P = 1.7 × 10⁻¹⁹). Several heme-biosynthesis proteins were also associated, including CPOX (β = -1.46; P = 1.8 × 10⁻²²), HMBS (β = -0.97; P = 7.7 × 10⁻¹⁴), and ARG1 (β = 0.55; P = 1.5 × 10⁻¹⁰). Directionally, proteins involved in iron acquisition and transport (TFRC, EPO, TF) increased with genetically proxied TMPRSS6 inhibition, while proteins involved in heme synthesis (HMBS, CPOX, ARG1) decreased, consistent with hepcidin-mediated restriction of iron availability for erythropoiesis.

Over-representation analysis (GO, KEGG, Reactome; background restricted to the Olink panel) showed that the 30 FDR-significant proteins clustered in three related processes: iron transport (MELTF, TF, TFRC), heme/porphyrin metabolism (CPOX, HMBS, HMOX1), and erythrocyte/myeloid homeostasis (AHSP, EPO, HBQ1, HBZ, HMOX1), with additional enrichment for HIF-1 signaling and ferroptosis (Supplementary Table 5). This is consistent with a proteomic signature centered on iron acquisition, heme synthesis, and erythropoiesis.

Phenome-wide Mendelian randomization

Genetically proxied TMPRSS6 inhibition was not associated with any of the 2,272 PheWAS endpoints at the FDR-significant threshold (Supplementary Table 6). The strongest signal was observed for anemia-related phenotypes, consistent with the established role of TMPRSS6 in iron homeostasis and validating genetic instrument selection.

Discussion

In this large-scale Mendelian randomization study, we leveraged human genetic variation at the TMPRSS6 locus to investigate the potential hepatic benefits and systemic safety implications of reduced TMPRSS6 activity across hepatic and cardiometabolic outcomes. Our analyses yield three principal findings with potential implications for therapeutic development. First, genetically proxied TMPRSS6 inhibition was associated with lower liver iron content but paradoxically higher cT1, with no clear association with PDFF or liver volume, suggesting that hepatic consequences of TMPRSS6 inhibition are specific to iron handling rather than broad metabolic perturbation. Second, genetically proxied TMPRSS6 inhibition was associated with increased risk of coronary artery disease (OR = 1.59), alongside a nominal association with aortic stenosis (OR = 1.42). Third, genetically proxied TMPRSS6 inhibition was associated with higher LDL-C, which exploratory mediation analysis identified as a potential contributor to the CAD association.

Hepatic effects: iron restriction without evidence of steatosis reduction

The genetic evidence provided by our main analysis suggests that TMPRSS6 inhibition reduces liver iron content, consistent with its established mechanism: impaired matriptase-2 serine protease activity elevates hepcidin, which promotes ferroportin internalization and degradation on enterocytes and reticuloendothelial macrophages, restricting dietary iron absorption and recycling (3, 38). Alongside the reduction in liver iron content, genetically proxied TMPRSS6 inhibition was associated with higher cT1, a quantitative multiparametric MRI marker used as a non-invasive index of hepatic fibroinflammatory activity (21). These associations were directionally opposite, yet colocalization analysis provided strong evidence that the associations share a causal genetic signal at the TMPRSS6 locus. This divergence in effects may reflect the influence of hepatic iron on the cT1 measurement itself rather than a true increase in fibroinflammatory activity. Iron is paramagnetic and shortens tissue T1 relaxation time; consequently, iron accumulation can suppress measured T1 values, whereas a reduction in hepatic iron may increase cT1 through removal of this T1-shortening effect (39). Thus, the higher cT1 associated with genetically proxied TMPRSS6 inhibition may, at least in part, represent an imaging consequence of reduced hepatic iron. In our complementary analyses examining clinically defined liver outcomes as orthogonal endpoints, genetically proxied TMPRSS6 inhibition was not significantly associated with NAFLD, cirrhosis or hepatocellular carcinoma. If the higher cT1 reflected a true increase in fibroinflammatory activity, a concordant increase in the risk of advanced liver disease would have been expected. The point estimates for cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma were in the direction of lower risk, opposite to that implied by the cT1 association. This is more consistent with the cT1 association being, at least in part, an imaging consequence of reduced hepatic iron. Nevertheless, these estimates were imprecise and cannot exclude clinically relevant effects in either direction.

Notably, no association was observed with hepatic steatosis as measured by PDFF, NAFLD nor with liver volume. This contrasts with the preclinical findings of Pettinato et al., in which GalNAc-conjugated antisense oligonucleotide-mediated Tmprss6 knockdown reduced both steatohepatitis and fibrosis in murine models of MASLD (6). Several factors may account for this apparent discrepancy. The anti-steatotic effects observed in preclinical models may not translate directly to humans, may depend on disease context or baseline iron burden, or may require a degree of target inhibition and consequent iron restriction that is not adequately recapitulated by lifelong exposure to common genetic variants. The absence of a detectable association with PDFF in the present study does not exclude an effect of pharmacological TMPRSS6 inhibition on hepatic steatosis. This is consistent with prior evidence from human genetic studies showing that the TMPRSS6 p.Ala736Val variant independently associates with hepatic iron accumulation in NAFLD, with the higher-hepcidin allele conferring lower hepatic iron stores (40), and with transcriptomic data demonstrating upregulated hepatic TMPRSS6 expression in NASH, suggesting a reactive counter-regulatory response to hepatic inflammation (41).

Cardiovascular safety and potential mechanisms

A key finding of this study was the association between genetically proxied TMPRSS6 inhibition and increased risk of CAD. This observation is particularly relevant given the complex and historically debated relationship between systemic iron status and cardiovascular disease. Epidemiological studies have reported associations between circulating iron biomarkers and coronary outcomes (42), while human genetic studies have provided evidence that perturbation of systemic iron status may influence atherosclerotic cardiovascular risk (43-45). Our findings extend this literature by suggesting that genetically mediated perturbation of the TMPRSS6-hepcidin axis may have cardiovascular consequences that warrant consideration during therapeutic development.

Hepcidin sits at the intersection of iron regulation and inflammatory signaling, with inflammatory pathways, particularly IL-6/JAK/STAT signaling, capable of increasing hepcidin expression independently of systemic iron stores (46). Conversely, changes in iron availability can influence oxidative stress, cellular metabolism, erythropoiesis and vascular biology. TMPRSS6 therefore operates within a regulatory network in which iron, inflammatory and cardiometabolic pathways intersect. However, our colocalization analyses provide little support for systemic inflammation as the principal explanation for the observed CAD association. Although genetically proxied TMPRSS6 inhibition was associated with lower CRP, the CRP signal did not colocalize with CAD at the TMPRSS6 locus. Similarly, systolic blood pressure showed only a nominal association in the biomarker-wide analysis and did not colocalize with CAD.

In contrast, lipid metabolism emerged as a potential pathway linking TMPRSS6 inhibition to coronary risk. Genetically proxied TMPRSS6 inhibition was associated with higher LDL-C, although evidence of a shared causal variant between LDL-C and CAD at the TMPRSS6 locus was moderate. Mediation analyses suggested that LDL-C may account for around one fifth of the CAD association, with uncertainty around the estimated mediated proportion. This finding should be regarded as hypothesis-generating, given the modest colocalization evidence and the reliance of the CAD estimate on a single variant. The remaining association may involve other pathways, although the mediation estimate does not establish their presence or relative contributions. Platelet count was also associated with TMPRSS6 inhibition and showed . moderate evidence of a shared signal with CAD, but genetically predicted platelet count was not associated with CAD, and we found no evidence of mediation. This suggests that the platelet signal reflects shared genetic architecture at the locus, rather than a causal pathway to CAD. The mechanisms underlying the CAD association therefore remain largely unexplained.

We additionally investigated whether the CAD association could reflect altered TMPRSS6 expression within the vasculature. No evidence of colocalization was observed between CAD and TMPRSS6 expression in aortic, coronary or tibial artery tissue. Interpretation of these analyses is limited, however, by the absence of genome-wide significant cis-eQTLs for TMPRSS6 in the available arterial datasets, which may reflect insufficient statistical power or low and context-dependent expression of TMPRSS6 in these tissues. Larger tissue-specific eQTL datasets and cell-type-resolved functional studies will be required to determine whether local vascular effects contribute to the observed association.

Systemic metabolic and haematological consequences of TMPRSS6 inhibition

The association with higher HbA1c, in the absence of corresponding effects on fasting glucose or insulin, may reflect alterations in erythrocyte biology rather than impaired glycemic control. HbA1c is influenced by erythrocyte lifespan and turnover independently of circulating glucose, an interpretation of particular relevance given the pronounced hematological effects of genetically proxied TMPRSS6 inhibition, including higher red blood cell and platelet counts. Similarly, the association with higher creatinine-based eGFR suggest potential effects on renal biomarkers, although these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of improved kidney function. Iron homeostasis is closely linked to renal physiology through the hepcidinferroportin axis, which is frequently dysregulated in chronic kidney disease (47), and systemic changes in iron availability may influence renal and hematological biomarkers through several pathways. The discordance between creatinine-and cystatin C-based eGFR associations further warrants caution in attributing these findings to changes in kidney function.

Clinical implications

These findings have potential implications for the clinical development of TMPRSS6-directed therapies. In trials targeting systemic iron overload, cardiovascular outcomes and lipid profiles should be evaluated alongside established pharmacodynamic measures of iron restriction. This consideration may be particularly important for potential liver indications, where treatment may be prolonged and patients with MASLD frequently have substantial underlying cardiometabolic risk. Lifelong genetically proxied perturbation is not equivalent to pharmacological inhibition over a defined treatment period, and our results therefore do not establish that therapeutic TMPRSS6 inhibition will increase cardiovascular events. Nevertheless, the convergence of the MR and colocalization findings identifies CAD as a potential on-target safety liability that warrants prospective investigation. Monitoring LDL-c and other atherogenic lipid measures during early-phase trials may help determine whether the genetic lipid signal is recapitulated pharmacologically and whether cardiovascular risk mitigation strategies are required during longer-term treatment.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several important strengths. The use of a functional missense variant in TMPRSS6 provides a biologically informed proxy for reduced matriptase-2 activity, strengthening attribution of the genetic perturbation to the therapeutic target. The use of quantitative MRI-derived liver phenotypes reduces phenotypic misclassification relative to diagnostic code-based definitions and enables mechanistic dissection of steatosis and fibro inflammation as separable endpoints. The breadth of the phenome-wide and proteome-wide screens provides a comprehensive pharmacological landscape that simultaneously informs therapeutic positioning and safety surveillance. Colocalization analyses allow us to assess whether regional associations are consistent with shared genetic signals rather than distinct variants in linkage disequilibrium.

Several limitations merit acknowledgment. As with all MR analyses, the exclusion restriction assumption, that genetic instruments act on outcomes exclusively through TMPRSS6 inhibition, cannot be formally verified, and pleiotropy cannot be entirely excluded. Our analysis relied on a single missense variant and the Wald ratio estimator. Although the functional annotation of rs855791 and its expected association with serum iron support biological relevance, they do not establish the exclusion restriction assumption. We used colocalization to help distinguish shared regional signals from linkage disequilibrium confounding, but we cannot exclude horizontal pleiotropy or demonstrate that all observed associations reproduce the effects of pharmacological TMPRSS6 inhibition. Analyses are conducted predominantly in individuals of European ancestry, which may limit the generalizability of findings to other populations. The genetic instruments capture lifelong, germline variation and may not fully recapitulate the pharmacodynamics of a potent pharmacological inhibitor, particularly with respect to dose magnitude and temporal dynamics.. Finally, the proteome-wide analyses require replication and mechanistic follow-up to distinguish robust downstream biological effects from associations that may reflect pleiotropy or other sources of bias.

Conclusions

This study provides human genetic evidence to support an association for genetically proxied TMPRSS6 inhibition with lower hepatic iron content, but does not provide clear evidence of reduced hepatic steatosis or clinical liver disease risk, despite encouraging preclinical findings. The accompanying increase in cT1 requires cautious interpretation in the context of altered iron homeostasis and does not establish worsening hepatic fibroinflammatory activity. The association with increased CAD risk identifies a potential cardiovascular safety signal, with LDL-C representing a candidate pathway requiring further investigation. Further clinical studies are needed to assess hepatic efficacy and cardiovascular safety, alongside prospective monitoring of iron, hematological and lipid parameters.
