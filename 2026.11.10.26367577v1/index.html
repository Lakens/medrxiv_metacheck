Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer related mortality, yet the role of lower airway microbiota in disease development and progression is incompletely defined.

We characterised the lung microbiome in an Australian cohort (lung cancer n=106, healthy n=10) using shotgun metagenomics and semi quantitative culture to evaluate microbial ecology across the tumour microenvironment (TME), contralateral airways, and oral cavity, to evaluate microbiome differences across early and late stage disease, and to quantify clinical covariate impacts on airway microbiota. Diversity analyses, multivariable linear modelling, and culture based comparisons were performed, with adjustment for smoking pack years, sex, and tumour characteristics.

Results: Cumulative smoking exposure caused significant, progressive Neisseria subflava, Gemella sanguinis, and Rothia mucilaginosa depletion, alongside Streptococcus mitis enrichment, with Mogibacterium diversum uniquely depleted within the TME of smokers. β diversity in both early-and latestage bronchial washes (BWs) significantly diverged from healthy controls, whilst α diversity was significantly reduced in early-stage vs. late-stage BWs. The early stage TME demonstrated significantly reduced microbial cultivability compared with healthy and other lung cancer BWs. A small number of taxa were uniquely enriched (Sporothrix and Pseudomonas species) or depleted (Streptococcus parasanguinis, Streptococcus cristatus, R. mucilaginosa) within the TME, and 13 taxa were significantly enriched or depleted in both TME and contralateral airways compared with healthy airways. Only adenocarcinoma showed distinct oral β diversity restructuring.

The lung cancer microbiome is characterised by early and progressive lower airway dysbiosis that is strongly shaped by tumour presence. Our findings point to potential new avenues for microbiome based diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer related mortality globally, and 1.8 million people are diagnosed every year 1 . Despite recent treatment advances, lung cancer remains an insidious disease with poor prognosis, with five-year survival rates of 27% (68% for Stage I and 3% for Stage IV disease) 2,3

Given its largely asymptomatic nature until late-stage disease, coupled with its high mortality rate and significant socioeconomic burden, earlier diagnosis, improved therapeutic interventions, and prevention are essential for improving outcomes.

There is increasing recognition of the role microbes play in oncogenesis, with current estimates suggesting single-pathogen infections are linked to 12-16% of all human cancers 4, 5 . However, major gaps remain in understanding the impact of microbial community changes (particularly microbiome dysbiosis) in lung cancer development, treatment response, and progression. Most lung cancer microbiome studies have used 16S rRNA variable-region amplicon sequencing (metataxonomics) to profile lung tissue and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), and to a lesser extent, sputum 6 . Whilst these studies have reported the extratumoural or intratumoural enrichment of taxa such as Veillonella, Streptococcus, and Acidovorax [7] [8] [9] [10] [11] , enormous variability in study design, participant inclusion/exclusion criteria, specimen type/s, geographic region, and disease stage has made establishment of a consensus link challenging. A recent meta-analysis of intratumoural 16S metataxonomic studies attempted to overcome this variability, identifying broad shifts such as increased Actinobacteria and Corynebacterium in tumour tissues 12.

Nevertheless, 16S rRNA metataxonomics remains inherently limited by poor species-level resolution, the exclusion of viruses and fungi, and high susceptibility to contaminant amplification in low-biomass environments.

Recent investigations have shifted towards shotgun metagenomics to more comprehensively profile lung cancer microbiota in tumour tissue 13, 14 or BALF [15] [16] [17] [18] [19] . However, like 16S sequencing, metagenomic profiling has revealed a high degree of inter-study variability, with inconsistent alpha (α) and beta (β) diversity findings. Furthermore, no single microbe has been found to be enriched or depleted in lung cancer airways (tumour tissues or BALF specimens) compared with control cohorts. Like 16S-based studies, the lack of consensus is unsurprising due to differences in control groups (healthy individuals, people with benign lung diseases, normal adjacent lung tissues), statistical methodologies, clinical covariates, specimen collection and processing workflows, bioinformatic approaches, handling of background contamination, and environmental/geographical factors, along with small sample sizes. To address some of these challenges, large-scale multicentre analyses have attempted to map tumourspecific microbiomes across various cancers, including lung cancer 20, 21 . One such study, by Nejman and colleagues, undertook 16S-based analysis of 1526 tumours across seven cancer types. Their analysis identified a significant enrichment of tobacco-degradation pathways in the lung tumours of smokers; however, due to the diverse and heterogeneous group of bacteria sharing these smoke-degrading metabolic capabilities, a uniform species-level signature was absent 20 . Concurrently, the recent retraction of a large, retrospective cancer microbiome study due to bioinformatic artefacts 22 underscores the challenges of low-biomass sequencing and highlights the critical need for rigorous negative controls to account for kitome, bronchoscope, and upper respiratory tract contamination.

Although prior findings highlight the clinical potential of lung cancer microbiomics, significant knowledge gaps remain. Specifically, the field has struggled to untangle the confounding effects of cumulative tobacco exposure from tumour-driven dysbiosis, often failing to isolate a true disease signature from the background smoking microbiome. Furthermore, longitudinal mapping across early-and late-stage disease, particularly distinguishing localised changes within the tumour microenvironment (TME) from broader, systemic airway shifts, remains poorly defined. Reliance on sequencing alone without functional or culture-based validation has also obscured true biomass and viability fluctuations. Finally, no comprehensive shotgun metagenomic studies have been performed in Oceania lung cancer cohorts, hampering international generalisability due to unexplored geographical and cohort-specific variations.

To address these knowledge gaps, we employed shotgun metagenomics alongside semi-quantitative conventional culture to comprehensively characterise the extratumoural lung cancer microbiome in a prospective, single-centre cohort in Queensland (QLD), Australia. Methods

Study participants.

One-hundred and ten individuals ≥18 years undergoing routine bronchoscopy for suspected lung cancer at Sunshine Coast University Hospital (Birtinya, QLD, Australia) between June 2020 and December 2025 were prospectively recruited. Individuals who previously or were currently receiving chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and/or immunotherapy, had undergone prior surgery or biopsy for lung cancer, or had received systemic antibiotics <3 months prior were excluded. A further ten subjects ( 9with no known lung disease and 1 with a confirmed benign nodule) undergoing routine bronchoscopy were recruited as healthy controls (Figure 1). Six additional healthy controls provided oral washes only.

Four suspected lung cancer subjects subsequently diagnosed with non-malignancy, and one subject with benign lung disease, were excluded from the study, leaving 106 in the evaluable cohort. Tumour staging was undertaken as described in Supplemental Methods.

Specimen collection.

Specimens were collected into sterile specimen pots in the following order during bronchoscopy: i) oral wash (~10mL sterile phosphate-buffered saline [PBS]), collected immediately prior to sedation, to define the oral microbiome; ii) pre-procedure bronchoscope wash (~10 mL PBS), collected immediately prior to bronchoscopy (n=44), to act as reagent-only controls; iii) BW (~10-40 mL PBS) from the contralateral lung site (i.e. non-cancer side for early-stage cases, or the non-primary tumour side for late-stage cases), to provide within-cohort lung microbiome controls; and iv) BW (~10-40 mL PBS) from the TME to profile extratumoural microbiota (Figure 1). Between the contralateral and TME BW collection, the bronchoscope was flushed with 10 mL PBS to minimise cross-contamination between lung sites. All specimens were collected prior to tumour tissue biopsy to mitigate the risk of intratumoural microbiome contamination. For the healthy control cohort, oral washes, scope washes, and BWs (from either the right or left upper lobe) were collected. All washings were stored at 4 o C until processed. In total, 293 specimens were aseptically obtained from 106 confirmed lung cancer subjects: 91 oral washes, 101 contralateral BWs, and 101 TME BWs. A further 10 BWs and 15 oral washes were obtained from the healthy control cohort. Nine reagent-only controls and 24 bronchoscope washes were also subjected to metagenomic sequencing to account for kitome contamination.

Specimen processing and microbiological culture.

All specimens were pelleted via centrifugation at 4,667 x g for 10 minutes at 4 o C; excess liquid was decanted into a fresh 3% Virkon solution, with ~800 µL specimen resuspended via flick-mixing. Twenty µL was aliquoted directly onto MacConkey, Sabouraud dextrose, chocolate, Luria-Bertani, and mannitol salt agars, and subjected to the dilution streak technique out to a 5 th dilution streak. Culture plates were aerobically incubated at 37 o C for up to 8 days to assess microbial growth.

Shotgun metagenomic sequencing and data analysis. DNA was obtained as detailed in Supplemental Methods. Illumina 150 bp paired-end libraries were sequenced on the NovaSeq 6000 (Australian Genome Research Facility, St Lucia, QLD, Australia) or NovaSeq X (AZENTA, Suzhou, China) platforms. Reads were filtered with Trimmomatic v0.39 23 using previously described parameters 24 . The Kraken2 v2.17.1 25 Standard v2/26/2026 database was used to flag 'taxid 9606' (i.e. Homo sapiens) and 'taxid 9605' (i.e.

Homo) reads, which were then depleted using Seqtk v1.3 (https://github.com/lh3/seqtk). Additional contaminant taxa were also depleted prior to analysis (Supplemental Methods). Depleted metagenomic reads have been deposited under BioProject PRJNA1539961.

Microbial cultivability assessment.

Chocolate agar is a non-selective, nutrient-rich, general-purpose medium that supports the growth of a broad range of respiratory microbes, including fastidious species like Haemophilus influenzae. This medium therefore provides a robust measure of the cultivable microbial load of aerobic and aerotolerant species in clinical specimens. Chocolate agar plates were prepared by four laboratorians using a standardised specimen volume (20 μL) to reduce inter-operator variability.

Microbial growth was semi-quantitatively scored at Day 7-8 of incubation from 0 to 6, with 0 representing no growth, and 6 representing dense growth extending into the fifth dilution streak.

Cumulative tobacco smoking is associated with lung microbiome alterations.

To determine the impacts of clinical confounders on lung microbiota, we included age, primary tumour location, biological sex, and tobacco exposure as variables across our BW dataset (Table S1).

Using multivariable testing, age and primary tumour location did not significantly contribute to global microbial variance or individual taxon abundance/prevalence differences (FDR q>0.05) and were therefore excluded from subsequent models. Using a multivariable linear model adjusting for clinical cohort, sex, and tobacco exposure, cumulative smoking history (measured in pack-years) exerted the most profound influence on the lung microbiome. Longer pack-years history was associated with significant depletion in the prevalence of three core respiratory commensals: Neisseria subflava (β=-0.75), Gemella sanguinis (β=-0.72), and Rothia mucilaginosa (β=-0.67) (FDR q<0.05; Figure 2). Whilst falling just outside the strict FDR boundary (q=0.055), Streptococcus mitis demonstrated a highly significant individual positive correlation with nonzero abundance based on cumulative smoking exposure (β=+1.05; p<0.001). Similarly, when examining biological sex, Bradyrhizobium betae demonstrated higher prevalence in male participants (β coefficient=+1.44) but fell just outside the strict FDR boundary (q=0.055). However, sex did not impact the smoking microbiome signature, with N. subflava, G. sanguinis, and R. mucilaginosa remaining significantly depleted. Localised Mogibacterium diversum depletion requires a combination of cumulative tobacco exposure and tumour presence. Assessment of overarching microbial ecological differences between healthy and lung cancer cohorts using multivariable PERMANOVA (adjusted for pack-years) demonstrated significant global divergence in species composition (β diversity) between cohorts (p=0.022) (Figure S2A). In contrast, unadjusted pairwise comparison of the Shannon diversity index identified similar α diversity between cohorts (p>0.05) (Figure S2B).

Discussion

This study delivers several key advances to the lung cancer microbiome field by combining anatomical site-specific metagenomic profiling, stage-stratified ecological modelling, and conventional culture validation. Our most striking and novel finding is the identification of a distinct functional window in earlystage disease: early-stage TME BWs exhibited significantly reduced microbial cultivability compared to all other airway sites, pointing to a profound, transient suppression of commensal growth during early tumourigenesis. Second, by leveraging a anatomically paired design across early-and late-stage cohorts, we demonstrate that lung cancer induces a progressive, systemic ecological destabilisation across the lower airways. Third, our multivariable models successfully untangle complex environmental confounders, revealing that cumulative smoking drives broad commensal depletion (e.g., N. subflava, G. sanguinis, R. mucilaginosa), and M. diversum depletion is uniquely governed by a synergy between heavy smoking and localised tumour presence. Beyond these novel biological insights, our workflow incorporates rigorous negative controls and multimodal culture-independent and culture-dependent validation, providing a robust framework for future biomarker discovery.

It is well established that tobacco smoking alters the lower airway microbiome 37, 38 . Given the high tobacco smoking burden in lung cancer cohorts, some prior lung cancer microbiome studies have factored in this covariate by focusing on non-smoker cohorts 14,21,39,40 , or by including smoking status 7, 15, 18, 19, [41] [42] [43] [44] or packyears 11, 43 in statistical models. For example, Acidovorax abundance was reportedly increased in the lung tumours of smokers with TP53-mutated LUSC 11 , while Staphylococcus haemolyticus, H. influenzae, Mycobacterium kansasii, S. parasanguinis, and Atopobium parvulum were enriched in smokers with lung cancer 15,18 . Conversely, healthy baseline bacteria such as Mogibacterium were reportedly depleted due to tobacco exposure 44 . Furthermore, bacteria within the lung tumours of current smokers are functionally enriched for metabolic pathways capable of degrading cigarette smoke chemicals such as nicotine, toluene, and phenol 20 , suggesting that tobacco exposure actively selects for microbial communities based on their metabolic utility 11, 20.

As our study recruited predominantly current or former smokers (95.3% of the lung cancer cohort, 60% of the healthy cohort; Table 1), we controlled for cumulative smoking pack-years using multivariable mixedeffects modelling. This continuous gradient approach revealed that cumulative smoking exposure is an independent ecological driver in lower airways, causing significant, dose-dependent depletion of N.

subflava, Gemella sanguinis, and R. mucilaginosa (Figure 2), alongside highly localised depletion of M. diversum strictly within the TME of heavier smokers (Figure S1). The M. diversum finding, also reported by others 44 , suggests that this bacterium is uniquely susceptible to the combination of tobacco smoke and localised cancer-driven inflammation. Supporting this hypothesis, our anatomical site-specific modelling revealed that M. diversum is uniquely enriched in contralateral airways relative to the oral cavity, but fails to establish similar enrichment within the TME or healthy airways (Figure 5). Together, these data suggest that M. diversum opportunistically colonises the broader cancer airway but is acutely vulnerable to the toxic microenvironment generated by heavy smoking at the tumour site.

In contrast to commensal depletion, S. mitis showed a strong positive correlation with abundance (β=+1.05) in heavier smokers, although significance fell just outside our strict FDR boundary (q=0.055).

Key oral bacteria, including S. mitis, can induce lower airway dysbiosis by upregulating T-helper cell type 17 response 45 , driving increased lower airway inflammation 46 and fostering a pro-tumourigenic environment 42 . Consistent with this pathology, S. mitis enrichment has been reported in lung cancer BALFs compared with benign lung disease 17, 18 . Whilst mirroring prior categorical descriptions of Streptococcus enrichment and Neisseria depletion in smoker airways 37 , our continuous model demonstrates that these shifts are progressive, cumulative events. Taken together, we found that tobacco exposure erodes protective commensal barriers and promotes S. mitis proliferation, chronic inflammation, and potential tumourigenesis. This finding raises the intriguing possibility of targeted microbiome restoration following smoking cessation 37 using N. subflava, G. sanguinis, and R. mucilaginosa to reduce lung cancer risk. Furthermore, the depletion of these specific taxa is noteworthy given that they are core oral commensals; recent evidence similarly highlights the critical role of oral-derived microbiota in shaping the NSCLC immune microenvironment 47 . Taken together, our continuous model demonstrates that tobacco exposure progressively erodes these protective, oral-derived commensal barriers. Crucially, our findings caution against proposing Streptococcus as a standalone lung cancer biomarker 42, 48, 49 , as this signature may simply reflect high cumulative smoking exposure.

α and β diversity are routinely reported in lung cancer microbiome studies; however, different metrics and analysis methods make cross-study consolidation difficult. Nevertheless, global comparisons show a clear trend towards significant β diversity differences between lung cancer and non-malignant lower airway specimens 9,15,17,41,48 . In line with these prior findings, our anatomically paired β diversity analysis revealed that the lung cancer microbiome undergoes profound global species compositional restructuring distinct from healthy controls (PERMANOVA p=0.022; Figure S2A), which we confirmed to be a true structural shift unconfounded by unequal variance (beta-dispersion p=0.678). On a more granular level, we also observed significant community composition divergence between healthy and lung cancer airways when stratified into TME and contralateral BWs (PERMANOVA p=0.04; Figure S3A). As this anatomical model also exhibited equivalent within-group variance (beta-dispersion p=0.743), findings reflect a genuine shift in core community composition rather than transient destabilisation. However, this signal lost significance upon post-hoc pairwise analysis, further supporting the observation that the lower airway undergoes generalised structural microbiome alterations rather than developing localised, site-specific divergence. In contrast, although some studies report increased 7, 11, 50 or decreased 15, 18, 19, 43, 44 α diversity in lung cancer, we did not observe global (Figure S2B) or anatomically-stratified (Figure S3B) α diversity differences in our dataset, in agreement with other studies 9, 13, 37 . Therefore, when assessing lung cancer airways purely by disease presence or anatomical site, microbiome disruption is characterised by structural compositional shifts (β diversity) rather than changes in community richness or evenness (α diversity).

Whilst global comparisons are informative, lung cancer biomarker discovery requires a thorough understanding of whether microbiome alterations represent a true disease signature versus an association with clinical covariates, such as disease stage. However, just two published studies 18, 42 have compared early-and late-stage lower-airway microbiomes in people with lung cancer. This literature paucity is likely due to the dominance of late-stage lung cancer diagnoses 44, 50 , which limits statistical power. Other studies lack tumour staging data 9, 16 , prohibiting stage-specific analyses. In the first disease stage-stratified study, Tsay and colleagues 42 found significant β, but not α, diversity differences in their NSCLC cohort. Interestingly, they noted an increased abundance of oral taxa, including Veillonella, Streptococcus, Prevotella, and Rothia, in late stage NSCLC. They also found that lower airway microbiota in late-stage participants was compositionally more similar to the buccal microbiota than in early-stage participants, suggesting that late-stage disease may be associated with a greater influx or retention of oral microbes, potentially facilitated by a progressive breakdown in localised mucosal immunity. In the second study 18 , a comparison of preinvasive LUAD in situ, minimally invasive LUAD, and invasive LUAD cohorts found no significant α or β differences, although the authors identified specific individual taxa with altered abundance in more invasive disease.

Like Tsay and colleagues, we identified significant global β diversity differences across healthy, earlystage, and late-stage cohorts (PERMANOVA p=0.004; Figure 3A). Upon cohort stratification, we confirmed that early-and late-stage BWs maintained significant β diversity differences (Healthy vs. Early-Stage BWs: FDR p=0.029; Healthy vs. Late-Stage BWs: FDR p=0.029; Early-Stage vs. Late-Stage BWs: FDR p=0.018). Interestingly, these pairwise microbial composition differences lost significance when stratified by lung anatomical site and stage (Figure 4) or just anatomical site (Figure S3). This widespread loss of localised significance, paired with significant overarching beta-dispersion (p=0.029), suggests that lung cancer induces generalised ecological destabilisation throughout the entire lower airway, rather than generating a localised tumour microbiome signature.

However, unlike Tsay et al., we found that disease stage significantly impacts α diversity. Omnibus testing revealed significant overarching α diversity differences across staged cohorts, driven specifically by much lower α diversity in early-stage BWs compared with late-stage BWs (FDR p=0.012; Figure 3B). Also dissimilar to the prior studies, we failed to identify any significantly enriched or depleted taxa strictly associated with disease stage using our MaAsLin3 model, even with a more relaxed FDR cut-off of 0.1 and the removal of biological sex as a covariate. Collectively, our results strongly indicate that lung cancer drives substantial site-specific restructuring and ecological destabilisation (β diversity) of the lower airway microbiome compared with healthy airways, a disruption that begins early and persists throughout disease progression. Conversely, α diversity alterations are strictly tied to disease progression, reflecting a transient collapse in community diversity during early tumourigenesis that recovers by late-stage disease.

Controlling for the oral microbiome and healthy baselines, analysis of TME and contralateral BWs revealed significant site-specific microbial differences. The TME was uniquely enriched in Sporothrix mexicana, Sporothrix humicola, L. wadei, T. serpentiformis, and a PFG signature assigned to P. trivialis.

In contrast, the contralateral airway was uniquely enriched in Streptococcus sp. FSL K6-1323, M. diversum, L. gingivalis, Stomatobaculum sp. F0698, and L. parvula (Figure 5). Furthermore, we identified eight significantly enriched taxa (S. pseudopneumoniae, P. thailandica, Starmerella floris, Cetraspora pellucida, Sporothrix brunneoviolacea, Hygrophorus pudorinus, C. Nansynbacter sp. HMT-352, Campylobacter concisus), and five significantly depleted taxa (S. sanguinis, S. gordonii, S. oralis, V. parvula, A. naeslundii), across lung cancer airways when compared with healthy controls (Figure 5). T. serpentiformis and L. wadei are periodontitis-associated anaerobes that we postulate may thrive in the low-oxygen, nutrient-rich necrotic TME, which mimics their native gum disease environment, whereas PFG species and Sporothrix spp. are ubiquitous plant-, soil-, or wood-associated microbes that are constantly inhaled, potentially via aerosolised cigarette tobacco. Although tumour-associated architectural distortion, airway obstruction, and cilia paralysis may enable these transient environmental microbes to accumulate and fester within the localised TME, we acknowledge the shortcomings of relative abundance profiling. Given our culture data demonstrated significantly reduced absolute microbial load within the early-stage TME (Figure 6), the apparent enrichment of these ubiquitous environmental taxa may potentially reflect kitome inflation in low-biomass specimens. Conversely, we observed significant S. parasanguinis, R. mucilaginosa, and S. cristatus depletion uniquely at the TME, and significant Actinomyces sp. oral taxon 414 depletion uniquely at the contralateral site (Figure 5). Although previous studies have reported enrichment of other pathogenic or opportunistic taxa within the TME, such as Veillonella, Megasphaera, Bradyrhizobium japonicum, Capnocytophaga, Sediminibacterium, Gemella, Blautia, and Oscillispira 7,15,16 , these differences likely reflect broader geographical and environmental influences on lung microbial ecology. Nonetheless, a consistent pattern is evident across studies: that is, that lung cancer drives the depletion of protective commensals alongside the selective enrichment of opportunistically pathogenic taxa. Collectively, our findings consolidate prior work showing that the TME fosters a distinct microbial niche that is independent of oral cavity topology and the contralateral lung.

Culture-independent sequencing approaches cannot readily detect absolute microbial load differences due to their inherent compositional data format. As these technologies measure relative proportions rather than absolute numbers, a massive physical bloom of one taxon mathematically forces the apparent decline of others, masking true biomass fluctuations. Integrating conventional culture into cultureindependent investigations therefore provides valuable orthogonal validation by quantifying cultivable microbial load across specimen types. Furthermore, conventional culture distinguishes organisms that are metabolically active and capable of shaping the TME from those merely detectable as remnant DNA.

Despite these advantages, just one prior study has integrated culture-dependent and -independent techniques. In their 2023 study, Sun and colleagues undertook culturomics alongside 16S rRNA metataxonomics to compare and confirm lung and oral microbiota 51 . They isolated 20 bacteria from BALF specimens from at least half of their lung cancer cohort, noting that Streptococcus and Veillonella were highly dominant, Prevotella and Veillonella decreased from the oral cavity to the lung, Pseudomonas increased, and G. sanguinis and Streptococcus intermedius were isolated exclusively from NSCLC.

Culturing viable microbes directly from lung cancer specimens is critically important for understanding how specific organisms and strains actively contribute to local inflammation, modulate tumour associated immune responses, or influence tumour proliferation pathways.

Although our study did not undertake culturomics, our semi-quantitative agar scoring approach crucially identified significantly lower cultivable microbial growth in the early-stage TME compared with all other BW types (Figure 6). This striking finding points to a critical period in early lung tumourigenesis, during which the TME transiently, yet profoundly, suppresses commensal microbial growth. This localised suppression may reflect a hyperactive mucosal immune response: during early tumour development, the TME is characterised by an influx of macrophages and neutrophils, and the production of reactive oxygen species, thus creating a hostile zone for commensal microbes 52 . Another possibility is the targeted secretion of antimicrobial peptides by the developing tumour, such as human β-defensins (e.g. hBD-1) 53.

Our observation offers unique opportunities for early-stage biomarker discovery and the development of new therapeutic strategies aimed at restoring a more favourable TME microbiome profile at a potentially critical juncture in lung cancer pathogenesis, and deserves further exploration.

Current literature is split on whether different histological subtypes are associated with microbiome alterations, although most studies have reported no significant α or β diversity differences [9] [10] [11] 17, 39, 42, 48, [54] [55] [56].

Similarly, we observed no significant α or β diversity differences, or differentially abundant taxa, when comparing LUAD, LUSC, SCLC, and NSCLC-NOS (Figure S4). Although most prior lung cancer microbiome studies have not undertaken subtype specific comparisons, our findings diverge from those of Greathouse and colleagues, who reported Acidovorax, Klebsiella, Rhodoferax, and Anaerococcus enrichment in LUSC relative to LUAD tissue 11 ; however, a key distinction is that Greathouse and colleagues analysed tumour tissue. Nevertheless, these contrasting observations underscore the need for future studies that directly interrogate tumour specific microbial signatures across histological and molecular subtypes, particularly in the context of increased genomic subclassification and precision based oncology 57 . Our findings suggest that the extratumoural lung microbiome is not shaped by tumour histology; however, larger sample sizes, particularly for SCLC and rarer histological subtypes, would be needed for definitive confirmation. Furthermore, unlike studies examining intratumoural tissue specimens, our analysis focused on extratumoural BWs, which may capture a broader airway community less acutely bound to local histological niches. Although not explored in this study, functional profiling of lung cancer subtypes using host-microbe epigenomics, proteomics, or metabolomics may uncover biomarkers that are strongly linked to subtype.

Oral microbial signatures have been proposed as an appealing, non invasive biospecimen for lung cancer biomarker development, with several studies reporting taxonomic and oral microbiome compositional differences in lung cancer cohorts 39, 48, 49, 58, 59 . In our cohort, global comparisons revealed significant overarching structural divergence in the oral microbiome across lung cancer subtypes (PERMANOVA p=0.031; Figure S5A), accompanied by significant ecological destabilisation (betadispersion p=0.046), indicating that lung cancer presence disrupts the oral microbiome compared with the healthy baseline. Upon pairwise testing, it became clear that this global divergence was primarily driven by the LUAD cohort, as we observed significant β diversity differences between LUAD and healthy oral microbiota (FDR p=0.020), with a borderline difference between LUAD and LUSC (FDR p=0.050).

Despite this significant β diversity shift, we found no strong evidence that tumour-associated microbial patterns originate from oral cavity dysbiosis, and, reflective of the broad ecological destabilisation seen in LUAD, no taxa were consistently enriched or depleted in any subtype. Instead, our findings support the hypothesis that distinct lung cancer-associated microbial signatures arise from local ecological selection pressures within the lower airways, aligning with prior work demonstrating clear divergence between TME and oral microbiota 48, 59 . Taken together, our results indicate that further investigation of oral β diversity disturbances in LUAD cohorts is warranted; however, they temper enthusiasm for the use of oral sampling as a non-invasive lung cancer biomarker for other histological subtypes.

Our finding of unique P. trivialis enrichment in TME BWs (Figure 5) prompted us to further investigate this fluorescent, grass-dwelling bacterium 60 in our metagenomic reads using comparative genomics. Despite this significant signal, none of our metagenomes possessed P. trivialis according to phylogenomic reconstruction; instead, a wide array of PFG taxa, including potentially novel species, were identified (Figure S6). Whilst an incorrect taxonomic assignment by Kraken2, our P. trivialis finding nevertheless suggests that PFG bacteria may play an outsized role in the TME microbiome. PFG species are rarely associated with human infections, typically affecting only immunocompromised people, but are common in soil and water, and can colonise human airways, skin, and the gastrointestinal tract. Given that cancer patients receiving treatment frequently become immunocompromised, PFG infection may represent an unrecognised risk for this cohort. As many PFG species likely cannot grow on MacConkey agar, we overlooked these taxa in our conventional culture-based assessments. Future work will involve isolation and whole-genome sequencing of these organisms from lung cancer specimens, which in turn, will facilitate improvements in Kraken2 and other taxonomy-based bioinformatic tools.

Strengths of our study include its prospective design and our near-universal collection of paired tumour and contralateral BWs, alongside matched oral washes (n=92; 86.8% of participants), which permitted precise mixed-effects modelling that inherently controlled for intra-patient variability and topographical contamination. However, we acknowledge several limitations. Our lung cancer cohort possessed a substantially higher mean smoking history (31.8±16.5 pack-years) compared with healthy controls (3.3±2.3 pack-years), reflecting the inherent clinical difficulty in recruiting heavy-smoking healthy volunteers without underlying respiratory disease. Although statistically adjusted, fully decoupling the smoking and lung cancer microbiomes requires longitudinally tracking heavy smokers who do not develop lung cancer. Reliance on short-read sequencing limited taxonomic resolution, and bioinformatic decontamination was required to remove microbial DNA introduced by reagent and kit components, which potentially included real signal in our BWs. Future low-biomass studies should prioritise certified DNA-free reagents to optimise signal-to-noise ratios and should integrate long-read sequencing to enhance species-level assignments. Sampling was restricted to the extratumoural space; incorporating matched endobronchial/transbronchial biopsies or surgical resections is needed to confirm whether these signatures persist within the intratumoural niche. We did not undertake selective culture and wholegenome sequencing of key taxa (e.g. PFG species, N. subflava, M. diversum, P. thailandica, Sporothrix spp.) to confirm their presence in our clinical specimens. The absence of matched blood and stool specimens precluded the evaluation of less invasive circulatory or faecal biomarkers. The addition of RNA-seq 11,14,21,41-43,61 , host-depleted metatranscriptomics, methylomics, proteomics, and metabolomics 17, 62 would have enabled the characterisation of microbial-host transcriptional activity, epigenomic fingerprints, and the detection of RNA viruses and metabolic by-products.

As these multiomics technologies become more accessible, their integration will be essential to unravel the complex microbe-microbe and microbe-host oncogenic pathways underpinning lung cancer development and progression.

Figure 5. Microbial anatomical gradient between the healthy oral cavity and lower airways in lung cancer and healthy participants. Multivariable mixed-effects modelling (MaAsLin3), controlling for smoking pack-years and biological sex, demonstrated significant enrichment (dark orange) and depletion (dark blue) of microbial taxa within the tumour microenvironment (TME) and the contralateral airway relative to the participant's matched oral cavity baseline. Both distal lung cancer sites shared strong microbiota commonalities (Pearson's r correlation: 0.986), characterised by the depletion of five core oral commensals (Streptococcus sanguinis, S. gordonii, S. oralis, Veillonella parvula, Actinomyces naeslundii), and enrichment of eight taxa, including five fungal species. Five taxa were uniquely enriched within the TME, including three non-commensal taxa (Pseudomonas trivialis and two Sporothrix species), whereas three commensal taxa (Streptococcus parasanguinis, Rothia mucilaginosa, Streptococcus cristatus) were uniquely depleted within the TME. The inclusion of a healthy cohort baseline (left) confirms these ecological shifts are disease-specific phenomena rather than universal pan-airway gradients. Solid bars denote statistical significance (FDR-adjusted q<0.05), whereas faded bars represent non-significant fluctuations.
