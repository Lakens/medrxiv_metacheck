Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 6 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Sex-specific genetic effects on platelet aggregation may contribute to differences in thrombotic risk between women and men, yet the underlying genetic mechanisms remain poorly defined. We performed whole-genome sequencing-based genome-wide association studies (GWAS) of 19 harmonized platelet aggregation phenotypes in response to adenosine diphosphate (ADP), epinephrine (EPI), and collagen across three independent cohorts: GeneSTAR, the Framingham Heart Study (FHS) and the Old Order Amish (AMISH). Our meta-GWAS identified a cluster of lowfrequency variants within a 20 kb region on chromosome 10 showing strong sex-specific associations with platelet aggregation in response to low-dose EPI. The lead variant, rs116725046, exhibited a genome-wide significant sex interaction (p = 6.7 × 10⁻⁹), with opposite phenotypic effects in women and men. Female carriers demonstrated increased platelet aggregation, whereas male carriers showed decreased aggregation, consistent across cohorts. Several variants in tight linkage disequilibrium with the lead variant yielded additional significant interaction signals for low-dose EPI.

The associated variants reside within an intronic region of the long noncoding gene LINC00702, with FUMA annotations indicating regulatory chromatin states. Megakaryocyte epigenome data also indicates potential regulatory activity in platelet precursor cells in this genomic region. eQTL analyses suggested sex-differentiated genetic regulation of LINC00702 in multiple tissues, with reduced expression in male heterozygotes only. An Androgen Response Element motif was identified upstream of LINC00702, supporting a potential hormone-responsive regulatory mechanism. Together, these findings identify a novel sex-specific regulatory locus associated with platelet reactivity and highlight LINC00702 as a biologically plausible mediator of sexually dimorphic platelet responses.

Introduction

Platelet activation and aggregation in arterial and venous blood vessels play a critical pathophysiologic role in myocardial infarction (MI), stroke (1, 2), and venous thromboembolism (3).

Aspirin, which inhibits platelet aggregation by blocking cyclo-oxygenase-1-dependent thromboxane production, is the standard of care for primary and secondary cardiovascular prevention in patients at high risk for MI and stroke (4) (5) (6) and for the prevention of deep venous thrombosis (DVT) after total hip/knee replacement (7). We reported marked inter-individual variability in platelet aggregability, and that platelet aggregation phenotype was highly heritable (h 2 between 0.27 and 0.76), both before and after aspirin (8, 9).

Using genome-wide association (GWAS) approaches based on whole genome sequencing (WGS)

and single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) array genotyping, numerous loci associated with platelet aggregation phenotypes at genome-wide significance have been identified (10) (11) (12). Additional studies, which integrated results from GWAS with platelet and megakaryocyte (MK, precursor cell in bone marrow that generates platelets) transcriptomes, identified additional loci associated with platelet aggregation phenotypes (11, 13). Most of these variants are located in non-coding regions of the genome, and the mechanism(s) through which genetic variation leads to alterations in platelet function remain incompletely understood. Marked sex differences in platelet function are well known, with women demonstrating greater platelet activation and aggregation than men (14) (15) (16) (17) (18). Estrogen and testosterone, which are wellrecognized to modify gene expression through transcriptional (19) (20) (21) (22) and epigenetic effects (23) (24) (25), are postulated to mediate these sex differences in phenotype; however, the downstream molecules responsible for sex differences in phenotype are unknown. Genetic variants have the potential to interact with the effects of sex hormone receptors by modifying their DNA binding sites (22, 26, 27) or the CpG sites that are targets for epigenetic methylation (28). Previously reported GWAS investigating platelet phenotypes have adjusted for sex in their statistical approach (Johnson et al. 4 2010). With few exceptions, previously published studies did not specifically investigate the possibility of heterogeneity in the allelic effects between sexes. The studies we are aware of that investigated such sex differences did not report significant findings (29). This includes approaches using a sex-stratified analysis that reported significant variants but did not directly address the allelic heterogeneity (30). The purpose of this study was to identify sex-specific genetic effects on platelet aggregation phenotype through a meta-GWAS using WGS data from three independent studies and a total of 19 harmonized platelet aggregation phenotypes.

Results

The 19 platelet aggregation phenotypes, harmonized across the three cohorts as previously reported (Keramati et al., 2021; Methods and Supplementary Table 1), were examined for sex-specific genetic effects on platelet aggregation. The quality control procedures for the WGS data yielded 10, 708, 633 SNPs among 465 female and 374 male GeneSTAR European ancestry (EA) participants in 210 families; 20,206,847 SNPs among 441 female and 248 male GeneSTAR African ancestry (AA) participants in 192 families; 25,812,790 SNPs among 1,047 female and 933 male FHS participants in 474 families; and 7,680,226 SNPs among 129 female and 127 male AMISH participants not recruited as families (Supplementary Table 2). The meta-GWAS combining the results from these 4 studies yielded p-values as low as 6.7 x 10 -9 for SNP x sex interactions after genomic control correction (Figure 1, Table 1, Supplementary Figures 1 2 3). The main findings were in a roughly 20kb region on chromosome 10, which showed strong evidence for sex-specific effects of multiple variants on low-dose EPI. These 20 SNPs were in near-perfect linkage disequilibrium (LD; Supplementary Figures 4a-c), with an overall minor allele frequency of just above 1%.

For SNP rs116725046 at hg38 position 4,230,494 (meta-GWAS p = 6.7 x 10 -9 ), the SNP x sex interaction hypothesis test among the 839 EA GeneSTAR participants yielded a p-value of 1. 1 x 10 - 5 for platelet aggregation to EPI LOW 3 (Figures 2a and 2d, Supplementary Figure 5, Supplementary Table 3). Among females, adjusted inverse normalized transformed percent aggregation to EPI LOW 3 (e.g., the inverse normalized residuals used as dependent variable in the association study) were 0.63 units higher on average (95% CI 0.11-1.15; p = 0.017) among carriers of the variant allele (n = 12) compared to the non-carriers (n = 433), while in men these transformed residuals were 1.18 units lower on average (95% CI 0.55-1.80; p = 0.0002) among carriers of the variant allele (n = 10) compared to the non-carriers (n = 384). The same interaction was also observed among the 676 FHS participants (p = 3.3 x 10 -4 , Figures 2b and 2e, Supplementary Figure 5, Supplementary Table 3). Among females, adjusted inverse normalized transformed percent aggregation to EPI LOW 3 were 1.03 units higher on average (95% CI 0.18-1.92; p = 0.018) among carriers of the variant allele (n = 5) compared to the non-carriers (n = 316), while in men these transformed residuals were 0.99 units lower on average (95% CI 0.30-1.68; p = 0.005) among carriers of the variant allele (n = 8) compared to the non-carriers (n = 347). Although not statistically significant due to lower allele frequency and sample size, similar estimates for the interaction (p = 0.09) and the differences in response were observed among the 689 AA GeneSTAR participants (Figures 2c and 2f, Supplementary Figure 5, Supplementary Table 3). Among females, adjusted inverse normalized transformed percent aggregation to low dose EPI-3 were 0.66 units higher on average (95% CI -0.29-1.60; p = 0.17) among carriers of the variant allele (n = 4) compared to the non-carriers (n = 437), while in men these transformed residuals were 0.50 units lower on average (95% CI -0.46-1.45; p = 0.31) among carriers of the variant allele (n = 3) compared to the non-carriers (n = 245).

The marker rs116725046 was monomorphic among the AMISH (Supplementary Figure 5).

Cochran's Q = 1.06 was smaller than its degrees of freedom (df = 2), yielding a heterogeneity I² = 0%, indicating no detectable between-study heterogeneity.

Absolute percent aggregation to EPI LOW 3 has a bimodal distribution (Figure 2, top row) and thus the above effect can also be described as an enrichment in the respective high and low values, here defined as above or below 50% aggregation to low-dose EPI. Among the EA GeneSTAR 6 participants, 248 out of the 430 (58%) non-carrier females have high aggregation percentages (above the 50% EPI-3 aggregation cut-point) and 10 out of the 12 female carriers (83%) have high aggregation percentages. However, this relation is different among males: 191 out of 383 (50%) non-carrier males have high aggregation percentages compared to only 1 out of 10 (10%) of the carrier males. Fisher's exact test for equality of proportions yields a p-value of 0.03, despite the low number of carriers. Among the FHS participants, 251 out of the 316 (79.4%) of the non-carrier females have high aggregation percentages, and all 5 female carriers (100%) have high aggregation percentages. Among males, 237 out of the 347 (68.3%) non-carriers have high aggregation percentages compared to 2 out of 8 (25%) of the carrier males. Among the AA GeneSTAR participants, 238 out of 436 (55%) of the non-carrier females have high aggregation percentages and 3 out of the 4 female carriers (75%) have high aggregation percentages. By contrast, 105 out of 238 (43%) of the non-carrier males had high aggregation percentages compared to 1 out of 3 (33%) of the carrier males.

Very similar results were observed for EPI LOW 4, which is expected because the low dose EPI phenotypes are strongly correlated (Supplementary Table 1). The lowest meta-GWAS p-value ( 3.5 x 10 -8 ) for the SNP x sex interaction was achieved by 5 markers in perfect LD (r² = 1) in our sample of GeneSTAR and FHS participants (rs189579493, rs141008381, rs139980782, rs147732132 and rs137890908, between loci 4,234,667 and 4,243,293, Table 1). In contrast to EPI LOW 3 though, not enough carriers of these variants were observed in the AA GeneSTAR participants (minor allele count MAC = 2) to be included in the meta-GWAS. Thus, these p-values were solely derived from the joint analysis of the GeneSTAR EA and FHS participants (Supplementary Figures 6 and 7).

We examined the genome for Androgen Response Elements (ARE) and Estrogen Response Elements (ERE) binding site locations and calculated their respective distances to the nearest downstream gene. Among the 71,075 detected ARE 15-digit binding site motifs, the median distance to the nearest downstream gene was 66,153 nucleotide bases (IQR 22, 160). We found an ARE binding motif AAGAACAGGATGTGC on chromosome 10 at hg38 genomic positions 4,197,274 -4,197,288, located 3,853 nucleotide bases upstream of its nearest gene element LINC00702. Among the 71,075 ARE binding site motifs, 3,527 had a distance less or equal to 3,853 nucleotide bases to their nearest downstream gene element, indicating that such a close proximity between binding motif and downstream gene element is somewhat unusual and not well supported by a random chance explanation (p = 3,527/71,075 = 0.0496). The closest ERE binding motif upstream of LINC00702 was detected at hg38 genomic positions 4,154,445 -4,154,459 with a distance of 46,682 nucleotide bases, a somewhat typical distance observed among the 49,840 ERE binding motifs and their nearest downstream genes (median distance 47,481 nucleotide bases). SuSiEx fine-mapping was not successful in pinpointing the causal variant(s). For EPI LOW 3, SuSiEx returned a single 95% credible region, containing the 20 SNPs of our region of interest (Table 1) with nearly uniform posterior inclusion probabilities (PIPs). SNP rs116725046 had the third highest PIP in this credible set for EPI LOW 3 at 5.1%; two SNPs with equal PIPs of 6.6% slightly exceeded this value. For EPI LOW 4 almost identical results were observed, with the exception that the sum of the highest 19 PIPs was above 95% and thus one SNP from the region of interest was not included in the credible set. SNP rs141008381 was in a 5-way tie for the highest PIP with 7.3%. These results were consistent with the very strong LD structure among the low MAF variants in the respective three cohorts.

The peak SNPs rs116725046 for EPI LOW 3 and rs141008381 for EPI LOW 4 are 4.8 kb apart, in perfect LD (r² = 1) in the GeneSTAR EA and AA cohorts and FHS, and map to noncoding RNA within an intron of the long intergenic non-protein coding gene LINC00702 (Supplementary Figures 5 and 6). The FUMA (31) chromatin state assignments for rs116725046 and rs141008381 are 5 and 4, respectively, suggesting these SNPs are associated with transcription sites. Genomic loci consisting of 31 SNPs including peak SNP and candidate SNPs (see Methods and Materials) mapped to 35 genes (Figure 3, Supplementary Figures 8a-b). Among these 35 genes, only the LINC00702 gene had evidence from eQTL associations, chromatin interaction, and positional mapping. All other genes had mapping evidence only from chromatin interaction. Of note, associations of platelet phenotypes with the closest protein coding gene KLF6 were previously reported in GWAS catalogue (32), but our in-silico functional analysis on RNA expression and chromatin accessibility implicates LINC00702 as the regulatory element. Interestingly, when we queried BLUEPRINT project epigenome data in primary MKs, we also found evidence for a DNase I hypersensitivity site overlapping rs141008381.

Expression QTL analyses also suggested possible sex-differentiated genetic regulation of LINC00702 expression for several tissues in the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) Project (33).

In males, expression of LINC00702 was significantly lower (p < 0.01) for rs116725046 minor allele heterozygotes than homozygotes in 12 of 35 tissues in the GTEx dataset, while no such differences on expression of LINC00702 was observed among females (Figure 4, Supplementary Table 4). Similar results were observed for rs141008381 (Supplementary Table 5). Formal tests for SNP x sex interactions were generally not possible or underpowered with MACs between 2 and 20 depending on tissue (which have different sample sizes in the GTEx dataset) and no minor allele homozygotes were observed. The lowest interaction p-value was achieved for nerve tibial tissue (p = 0.033, MAC = 16) where among females no difference between carriers and non-carriers in average LINC00702 expression was observed (p = 0.95), but in contrast male carriers had much lower LINC00702 expression on average than male non-carriers (p = 3.8 x 10 -6 ).

Discussion

The goal of this study was to identify sex-specific genetic determinants of platelet function. In a sequencing-based meta-GWAS that included participants of European and African ancestry, genetic variants (lead SNP rs116725046) in LINC00702 -a long intergenic non-coding region with ill-defined biological function -were associated with a significant SNP x sex interaction for agonist-induced platelet aggregation. Platelet phenotype differed by sex in allele-specific manner, with greater aggregation observed in female minor allele carriers and lesser aggregation observed in male minor allele carriers. We were unable to locate the putative causal variant from the high-LD cluster on chromosome 10 by SuSiEx fine-mapping; however, eQTL associations, chromatin interaction, and positional mapping from FUMA analysis suggested that LINC00702 was the most likely regulatory element. Sex-differentiated genetic regulation of LINC00702 was observed in numerous tissues in the GTEx dataset, with lesser expression observed for male, but not female, heterozygotes. We also detected an ARE upstream of in LINC00702, with a distance lower than expected by random chance.

In sum these results identify LINC00702 as a novel locus associated with platelet function and suggest a putative biological pathway for sex differences in platelet function that involves sexually dimorphic expression of a non-coding RNA.

We identified a novel SNP in LINC00702 showing a significant interaction with sex for platelet function. This finding was consistent in two European cohorts and one African American cohort.

Although marked sex differences in platelet function are well-recognized (14) (15) (16) (17) (18) 34), the molecular basis for these differences is poorly characterized. Because blood platelets are anucleate cell fragments derived from MKs-polyploid precursor cells located in bone marrow, it is extremely difficult to conduct experiments to directly assess the impact of sex and sex hormones on transcriptional regulation of cells directly involved in platelet biology. Our study identifies a putative molecular pathway that can be tested in future studies investigating the molecular basis for sex differences in platelet function. Specifically, results from this study give rise to the hypothesis that allelic differences in LINC00702 interact with sex to yield differences in LINC00702 RNA expression and downstream platelet phenotype. The direction of effect we observed -i.e. greater allele-specific platelet aggregation in women and lesser aggregation in men -is consistent with the known sex differences in platelet phenotype; however, given the low minor allele frequency of rs116725046 (and other SNPs in the LD block), the majority of variance in platelet aggregation by sex cannot be accounted for through this pathway alone. Nonetheless, our results provide evidence to support the long-suspected hypothesis that sex differences in platelet function may occur through transcriptional regulation of specific genes in MK precursors. Experiments to test this putative pathway are beyond the scope of this work.

The conventional genome-wide significance threshold of p < 5 × 10⁻⁸ derives from a Bonferroni-type correction for approximately one million independent common-variant tests, based on the linkage disequilibrium structure of European-ancestry genomes. WGS now interrogates many more variants, and the appropriate threshold also depends on ancestry, so it is reasonable to ask whether 5 × 10⁻⁸ remains adequate. Kanai et al (35) addressed this empirically by simulating GWAS in the five ancestral populations of the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3, each with more than ten million variants at a minor allele frequency of at least 0.5%. The estimated thresholds controlling the family-wise error rate at 5% were 9.26 × 10⁻⁸ (European), 9.46 × 10⁻⁸ (South Asian), 1.61 × 10⁻⁷ (East Asian) and 1.83 × 10⁻⁷ (admixed American), all less stringent than 5 × 10⁻⁸. Only the African-ancestry population (3.24 × 10⁻⁸) and the trans-ancestry meta-analysis (3.25 × 10⁻⁸) required a more stringent threshold, and even then only modestly so. We therefore retained the conventional 5×10⁻⁸ threshold.

The number of phenotypes examined in this study (19) may also impact the choice of significance threshold. Bonferroni adjustment assumes independence of phenotypes; however, the 19 phenotypes we examined are highly correlated (Supplementary Figure 9). Principal components analysis demonstrated that 90% of variance was explained by the first 6 components (Supplementary Figure 10), which is consistent with the underlying biology of the platelet phenotypes, i.e. aggregation in response to 3 agonists (ADP, EPI, and collagen) at 2 doses (low and high). Applying a Bonferroni correction for six phenotypes gives a study-wide threshold of 5 × 10⁻⁸ /6 = 8.3 × 10⁻⁹. The EPI LOW 3 association (p = 6.73 × 10⁻⁹) remains significant at this threshold; equivalently, its adjusted p-value of 4.0 × 10⁻⁸ is below the conventional genome-wide threshold.

LINC genes are transcribed to yield lincRNAs, which are recognized to regulate cis and trans gene expression through multiple mechanisms, including chromatin remodeling, epigenetic modifications, mRNA stability and availability, and interactions with RNA and proteins both inside and outside the nucleus (36, 37). LINC00702 transcript was detected in 35 GTEx tissue types, and sex-differentiated genetic regulation was identified in twelve. Specifically, the minor allele of rs116725046 was associated with significantly less transcript expression in male arteries, heart, colon, esophagus, nerve, and cultured fibroblasts; a significant difference was not observed in any tissue specimen from females. Different bulk tissues are reported to commonly share eQTLs (38) but this has not previously been reported in a sex stratified manner or for the subset of LINC genes. Our findings provide proof of concept for sex-differentiated genetic regulation of LINC00702; however, we cannot confirm that this phenomenon occurs in MKs because those cells were not available in GTEx. GTEx does contain blood tissue, which is phenotypically closer (but not identical) to MKs and platelets; however, transcript expression and minor allele frequency of the lead SNPs in LINC00702 were too low to perform eQTL analysis, and we are unaware of sex stratified eQTL analyses from other tissue repositories. Results from this study may help motivate sex-stratified eQTL analyses in the future, but direct application of results to MKs and platelets will remain a challenge because of the absence of these cell types from large repositories. There is currently very limited data available regarding epigenome regulation and other omics measurements in primary MKs. However, a query of several samples in the BLUEPRINT project does suggest that the locus identified in this study may be epigenetically active in MKs.

The role of LINC00702 in platelet biology is unknown, and this is the first study to suggest the potential for such a role. Because LINC genes are non-protein coding, it is reasonable to hypothesize that an association between LINC00702 and anucleate platelet function is mediated by cis or trans regulation of gene transcription in MKs. The putative molecules downstream of LINC00702 cannot be determined from this study. Limited information is available on the biology of LINC00702 in human health and disease. Several studies have described a potential role for LINC00702 in human cancers; both pro- (39, 40) and anti-oncogenic (41, 42). Interestingly, expression of LINC00702 in cancer tissues is reported to regulate Wnt/-catenin and PTEN signaling (39, 41), pathways that are known to regulate activation and differentiation of platelets (43, 44). SNPs near LINC00702 are reported to be associated with a number of phenotypes, including blood pressure (45) (46) (47) (48), leukocyte count (48, 49), and subarachnoid hemorrhage (50); however, the SNPs reported in these studies differ from the lead SNPs we identified and these other studies have not reported associations stratified by sex.

Finally, we identified a 15 bp sequence consistent with an ARE (but not an ERE) 3.8 kb upstream of the long non-coding RNA gene LINC00702. AREs are DNA sequences in promotor or enhancer regions that bind activated androgen receptors (e.g. testosterone bound to AR) and regulate gene transcription (51). AREs can have positive or negative transcriptional effects, and these positive and negative AREs, which share DNA homology, can be found clustered near the same gene (52). The presence of an ARE in proximity to LINC00702 suggests the potential for transcriptional regulation by androgens and modification of transcription by genetic variation at the locus. In vitro studies are needed to confirm the ability of sex (and sex hormones) to regulate LINC00702 gene expression in human MKs, the downstream molecular targets (if any) that are regulated, and their impact on platelet function.

In summary, we identify a novel SNP x sex interaction for LINC00702 with agonist-induced platelet aggregation and sex-differentiated genetic regulation in multiple human tissues. The direction of effect was consistent with known sex differences in platelet aggregation (female > male), confirming the potential for sex differences in platelet function to be mediated by regulation of gene expression. Furthermore, our indirect results from annotations suggest a putative molecular pathway from sex → gene → transcript → phenotype, generating hypotheses that can be examined in future studies.

Materials and Methods

Study populations. Sex-specific genetic effects for platelet aggregation in response to multiple agonists were assessed using phenotypes and WGS data from three independent cohort studies: the Genetic Study of Atherosclerosis Risk (GeneSTAR), the Framingham Heart Study (FHS), and the Old Order Amish (AMISH), previously described (53, 54).

GeneSTAR families were identified from probands with documented premature (age <60) coronary artery disease (CAD) in one of ten Baltimore area hospitals; unaffected, apparently healthy siblings, offspring of the siblings and probands, and parents of the offspring were recruited from [2003] [2004] [2005] [2006] for a study of platelet reactivity. Participants came from European and African ancestry families and were without known coronary artery disease (CAD) at the time of phenotypic assessment.

Participants were excluded for any of the following: history of any bleeding disorder or hemorrhagic event, serious comorbidity, history of aspirin intolerance, abnormal platelet count, hematocrit, or white blood cell count, or current use of anticoagulants or antiplatelet agents. Use of aspirin and/or nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs was prohibited for ten days before the first study visit. Platelet function was assessed before and after two weeks of aspirin (81 mg/day) (14).

FHS is a community-based, prospective, longitudinal study following 3 generations of participants to identify risk factors that contribute to cardiovascular disease. The Offspring cohort studied here represents the second generation, including spouses (55). In the present study, we used data from the Offspring cohort that have been examined approximately every 4 years since being recruited in 1971. The platelet reactivity data were measured at the fifth examination (1991) (1992) (1993) (1994) (1995). To exclude use of aspirin at the baseline visit, platelet aggregation was assessed in response to arachidonic acid (10). AMISH is a prospective cohort trial to examine the relationship between genetic variants and agonistinduced platelet function at baseline and in response to clopidogrel and aspirin (56). AMISH participants who were over the age of 20, generally healthy, and agreed to discontinue the use of medications, supplements, and vitamins for at least one week prior to study initiation were eligible for recruitment. Participants were excluded for any of the following: bleeding disorder, abnormal platelet count, or anemia; severe hypertension; renal, liver or thyroid dysfunction; coexisting malignancy; allergy to aspirin or clopidogrel. 14 Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and each study was approved by their local review board (GeneSTAR-Johns Hopkins Institutional Review Board, IRB00143190); FHS-Boston University Institutional Review Board, H-33525); and OOA-University of Maryland, Baltimore Institutional Review Board, HP-00043419).

Platelet Phenotypes. Platelet function in response to the agonists adenosine diphosphate (ADP), epinephrine (EPI), and collagen (COL) was assessed in platelet-rich plasma in vitro. In all cohorts, whole blood was collected by venipuncture into sodium citrated tubes and platelet-rich plasma was prepared by differential centrifugation. Platelet aggregation was measured by optical aggregometry after stimulating samples with multiple different concentrations of ADP, EPI, or COL. A total of 19 harmonized phenotypic measures of platelet aggregation were assessed, similar to previous studies (10, 11, 54). This included seven outcomes for ADP, eight for EPI, and four for COL (Supplementary Table 1).

Genome-Wide Association.

GWAS were previously performed to identify genetic variants that determine agonist-induced platelet aggregation (10, 12, 57). In this study, we used WGS data available through the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute's (NHLBI's) Trans-Omics for Precision Medicine (TOPMed, https://topmed.nhlbi.nih.gov) program from Freeze 8. We analyzed 19 harmonized platelet traits in response to using ADP, EPI or COL, in three studies: GeneSTAR (n=857 participants of European and n=683 participants of African ancestry), the Framingham Heart Study (n=1,476 participants), and the AMISH cohort (n=255 participants). BCF files were converted to PLINK Binary files (bim/bed/fam) using plink2 to obtain additive coding of the count for the number of minor alleles per person for each SNP. SNPs that met the QC filtering criteria (MAC of at least 5, interaction standard error below 10, Hardy Weinberg p-value above 1 x 10 -6 and missing genotypes less than 5%) were considered in the meta-GWAS. A linear mixed effects model as implemented in the GENESIS Bioconductor package (58) including an interaction term for bi-allelic SNPs and sex (equation 1, Supplementary Materials) were conducted separately for each study using inverse normalized age-and sex-adjusted residuals of the platelet traits as dependent variables, for the WGS genetic association (FHS residuals were also adjusted for aspirin use, see (11). A genetic relationship matrix (GRM) was created using PC-AiR and PC-Relate within each cohort to account for phenotype correlations due to genetic similarities and relatedness among samples. GWAS WGSbased association analysis was conducted using the adjusted inverse normalized transformation of the platelet aggregation trait. A linear mixed effects model was used to test genome-wide for SNP x sex interactions in each population (AMISH, GeneSTAR and FHS). First, a null model (without SNP in the model) was run using the fitNullModel function in R, with sex included in the model. Next, the output from the null model was applied to the assocTestSingle function, along with SNP data.

The output from GENESIS consisted of a data frame with summary information from the association tests for each SNP. All cohorts used the same script to run the GENESIS model: a main effect model adjusting for sex and an interaction model that included a multiplicative interaction term between the SNP variants and sex for all autosomes. A total of 19 platelet aggregation results were harmonized across the cohorts and the platelet aggregation traits (11).

Meta-Analysis.

Meta-GWAS were conducted using the inverse variance-weighted fixed effects method using METAL software (59), based on a one degree of freedom Wald test of interaction (1df). SNPs present in any population were included in the meta-GWAS. Signs of parameter estimates were flipped as needed to match the coded alleles in both populations. Meta-GWAS pvalues were adjusted using genomic control (60). Some of the parameter estimates, confidence intervals and p-values for the sex specific effects reported in the Results were calculated from the bivariate normal distribution for the main and interaction term obtained in the linear mixed model, similar to (61). The correlation was obtained by altering the source code and output of the assocTestSingle function in the GENESIS package (62) in R.

ARE/ERE MOTIF Search Analysis.

We conducted a MOTIF search to identify all 15-digit nucleotide sequences that represent ARE and ERE motifs. JASPAR (63), the largest open-access database of curated and non-redundant transcription factor (TF) binding profiles, was used as the reference site to identify these sex hormone binding sequences (https://jaspar.elixir.no/). Using Biostrings (64) in R, we searched and identified the location of all of these unique 15-digit sequences throughout the genome. There are 71,075 locations in the genome (autosomes) where the 15-digit ARE sequences could be located, and 49,840 locations in the genome where the 15-digit ERE sequences could be located.

Cross Population Fine Mapping (SuSiEx).

To identify putative causal variants underlying GWAS associations in our meta-GWAS across cohorts of diverse ancestry, we used SuSiEx (65), a multiancestry extension of the previously published SuSiE framework (66). The Sum of Single Effects (SuSiE) model is a Bayesian regression framework that efficiently models the effects of potentially multiple causal variants in LD and outputs credible sets with a high probability (default, 95%) of containing true causal variants. The Posterior Inclusion Probability (PIP) reported for each SNP in a credible set is the probability that the SNP is causal for the phenotype of interest, given the data and the model. A credible set contains the minimal number of variants whose cumulative PIP sums to (at least) the level of credibility chosen. SuSiE is particularly well-suited for fine mapping in settings where multiple variants may jointly contribute to the association signal. SuSiEx leverages summarylevel association statistics together with ancestry-specific LD reference panels, modeling both shared and ancestry-specific signals, which improves fine-mapping resolution across genetic backgrounds. In our implementation, we supplied meta-analytic summary statistics from all cohorts, along with LD matrices (.ld.bin files produced by PLINK) matched to each ancestry contributing to the meta-GWAS. We specified the maximum number of effect components per locus as 5 (a software requirement) to accommodate possible allelic heterogeneity. Equivalently, SuSiEx produces highprobability credible sets with PIPs for each signal, facilitating the prioritization of variants most likely to be causal. By integrating diverse LD structures and effect size patterns across ancestries, this approach increases power and refines the set of candidate causal variants.

Functional Mapping and Annotation. Functional Mapping and Annotation of Genome-Wide

Association Studies (FUMA GWAS) was used for in-silico functional annotation of key loci (31).

FUMA uses information from multiple biological resources to facilitate functional annotation of GWAS results and for gene prioritization by annotating peak and candidate SNPs with biological functionality and by mapping SNPs to genes based on position, as well as eQTL and chromatin interaction information. We defined candidate SNPs as markers with in-sample LD (r 2 > 0.6) with a peak SNP. These markers are functionally annotated in FUMA for consequences on gene functions delineated by ANNOVAR (67), CADD deleteriousness score (68), potential regulatory functions based on the RegulomeDB score (69) and chromatin state predicted by ChromHMM (70), effects on gene expression using eQTL information from a variety of sources including the GTEx Consortium (33), and 3D structure of chromatin interactions with Hi-C data (71). In a second FUMA step, the functionally annotated SNPs were mapped to genes using positional, eQTL, and chromatin interaction mappings. For positional mapping, we applied a distance cut-off of 10 kb. For eQTL mapping, we investigated cis-eQTLs within a 1 Mb window using GTEx v8 data (more details below), applying a significance threshold of p < 1×10⁻⁵ in at least one of 54 tissues. For chromatin interaction mapping, we used Hi-C data from 21 tissue/cell types (GEO: GSE87112) with an FDR-corrected significance threshold of p < 1×10⁻⁶. SNP positions were queried for direct overlaps in BLUEPRINT megakaryocyte DNase-I hypersensitivity sites (reflecting open chromatin) from 2 samples (C0006NS, S004BT) and 6 histone ChIP-seq peaks from 3 samples (S007AV, S004BT, S00VHK). eQTL Analysis. We used the same gene expression counts, covariates, and genotypes as in (33) for all GTEx tissues. We performed sex-stratified analyses using tensorQTL (72) and default parameters. For each of the sexes, we tested rs116725046 and rs141008381 effects on LINC00702 in an eQTL analysis, using five genotype PCs, PCR, sequencing platform, and PEER factors as additional covariates. The number of PEER factors changed by tissue depending on sample size in accordance with Oliva et al. (33). We calculated the z-statistics from the male-only and female-only eQTL results by dividing the slope by its estimated standard error. Assuming asymptotic normality of the parameter estimates, we tested for a difference in effect sizes (slopes) between males and females (e.g., the interaction) using the asymptotic normality of the difference in effect sizes and its variance as the sum of the squared estimated standard errors. 1. rs141008381. The z-statistic was defined as the slope estimate divided by its estimated standard error. Among females, no p-value lower than 0.01 were observed, corresponding to a z-statistic of 2.58 in absolute value for two-sided tests and indicated by the light blue region.
