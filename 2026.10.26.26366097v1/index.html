Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Individuals with cam-type femoroacetabular impingement (FAI) demonstrate altered lower-limb biomechanics during functional activities, yet little is known about inter-joint coordination during sit-to-stand (STS). This study compared sagittal-plane kinematics, inter-joint coordination, and coordination variability during STS in individuals with and without cam-type FAI.

Methods: Twenty-four adults with symptomatic cam-type FAI and 19 healthy controls completed five STS trials while three-dimensional motion capture and ground reaction force data were collected. Sagittal-plane hip, knee, ankle, pelvis, and spine kinematics were analyzed using statistical parametric mapping and discrete measures. Spine-hip and hipknee coordination variability were quantified using modified vector coding. Group differences were evaluated using independent t-tests and mixed ANOVAs.

Results: Participants with FAI demonstrated reduced early-phase hip range of motion and greater peak knee flexion during STS compared with controls. Statistical parametric mapping did not reveal statistical group differences in single joint kinematics during early and late phase STS. A significant group-by-phase interaction was observed for spine-hip coordination variability, with individuals with FAI exhibiting greater variability during.

[div-01]

early STS (p = 0.010, d = 0.92), but not during late STS. No between-group differences were identified for hip-knee coordination variability.

Conclusions: Individuals with cam-type FAI demonstrate altered spine-hip movement organization during STS, characterized by greater early-phase coordination variability and reduced hip flexion range of motion.

Clinical significance: These findings suggest that movement strategies in people with FAI during STS may be more strongly reflected in variability of inter-joint coordination patterns than single joint kinematics.

1. INTRODUCTION

Femoroacetabular impingement (FAI) is a well-established cause of hip pain among active young adults (Beaulé et al., 2007; Wisniewski and Grogg, 2006) and has been linked to the development of early-onset osteoarthritis (Agricola et al., 2024 (Agricola et al., , 2013;; Ganz et al., 2003). Cam-type, the most common morphologic subtype of FAI, is characterized by an aspherical head-neck junction that can produce abnormal femoroacetabular contact during terminal hip motion, such as those involving flexion, adduction, and internal rotation (Ganz et al., 2003; Griffin et al., 2016). Functional tasks that load the hip in or near these positions can provoke symptoms and are associated with altered hip, pelvis, and lower-extremity movement patterns in individuals with cam-type FAI (Samaan et al., 2017; Spiker et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2025). These adaptations may arise partly in response to the interaction between cam morphology and acetabular or spinopelvic anatomy (Grammatopoulos et al., 2018) and may reflect altered femoropelvic coordination or joint loading strategies which could contribute to secondary cartilage or labral stress (Diamond et al., 2017; Kumar et al., 2014).

Prior biomechanical research in individuals with cam-type FAI has commonly focused on gait and squatting, with evidence of reduced peak hip extension and sagittalplane hip range of motion during gait, as well as altered hip-pelvis mechanics and reduced sagittal-plane pelvic motion during squatting (Bagwell et al., 2016; Lamontagne et al., 2009; Yarwood et al., 2022). Further, following corrective surgery, FAI patients have shown greater squat depth which was accompanied by increases in knee flexion and ankle dorsiflexion, suggesting that performance may be modified through redistribution of movement across the lower-extremity kinetic chain (Lamontagne et al., 2011). In contrast, the sit-to-stand (STS) task has received comparatively less attention despite being a highfrequency functional movement that imposes substantial biomechanical demands on the lower limbs (Dall and Kerr, 2010; Janssen et al., 2002). Existing STS work suggests that individuals with FAI demonstrate altered functional performance, lower-extremity moment contributions, and muscle activity during this task (Samaan et al., 2017; Spiker et al., 2022). However, these studies have emphasized discrete biomechanical measures, which may not fully capture the coordinated trunk, hip, knee, and ankle motions required to transfer body mass from a seated to standing position. Whether individuals with camtype FAI show changes in inter-joint coordination and/or coordination variability during STS remains unclear.

Inter-joint coordination and variability provide insight into neuromuscular control strategies beyond discrete kinematic measures. Modified vector coding quantifies dynamic relationships between joint motions, yielding coupling angles and coupling angle variability that describe both the organization and consistency of movement across repeated trials (Needham et al., 2014 (Needham et al., , 2015)). This is clinically relevant because reduced coordination variability has been interpreted as a less adaptable movement strategy that may concentrate repetitive loading on a smaller region of tissue (Hamill et al., 2012 (Hamill et al., , 1999)).

Given the proposed role of altered hip mechanics and joint loading in cam-type FAI, applying this approach to STS may reveal subtle differences in movement strategy that are not evident from sagittal-plane kinematics alone.

The purpose of this study was to estimate the effect to which sagittal plane kinematics, inter-joint coordination, and coordination variability during STS differ in people with and without FAI. We hypothesized that cam-type FAI patients would show reduced hip joint ROM and altered spinal kinematics and coordination variability, compared to healthy controls.

2. METHODS

>METHODS

2.1. Overview

This cross-sectional study was completed at the Human Movement Biomechanics Laboratory, at the University of Ottawa (Ontario, Canada). Each participant underwent biomechanical testing and completed questionnaires in a single testing session.

Institutional ethics approvals were obtained prior to study initiation (Ottawa Health Science Network and uOttawa -Research Ethics Board Number: 20190456-01H and H-09-19-4994 -OTH-4994 respectively). All study participants provided informed consent.

2.2 Participants

Participants were recruited from a hip preservation practice with two orthopedic surgeons through various sources (e.g., orthopedic clinic referrals, pre-operative screening, or departmental research registry). Individuals in the cam-type FAI cohort were skeletally mature adults (>18 years) scheduled for arthroscopic cam resection, who reported localized groin or lateral hip pain for longer than six months and demonstrated a positive impingement sign. Diagnosis of cam-type FAI was confirmed through clinical examination and radiographic evaluation. Radiographic criteria included a Tönnis grade of 0 -1, absence of acetabular dysplasia (lateral center-edge angle >25°), absence of acetabular overcoverage (LCE >39°), and an α-angle greater than 55° on multiplanar imaging. In the event patients had bilateral cam-type impingement, only the pre-surgical, symptomatic limb was used for our analyses.

Healthy control participants were required to be over 18 years of age, demonstrate a normal femoral head-neck contour with no evidence of dysplasia, and be willing to complete all required assessments. Participants were excluded if they had a prior joint replacement in any lower-limb joint, a lower-limb or lumbar spine injury within the previous year, metal prostheses, BMI greater than 30 kg/m² or excessive waist circumference (>102cm in men and >88cm in women), a strong family history of early osteoarthritis, or other serious medical conditions.

2.3. Demographics and questionnaires

The following demographic data were collected: age, biological sex, height, and weight. General hip symptoms and functional limitations were evaluated using the Hip disability and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (HOOS), which assesses self-report pain, symptoms, activities of daily living, sport and recreation, and hip-related quality of life (Nilsdotter et al., 2003). The HOOS is a well-validated patient-reported outcome measure with established reliability, validity, and responsiveness in populations with hip pathology, and can discriminate between individuals with hip disorders and healthy controls (Kemp et al., 2013).

2.4. Protocol

This manuscript describes one task (STS) from a broader biomechanical data collection where participants with and without FAI completed a battery of functional tasks.

For the standardized STS task, each participant began seated upright on an adjustable bench-mounted force plate. The seat height was individually adjusted to achieve approximately 90° of knee flexion to control for differences in participant anthropometrics.

Each foot was positioned at the center of two separate floor-mounted force plates (Figure 1). Data were collected from the FAI group prior to their surgery.

Participants were asked to flex their shoulders to 90° in the scapular plane and flex their elbows to 90°. From this starting position, participants were instructed to rise to full knee extension at a self-selected speed while maintaining the initial arm position. Upon reaching full extension, they held the standing posture for 5 seconds before returning to the seated position on the bench to reset. Five consecutive trials were performed under these conditions without a prescribed rest interval. To ensure consistency, participants were instructed not to use their upper extremities for momentum generation. Foot placement was standardized to shoulder-width apart and verified prior to each trial. All participants wore comfortable, closed-toe footwear and performed the task unassisted.

2.5. Data Acquisition and Processing

Three-dimensional kinematic data were collected at 200 Hz with 45 reflective markers using a 10-camera system (Vicon MX, UK). Markers were placed symmetrically across the head, trunk, pelvis, upper and lower extremities based on the University of Ottawa Motion Analysis Model marker set (Mantovani and Lamontagne, 2017). Ground reaction forces (GRFs) were recorded at 1,000 Hz using force plates (FP4060-08, Bertec Corporation, Columbus, USA) positioned under each foot and a bench-mounted force plate (Model 9262BA, Kistler Group, Winterthur, Switzerland) on the seat. A static standing calibration trial was conducted prior to dynamic testing to define participant joint centers and segment coordinate systems. Data were synchronized and processed in Nexus software using the University of Ottawa Motion Analysis Model (UOMAM). Specifically, data were labeled, gap filled, and filtered (zero-lag, fourth order Butterworth filter, cut-off frequency = 6 Hz). Lower limb 3-D motions were calculated according to established methods (Davis III et al., 1991; Kadaba et al., 1990). Joint angles were calculated based on a YXZ Cardan sequence, and pelvic angles were measured relative to a global coordinate system. Joint and segment angles were zeroed based on each participant's neutral position as determined by a standing static trial. Trials with extensive marker loss or unrecoverable data gaps during processing were excluded. Data were then exported to MATLAB (R2023b) using custom scripts for further processing and analyses.

2.6. Event identification

The STS task was partitioned into early and late phases using a custom MATLAB script. The early phase was defined from movement onset to seat-off, and the late phase from seat-off to movement end. Seat-off was detected from unloading of the bench vertical ground reaction force, defined as the first sustained point at which bench load fell below 5% of the initial seated baseline load (Ippersiel et al., 2018). Movement onset and movement end were automatically identified from a composite sagittal-plane movement velocity signal derived from available trunk, spine, hip, and knee angle trajectories and predetermined threshold values. Event-detection plots for each trial were visually inspected by an experienced researcher and manually adjusted when necessary. After identifying key movement events, kinematic data were segmented into early-phase, late-phase, and fullcycle intervals, and each interval was time-normalized to 100% of the STS phase.

2.7. Outcome Measures

>Outcome Measures

2.7.1. Joint angles and range of motion

Kinematic outcomes included peak joint angles and ranges of motion for the hips, knees, ankles, pelvis, and spine in the sagittal plane. For FAI patients, data were extracted for their affected limb. For healthy controls, data were extracted for their limb (right vs. left) based on a randomization process. For each participant, data were averaged across all available trials, for the early phase, late phase, and full cycle.

2.7.2. Vector coding analyses (coordination and coordination variability)

Sagittal plane hip-knee and spine-hip inter-joint coupling angles (CA) and coupling angle variability (CAV) were calculated using modified vector coding for the STS task (Chang et al., 2008; Ippersiel et al., 2024; Needham et al., 2014). For the hip-knee pairing, the proximal and distal segments were defined as hip and knee, respectively. For the spinehip pairing, the proximal and distal segments were defined as spine and hip, respectively.

Distal and proximal sagittal plane joint angle waveforms were plotted on the vertical and horizontal axes of an angle-angle plot.

Vector coding was performed separately for time-normalized early and late phases of STS. CAs were calculated from the vector connecting consecutive points of the angleangle plot and expressed from 0° and 360° and categorized into four coordination patterns: in-phase distal dominant, in-phase proximal dominant, anti-phase distal dominant, and anti-phase proximal dominant (Figure 2, Supplemental Figure 1). In-phase indicates that two joints are rotating in the same direction, while anti-phase indicates rotations in opposite directions. Proximal or distal dominant coordination reflects that the angular change of one joint was greater than that of the other one over a set time interval.

Coordination pattern frequencies (i.e., bin frequencies) were calculated at the trial level and then averaged within participants to preserve trial-level information prior to group summaries (Napoli et al., 2025). Mean CAs were calculated using circular statistics.

Coordination variability was quantified as coupling angle variability (CAV). For each participant, pointwise CAV was calculated across repeated trials using circular dispersion of CAs. Early-and late-STS CAV was then calculated by averaging the pointwise CAV waveform across individual phases. Simply, pointwise CAV represents how consistent or variable the coordination pattern was across repeated trials at each moment of the STS phase.

2.8. Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all demographic factors, patient questionnaires, and outcome variables.

To examine between-group differences in sagittal plane kinematics, statistical parametric mapping (SPM) was applied across the time-normalized early and late STS phases, separately for the hip, knee, ankle, spine, and pelvis (Pataky, 2010). Two-tailed independent sample t-tests (spm1d.stats.ttest2) were used. To account for potential between-participant differences in initial joint or segment position, each waveform was offset-referenced by subtracting the mean value of the first five samples from the entire waveform prior to analysis. Thus, SPM analyses evaluated between-group differences in angular change from the initial seated position rather than absolute joint or segment angle.

As a secondary analysis, Welch's t-tests were run to compare FAI and healthy groups on discrete biomechanical measures, namely peak angle and joint range of motion for the hip, knee, ankle, spine, and pelvis, separately for the early, late, and full STS phases. Hedges g quantified effect sizes.

To examine group differences in spine-hip and hip-knee coordination variability (CAV), two-way mixed ANOVAs were used. This test examined the effects of group (FAI vs. Healthy) and their interaction (group x phase), separately for the spine-hip and hip-knee joint pairs. When a statistically significant interaction occurred, post-hoc Bonferroniadjusted pairwise comparisons were performed. Effect sizes were reported using ηₚ² and Cohen's d.

Coordination-pattern frequencies were summarized descriptively because the four categories form interdependent proportions of the same movement phase and summed to 100%. Therefore, inferential testing was not considered appropriate.

Assumptions for statistical tests were verified. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and inspection of Q-Q plots, and homogeneity of variance was examined via Levene's test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all tests. Statistical analyses were conducted using Jamovi (V.2.7.7.0) and MATLAB.

2.9. Sample size

A retrospective sample-size calculation was performed using a previously reported between-group difference in hip-lumbar coordination variability during STS (Ippersiel et al., 2018). Using α = 0.05.80% power, and an estimated effect size of Hedges g = 0.86, approximately 22 participants per group would be required. Analyses were performed in G*Power 3.1.

3. RESULTS

>RESULTS

3.1. Data and participant characteristics

Data from three participants (2 FAI and 1 healthy) were excluded due to technical problems during data collections. Biomechanical data were screened for statistical outliers which resulted in the omission of 47/1648 waveforms (2.85%). The final sample comprised cam-type FAI while 15 had a bilateral diagnosis. In all cases, the symptomatic, pre-surgical limb was used for analyses. Based on Welch's t-tests, participants with FAI were statistically older than healthy controls and had smaller scores on all HOOS subscales (p < 0.05). The FAI group had a descriptively higher proportion of men than the control group (95.8% vs. 73.7%); however, this was not statistically different (p > 0.05). Full participant characteristics are listed in Table 1.

3.2. Group differences in sagittal plane kinematics

Statistical parametric mapping revealed no significant group differences in sagittal plane hip, knee, ankle, pelvis, and spine angles for the early or late phases of the STS task (p > 0.05, Figure 3, Supplemental Figure 2). Trends were observed and suggested decreased hip flexion and pelvic anterior tilting, alongside greater spinal flexion in FAI patients during the latter portion of early STS (i.e., close to seat-off); however, these were not statistically significant.

Results of Welch's t-tests reporting group differences in ROM and peak angles by joint and phase are listed in Table 2. Of note, compared to healthy controls, FAI patients had less hip ROM during the early phase (mean difference = -3.50°; 95% CI: -6.85 to -0.15) and greater knee ROM during the late phase (mean difference = 5.60°; 95% CI: 0.04 to 11.16). Otherwise, greater peak knee flexion was observed during early-phase STS (mean difference = 4.90°; 95% CI: 0.54 to 9.26), late-phase STS (mean difference = 5.10°; 95% CI: 0.75 to 9.45), and the full task (mean difference = 4.90°; 95% CI: 0.54 to 9.26).

3.3. Group differences in coordination variability

For the spine-hip coupling, a group*phase interaction (p = 0.001, ηₚ²= 0.252) was observed. Bonferroni-adjusted follow-up Welch's t-test comparisons indicated a more variable coordination strategy during the early phase in the FAI group (mean difference = 6.21°, p = 0.010, Cohen's d = 0.915), but not during the late phase (mean difference = -0.926°, p = 0.537, Cohen's d = 0.205) (Figure 4). No other group effects or interactions were observed for the spine-hip and hip-knee couples (Table 3). Main effects of phase were observed for both joint pairs (p < 0.05), indicating that coordination variability differs between early and late STS, independent of group.

3.4. Coordination strategies

Frequencies were interpreted descriptively as interdependent components of the overall coordination profile (Figure 5).

During spine-hip early-phase STS, the FAI group showed a greater proportion of in-phase, spine-dominant coordination compared to healthy controls. In the late phase, both groups were predominantly in-phase, hip-dominant, with a modestly higher frequency in FAI.

During the early phase, healthy controls showed somewhat more in-phase, hipdominant hip-knee coordination; otherwise, profiles were broadly similar. In the late phase, the FAI group showed relatively more in-phase, knee-dominant coordination, while healthy controls showed relatively more in-phase, hip-dominant coordination. The hipknee late STS observation is the strongest pattern because it describes a redistribution between hip-and knee-dominant in-phase coordination.

4. DISCUSSION

This study compared sagittal plane kinematics and inter-joint coordination variability during STS in pre-operative individuals with cam-type FAI and healthy controls. SPM analyses revealed the FAI group and healthy controls had similar sagittal plane waveforms across the kinetic chain during STS, while secondary analyses revealed, on average, less hip ROM (early phase) and greater knee ROM (late phase) in FAI patients.

Greater peak knee flexion across all phases of STS was observed in FAI patients; however, this likely reflected differences in initial seated posture. Individuals with cam-type FAI also demonstrated greater spine-hip, but not hip-knee, coordination variability during early STS. These findings partially supported our hypotheses and move beyond discrete biomechanical measures to provide insight into whole-body movement strategies in people with FAI.

4.1. STS kinematics were broadly similar, with subtle phase-specific differences

Compared to healthy controls, FAI patients did not show statistically different hip, knee, ankle, pelvis, or spine kinematics via SPM analyses. The discrete analyses did show reduced hip ROM during early STS, greater peak knee flexion across all phases, and greater knee ROM during late STS in the FAI group.

The most directly comparable study examining STS kinematics in people with and without FAI reported findings that were broadly consistent with ours, observing no between-group differences in peak sagittal plane hip, knee, pelvis, and trunk flexion angles (Spiker et al., 2022). Our discrete analyses, however, identified greater peak knee flexion in FAI patients. The consistency of this difference across STS phases, together with visual inspection of the group mean waveforms suggests that it may primarily reflect a difference in starting position rather than a distinct movement adaptation (Figure 3). Post hoc Welch's t-tests comparing group differences in the mean knee flexion angle across the first 5 frames of the STS task supported this notion (mean difference = 4.94°; 95% CI = 0.54 to 9.35; p = 0.029), while no other differences in starting position were identified (Supplemental Table 1). Thus, despite efforts to standardize starting the STS position, the symptomatic limb of the FAI group was positioned, on average, in greater knee flexion at movement onset. Similarly, the greater knee ROM during late STS may also reflect the FAI group starting the phase in more knee flexion and extending towards a comparable standing position. It remains unclear whether this difference reflected a pain-related strategy, habitual seated posture, or residual variability in the starting position.

To account for differences in initial posture, the SPM analyses were referenced to the initial seated position. The lack of significant hip, spine and pelvic kinematic differences during STS in the SPM analyses was somewhat unexpected. Nevertheless, visual inspection of our group mean kinematic waveforms and SPM{t} trajectories (Figure 3, Supplemental figure 2), suggested small directional differences near seat-off, including smaller excursions towards anterior pelvic tilt and hip flexion, and greater spinal flexion excursion in the FAI group. Together, with the reduced early-phase hip ROM identified in the discrete analysis, these descriptive patterns raise the hypothesis that some people with FAI may redistribute sagittal plane motion away from the hip and towards the spine during the transition towards seat-off. More broadly, radiographic studies in hip pathology have shown that sagittal-plane deep-seated postures can be redistributed across the hip-pelvisspine chain towards greater posterior pelvic tilt and lumbar flexion when hip motion is limited (Verhaegen et al., 2026). However, the population, task, and measurement approach differed from our present study. Future studies using imaging-based measures of spinopelvic posture (e.g., EOS x-ray) may help identify subtle differences not captured by marker-based kinematics.

Reduced pelvic mobility has been reported during deep-squatting in cam-type FAI cohorts (Bagwell et al., 2016; Catelli et al., 2018; Lamontagne et al., 2009; Ng et al., 2015).

Interestingly, the direction of apparent pelvic motion in the present study (i.e., less excursion towards anterior tilt) differed from that commonly described during deep flexion (i.e., reduced posterior pelvic tilting). Less excursion towards anterior tilt may reflect the distinct mechanical demands of STS -which requires forward progression of the body's centre of mass through varying contribution from the spine, pelvis, and hips -rather than a more absolute posteriorly tilted pelvis. We can speculate that these subtle differences (reduced excursion into hip flexion and anterior pelvic tilting, alongside greater spinal flexion) hint at a redistribution of sagittal-plane motion towards the spine; however, because the hip, pelvis, and spine SPM trajectories remained below the critical threshold, the proposed interpretation should be considered hypothesis-generating. More demanding tasks, such as single-leg squat, may be more likely to reveal consistent movement differences between people with and without FAI (Luijendijk et al., 2026).

4.2. Greater spine-hip coordination variability in FAI patients during early-phase STS

Compared to healthy controls, people with FAI demonstrated greater between-trial coordination variability at spine-hip, but not hip-knee, joint pairs during early STS. In related work, Hao et al., used vector coding to compare hip flexion-adduction coordination variability in people with and without FAI during a single-leg squat, reporting no betweengroup differences (Hao et al., 2026). Importantly, although this differed from our findings, Hao et al., examined multiplanar coordination within the hip, rather than a spine-hip coupling. Similar work in FAI reported altered coordination via smaller pelvic-femoral ratios (i.e., less relative pelvic contribution) during tasks such as squatting and stepping (Bagwell and Powers, 2019); however, the between-trial variability of this strategy was not explored. Otherwise, greater coordination variability has also been reported during earlyphase STS in people with low back pain, at the lower lumbar-hip couple (Ippersiel et al., 2018), although different clinical populations complicate comparisons with our work. Few comparable studies exist, which makes situating our findings difficult.

Greater spine-hip coordination variability in early-phase STS could have multiple interpretations. First, this behavior might indicate that variability exceeded a functional range, reflecting less stable or robust neuromuscular control and potentially exposing tissues/structures to abnormal loading (Hamill et al., 2012; Stergiou and Decker, 2011). This interpretation is broadly aligned with a systematic review which reported a trend towards greater variability in people with lower-extremity musculoskeletal injury, although this review was neither specific to coordination variability nor STS (Baida et al., 2018). In FAI patients, such variability may become more apparent during the mechanically demanding transition towards seat-off, which requires substantial hip flexion. However, whether it alters joint loading or contributes to impingement remains speculative.

Alternatively, greater spine-hip coordination variability may be an adaptive strategy to vary/redistribute load away from pain sensitive structures (Hamill et al., 2012; Hodges and Tucker, 2011). While hip joint contact forces have not been quantified in FAI patients during STS, modelling studies have demonstrated lower hip contact forces during key aspects of gait and squatting (Catelli et al., 2021; Ng et al., 2018; Savage et al., 2025). At face value, lower hip contact forces may be consistent with a protective unloading mechanism; however, Savage et al., reported that people with FAI walked with lower magnitude contact forces that were constrained to smaller regions of the acetabulum and femoral head (Savage et al., 2022), a pattern that may contribute to cartilage maladaptation.

Thus, we cannot say whether greater spine-hip coordination variability in FAI patients is adaptive or maladaptive; studying coordination alongside joint and regional tissue loading may help answer this question.

Despite greater spine-hip coordination variability in FAI, no group differences were identified in the hip-knee joint pair. However, the cam-type FAI group demonstrated relatively more in-phase, knee-dominant coordination during the late phase of STS, whereas the healthy group exhibited relatively more in-phase, hip-dominant coordination (Figure 4). Although this late-phase finding is a qualitative description, it may suggest a relative shift from hip-toward knee-dominant motion during rising.

4.3. Limitations

This work has certain limitations. First, we used a cross-sectional study design.

Thus, causal relationships between cam-type FAI and the observed biomechanics cannot be established. Additionally, our sample was predominately male with unbalanced group sizes. While cam-type FAI is more common in males, this nevertheless limits the generalizability of our findings for females. Additionally, the older age in our FAI sample may have impacted our findings. Further, the self-selected comfortable speed and STS motion itself may not have been challenging enough to elicit the full range of compensatory strategies in FAI patients. For context, Bagwell et al., reported peak hip flexion during squat in cam-type FAI patients reached 106.6° (Bagwell et al., 2016), while our peak hip flexion during STS was 77.1°; thus, perhaps STS does not challenge the hip sufficiently to elicit adaptation. Also, the CAV was calculated using a maximum of 5 trials, which may limit the reliability of our variability estimates. Despite our efforts to standardize the STS starting position, it appears the FAI group had more knee flexion on the affected side. To account for this, we referenced our SPM analyses to the starting position; however, this may have influenced our results. Last, compared with imaging-based subject-specific models, the use of generic marker-based models has been shown to introduce systematic offsets in sagittal plane hip and pelvic angle estimates in tasks involving substantial hip flexion. While our SPM analyses were offset to seated posture which helps mitigate this issue, the use of generic models may have influenced our estimates of absolute joint angles and peak angles measures (Al Otti et al., 2024).

5. CONCLUSION

Compared to healthy controls, people with FAI had greater spine-hip coordination variability and reduced hip ROM during early-phase STS, despite broadly similar kinematic waveforms. This suggests that movement adaptations during STS may be reflected more strongly in inter-joint coordination than in changes in individual joint kinematics.
