Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Abstract

Acute child malnutrition has historically been approached in public health as an individual nutritional deficit, despite its persistence in territories marked by structural inequity. This study analyzed how the general, particular, and singular domains of the social determination of health articulate with the intersectional axes of gender, ethnicity, class, and territory in the social production of child hunger in Chocó and Risaralda, two Colombian departments with a high burden of acute child malnutrition.

We conducted a qualitative comparative case study grounded in a socio-critical paradigm and a critical feminist epistemology, based on fieldwork carried out in 2024.

In-depth interviews with 28 participants-mothers and caregivers, health workers, and decision-makers-were combined with a field diary and a documentary review. Analysis followed open coding, axial coding, and categorical integration, yielding 722 coded units and 207 analytical categories, which were organized through a convergence matrix across the three domains of social determination and the intersectional axes. In the particular domain, informal work, food restriction, and institutional fragmentation shaped ways of life marked by the feminization of care, while formally established care pathways failed to sustain nutritional recovery. In the singular domain, trajectories of accumulated violence, forced early unions, and female genital mutilation preceded and shaped motherhood under precarious conditions, together with guilt, anguish, and fear produced by the institutional moralization of maternal care. Across domains, structural conditions were subsumed into collective ways of life and embodied in singular trajectories, without cancelling situated forms of agency, resistance, and care. A multilevel integration of social determination and intersectionality allows child hunger.

Introduction

Acute child malnutrition has historically been addressed by public health as an individual problem of nutritional deficit, correctable through anthropometric surveillance and food supplementation. However, in Colombian departments such as Chocó and Risaralda, where child mortality associated with malnutrition remains a persistent burden despite the normative and programmatic strengthening of recent decades, this individualizing approach shows clear explanatory limits. In response, the social determination of health-a perspective developed within Latin American collective health-proposes understanding health-disease processes as the result of the way society organizes the production, distribution, and reproduction of life, rather than as the sum of isolated risk factors [1]. From this perspective, child hunger ceases to be a biomedical event confined to the child's body and becomes the situated expression of historical power relations that unequally distribute the possibilities of living, falling ill, and dying [2].

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a qualitative comparative case study [7], framed within a socio-critical paradigm and a critical feminist epistemology, as part of a broader doctoral research project on the social determination of acute child malnutrition in Chocó and Risaralda, Colombia. The comparative case design pursued analytical rather than statistical generalization, in the sense proposed by Yin, which made it possible to identify patterns . and configurations that transcend the particular cases [7]. The two departments were selected through purposive theoretical sampling aimed at maximizing structural contrast: Chocó, with historical conditions of exclusion and generalized structural poverty, and Risaralda, with better aggregate departmental indicators that nonetheless conceal critical pockets of ethnic-territorial exclusion in municipalities with Emberá Chamí Indigenous populations. Fieldwork was carried out in 2024 in both departments and included nutritional recovery centers and rural communities.

Participants and sampling

Twenty-eight participants were included: mothers and caregivers of children under five years of age treated for moderate or severe acute malnutrition in the previous two years; health care personnel with at least one year of experience in the care of affected children; and institutional actors with decision-making capacity in epidemiological surveillance, policy formulation, or program implementation. A caregiver was defined as the person who devotes most of their time to the child's care. Sampling was purposive, aimed at maximizing the diversity of experiences and positions relevant to the study aims. Caregivers were contacted at the nutritional recovery centers in the study territories, and all people invited agreed to participate. Participants were recruited between 1 July 2024 and 30 September 2024. Participants were distributed between Chocó (n = 16) and Risaralda (n = 12) and comprised caregivers (n = 8, all adult women, ranging in age from 18 to over 50 years), nutritionists (n = 4), a nurse (n = 1), a physician (n = 1), program coordinators (n = 3), a social worker (n = 1), decision-makers in public and institutional management (n = 8), and traditional midwives or breastfeeding counselors (n = 2). People who did not provide informed consent and caregivers of children whose malnutrition was associated with specific secondary clinical conditions-cerebral palsy, severe dysphagia, or esophageal atresia-were excluded, in order to keep the focus on malnutrition linked to social determinants.

Data collection

Data were collected through semi-structured in-depth interviews guided by thematic guides that prioritized biographical accounts of the child's malnutrition, household living conditions, and experiences of institutional care [8]. Two types of guide were used: a thematic biographical interview for caregivers and a semi-structured interview for health professionals and decision-makers; both were applied flexibly, following the dynamics of each conversation. The guides were pilot tested before fieldwork, and each participant was interviewed once. Interviews were conducted by the first author, a nutritionist-dietitian and doctoral candidate in public health with prior professional experience in the treatment of severe acute malnutrition and in the epidemiological surveillance of malnutrition deaths, who adopted a reflexive and relational stance toward participants [8]; they lasted approximately one hour on average and took place in locations agreed with participants that ensured privacy. With the exception of one decision-maker in Chocó who was previously known to the interviewer, there was no prior relationship with participants. Interviews with Emberá participants were conducted with the support of local translators. All interviews were audio-recorded with informed consent, transcribed verbatim-including pauses, silences, and relevant emotional expressions-by trained external staff under confidentiality agreements, and . reviewed and anonymized by the first author before analysis; transcripts were not returned to participants. In addition, the first author moderated a focus group lasting approximately two hours with eight hospital workers involved in the care of children with acute malnutrition, in order to explore collective institutional perspectives; quotations from this source are identified as "focus group". A field diary was kept with contextual observations, emerging analytical notes, and reflections on the researcher's position, including the territorial security conditions that shaped fieldwork, among them an armed strike that delayed access to one of the study sites. A documentary review of public policies, technical guidelines, constitutional case law related to the right to food, and institutional documents from both departments was also carried out, with a contextual and interpretive function rather than as an independent component of the analysis.

Data analysis

Qualitative analysis was conducted through coding in three phases: open coding to identify units of meaning, axial coding to establish relationships between categories, and categorical integration to articulate interpretive configurations across the general, particular, and singular domains of social determination [9]. Analysis was carried out in parallel with fieldwork, which allowed data collection to be adjusted to explore emerging themes; data collection ended when new interviews no longer yielded new categories (data saturation). The process generated 722 coded units and 207 analytical categories, organized in a Microsoft Excel matrix that facilitated the systematization and comparison of patterns across actors and territories without replacing the interpretive . process. Analysis was conducted by the first author and discussed with the co-authors throughout the process. Final integration was performed through a convergence matrix organized by objective, territory, and domain of determination, which made it possible to examine convergences, complementarities, and divergences between documentary sources and interviews, preventing one component from functioning merely as an illustration of another. Epidemiological findings for the general domain, reported in a separate manuscript, were incorporated into this analysis only as a contextual reference for interpreting their articulation with the particular and singular domains. Quotations are identified by anonymized participant codes indicating actor type (M, mother or caregiver; Abuela, grandmother caregiver; Trab, health worker; Tom, decision-maker) and department (Ch, Chocó; R, Risaralda); they were translated from Spanish into English by the authors. Once analysis was completed, results were returned to participants through a narrated story, built from the accounts of the different actors and shared as a video, which was sent to all participants together with a summary of the results.

In both departments, household income did not constitute a stable base from which to plan food, but rather a daily variable organized around urgency. Accounts described fragmented income from occasional informal work ("below the minimum wage", M-Ch-1) or from informal work combined with occasional sales (M-R-1). When daily income was insufficient, households reorganized the frequency, quantity, and quality of meals: one mother described her child's daily diet as "plantain, rice and, when there is some, a little panela [unrefined cane sugar]" (M-R-1); another noted that she sometimes merged breakfast and lunch because there was not always food (M-Ch-2). These strategies of reducing and merging meals do not reflect a lack of nutritional knowledge: when one mother was asked what she would like to receive as food assistance, she answered "vegetables, fruit, milk, things like that" (M-Ch-1), revealing the gap between knowing, wanting, and being able to access food [14].

The same precariousness permeated the institutional response: a decision-maker in Chocó described epidemiological surveillance sustained by contractors hired late and intermittently-"contracts start late… deaths are frequent" (Tom-Ch-2)-showing that informality not only affects families but is reproduced within the very state apparatus responsible for responding to the problem.

Organization of care.

In both territories, the organization of child care followed a consistent pattern: women assumed full responsibility for children's feeding, supervision, and well-being, a pattern legitimized even by the women themselves: "as women we are specialized in that" (M-Ch-1). A health professional confirmed this from their territorial experience: "the head of a household is the woman… the mother is the one who mostly has the responsibility" (Trab-Ch-3). Male control also extended to economic resources intended for care: "the father is waiting at the door for her to hand him the money [from the subsidy]" (health worker focus group), which may reduce the protective effect of conditional cash transfers aimed at children.

Institutional response and cultural mediations.

Care pathways exist formally, but, as one decision-maker summarized, "the pathway exists, but it does not walk" (Tom-Ch-2).

Post-discharge follow-up revealed the clearest limit of the model: "when those children go back home, they return to the same situations… that ends up becoming a vicious circle for us" (Tom-Ch-1), showing that the system intervenes correctly on the individual body without being able to modify the conditions to which the child returns. In Emberá communities, malnutrition was not uniformly classified as a biomedical entity: the Jaibaná (traditional Emberá healer) acts as a therapeutic authority who intervenes in the decision to seek care in the health system-"until the Jaibaná says: this is not mine… you can take him" (Tom-Ch-2)-within a culturally structured, non-arbitrary system of medical pluralism [15, 16]. One mother attributed her child's deterioration to malaria rather than malnutrition, an interpretation consistent with a context where this disease is endemic and where the biomedical category of malnutrition may have no direct equivalent in the Emberá classification system (M-R-1). The institutional response showed limited capacity for dialogue with these systems: technologies delivered without appropriation-"they have built them smokeless stoves… and they are cooking on stones" (focus group)-and donated foods that go unprepared because they are incompatible with the available domestic conditions.

Singular domain: trajectories, emotions, and agency in precarious motherhood

The singular domain showed how general conditions and collective ways of life are embodied in the concrete trajectories, bodies, and experiences of women caregivers [3, 17].

Trajectories of accumulated violence. One young mother, when asked about the most difficult moments of her life before becoming a mother, reconstructed a chain of structural ruptures that began in early childhood: sexual abuse, family disbelief ("but she didn't believe me", M-Ch-5), separation from her family of origin, a legal complaint that did not prosper ("they told me no… that there were no witnesses", M-Ch-5), and an adolescent pregnancy that interrupted her schooling, years before her own child required nutritional recovery. When asked whether she still felt resentment, she replied: "I still feel resentment" (M-Ch-5). This trajectory cannot be read as an exceptional story of individual misfortune: the child's malnutrition does not begin at birth, but much earlier, in the mother's unprotected childhood [18]. A narrative with a similar structural weight came from another mother, interviewed through a translator, who had been forced into a union during childhood [19] and was raising her children alone. She described being unable to make decisions about her own reproduction ("she did not use family planning… the husband did not want to", M-R-1) and a structural loneliness sustained over time: "she is always alone with the children, she feels weary with them" (M-R-1). In addition, the field diary recorded-although it was not the subject of the structured interviews-the silenced persistence of female genital mutilation among Emberá girls in the region. In Colombia, this practice has been documented mainly in Emberá communities of Risaralda and Chocó: 204 cases were reported between 2020 and 2025, 177 of them in Indigenous girls and more than half in girls aged zero to five years; cases reach the health system predominantly through complications such as sepsis, which also indicates considerable underreporting [20].

Emberá community leadership itself has promoted its eradication since 2010, a process that in 2026 led to Law 2609, the first specific legislation in Latin America prohibiting the practice and mandating a public policy for its prevention, care, and eradication with an intercultural approach [21]. The co-occurrence of forced early union, motherhood without reproductive autonomy, and genital mutilation in the same territory allows these processes to be read as a single inscription of patriarchal control over girls' bodies, which precedes and prefigures the malnutrition of their future children.

Emotions: institutionally produced guilt, anguish, and fear. The emotions running through the narratives were not random individual reactions but socially produced processes [22]. Anguish in the face of diagnostic uncertainty emerged most persistently: "it distresses me a lot… a thousand things go through my head" (M-Ch-1). Maternal guilt was the most cross-cutting emotional process: "yes, I feel guilty, because financially… there were no… resources" (M-Ch-1); another mother, who had stopped breastfeeding to care for a seriously ill relative, wondered: "if I had been there… I think he would not have lost weight" (M-Ch-4). In no case did guilt correspond to an objective failure of care, but rather to structural conditions-insufficient income, the impossibility of being with two sick relatives at the same time, the child's previous illnesses-reinterpreted by the mothers themselves as personal failure, which can be read as a form of symbolic violence [23]. This guilt did not emerge spontaneously: one mother reported that, after . missing a check-up to care for another of her children, institutional staff warned her that her daughter could be taken away ("that if something happens to the girl they will take her away from me", M-Ch-1), producing a fear that, as a health worker in Risaralda confirmed, keeps other families away from the system that could care for them ("they don't go to the hospital… I don't know whether to take him", Trab-R-4).

Agency and sustained care. The narratives were not limited to suffering. Asked about the happiest moment of her life, the mother who had been abused in childhood answered without hesitation by naming her children (M-Ch-5); another framed happiness as the very survival of her children: "knowing that they are alive, that they are well… there we go" (M-Ch-1). Faith operated as a genuine protective resource: "I believe in God… I paid attention to his feeding… and he recovered" (M-Ch-1). And the demand for food autonomy expressed by a caregiver in Risaralda-"support is needed to grow crops in the resguardo [Indigenous reserve]… to have their own crops to draw from" (Abuela-R-2)-did not express a request for charity but the affirmation of a right to produce one's own food [24]. These forms of agency, resistance, and situated knowledge do not negate the conditions of oppression described; they show that, even in precariousness, women caregivers deploy active strategies to sustain their children's lives [25, 26].

Articulation between domains: subsumption and relative autonomy

The joint reading of the three domains revealed the two dialectical movements that social determination attributes to its analytical architecture: subsumption and relative autonomy [3]. Subsumption became visible in the trajectory of M-Ch-5: the structural . deprivation of the general domain-poverty and the institutional fragmentation of child protection-was translated into a particular way of life marked by the absence of family protection networks, and was embodied in the singular domain as sexual abuse that was not believed, being handed over to third parties, and adolescent motherhood without reproductive autonomy. Similarly, the loss of food sovereignty in the general domain was translated in the particular domain into household strategies of reducing and merging meals, and was embodied in the singular domain in the anguish and guilt of mothers who knew, without being able to guarantee it, the diet that would have better protected their children.

At the same time, relative autonomy became visible in the forms of agency identified across domains: in the particular domain, in the persistence of Emberá knowledge systems that guide therapeutic decisions according to their own logic; in the singular domain, in faith, filial love, and the explicit demands for food sovereignty voiced by women caregivers. None of these expressions reverses the structural conditions that produce them, but they show that individuals and collectives have situated margins of action and resistance that analysis cannot omit without reducing families to passive victims [3]. This articulation also explains why the cross-cutting axis of gender and structural racism traverses the three domains with different densities: it is expressed in the general domain as the territorial and ethnic-racial concentration of risk, in the particular domain as the feminization of care work and the male capture of resources intended for children, and in the singular domain as an institutional moralization that shifts onto women responsibilities that exceed their material possibilities.

Discussion

The main contribution of this article is the empirical operationalization of the articulation between social determination and intersectionality through a three-domain model that does not remain at the general level of aggregate indicators, but explicitly shows how structural inequities are translated into collective ways of life and embodied in singular biographical trajectories. This exercise responds to a recurrent critique of the social determinants of health approach: its tendency to fragment reality into isolated variables and to propose interventions that do not question the structures that produce inequality [1]. By showing the subsumption of structural processes into concrete bodily experiences, the model proposed here provides empirical support for the argument that child hunger cannot be understood or addressed solely through the biomedical management of the nutritional event.

The findings of the singular domain constitute, in particular, a little-documented contribution to the literature on child malnutrition in Colombia: the identification of a chain in which childhood sexual violence, forced early union, and female genital mutilation precede and shape motherhood under conditions of extreme precariousness. This finding is consistent with evidence on historically structured biographical trajectories, according to which inequality accumulates and settles at different moments of the life course [27], and suggests that a child's malnutrition does not begin at the moment of clinical diagnosis but in processes that started much earlier in the mother's life [18]. Although female genital mutilation, recorded in the field diary, was not directly addressed in the interviews, it cannot be treated as anecdotal: recent Colombian . legislation prohibiting the practice and mandating its care with an intercultural approach [21] shows that it is a recognized public health and human rights problem beyond this study, and reinforces the need for child malnutrition surveillance systems to incorporate indicators of gender-based violence in childhood.

The analysis of the particular domain showed that the institutional moralization of maternal care-expressed in threats to remove custody when check-ups are missed because of precariousness itself-constitutes a mechanism through which the health system shifts onto women structural responsibilities that it cannot itself guarantee. This reading is consistent with the notion of a crisis of care, according to which economies depend on women's reproductive work without guaranteeing the conditions for its sustainability [28], and with the characterization of maternal guilt as a form of symbolic violence in which the consequences of structural conditions come to be perceived as personal failure [23]. Far from being a side effect of care, our findings suggest that this moralization operates as an active barrier to access: mothers and health workers agreed that the fear of losing custody of a child keeps families away from the system that could care for them.

The medical pluralism identified in Emberá communities in Risaralda-the mediation of the Jaibaná and the classification of malnutrition as malaria within their own knowledge system-should not be interpreted as a cultural barrier to be overcome through health education, but as a culturally structured system of diagnosis and care with which the biomedical system has failed to engage in effective dialogue [15, 16]. Attributing delays in care-seeking exclusively to "cultural beliefs"-as some of the institutional staff . interviewed did-shifts responsibility onto families and obscures the geographic, linguistic, and institutional-trust barriers that actually shape access [29].

From a public health perspective, these findings suggest three priority lines of action.

First, incorporating routine screening for gender-based violence and early unions into care protocols for acute child malnutrition, in coordination with child protection pathways. Second, replacing custody threats with social support strategies that recognize families' material barriers, in line with the reinforced protection recognized by the Colombian Constitutional Court regarding timely, continuous, and comprehensive access to health care [30] and minimum guarantees of water and sanitation in Indigenous territories [31]. Third, building intercultural care models that incorporate, under conditions of horizontal dialogue rather than subordination, the knowledge systems and therapeutic authorities of the Emberá people, instead of treating them as obstacles to be overcome through one-way health education.

This study has limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. The aggregate epidemiological component of social determination in these territories is reported in a separate manuscript; therefore, this article does not include its own quantitative analysis of the general domain but draws on it as a contextual reference.

The qualitative sample size, although adequate for the comprehensive purpose of the study, does not allow statistical generalization; its value lies in the analytical generalization of the proposed model [7]. The study centered the narratives of mothers and caregivers, which entailed lower representation of male voices and of higher-level institutional decision-makers. Although interviews with Emberá mothers were . conducted with translation, rendering culturally situated experiences into academic categories entails losses of meaning. Fieldwork was also constrained by territorial security conditions, including an armed strike that delayed access to one of the sites.

Finally, female genital mutilation emerged from the field diary rather than from the interview guides, which limits the depth of its documentation in this study and points to the need for specific, culturally appropriate research on the topic, developed in direct dialogue with the Emberá authorities and women's organizations that have led its eradication.

Conclusions

The multilevel integration of the social determination of health and intersectionality makes it possible to understand child hunger in Chocó and Risaralda as a historically produced process that simultaneously traverses structures, collective ways of life, and singular bodily trajectories, without cancelling the situated forms of agency and resistance through which women caregivers sustain their children's lives. Institutional responses centered on individual clinical recovery are necessary but insufficient unless they are articulated with transformations in working conditions, intercultural barriers to access, effective protection against gender-based violence in childhood, and the social redistribution of care. The three-domain model proposed here offers a transferable analytical tool for future critical public health research seeking to integrate structural, collective, and biographical evidence in understanding other expressions of the social determination of health in contexts of high territorial and ethnic inequity.
