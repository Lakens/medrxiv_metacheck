Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 25 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. Abstract

Care (Education) and Treatment Reviews (C(E)TRs) were introduced in 2015 to prevent unnecessary hospital admissions amongst people with intellectual disability and autistic people. Little is known about people who receive community C(E)TRs or the effectiveness of community C(E)TRs in reducing admissions.

To estimate the causal effect of community C(E)TRs on psychiatric hospital admission, and compare length of hospital stay between patients who did and did not have a pre-admission community C(E)TR.

We conducted a retrospective observational study using de-identified electronic health record data from 2,495 patients. We characterised the sociodemographic and clinical profiles of people with intellectual disability and autistic people who received a community C(E)TR between 2015 and 2025.

We used propensity score weighting and a difference-in-differences analysis to compare changes in psychiatric hospital admissions in the six months before and after community C(E)TRs, with changes over corresponding periods in matched controls. Kaplan-Meier analysis was used to compare lengths of hospital stay between patients who had a pre-admission C(E)TR and those who did not.

TRs were identified in the intellectual disability group and 382 in the autistic group. The intellectual disability group had a median age of 21 years and were predominately male (60.7%) whereas the autistic group were younger (median age 16 years) with a higher proportion of females (55.2%). Community C(E)TRs reduced the odds of psychiatric hospital admission over six months compared with controls (pooled OR = 0.47, 95% CI 0.26-0.85) but there was no difference over 12 months (pooled OR = 0.81, 95% CI 0.48-1.35). Length of hospital stay did not differ between those with and without a pre-admission C(E)TR.

These findings suggest that C(E)TRs may delay rather than prevent admissions and highlight the need for adequately resourced, sustainable community services to support people at high risk of admission.

Introduction

Intellectual disability and autism are lifelong neurodevelopmental conditions, each present in 1-2% of the population. People with intellectual disability and autistic people experience higher rates of mental disorder than those without these conditions, reflecting a combination of biological, psychological, and social vulnerabilities 1, 2 . A significant minority of people with intellectual disability and autistic people display behaviours that challenge, which may contribute to psychiatric hospital admission when associated with significant risks 3, 4.

Although psychiatric hospital admission is sometimes necessary, there have been longstanding concerns about an over-reliance on in-patient care for people with intellectual disability and autistic people, both in England and internationally [5] [6] [7] [8] [9] . These concerns include prolonged admissions without clear therapeutic goals, the use of restrictive practices such as physical restraint, and inappropriate psychotropic prescribing 10 . Care (Education) and Treatment Reviews (C(E)TRs) were introduced through national policy published in 2015 as structured person-centred review meetings 5 , chaired by a commissioner, which involve two independent advisors (a clinical expert and an expert-by-experience), the person, their family or carers, and relevant professionals. A community C(E)TR is convened when someone is considered at high risk of psychiatric hospital admission, with the aim of identifying and mobilising community supports that could enable the person to remain living safely in the community. If admission is required, a community C(E)TR can define therapeutic goals and establish a clear pathway towards discharge.

There has been very limited evaluation of community C(E)TRs, including who receives them and whether they reduce psychiatric hospital admissions or length of stay. A small clinical audit found that few admitted patients had a pre-admission C(E)TR and those who had experienced longer hospital stays than those who did not 11 . A time-series analysis using national data found that higher rates of pre-admission C(E)TRs were associated with a higher ratio of psychiatric hospital admissions to discharges 12 . These findings may reflect greater clinical complexity and higher admission risk of people receiving community C(E)TRs, rather than effects of the reviews themselves. However, as the first study included only admitted patients and the second used aggregated inpatient data, neither could establish whether community C(E)TRs prevent hospital admission.

In this study, we aimed to 1) characterise the sociodemographic and clinical profiles of people receiving community C(E)TRs, 2) estimate the effect of community C(E)TRs on psychiatric hospital admissions, and 3) examine the association between community C(E)TRs and length of hospital stay amongst those admitted.

Method Study design and population

The protocol for this retrospective observational study was published before data analysis 13 . Data were extracted from the South London and Maudsley (SLaM) NHS Foundation Trust electronic health record (EHR) using the Clinical Record Interactive Search (CRIS) system. The study included people with a recorded diagnosis of intellectual disability or autism, ascertained from structured fields (ICD-10 diagnostic codes F70-79 and any F84.*, respectively) and through free text searches across individuals' EHR using natural language processing (NLP) 14 (also see Supplementary Text S1).

Descriptive analyses were conducted separately for people with intellectual disability with or without co-occurring autism, and autistic people without intellectual disability. Diagnostic groups were combined for the difference-in-differences analysis to increase statistical power.

Procedures

Eligible participants had at least a one-year continuous period of care within a SLaM community mental health service between January 2015 and December 2023. C(E)TRs were identified in the EHR using free-text searches (Supplementary Text S2), with dates cross-checked against a record of in-patient admissions to ensure that only community C(E)TRs were included. Relevant demographic and clinical data (e.g. year of birth, diagnoses) were extracted.

Up to five controls (people who did not have a community C(E)TR) were extracted for each case (those with a community C(E)TR) based on age (±3 years), sex, and total care episode length before the date of the community C(E)TR (index date, ±2 years). Controls were assigned a pseudo-index date corresponding to the C(E)TR date. Controls needed to have an active community team episode and no inpatient episode on their assigned pseudo-index date. Individual controls could be matched to multiple C(E)TR events. A preprocessing pipeline was applied to ensure distinct community C(E)TRs were included with a requirement for active SLaM involvement in the six-month periods before and after each C(E)TR (Figure 1 and Supplementary Text S3).

A separate dataset was extracted for all psychiatric hospital admissions to SLaM in-patient services lasting at least 14 days amongst people with intellectual disability or autistic people. 124 characteristics are presented using these data. Primary outcomes

Primary outcomes were psychiatric hospitalisation within 6 months of a community C(E)TR (or pseudo-index date for controls) and length of psychiatric hospital stay amongst patients who were admitted.

Descriptive and secondary outcome variables

Variables included age, sex, ethnicity, socioeconomic deprivation (measured using the Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD) 2019), psychiatric diagnoses (by ICD-10 sub-chapter), psychotropic medication prescription (antipsychotic, mood stabiliser, anti-depressant, hypnotic/anxiolytic, medication for ADHD) (see Table 1). We compared the six-month periods before and after each community C(E)TR (or pseudo-index date for controls) for recorded psychiatric symptoms (e.g. aggression, agitation, suicidal thoughts), number of face-to-face community contacts with mental health professionals, and recorded mentions of Home Treatment Team (HTT), care package, or home care interventions ascertained using relevant NLP applications. A clinician-rated outcome measure, the Health of the Nation Outcome Scales (HoNOS) 15 , was also extracted within six months before or after the community C(E)TR, where available (Supplementary Text S4).

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using R Studio version 4.4.0 and Stata version 18. Frequencies and percentages are reported for categorical variables and mean (SD) or median (IQR) for continuous and count variables according to their underlying distributions. Within-individual statistical differences in prevs. post-community C(E)TRs (or pre-vs. post-pseudo-index dates for controls) were captured using McNemar's Chi-squared test, and between-group differences were calculated using the Wilcoxon rank sum test (Mann-Whitney U-test).

The primary outcome analysis used propensity weighting followed by difference-in-differences (DiD) modelling to estimate the effect of community C(E)TRs on psychiatric hospital admission.

Propensity scores were calculated using a multivariable logistic regression model 16 incorporating baseline (pre-C(E)TR / index date) variables considered to be potential confounders of the association between community C(E)TRs and hospital admission (included age, sex, ethnicity, deprivation, diagnostic group, psychiatric diagnosis, psychotropic medication prescription). Propensity scores were converted to weights via the inverse probability of treatment weighting method 17 . A DiD model 18 was then used to estimate the effect of community C(E)TRs, by estimating the change in hospitalisation over time for the C(E)TR compared to control group, reported as an odds ratio (OR) (Supplementary Text S5).

Propensity model variables were assessed for missingness, and multiple . imputation by chained equations was applied to produce five complete datasets 19 . The primary outcome analysis was run separately to each of these datasets and results were merged using Rubin's rules 20.

Three sensitivity analyses were conducted on the odds of hospital admission: 1) excluding C(E)TRs where the exact date was uncertain, 2) extending the propensity score model to include recorded psychiatric symptoms and mental health service use in the six-month period before the C(E)TR / pseudo-index date; and 3) restricting the analysis to the first community C(E)TR for each individual. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were plotted for length of psychiatric hospital stay for the admitted patients dataset and compared patients with and without a community C(E)TR in the six months preceding admission.

Ethical approval

CRIS has overarching ethical approval from the Oxford Research Ethics Committee C (reference 23/SC/0257) and the current study was approved by the local CRIS Oversight Committee (reference 2455/24-088).

Results

We identified 446 community C(E)TRs for 204 individuals with intellectual disability and matched 1,701 pseudo-index dates across 1,023 people with intellectual disability who acted as controls between 2015 and 2023 (Figure 1). There were 382 community C(E)TRs for 217 autistic people, matched to 1,308 pseudo-index dates across 1,051 autistic controls. The annual number of community C(E)TRs increased gradually and peaked in 2021, followed by a sharp decline, particularly in the autistic group (Supplementary Figure S1).

Characteristics of people receiving a community C(E)TR

People with intellectual disability who received a community C(E)TR had a median age of 21 years (IQR 14.8) and 60.7% were male. Just under half were Black (45.5%), with the next most frequent ethnic group being White (39.0%) (Table 1). These characteristics are broadly consistent with those of the intellectual disability control group and the wider population of people with intellectual disability accessing SLaM services (Supplementary Text S6). Socioeconomic deprivation and the prevalence of psychiatric diagnoses were also similar between C(E)TR recipients and controls.

Autistic people who received a community C(E)TR had a median age of 16 years (IQR 3.0), with more females than males in the sample (55.2% female), in contrast to the wider SLaM autistic population, of whom approximately 65% are male (Supplementary Text S6). Compared with controls, a higher proportion of C(E)TR recipients were White (76.3% vs 62.3%; p<0.001) and a lower proportion were Black (9.3% vs. 26.6%; p<.001). Autistic people who received a community C(E)TR were significantly less deprived than controls (mean IMD 18.5 vs 24.0, p<.001) and had a higher prevalence of anxiety disorders (38.7% vs 25.9% of controls, χ 2 (1)=23.1, p<.001).

Psychotropic medication prescribing before the C(E)TR (or pseudo-index date) was substantially higher across all medication categories among both people with intellectual disability and autistic people who received a community C(E)TR compared with controls (Table 1).

Impact of community C(E)TRs on six-month psychiatric hospitalisation

Hospitalisation data were combined and analysed across both intellectual disability and autism groups (289 community C(E)TRs for 165 patients in the intellectual disability group, and 204 C(E)TRs for 161 patients in the autistic group; Figure 1). In people with intellectual disability receiving a community C(E)TR, psychiatric hospital admissions decreased from 12.5% in the preceding six months to 8.7% in the subsequent six months, compared with an increase from 3.3% to 4.4% in the control group (Table 2). A similar reduction was observed in the autistic C(E)TR group of 13.2% in the preceding six months to 9.3% in the six months after the C(E)TR, relative to almost no change in controls (2•9% to 2•7%). A propensity score weighting (see Supplementary Table S1) and DiD analysis showed that community C(E)TRs led to a greater reduction in the odds of psychiatric hospital admission over six months compared with controls (OR = 0.47, 95% CI 0.26-0.85).

Twelve-month impact of community C(E)TRs on psychiatric hospitalisation

The primary outcome analysis was extended to examine psychiatric hospital admissions over the 12 months before and after a community C(E)TR. After preprocessing, the analysis included 215 community C(E)TRs in 140 people with intellectual disability and 453 pseud-index dates in 311 controls, and 141 community C(E)TRs in 120 autistic people, matched to 168 pseudo-index dates in 161 controls. Hospitalisation proportions for the combined intellectual disability and autism cases were 15.2% in the 12 months before a C(E)TR and 16.0% in the 12 months after a C(E)TR (Table 2).

The control groups had hospitalization proportions of 7.8% in the 12 months pre-index date and 7.6% in the 12 months after. After propensity weighting and DiD analysis, there was no clear evidence of an effect of community C(E)TRs on psychiatric hospital admission over 12 months compared with controls (pooled OR = 0.81, 95% CI 0.48-1.35).

Changes in psychiatric symptoms and service use

Changes in recorded psychiatric symptoms and service use are shown in Supplementary Table S2. In people with intellectual disability receiving a community C(E)TR, the proportion of patients with a mention of aggression and agitation was significantly higher in the six-month period after compared to before a community C(E)TR, with no corresponding changes in controls (p<.05, see Supplementary Table S2). In autistic people who received a community C(E)TR, the proportion with a mention of suicidal ideation increased after a community C(E)TR, compared with a small reduction in controls (p<.05). Increases in recorded anxiety, aggression, agitation, and hostility were also significantly greater in autistic C(E)TR recipients than in controls (all p<.05). Too few patients had HoNOS measures in the relevant period for formal analysis (29 ID patients, Supplementary Text S4).

Mentions of care packages increased significantly following community C(E)TRs in both groups compared to controls (p<.05). There were no differences in the number of community contacts with healthcare professionals (Supplementary Table S3).

Length of psychiatric hospital stay

A separate dataset was extracted for those patients who were admitted to psychiatric hospital. Of 545 admissions in the intellectual disability group (340 patients), only five admissions (five patients) were preceded by a community C(E)TR within six months. There were 308 admissions (202 patients) in the autism group, with ten of those admissions (ten patients) occurring within six months after a community C(E)TR. Across both groups, only 1.8% of psychiatric hospital admissions were preceded by a recent community C(E)TR. The small numbers precluded robust statistical comparisons of length of stay between groups. A Kaplan-Meier survival plot on combined data across both the intellectual disability and autism groups shows substantial overlap in admission length between admissions with and without a preceding community C(E)TR (Figure 2). Extending the period to 12 months before admission identified 9 admissions in the intellectual disability group and 17 in the autistic group with a preceding C(E)TR, again with no clear separation between the survival curves (Figure 2).

Discussion

This study provides new evidence on the use and effectiveness of C(E)TRs, a cornerstone of national policy in England to improve support for people with intellectual disability and autistic people in the community and to prevent unnecessary hospital admissions. In our primary analysis, community C(E)TRs led to a 53% reduction in the odds of psychiatric hospital admission compared with controls.

However, when we extended the analysis to admissions over a 12-month period, the reduction in admissions was no longer significant. This suggests that the benefits of a community C(E)TR may diminish over time; it may be that additional support is mobilised immediately following the C(E)TR, helping to avert an admission, which is then not sustained or sufficient to address the underlying factors contributing to risk of admission. Hence, in some cases, community C(E)TRs might delay rather than prevent admissions.

We found that community C(E)TRs were predominantly undertaken for adolescents and young adults.

While the diagnostic and socio-demographic characteristics of people with intellectual disability who received community C(E)TRs was similar to their controls, autistic people who received a community C(E)TR had a lower level of deprivation compared to the control group and higher levels of anxiety disorders. The higher rates of anxiety disorders in autistic females, particularly in adolescents, is consistent with existing research in urban populations 21 . The young profile of C(E)TR recipients, particularly autistic people, may reflect differences in clinical need or historical under-recognition of autism in older adults 22 . Alternatively, the availability of keyworker support offered to younger people may facilitate the identification of unmet needs and coordination of C(E)TRs 23 . The higher proportion of White autistic people and a lower proportion of Black autistic people receiving community C(E)TRs compared to controls raise concerns about potential ethnic inequalities in access to reviews. Across both diagnostic groups, C(E)TR recipients were more likely to be prescribed all categories of psychotropic medications compared to controls, despite recorded rates of psychiatric diagnoses being similar, which may reflect the presence of more severe or persistent symptoms in people who received a community C(E)TR, prescribing for behaviours of concern, or potentially limited access to nonpharmacological interventions. The notable effect of community C(E)TRs on hospital admission was attenuated after additional adjustment for psychiatric symptoms and service use preceding the review. Although the point estimate remained below one, confidence intervals notably overlapped one. This suggests that differences in clinical presentation and service use between C(E)TR recipients and controls may partly explain the observed effect. Residual confounding by factors that are not adequately captured by routinely-collected data (e.g. severity, risk, and stability of community placements) may also be important. A sensitivity analysis taking only the first community C(E)TR substantially attenuated the six-month benefit in terms of reduction of admissions, although this was based on a smaller dataset . and under-powered. It may be that repeated community C(E)TRs provide additional benefit perhaps through ongoing monitoring and follow-up of recommendations, although this requires further investigation. We investigated whether community C(E)TR were associated with changes in clinical presentation or community support. Mentions of certain symptoms increased following community C(E)TRs (aggression and agitation in people with intellectual disability group, and suicidal ideation autistic people) which may reflect increased clinical scrutiny and documentation of concerns that prompted the review, rather than a deterioration in clinical status. There was no change in community contacts with mental health professionals following community C(E)TRs, although our data did not capture input from social care professionals or paid support staff. Mentions of a 'care package' increased following community C(E)TRs which potentially indicates the care and support that a person receives is formally reviewed or altered following a C(E)TR. However, the context of mentions in the patient record was not available, which limits interpretation of the data obtained through NLP methods.

Although some psychiatric hospital admissions are avoidable, inpatient care remains necessary when a person's assessment or treatment needs cannot be safely met in the community 24 . In this case, a community C(E)TR may be beneficial to clarify the purpose of an admission and support early discharge planning, potentially reducing the length of stay. The small proportion of admissions that were preceded by a community C(E)TR in our study is consistent with national data 25 and proved insufficient for a robust analysis on differences in length of stay between groups.

Our data show time trends in the delivery of community C(E)TRs, with the highest numbers seen during the COVID-19 period and immediately afterwards, followed by a marked decline. The increased use during the COVID-19 pandemic may reflect disruption to usual care and support, potentially increasing the risk of hospital admission. It is possible to deliver C(E)TRs remotely which may also have facilitated their delivery. The subsequent sharp decline is notable given the continued emphasis on C(E)TRs in national policy 26 . Possible explanations include greater reliance on less formal multi-disciplinary professional meetings or the Local Area Emergency Protocol (LAEP), and changing attitudes towards C(E)TRs amongst professionals, patients, and carers 27 . Convening a C(E)TR requires considerable resource and co-ordination across agencies which may discourage their use and the period covered by our study also includes significant changes to the organisation and commissioning of mental health services following publication of the NHS Community Mental Health Framework 28.

Clinical and research implications

Our findings demonstrate the potential value of community C(E)TRs in reducing admissions but also raise concerns about potential inequalities in access to reviews and declining implementation.

Community C(E)TRs should form part of an integrated admission avoidance pathway, supported by effective use of local Dynamic Support Registers to identify people at risk of admission. Evidenceinformed risk stratification and decision support tools could help improve consistency of decisions about when to convene a community C(E)TR, reducing reliance on subjective judgment 29, 30 . Although community C(E)TRs may mobilise support in the short-term, panel recommendations are not binding and stronger mechanisms for monitoring implementation and ensuring accountability may improve their effectiveness. The low proportion of admissions preceded by a community C(E)TR raises the prospect of missed opportunities to define the purpose of admissions and plan for discharge from the outset. However, pre-admission reviews may not be feasible in emergency situations.

Future research is required to establish definitively the effectiveness of community C(E)TRs and their impact on a wider range of outcomes, including quality of life and restrictive practices, ideally using larger multisite datasets. Implementation research could explore barriers and facilitators to effective delivery and how community C(E)TRs interact within wider admission avoidance infrastructure, including LAEP meetings, Home Treatment Teams and Intensive Support Teams, in responding to escalating needs or crisis situations.

Strengths and limitations

This study is the first large-scale evaluation of the use and impact of community C(E)TRs in routine clinical practice, using a systematic approach to identifying C(E)TRs from electronic health records.

Longitudinal, individual-level data enabled us to examine characteristics of people receiving The relatively small number of community C(E)TRs limited statistical precision, particularly in the length of hospital stay analysis. Pooled estimates for the primary outcome analysis might obscure differences between the intellectual disability and autistic groups. Reliance on routinely-collected electronic health data has disadvantages, including some variables being missing or inadequately reported (e.g. HoNOS), and hospital admissions outside SLaM are not measured, although we expect them to be minimal. Residual confounding remains possible and the attenuation of the effect in sensitivity analyses demonstrates some uncertainty around the findings. We were unable to capture the full range of community services and interventions; this is particularly relevant for people with intellectual disability and autistic people who may receive substantial support from family or paid carers, social services, and third sector organisations. Our primary outcome was hospital admission and we could not determine whether admissions were potentially avoidable. Finally, the study was conducted in a single mental health Trust in London and findings may not be representative of other regions where the organisation of services and implementation of community C(E)TRs may differ.

Conclusions

Community C(E)TRs may help to prevent hospital admissions for people with intellectual disability and autistic people in the short-term. However, their declining use and the small proportion of admissions preceded by a C(E)TR raises concerns about implementation. Achieving sustained reductions in unnecessary admissions will depend on equitable access to reviews and ensuring their recommendations are translated into meaningful and sustained changes in care and support.

Causal framework assumptions

The causal framework comes with several assumptions 5 . For DiD methods, the parallel trends assumption is that the treatment and control groups would have had the same trends across time if nobody had had a community C(E)TR. This assumption is not testable but was made more reasonable by the initial matching of the control group and propensity model IPTW adjustment for covariates. The two types of associated selection bias that can occur are: across time and across group. Our study design reduces concerns regarding selection bias across time because outcome measurements were obtained from the same individuals during both the pre-and post-periods. Thus, any observed differences were not driven by changes in the composition of either the C(E)TR or control groups over time.

We also ensured that we only included patients who were under SLaM services for the full six-months before and after the C(E)TR / index date. Furthermore, during initial matching, up to five controls were matched to a C(E)TR patient on age and sex, and to assign an index date to the controls that aligned with the C(E)TR date of the case. As such, pre-and post-index date time periods of controls were matched to their respective case pre-and post-C(E)TR dates, that could have occurred anytime between 2015 and 2025. Patients in both groups were therefore likely to be affected in a similar way by any other factors such as changes in clinical practice over time or the response at SLaM to the COVID pandemic. The initial propensity model was used to balance co-variates that may differ between treatment and control groups, thereby addressing the across-group selection bias that may undermine the parallel trends assumption.

The positivity assumption posits that all patients have a positive probability of being in each of the groups; here, all patients had a learning disability and/or were autistic, and were under secondary mental healthcare services at SLaM, and thus were all eligible to receive a community C(E)TR. Unconfoundedness assumes that assignment to the community C(E)TR group is not related to the hospitalization outcomes that would be observed in the controls, conditional on the observed covariates. The initial matching and propensity weighting model were established to match controls to C(E)TR patients on a range of (risk) variables to meet this assumption. We extracted all covariates from the period before (or on) the community C(E)TR date / index date, thereby meeting the exogeneity assumption of covariates not being affected by the treatment itself. Supplementary Text S1. Extracting variables using Natural Language Processing (NLP) applications

NLP applications have been used to extract structured information from the free text of electronic health records at SLaM since 2009, and have been developed to target specific clinical symptoms, physical health conditions, and interventions/services mentioned in patient records. In this study we use NLP apps to identify diagnoses, medications, and free text mentions of psychiatric symptoms and interventions, such as e.g. aggression, for specific periods of time. For the 'aggression' app, for example, the associated search term was *agitat*, which included the past or present participle of the word. The development process for each app includes performance metrics on tested batches of documents for various populations. Information on the NLP apps for each symptom and intervention used in this study can be found in the Maudsley BRC CRIS NLP Applications Library 1.

Supplementary Text S2. CETR identification and reliability of CETR labelling

C(E)TRs were identified in the EHRs of the sample using the following key word search terms: "Care and Treatment Review", "Care Education and Treatment Review", "CTR", or "CETR". Records were reviewed to determine if and when C(E)TR meetings were held. Separate lists were made by two independent raters to reflect the level of confidence of a C(E)TR having occurred on a specific date. CETR identification and labelling were carried out by two independent raters. Of the pool of patients who had a learning disability diagnosis, 936 had a mention of one of the C(E)TR keyword search terms in their records The first 100 of these patients were used to pilot C(E)TR labelling and categorization. Patients were assigned to one of the five lists below, to capture the level of confidence in a C(E)TR having occurred on a specified date: 1) C(E)TR occurred on a specified date, 2) C(E)TR proposed for a specified date but no evidence it occurred, 3) C(E)TR previously occurred but without a specified date captured in the records, 4) C(E)TR mentioned/proposed for a patient with no specified date and no evidence it occurred, 5) False positive, with the search terms pulling back records that were not relevant to a C(E)TR occurring for that patient. Inter-rater reliability on the level of agreement of assigning patients to one of these five categories was calculated and indicated strong agreement between the two raters (Cohen's k = 0•86). Three patient IDs were missed by one of the raters, so of the 97 remaining patients, 38 of these were confirmed to have had at least one C(E)TR occur on a specified date. Following up on those patients for whom raters agreed a C(E)TR was held, the percentage agreement on different aspects of these were as follows: agreement on date of one CETR: 92%; agreement on multiple CETRs having occurred: 89%, and agreement on dates of more than one CETR: 81%. The two raters then went through all records where there was disagreement to clarify misidentifications and/or alternative interpretations, to ensure their independent assessment of records of the remaining 836 patients (~400 each) was fully aligned.

A flag to denote the level of confidence in the date of the C(E)TR was also included (confident, not confident). The 'not confident' option mainly applied to two scenarios: 1) when the exact C(E)TR date was not clear but seemed have occurred between two records, and it was written about as having recently occurred in the second, reviewers include a note that refers to the date of the record where a CETR is mentioned as having recently happened, and 2) when a C(E)TR was mentioned as having occurred during a particular month, but without a date, we included the C(E)TR as having occurred on the 15 th of that month.

Supplementary Text S3. Preprocessing for change in hospitalization primary outcome measure

We excluded 1) inpatient C(E)TRs, 2) C(E)TRs falling within 6 months of a previous community C(E)TR for the same patient, 3) C(E)TRs occurring after 30/06/2024 (to ensure full data captured within study time window), 4) patients with less than 6 months of data available following a C(E)TR due to e.g. being transferred to another service, and 5) patients who died within 6 months of having a C(E)TR (see figure 1 in manuscript for flowchart). As control patients were initially matched to cases based on sex, age, and referral length, we retained controls if they were a match with the remaining cases after preprocessing. Sociodemographic and clinical profiles of people undergoing community C(E)TRs were extracted before applying the rest of the preprocessing pipeline, i.e. anybody receiving a community C(E)TR, and their age-and sex-matched controls (manuscript Figure 1). The remaining preprocessing steps were applied before statistical analyses on psychiatric hospitalization and other clinical variables describing changes in the six months before versus six months after the index date.
