Working Directory (setwd)

Best programming practice is to avoid setwd() in analysis code: it hardcodes an assumption about the working directory (often an absolute path on the author’s own machine), so the code breaks when run anywhere else. Keep the working directory as the caller sets it and use relative paths. No setwd() calls were found in any of the code files.

Package Installation (install.packages)

Best programming practice is to avoid install.packages() in analysis code: it installs software on whoever runs the script without asking, which is at minimum disruptive (an unexpected install, network access, a package version the author never tested against) and at worst a security risk if it points at an untrusted repository. Document required packages instead (a README, or a list of them: an renv.lock/DESCRIPTION for R, a requirements.txt for Python). No install.packages() calls were found in any of the code files.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Abstract

Rapid bacterial identification could help guide antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST), but image-based classification must generalise across bacterial strains. We developed SC-MAPS, a multichannel microscopy dataset comprising 916,435 single cells from 13 bacterial species, with independent strains reserved for evaluation. Combining phase-contrast and fluorescence information using deep learning achieved 65.4% single-cell species-classification accuracy on held-out strains. To assess whether imperfect species predictions retained useful information for downstream testing, we defined eight antimicrobial-resistance-informed AST-routing groups. Pooling species probabilities increased routing accuracy to 77.1%, rising to 91.1% when predictions from five cells were combined and 96.2% with 20 cells. Synthetic mixtures revealed limitations in minority-species recovery, while exploratory analysis of unseen organisms demonstrated the potential for out-of-distribution detection. SC-MAPS provides a framework for investigating transferable bacterial phenotypes and developing image-based identification and AST-routing strategies. Importantly, the clinically relevant question is not necessarily limited to whether every bacterium can immediately be assigned to its exact species. Early information that distinguishes organisms associated with different downstream antimicrobial-testing strategies could potentially help guide the selection of an initial AST pathway.

Microscopy offers a potential route towards such early phenotypic information because bacterial morphology and cellular organisation can be examined directly at single-cell resolution. Conventional morphology alone has limited discriminatory power, as phylogenetically distinct bacteria can share similar dimensions and shapes, while cells of the same species can display substantial phenotypic variation 9.

Deep learning provides a means of extracting more complex image features that are difficult to capture using predefined morphological measurements 10, 11 . Previous work has demonstrated the potential of this approach across increasingly sophisticated imaging tasks. Deep-learning analysis has been applied to bacterial colonies and gram-stained blood-culture images 12, 13 , while single-cell approaches have shown that bacterial morphology can contain information relevant to species identity, physiological state and antimicrobial response [14] [15] [16] [17] . Recent work further indicates that morphological differences can be associated with antimicrobial-resistance phenotypes even in the absence of antibiotic exposure 18.

More information-rich techniques, including hyperspectral microscopy [19] [20] [21] [22] , coherent imaging 23 , quantitative-phase microscopy 24 and Raman spectroscopy [25] [26] [27] , have further demonstrated that individual bacterial cells contain phenotypic signatures capable of supporting classification. For example, quantitative-phase imaging achieved 82.5% species-identification accuracy from a single threedimensional refractive-index tomogram of an individual bacterial cell or small cluster across 19 bloodstream-infection-associated species, increasing to 99.9% when predictions from seven independently measured cells or clusters were combined 24 . This distinction is important for diagnostic applications: individual bacterial cells are heterogeneous and may be difficult to classify reliably in isolation, whereas evidence accumulated across a small population can provide substantially more robust sample-level information 28, 29.

The shift towards single-cell bacterial phenotyping has also been enabled by advances in image segmentation 30 . Deep-learning-based tools such as DeLTA 31 , MiSiC 32 , Omnipose 33 and Cellpose 34 can identify individual bacteria across crowded fields and variable cell morphologies, allowing image information to be analysed at the level of discrete cells rather than whole fields of view. This is particularly important for bacteria, where small cell size, close spatial packing and substantial morphological variation can complicate segmentation. Reliable single-cell extraction preserves phenotypic heterogeneity that may otherwise be obscured by population averaging and provides the basis for linking morphology, fluorescence sub-cellular distribution, and intracellular organisation to downstream classification.

However, high reported classification accuracy does not necessarily imply that a model will generalise to a previously unseen strain of the same bacterial species. This is highly relevant to single-cell datasets, where thousands of cells may originate from only a small number of strains, cultures or experimental preparations. Strain-to-strain variation introduces additional phenotypic heterogeneity within a species, meaning that models trained on a limited number of strains may learn strain-associated features that do not transfer to other members of the same species. Moreover, cells originating from the same culture or experimental unit share biological history and technical characteristics and therefore cannot be considered fully independent observations. If such cells are distributed between training and test sets, models may learn preparation-or experiment-associated features rather than phenotypes that generalise to independent material. Species-level performance. Across DenseNet121, ResNet18 and ResNet34, classification performance depended more strongly on imaging modality than on CNN architecture. Single-cell prediction heterogeneity reveals mixed populations and limits indiscriminate aggregation

Because combining predictions across cells substantially improved classification in homogeneous samples, we next examined how aggregation behaved when populations contained more than one species. Synthetic two-species populations were constructed from cells belonging to the independently held-out strains at defined relative abundances. In 200-cell populations, minority abundances of 5%, 10%, 25% and 50% corresponded to 10, 20, 50 and 100 cells from the less abundant constituent, respectively. Cell-level species probabilities were averaged across each population, and recovery was defined by whether the constituent species appeared among the two highest-probability aggregate species predictions.

Minority-species recovery increased from 16.3% of populations at 5% abundance to 25.9% at 10% and 60.0% at 25%; in balanced 50:50 populations, both constituent species were recovered among the two highest aggregate predictions in 77.0% of mixtures (Figure 3C). Thus, the 77.0% value represents successful recovery of both constituent species at the population level rather than correct classification of 77 of the 100 minority-species cells. In contrast, recovery of the dominant species remained comparatively stable across asymmetric mixtures (Figure 3D), indicating that population averaging preferentially preserves the dominant organism while potentially obscuring a less abundant constituent.

Mixed populations also showed greater disagreement among their constituent cell-level predictions than homogeneous populations. This was quantified using generalized Jensen-Shannon dispersion, which measures divergence between individual cell-level species-probability distributions and their population mean. For 20-cell populations, median dispersion increased from 0.00 bits in pure populations to 1.32 bits in synthetic mixtures (Figure 3E), demonstrating substantially greater prediction heterogeneity in mixed populations.

Together with the greater recovery of AST-routing groups than exact species in balanced mixtures (Figure 2F), these analyses illustrate two complementary uses of the single-cell representation. When cell-level predictions are concordant, combining evidence across cells can substantially improve sample-level classification. When predictions are heterogeneous, retaining the underlying single-cell information can instead reveal mixed phenotypes and reduce the risk that a minority population is obscured by population averaging. We next examined model behaviour for five organisms belonging to species excluded entirely from model development: Streptococcus agalactiae (BSB), Enterococcus faecalis (EF), Enterococcus faecium (EFM), Haemophilus influenzae (HFLU) and Listeria monocytogenes (LMON). Because the classifier contained only the original 13 SC-MAPS species as possible outputs, unseen-organism predictions were necessarily distributed among the known classes. Nevertheless, their mean probability distributions were strongly non-uniform (Figure 4A). BSB showed preferential assignment towards SAUR, EFM towards ACAA and LMON towards PSMA, while HFLU distributed substantial probability between SAUR and SEPI. These assignments therefore represent similarity to learned phenotypic classes rather than valid species identification.

The unseen organisms were subsequently projected into the fixed known-species UMAP reference without refitting the embedding (Figure 4B). Their cells occupied structured regions relative to the known-species representation, consistent with their non-uniform forced probability distributions. This highlighted a limitation of closed-set classification: even an organism absent from model development must receive probability among one of the known classes.

We therefore tested whether the learned representation could support explicit out-of-distribution (OOD) rejection. Feature-space k-nearest-neighbour (kNN) distance provided the strongest single-cell discrimination between known and unseen cells, with an AUROC of 0.944 and 77.8% unseen-cell rejection at the selected operating threshold (Figure 4C). Mahalanobis distance achieved an AUROC of 0.907 with 57.7% rejection, whereas prediction entropy and maximum softmax probability performed substantially less well (AUROC 0.632 and 0.616, respectively). At the kNN operating point, 94.3% of known cells were retained. OOD rejection was observed across all five unseen organisms, ranging from 71.4% for HFLU to 88.0% for LMON (Figure 4D). Combining evidence across cells further improved detection: unseen-organism detection increased from 79.7% in the one-cell population analysis to 99.8% with five cells and 100% with 10 or 20 cells, while known-cell retention remained approximately 94% (Figure 4E). These findings indicate that the learned feature representation can support an explicit unknown/OOD output rather than forcing every observation into a known species class, while phenotypic similarity to the known reference can be considered separately. Dimensionality-reduction and open-set procedures are described in Supplementary Methods Sections 7-8.

Discussion

SC-MAPS reframes bacterial classification around the downstream decision rather than perfect taxonomy.

A central implication of SC-MAPS is that uncertainty in bacterial identity does not necessarily imply loss of clinically useful information. Although single-cell species classification remained challenging on strains excluded from model development, accuracy increased from 65.4% at species level to 77.1% after AST-routing group probability pooling, and 34.5% of species-level errors still mapped to the correct AST-routing group. The stronger performance at the AST-routing level suggests that broader decisionrelevant phenotypic information transfers across strains more robustly than exact taxonomic discrimination. Taxonomic error and downstream decision error are therefore not equivalent: confusing organisms that share an AST pathway may be less consequential than errors between groups requiring different testing strategies. Similar principles have been reported using single-cell Raman spectroscopy, where treatment-relevant grouping can outperform exact isolate classification25-27,38. SC-MAPS extends this concept to multichannel imaging and prospectively defined AMR-informed routing groups, while not . attempting to predict susceptibility, MIC or resistance mechanisms directly. Direct training on the eight routing groups did not improve performance over species-level training followed by probability pooling, suggesting that finer species supervision preserves useful phenotypic structure that can subsequently be translated into a broader clinical decision.

Biological separability appears to matter more than increasingly complex models. The classification experiments suggest that performance was constrained more by the phenotype available to the model than by network architecture. DenseNet121, ResNet18 and ResNet34 showed broadly similar channeldependent behaviour, whereas imaging modality had a larger effect on generalisation, and a specialist Enterobacterales classifier did not improve independent-strain holdout performance. Species separability was also uneven: ACAA, ECOL, PSAR and SLUG were comparatively well resolved, while several Enterobacterales and staphylococci remained more difficult to distinguish.

The learned DenseNet representation showed corresponding organisation when visualised using UMAP.

Many species occupied recognisable regions of the embedding, while overlap remained among classes that were also difficult to separate by classification. A broader separation between Gram-positive and Gram-negative organisms was also apparent. These UMAPs were interpreted qualitatively rather than as quantitative measures of inter-species distance. Additional jointly fitted known/unseen and independently fitted unseen-only representations are provided in Supplementary Figure S10.

Channel-specific performance reinforced this biological interpretation. Phase contrast provided the strongest overall generalisation, while DAPI, WGA, FM1-43 and bright field contributed species-specific strengths. Multichannel fusion therefore appears to benefit from complementary biological information rather than simply increased image complexity. FM1-43 further illustrates that an informative phenotype may also be sensitive to biological or experimental variation: its strong development performance but poorer cross-strain generalisation suggests that membrane-associated fluorescence may be particularly sensitive to strain, physiological state or preparation conditions. Because strains were acquired from separate culture preparations, these effects cannot be fully disentangled here. More broadly, these findings reinforce that large cell numbers cannot substitute for biological independence. To further explore how the multichannel model uses image information, we performed an exploratory SmoothGrad-Integrated Gradients analysis on 26 correctly classified holdout cells (two per species; Supplementary Fig. S13).

Attribution patterns varied across species and imaging channels, while phase-contrast attributions were preferentially concentrated at bacterial cell boundaries. These observations provide descriptive insights into model behaviour but do not establish causal feature importance or specific biological mechanisms.

Multi-cell aggregation substantially improves population-level inference. The aggregation results

suggest that the most useful diagnostic unit may be a small bacterial population rather than an isolated cell. A single bacterium captures only one morphological and physiological state, and may also represent only one temporal point in the cell cycle. Combining several cells instead provides repeated sampling of the population phenotype. This principle is consistent with other optical approaches, including quantitative-phase and Raman-based identification. SC-MAPS showed the same effect using conventional multichannel microscopy: AST-routing accuracy exceeded 90% with five cells and 96% with twenty. These gains arose from combining probability distributions from individual cells rather than retraining the classifier. The value of single-cell imaging may therefore lie less in perfectly classifying every bacterium and more in preserving cell-to-cell variation before evidence is combined. This permits robust aggregation when populations are homogeneous while retaining the ability to detect heterogeneity when they are not.

Unseen organisms reveal transferable phenotype structure and support open-set rejection.

The unseen-organism analysis tested whether the learned representation contained useful structure beyond the 13 species used for model development. Cells from all five unseen organisms showed non-uniform . prediction distributions and occupied characteristic regions when projected into the fixed known-species UMAP reference. Because the classifier is closed-set, however, these assignments represent similarity to learned phenotypes rather than successful identification.

Importantly, the underlying feature representation also contained information that could be used to recognise organisms outside the known reference. k-nearest-neighbour distance calculated from PCAreduced DenseNet feature representations distinguished known from unseen cells with an AUROC of 0.944, rejecting 77.8% of unseen cells while retaining 94.3% of known cells. Population-level OOD detection increased to 99.8% when novelty scores from five cells were averaged. Feature-space distance substantially outperformed SoftMax confidence and prediction entropy, indicating that unfamiliarity was better captured by position within the learned representation than by classifier confidence alone. This is important for closed-set models, in which a confident prediction does not necessarily imply that the organism belongs to a validated class.

These findings suggest a potential framework in which samples outside the validated reference are first flagged as unknown/OOD, while their closest learned phenotypic relationship is considered separately. Such an approach could support an expanding reference atlas as additional organisms are independently characterised and incorporated into the validated class set. The present analysis included only five unseen organisms and should therefore be regarded as proof of principle rather than validation of a general openset classifier.

Polymicrobial analysis defines when aggregation is appropriate.

The synthetic polymicrobial analysis highlights an important limitation of population averaging. Minority-species recovery was strongly abundance-dependent, whereas dominant-species recovery remained comparatively stable. In 200-cell populations, recovery of both constituent species among the two highest-ranked predictions increased from 16.3% at 5% minority abundance to 77.0% in balanced mixtures. This suggests that aggregation can reinforce the dominant population while progressively obscuring less abundant constituents. Recovery is also likely to depend on the phenotypic similarity of the organisms involved, although this relationship was not explicitly quantified here.

Retaining single-cell predictions may therefore provide a safeguard against inappropriate averaging. Mixed populations showed substantially greater disagreement among cell-level probability distributions than pure populations, with median generalized Jensen-Shannon dispersion increasing from 0.00 to 1.32 bits. This supports a framework in which prediction heterogeneity is assessed before aggregation: concordant populations can benefit from combining evidence across cells, whereas heterogeneous populations may warrant examination of their constituent single-cell predictions. AST-routing information was also more robust than exact taxonomy. In balanced mixtures containing organisms from different routing groups, both correct AST-routing groups were recovered in 95.4% of mixtures compared with 77.0% recovery of both exact species. This exceeded the 78.9% mean recovery obtained under group-size-matched randomisation (empirical P = 0.0015), indicating that the improvement was not explained solely by collapsing species into broader categories. These mixtures were nevertheless generated computationally from independently imaged monocultures and therefore do not capture physical co-culture, staining interactions or sample-matrix effects.

Broader strain diversity, external validation and phenotypic AST are the critical next steps. The independent-strain holdout provides evidence that SC-MAPS phenotypes can generalise beyond strains used for model development, but the number of strains represented per species remains limited. Larger collections spanning greater within-species diversity will therefore be needed to establish the breadth of cross-strain generalisation. Methods

Species selection and definition of AST-routing groups

Thirteen bacterial species were selected to provide a clinically relevant and taxonomically diverse panel spanning major Gram-negative and Gram-positive organisms encountered in diagnostic microbiology: Species were grouped a priori into eight clinically informed AST-routing categories according to broad similarities and differences in their initial antimicrobial susceptibility-testing requirements: routine Enterobacterales (ECOL, KLPN), AmpC/caution Enterobacterales (KLAE, ENTC, SERM), Salmonella (SAENA), Pseudomonas (PSAR), Acinetobacter (ACAA), Stenotrophomonas (PSMA), an S. aureus-like group (SAUR, SLUG) and non-lugdunensis coagulase-negative staphylococci (CoNS; SEPI, STCP). Groups were defined to combine species for which identification would lead to a broadly similar initial AST pathway while retaining separate categories where organism identity carries distinct resistance or testing considerations 40, 41 . KLAE, ENTC and SERM were separated from routine Enterobacterales because of clinically relevant chromosomal AmpC β-lactamase biology and associated interpretive considerations 42, 43 . SAENA, PSAR, ACAA and PSMA were retained as separate categories because of their distinct susceptibility-testing and resistance characteristics [44] [45] [46] [47] . SLUG was grouped with SAUR because of its comparatively invasive clinical behaviour relative to other CoNS, while SEPI and STCP formed the nonlugdunensis CoNS group 48 . These study-defined categories were used to evaluate whether image-derived species predictions retained information relevant to initial AST-routing decisions. They were not intended as a validated clinical testing algorithm and do not represent predictions of antimicrobial susceptibility, minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC), resistance mechanisms or treatment response. The complete species composition and clinical rationale for each group are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Bacterial culture and sample preparation.

The bacterial strains used in SC-MAPS, together with their corresponding plate assignments and dataset partitions, are listed in Supplementary Table 1. Each plate designation represented a distinct strain of the corresponding species.

Isolates were recovered from cryogenic stocks onto Columbia agar base supplemented with 5% (v/v) defibrinated sheep blood and incubated overnight at 37 °C. For each species, up to four distinct strains were processed independently, with each strain corresponding to one plate designation (Plates 1-4).

Colonies were picked from the overnight agar culture and dispersed in Mueller-Hinton broth before fixation, without an intervening outgrowth step to a defined optical density. Aliquots of 200 µL were transferred to 96-well plates, fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 20 min, pelleted and washed before fluorescent labelling (Figure 5A).

Fixed bacterial cells were stained with DAPI (10 µg/mL), FM1-43 (5 µg/mL) and wheat-germ agglutinin conjugated to Alexa Fluor 647 (WGA-AF647; 10 µg/mL) to provide complementary information on intracellular and cell-envelope morphology (Figure 5A). DAPI is a DNA-binding fluorescent dye and was used to visualise the bacterial nucleoid, allowing differences in nucleoid size, number, spatial distribution and degree of compaction within individual cells to be captured. FM1-43 is a lipophilic styryl dye that associates with lipid membranes and was used to delineate the bacterial cell membrane, providing information on overall cell shape, membrane morphology and division-associated structures. WGA is a lectin with affinity for N-acetylglucosamine-containing structures and, when conjugated to Alexa Fluor 647, provided an additional readout of cell-wall architecture and surface-associated morphology. The relative staining patterns obtained with WGA-AF647 and FM1-43 were expected to differ according to cellenvelope composition and therefore provided complementary structural information across Gram-positive and Gram-negative organisms. Together, the DAPI, FM1-43 and WGA-AF647 fluorescence channels captured nucleoid, membrane and cell-wall-associated phenotypes, respectively, generating multichannel representations of individual bacterial cells for subsequent image-based classification.

Following staining and washing, bacterial suspensions were mounted on 1.5% (w/v) agarose pads on glass slides and covered with coverslips for microscopy. Multichannel imaging was performed using the Trillian wide-field microscopy system built around a ZEISS Axio Observer.Z1/7 inverted microscope equipped with a Plan-Apochromat 63×/1.40 NA oil-immersion Ph3 objective and an Axiocam 506m camera with a 1× camera adapter. The objective had a working distance of 193 µm and was used with immersion oil of refractive index 1.518. Images were acquired at 1 × 1 binning with a camera pixel size of 4.54 × 4.54 µm, corresponding to an effective sample-plane pixel size of approximately 0.072 µm.

Fluorescence images were acquired by wide-field epifluorescence using the 90 HE DAPI/GFP/Cy3/Cy5 reflector set. DAPI was imaged using 353-nm excitation and 465-nm emission at 30% illumination intensity, WGA-AF647 using 653-nm excitation and 668-nm emission at 30% intensity, and FM1-43 using 473-nm excitation and 579-nm emission at 20% intensity. Bright-field and phase-contrast images were acquired using transmitted white-light illumination at 10% and 30% intensity, respectively. Exposure times varied between channels and acquisitions rather than being fixed across the dataset. In representative acquisitions, exposure times were approximately 26-33 ms for DAPI, bright-field and phase contrast, 50-100 ms for WGA-AF647, and 20 ms for FM1-43. Image acquisition was controlled using ZEN 2.6 (blue edition; version 2.6.76.00).

Single-cell segmentation and dataset generation

Phase-contrast images were segmented using Cellpose to generate single-cell instance masks. A specialised CPSAM-based segmentation model was used for Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus and Staphylococcus epidermidis, while the remaining species were processed using the standard Cellpose segmentation workflow. Objects intersecting image boundaries were excluded during segmentation.

Images from the five imaging channels were matched using common sample and field identifiers, allowing . each phase-contrast-derived instance mask to be mapped onto the corresponding bright-field (BF), DAPI, WGA-AF647 and FM1-43 images (Figure 5B).

Segmented objects with areas outside 40-5000 pixels were excluded to remove small fragments, segmentation artefacts and unusually large merged objects while retaining small bacterial cells. At the measured pixel size, the lower threshold corresponded to approximately 0.21 µm². These thresholds were selected following visual quality control across the species panel. For each retained cell, the segmentation-derived bounding box was expanded by 8 pixels in each direction to define the extraction region. The same instance mask was applied across all five spatially matched channels, excluding surrounding pixels and isolating the focal cell. The bounding box therefore defined the extraction region, while the segmentation mask determined which pixels were retained.

Masked single-cell crops were preserved at their native spatial resolution, centred within a 96 × 96-pixel canvas and zero-padded where necessary, without resizing. At the effective sample-plane pixel size of approximately 0.072 µm, the 96 × 96-pixel canvas corresponded to a physical area of approximately 6.9 × 6.9 µm, substantially exceeding the typical dimensions of individual bacterial cells in the dataset. This allowed single-cell crops to be accommodated at their native spatial resolution without resizing. Crops exceeding the canvas dimensions were excluded rather than rescaled. Each cell was represented by a fivechannel image stack of dimensions 5 × 96 × 96 pixels, stored in the order phase contrast, BF, DAPI, WGA-AF647 and FM1-43. The image stacks were linked to a cell-level manifest containing species identity, plate/strain assignment, source image, segmentation ID and quantitative morphological and intensity measurements. Unmasked crops from the same spatial coordinates were retained separately for representative visualisation. Additional segmentation, extraction and quality-control details are provided in Supplementary Methods Section 3 and Supplementary Figure S1.

The final SC-MAPS dataset comprised 916,435 segmented cells from 697 microscopy fields and 49 species-strain preparations. For each species, the strain corresponding to Plate 1 was reserved exclusively for independent holdout evaluation, while strains corresponding to Plates 2-4 were used for model development. Ten species contributed three development strains, whereas ACAA, PSMA and SEPI contributed two following image-quality exclusions. Cells originating from the same microscopy field were assigned to the same dataset partition, and no cells from the held-out strains were used during training, validation, hyperparameter optimisation or model selection. This design enabled evaluation of model generalisation to strains not encountered during development.

Quantitative phenotypic analysis

The single-cell manifest contained morphological and imaging-derived measurements for each segmented bacterium. Species-level phenotypic profiles (Figure 1C) were constructed using eight morphological features-cell area, major-and minor-axis lengths, maximum Feret diameter, aspect ratio, eccentricity, solidity and compactness-together with one contrast measurement from each of the five imaging channels: phase contrast, BF, DAPI, WGA-AF647 and FM1-43. For each feature, the median was calculated across cells belonging to each species and subsequently standardised across the 13 species using z-score normalisation.

To characterise the hierarchical sources of phenotypic variability (Figure 1D), the same features were analysed at the single-cell level using a descriptive nested sum-of-squares variance decomposition. Total variation was partitioned into three components: between-species variation, strain/preparation-withinspecies variation and residual cell-within-strain/preparation variation. As each strain corresponded to a distinct culture plate and sample preparation, genetic strain-associated and preparation-associated contributions could not be independently distinguished and were therefore represented as a combined component. Each component was expressed as a percentage of the total sum of squares. This analysis was descriptive and was not intended to establish causal contributions of genetic or experimental factors . to phenotypic variation. Feature definitions, standardisation procedures and variance-decomposition equations are provided in Supplementary Methods Section 4.

Deep-learning classification and multichannel integration

Deep-learning analyses were performed using 96 × 96-pixel single-cell image crops. Each imaging channel was independently normalised using per-cell z-score normalisation, with the mean and standard deviation calculated across the complete 96 × 96-pixel crop, including zero-valued background and padding pixels where present. Training augmentation included horizontal and vertical flips, rotations, translations and scaling, with identical spatial transformations applied across corresponding channels to preserve image registration. Normalisation was performed after augmentation, and no augmentation was applied during validation or holdout evaluation.

DenseNet121 49 , ResNet18 and ResNet34 50 convolutional neural networks were evaluated for 13-class species classification. Models were trained using cross-entropy loss and AdamW 51 optimisation for up to 100 epochs. Learning-rate scheduling and early stopping were guided by validation performance, and the checkpoint achieving the highest validation macro-F1 was retained. Learning rate, weight decay, dropout and batch size were optimised using Optuna 52 , exclusively using data from the development strains. No cells from the independently held-out strains were used for training, validation, hyperparameter optimisation or checkpoint selection. The Optuna optimisation procedure, an illustrative selected configuration and additional architecture-benchmarking details are provided in Supplementary Section 5.

Each architecture was initially trained and evaluated independently using phase contrast, bright field (BF), DAPI, WGA-AF647 or FM1-43 as a single input channel (Figure 6A). Multichannel information was subsequently integrated using probability-level ensembling or feature-level fusion. For probability ensembling, species-class probabilities from independently trained single-channel models were averaged for each cell, and the species with the highest mean probability was selected (Figure 6B). The evaluated combinations comprised DAPI + FM1-43, DAPI + WGA-AF647 + FM1-43, DAPI + WGA-AF647 + FM1-43 + phase contrast, and all five imaging channels.

Feature-level fusion combined DAPI, WGA-AF647, FM1-43 and phase contrast using four channel-specific networks initialised from the corresponding previously trained single-channel classifiers (Figure 6C).

Features from each branch were projected into 256-dimensional representations and integrated using either direct concatenation or squeeze-and-excitation (SE) attention before classification. Fusion training was performed in two stages: initially, the channel-specific branches were frozen while the fusion classification layers were trained; subsequently, higher-level feature-extraction layers were unfrozen and fine-tuned at a reduced learning rate. Both integration strategies were evaluated using DenseNet121, ResNet18 and ResNet34 architectures. The DenseNet121 concatenation model was used for subsequent AST-routing, aggregation, feature-space and unseen-organism analyses. Complete independent-strain benchmarking results for single-channel classifiers, probability ensembles and feature-fusion models are provided in Supplementary Tables 4-6 and Supplementary Methods Sections 5.3-5.5.

AST-routing classification

The predefined eight AST-routing groups were used to derive routing predictions from the 13-species classifiers using two approaches. First, the species with the highest predicted probability was assigned to its corresponding AST-routing group (top-one species mapping). Second, the full 13-species probability distribution was retained, and probabilities for species belonging to the same AST-routing group were summed. The group with the highest pooled probability was selected as the final routing prediction (Figure 6D). This probability-pooling approach allowed evidence distributed across multiple species within the same group to contribute to the routing decision.

. To assess whether AST-routing groups could instead be predicted directly from the microscopy images, DenseNet121 feature-fusion models were trained to classify cells into the eight predefined groups rather than the 13 individual species (Figure 6E). These models used the same imaging data, strain-level dataset partitions and overall training framework as the species classifiers. Directly trained eight-class models were evaluated on the same independent-strain holdout and compared with routing predictions derived from the species-trained models.

To determine whether the observed routing performance could be explained by reducing the number of prediction categories, a group-size-matched randomisation analysis was performed. Species were reassigned to eight groups while preserving the number of species in each original AST-routing group.

Routing performance under these randomised assignments was compared with that obtained using the clinically informed group definitions. Complete comparisons of routing strategies and randomisation procedures are provided in Supplementary Table 7 and Supplementary Methods Section 6.

Learned feature-space and unseen-organism analysis

To visualise the learned multichannel feature representation, features were extracted from the penultimate layer of the DenseNet121 concatenation model. A class-balanced subset of 2,000 independently held-out cells per species was selected, comprising 26,000 cells across the 13 known SC-MAPS species. Feature vectors were reduced to 50 dimensions using principal-component analysis (PCA) and subsequently embedded into two dimensions using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP 53 ). UMAP was performed using 30 nearest neighbours, a minimum distance of 0.15, Euclidean distance and a fixed random seed of 42. The embedding was fitted using the 13 known species and served as the reference representation shown in Figure 3B. Two complementary UMAP visualisations were additionally generated: a joint embedding of known and unseen organisms and an independently fitted embedding of the five unseen organisms (Supplementary Figure S10). The projection procedures and their limitations are described in Supplementary Section 8. 8. No retraining or model adaptation was performed. As the classifier was trained exclusively on the original 13 SC-MAPS species, predictions for unseen organisms were necessarily distributed among these known classes. These closed-set probability distributions were interpreted as model-assigneda affinities to learned species representations rather than valid identifications of the unseen organisms. Mean predicted species-probability distributions were calculated separately for each unseen organism (Figure 4A).

To examine the positions of unseen organisms within the learned feature space, 2,000 cells per unseen organism were transformed using the same PCA model and projected into the previously fitted knownspecies UMAP embedding without refitting (Figure 4B). This approach preserved the positions of the 13 known species, enabling the unseen organisms to be visualised relative to a common reference representation. UMAP projections were interpreted qualitatively and were not used as quantitative measures of inter-species phenotypic similarity or distance. For descriptive AST-routing analysis, the forced 13-species probability distributions of unseen organisms were pooled into the predefined ASTrouting groups using the same procedure applied to the known-species holdout. These assignments were interpreted as model preferences among existing routing groups rather than clinically valid routing recommendations for organisms outside the training set. Additional details of unseen-organism cell . numbers, probability analysis and feature-space projection are provided in Supplementary Methods Sections 7-8.

Open-set and out-of-distribution analysis

To investigate whether organisms absent from model development could be distinguished from the 13 known SC-MAPS species, exploratory out-of-distribution (OOD) detection was performed using classifier probabilities and penultimate-layer feature representations from the DenseNet121 concatenation model.

Four complementary OOD scoring methods were evaluated: maximum SoftMax probability (MSP), normalised prediction entropy, k-nearest-neighbour (kNN) feature distance and class-conditional Mahalanobis distance. For feature-based methods, representations were standardised and reduced to 50 dimensions using principal-component analysis (PCA). The kNN score was calculated as the mean Euclidean distance to the 25 nearest cells in the known-species reference set, while Mahalanobis distance was calculated relative to the known-species feature distributions. These approaches assessed prediction uncertainty or deviation from the learned feature representation rather than attempting to identify the unseen organisms.

OOD detection thresholds were determined using known-species calibration data only, without incorporating cells from the five unseen organisms during threshold selection. The primary operating point was selected to retain 95% of known calibration cells. Detection performance was evaluated using AUROC, known-species retention and unseen-organism rejection rates (Figure 4C). Rejection rates were additionally assessed separately for each unseen organism (Figure 4D).

To assess whether combining evidence from multiple cells improved OOD detection, cell-level novelty scores were averaged across populations containing 1, 5, 10, 20 or 50 cells, with 200 repeated subsamples evaluated for each population size (Figure 4E). Population-level detection performance was assessed alongside retention of known-species populations. Additional details of OOD scoring, threshold calibration and population-level evaluation are provided in Supplementary Section 7. Organism-specific rejection rates are shown in Figure 4D.

Within-sample performance and multi-cell aggregation

Classification performance was evaluated across individual sample/image-acquisition units from the independently held-out strain of each species. A sample was defined by the combination of species, plate/strain, replicate and imaging series, with all cells assigned to a given sample originating from the same experimental and imaging unit. Within-sample classification was evaluated across all 134 independent holdout sample/image-acquisition units. Of these, 120 contained at least 200 segmented cells and were retained as a fixed cohort for the multi-cell aggregation analysis. To assess whether classification difficulty was consistent between fusion strategies, within-sample accuracies from the DenseNet121 concatenation and SE-attention models were compared using Spearman's rank correlation at both species and AST-routing-group levels. Additional within-sample analyses are provided in

Supplementary Methods Sections 6.5-6.6.

To investigate whether combining information from multiple cells improved classification, probabilitybased aggregation was performed using predictions from the independent-strain holdout. For each sample, species-probability vectors from randomly selected cells were averaged, and the species with the highest mean probability was assigned as the aggregated prediction. AST-routing predictions were obtained by summing the averaged species probabilities within each predefined routing group and selecting the group with the highest pooled probability.

Aggregation performance was evaluated using populations of 1, 2, 3, 5, 10, 20, 30, 50, 75, 100, 150 and 200 cells per sample (Figure 2D). Cells were randomly sampled without replacement within each population, . and the procedure was repeated 200 times for each population size. The same 120 samples were used throughout, ensuring that each sample contributed equally to the overall evaluation regardless of its total cell count. Consequently, the one-cell condition represented a sample-balanced single-cell baseline rather than the cell-weighted accuracy calculated across the complete holdout dataset. Performance using all available cells from each sample was additionally calculated without random subsampling. Further details of probability averaging, repeated subsampling and sample-level aggregation consistency are provided in Supplementary Section 6.6.

Synthetic mixed-species analysis

Synthetic two-species populations were constructed using single-cell species-probability predictions from the independently held-out Plate 1 strains, encompassing all 78 unordered pairwise combinations of the 13 SC-MAPS species. For each species pair, up to 10 source-sample pairs were evaluated. Synthetic populations contained 5, 10, 20, 50, 100 or 200 cells, with relative species abundances of 50:50, 75:25, 90:10 and 95:5. For asymmetric mixtures, each species was evaluated in turn as the dominant constituent (Figure 7C). Each source-sample-pair, abundance-ratio and population-size combination was independently resampled 200 times. Cells were sampled without replacement within each synthetic population, although individual holdout cells could contribute to different populations across independent repeats or experimental conditions. For each synthetic population, cell-level species-probability vectors were averaged to generate a single aggregated probability distribution. The two species with the highest aggregated probabilities were used to assess recovery of the constituent organisms. Minority-species recovery was evaluated using 200-cell populations across different abundance ratios (Figure 3C), while dominant-species recovery was additionally compared between 20-and 200-cell populations (Figure 3D). For 50:50 mixtures, recovery was additionally assessed at the AST-routing level. Species pairs were grouped according to whether the two constituent species belonged to different or the same AST-routing group. For different-routing-group pairs, recovery of both constituent species and both corresponding ASTrouting groups among the two highest aggregate predictions was assessed. Performance metrics and statistical analysis

Classification performance was evaluated using accuracy, balanced accuracy, macro-F1, weighted F1, class-specific precision and recall, together with raw and row-normalised confusion matrices. Balanced accuracy was calculated as the mean recall across classes, while macro-F1 represented the unweighted mean of class-specific F1 scores. Macro-F1 was used as the principal model-selection metric because it gives equal weight to each class irrespective of abundance. Top-two accuracy was calculated as the proportion of predictions in which the correct species or AST-routing group was among the two highestprobability outputs.

To assess whether improvements in AST-routing accuracy exceeded those expected from reducing the number of prediction categories, group-size-matched randomisation analyses were performed. Species were randomly reassigned to eight groups while preserving the number of species in each original ASTrouting group. Observed top-one species mapping, probability-pooled routing and top-two routing performance were compared with the corresponding randomised distributions (Figure 2A-C). For synthetic mixtures containing species from different routing groups, recovery of both AST-routing groups was additionally compared against 2,000 group-size-matched randomisations in which the constituent species remained assigned to different groups (Figure 2F). Empirical P values were determined by comparing observed performance with the corresponding randomised null distributions.

Uncertainty in AST-routing performance was assessed using sample-level bootstrap resampling of independent holdout image-acquisition units. Bootstrap-derived 95% confidence intervals were calculated for species-derived probability pooling and directly trained eight-class routing models (Figure 2B). For multi-cell aggregation and synthetic-mixture analyses, repeated random subsampling was used to evaluate performance across different population sizes and species compositions. Repeated subsamples from the same source population were not treated as independent biological replicates. Full randomisation, bootstrap and repeated-sampling procedures are provided in Supplementary Methods Sections 6.3-6.4, 6.6 and 9.2.
