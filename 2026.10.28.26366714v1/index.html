Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Early childhood development programs play a crucial role in fostering child development.

However, scaling-up these programs in low-and middle-income countries poses significant challenges. We conducted a mixed-method study of an evidence-based early childhood parenting program integrated into the primary health care services (community clinics) across four districts in rural Bangladesh. The program caters to caregivers of children aged 6-36 months with mild-to-moderate malnutrition. The parenting program is delivered by government community health workers (CHWs) with small groups of mother-child dyads within the community clinic setting as part of their routine duties. Mothers-child dyads attend fortnightly sessions for one year. CHWs are trained and monitored by government supervisors with overall oversight provided by government health staff at the sub-district and district level. The aim of the study was to identify the strengths and weaknesses of the program and strategies for improvement. Quantitative data included data from clinic logbooks, observations of session quality (n=60), and in-person interviews with participating mothers (n=185) in sixty clinics and phone interviews with mothers (n=164) in a further eighty clinics. Qualitative data included in-depth interviews with CHWs (n=20), government inspectors (n=16), and managers (n=4), and focus group discussions with mothers (n=4 groups, n=30 mothers), and grandmothers (n=4 groups, n=28 grandmothers) across twelve clinics. Results indicated that the key enablers to program implementation included strong government ownership, high compliance in implementing the program by CHWs, adequate session quality, satisfactory session attendance, and positive attitudes towards the program by all stakeholders. Key barriers included low levels of supervision, insufficient advocacy messaging within the community, poor communication across health staff at the different levels, and challenges in providing play materials at scale. The findings are being used to inform revisions to the program to enhance implementation fidelity and sustainability within the health system.

Introduction

Globally, over 250 million children (43%) under the age of five in low and middle-income countries (LMICs) do not reach their full developmental potential. This is due to poverty, undernutrition, poor health, and a lack of stimulation at home [1]. These accumulated risks are associated with long-term negative effects on educational attainment and adult productivity. There is strong evidence that early childhood parenting interventions benefit young children's development [2]. The challenge now is to scale up these interventions to extend their reach to more children at risk of poor development [1, 3]. Several studies have shown that parenting interventions integrated into existing government services can maintain effectiveness when implemented with sufficient quality and fidelity [4 -7]. However, there are challenges in maintaining implementation quality including increased staff workload [8] [9] [10], staff turnover and non-compliance [8, 11], low levels of supervision [10], competing priorities [10), and poor engagement of caregivers [10, 12]. There are several examples of evidence-informed interventions that have failed to improve children's development when implemented on a larger scale [13] [14] [15]. Other large-scale studies have found only very small benefits (0.1SD) to child development [16] [17]. To increase access to effective parenting services, we need a greater understanding of how these interventions can be delivered across different contexts and within different systems.

The Reach-Up parenting program, (originating from the Jamaican Early Childhood Stimulation Home Visiting Intervention), is a play-based intervention aimed at supporting mothers with children aged 0 to 48 months [18]. A meta-analysis of eighteen trials of Reach-Up across eight low-and middle-income countries found average effect sizes of 0.49 SD, 0.38 SD and 0.27 SD for child cognition, language and motor skills, respectively and an average effect size of 0.37 SD for stimulation in the home [19]. There is also evidence from a study in Jamaica that benefits are sustained over the long term, including benefits to cognition, education, mental health and earnings up to age 30 years [20] [21]. In Bangladesh, we previously adapted the Reach-Up home-visiting program for integration into the primary health care services in rural Bangladesh [22]. Government health workers in community clinics delivered parenting sessions with pairs or small groups of mother-child dyads as part of their routine duties. In two cluster-randomized trials, we found positive impacts on children's cognitive and language development, with the effect sizes ranging from 0.7 to 1.3 SD [23] [24]. However, in those trials, the community health workers (CHWs) were trained and monitored by the research team. We then tested the feasibility of utilizing government health supervisors to train and supervise the CHWs to implement the parenting program, and although supervision levels were low, we found high compliance by CHWs in conducting the sessions, high levels of mother attendance and significant benefits to home stimulation with an effect size of 0.53 SD [25]. The positive findings from these studies led to the initiation of the 'Saving Bangladeshi Babies Brains' (SB3) program.

The aim of SB3 was to scale-up the group parenting program both vertically and horizonatally through the primary health care service. Vertical scale involved fully integrating the program into the primary healthcare service in rural Bangladesh, with responsibility for program implementation residing with the government health service. We aimed to establish an organisational structure to promote the quality and sustainability of the program when implemented on a large scale. This includes establishing training, supervision, monitoring and reporting protocols at the national, district, sub-district, and union levels.

Horizontal scale involved extending the reach of the program by expanding the program to more districts. Mothers of children aged 6-24 months, with mild to moderate malnutrition, living in the catchment area of the clinics, are eligible to participate in the program. The SB3 program is currently operating in 613 community clinics of 21 sub-districts under four districts of Narsingdi (n=119), Habiganj (n=199), Brahmanbaria (n=200), and Lakshmipur (n=103). The latter three districts were selected due to the high prevalence of childhood malnutrition, while the first district was selected because we had conducted a previous study there. Over 10,000 mother/child dyads have participated in this program to date.

The aim of the current research was to: 1) investigate the extent to which the program was being implemented as intended within the community clinics, 2) explore the enablers and challenges to intervention implementation, and 3) use the data to identify strategies to strengthen the program.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a process evaluation of a large-scale implementation of the group parenting program integrated into the primary health care system in rural Bangladesh. The process evaluation focused primarily on the quality and fidelity of implementation [26]. We used a parallel mixed-method design in which quantitative and qualitative data were collected and analyzed concurrently [27]. The quantitative data included measures of the quality and fidelity of intervention implementation through structured observations, clinic records and mother surveys. The qualitative data included in-depth interviews with government health staff at different levels within the health service and focus group discussions with mothers and mothers-in-law to investigate the enablers and barriers affecting intervention implementation and suggestions for improvement. The combination of quantitative and qualitative data thus gave information ont the extent to which the intervention was implemented as intended and stakeholder perspectives of factors that affect the quality of intervention delivery and mothers' engagement and participation in the program. Data was collected from December 2023 to May 2024. In the SB3 program, the parenting intervention is fully integrated into the health service with sessions conducted by the three cadres of CHWs within the community clinics . and training and supervision provided by government inspectors at the union level (the smallest administrative unit in rural Bangladesh). The government inspectors were in turn trained and supervised by senior officers at the sub-district level with oversight of the program by district-level staff. In the intervention, mothers of children aged between 6-24 months are invited to attend fortnightly parenting sessions in groups of 4-5 mother-child dyads for one year. After one year, another set of mother-child dyads are invited to participate in the program. The core components of the intervention are: 1) encouraging parents to be responsive to their child's vocalizations, actions, emotions and interests, 2) supporting parents to use sequenced and developmentally appropriate activities with their child that target all developmental domains but with a specific emphasis on cognitive ability and language skills, and 3) use of low-cost play materials and picture books [18]. In terms of intervention delivery, the core components include: 1) the use of demonstration and practice to introduce activities to parents, 2) positive reinforcement for both parents and children, 3) integrating activities into everyday routines and activities, 4) high quality training of facilitators and ongoing supportive supervision, 5) making sessions enjoyable for parents and for children, and 6) a focus on positive relationships at all levels of implementation. At the time this study was conducted, the intervention had been fully operational for two-to-three years, and health staff were implementing the intervention with a second or third cohort of mother-child dyads.

Content

The curriculum is adapted from Reach-Up curriculum and focuses on promoting responsive parenting, increasing stimulation at home and increasing mothers' parenting self-confidence. During the session, mothers are encouraged to engage in responsive, playful interactions with their child using low-cost play materials, books and materials that are available in the home, and in everyday caregiving routines. The play and book activities for children under 24 months are designed based on the concept of object permanence, verbal and physical imitation, and exploration of objects, while for children above 24 months, they are based on concepts of size, number, color, shape, position, similarities /differences and classification.

Materials

Materials for Facilitators: comprise a curriculum manual and a summary card for each session. The curriculum manual includes detailed guidelines for twelve parenting sessions which are repeated after 6 months. The summary card is a shortened version designed to be used by the Community Health Workers (CHWs) during the session. The curriculum is designed for children aged 6-36 months with activities divided into three age bands: 6-11 months, 12-23, and 24-36 months. Materials for Participants: include picture books, play materials made from recycled materials (e.g. shakers, stacking toys, push-a-long toys, nesting toys), wooden blocks, soft toys (e.g. doll, ball, bean bag), puzzle boards, matching games, and crayon and paper. Mothers are given an ageappropriate toy and a book at each session, and these are exchanged with a different toy and book at the next session.

Procedure

Mothers attended the sessions with their child in groups of 4-5 mother-child dyads. Sessions were conducted at community clinics. Each session includes the following activities: 1) feedback from the previous session, 2) a song, 3) demonstration and practice of a toy, book and language activity, 4) a nutritional or developmental message, and 5) review and reminder of home activities. During the session, the CHWs demonstrate each activity and then support mothers as they practice the activity with their child. Mothers are also encouraged to share ideas for other activities and songs. There is a strong focus on building positive relationships with the mothers, using praise and encourage to increase their parenting self-confidence encouraging mothers to praise their children.

Staffing

Facilitators: 1,801 frontline government CHWs, including 568 Community Health Care Providers (CHCPs), 571 Family Welfare Assistants (FWAs) and 662 Health Assistants (HAs)) from 613 community clinics were trained to conduct the parenting sessions. All FWAs, 388 (56%) CHCPs, and 346 (44%) HAs were female. They received 5 days of initial training in groups of 12-15 participants and a 1-day refresher training every 6 months. An honorarium was provided for each day of training attended according to the established government rates. A fully structured training manual was used for all training. Trainers: We used a cascade model of training. The research team trained medical officers and family planning officers (n=64) at the district level who then trained the government health inspectors and family planning inspectors (n=421) at the sub-district level. The inspectors then trained the frontline health workers. Supervision: Government inspectors are tasked with monitoring the SB3 activities in the clinic as part of their routine duties. This involves observing a session conducted by each CHW once a month. Inspectors report to medical officers and family planning officers.

Study sample Sample for observations of session quality

Within each district, one field coordinator has responsibility for monitoring program activities. Each field coordinator was asked to identify clinics within their respective district that had started intervening with a new cohort of mother-child dyads more than four months ago. From those clinics, each field coordinator provided a list of 60 clinics classified into three groups: high-performing clinics, medium-performing clinics, and low-performing clinics. Field co-ordinators based their judgements on their interactions with clinic staff including their level of cooperation, timeliness in submitting reports and engagement with the program. A total of 240 clinics were included in the listing (sixty per district). We used stratified random sampling to select a total of sixty clinics, with twenty clinics in each category. We selected twenty clinics in both the Habiganj and Brahmanbaria district and ten clinics in Narshingdi and Lakshmipur district, proportionate with the number of the clinics in each district. Within each clinic, we observed one parenting session (n=60) to evaluate the quality of implementation.

Sample for mother interviews at home

From each of the community clinics selected for the observational sample (n=60), we randomly selected four mothers who were currently enrolled in the program. Interviewers were requested to schedule interviews with three of the four mothers with one mother acting as a reserve. A total of 185 mothers were interviewed: 60 from Habiganj district, 60 from Brahmanbaria, 32 from Narshingdi and 33 from Lakshmipur.

Sample for mother interviews by phone

Phone interviews were conducted with mothers in an additional eighty clinics to extend the number of clinics included. Twenty clinics from each of the four districts were selected including forty high-performing and forty medium-performing clinics. We dropped lowperforming clinics as it was difficult to collect mothers' phone numbers. We collected the list of mothers' phone numbers from the CHWs and phone interviews were conducted with twoto-three mothers from each clinic. If a mother was unavailable, they called the next mother in the list. A total of 164 mothers were interviewed over phone: 31 from Habiganj district, 46 from Brahmanbaria, 45 from Narshingdi and 42 from Lakshmipur.

Sample for qualitative interviews and focus group discussions

From the sixty clinics participating in the observations, we selected a purposive sample of twelve clinics to participate in an in-depth qualitative investigation representing high, medium and low performing clinics across all four districts.

In-depth interviews were conducted with health staff including community health workers, government inspectors and health managers. Within each of the twelve clinics, we selected one or two community health workers and within each district, we invited four inspectors to participate in in-depth interviews. A total of twenty community health workers participated: eight Community Health Care Providers, seven Health Assistants and five Family Welfare Assistants. Sixteen inspectors, including twelve Health Inspectors and four Family Planning Inspectors were interviewed. We also conducted in-depth interviews with five, district-level managers including three Family Planning Officers and two Medical Officers.

Focus group discussions were conducted with mothers and grandmothers. We invited mothers from four high-performing clinics (one per district) and mothers-in-law from four mid-and low-performing clinics (one per district) to participate in focus group discussions.

We recruited mothers from high-performing community clinics to allow us to investigate perspectives of mothers who were more likely to be knowledgeable about the program. We selected mothers-in-law (grandmothers) from mid to low performing CCs to understand potential challenges with program implementation within the community. We have included grandmothers as they are the primary gatekeepers to mothers' participation. Mothers and grandmothers were identified with the assistance of the respective CHWs. A convenience sampling approach was used based on the availability of the participants. In each FGD, there were six to eight participants. We conducted four FGDs with a total of thirty mothers and four FGDs with a total of 28 grandmothers across the four intervention districts.

Ethical approval for the study was given by the Institutional Review Board of the International Centre for Diarrhoeal Disease Research, Bangladesh (icddr,b) (Approval No IRB 19040).

Written informed consent was obtained from all participating caregivers, grandmothers, CHWs, inspectors and managers.

Quantitative Measurements

Session quality The quality of the parenting sessions was measured using a structured observation checklist that included ratings of CHW preparation, quality of demonstration and practice of activities, use of praise, caregiver engagement and the overall atmosphere of the session.

Most items were scored on a 3-point scale with descriptors on each point of the scale. The overall quality of the different session activities was rated on a 4-point scale.

Session duration, the number of mothers invited to the session, and the number of mothers in attendance was also recorded.

Clinic record forms

Information on mothers' attendance, the number of parenting sessions conducted, and the number of monitoring visits made by the supervisors was collected from the clinic logbook. Information was collected about activities during the past three months.

Parenting questionnaire

The questionnaire consisted of items on mothers' attendance at sessions during the past three months, how often they do the play activities with their child at home, their recall of the session activities, who conducted the session, session duration, how mothers were reminded of the session, number of participants in the session, and how long they waited at the clinic for the session to start.

Qualitative Measurements

Perceptions of health staff at the clinic, subdistrict and district level.

We conducted in-depth interviews with frontline facilitators (health staff), government inspectors, and health managers. Interviewers used a topic guide to explore participants' perspectives of the enablers and barriers to implementation and suggestions for improvement. The topic guides for the in-depth interviews are provided in supplementary appendix 1 (S1). The interviews focused on three core themes: 1.

Supervision and monitoring: e.g. What aspects of supervision and monitoring are working well? How can supervision and monitoring be improved/strengthened? What alternative approaches to supervision could be used? 2. Motivation of health staff: e.g. What motivates health workers to engage with the program? What hinders motivation? What non-monetary factors would boost CHWs motivation? What can supervisors and health authorities do to enhance CHW motivation? 3. Engaging caregivers: e.g. What factors affect mothers' attendance and engagement in the program? What hinders engagement? How can we increase mother attendance and engagement? What strategies can be used to increase community support for the program? Perceptions of mothers and grandmothers We conducted focus group discussions with separate groups of 6-8 mothers and groups of 6-8 grandmothers to explore their perspectives of the enablers and barriers to mothers' attendance and engagement in the program and suggestions on how to promote engagement. Topic guides for the focus group discussions are provided in supplementary appendix 2 (S2).

Quantitative data collection

Five research assistants (RAs) who were not involved in intervention implementation and with no prior contact with the community clinics collected the quantitative data. All had prior experience in collecting similar data within the health system in previous studies. The RAs were trained by SFM and MK who have extensive experience in collecting observational data and administering parent questionnaires in community clinic settings. RAs were trained for five days in the use of the observation checklist and clinic record form to measure the quality and fidelity of implementation. They were trained for an additional five days in the administration of the parenting questionnaires. Each training comprised three days in-office training followed by two days of field practice. Observations were conducted within the community clinic during a routinely scheduled session and data was collected using paper and pencil methods. RAs completed the structured observational checklist based on their observations of the entire parenting session and then used the clinic record book to record the data on mother attendance, number of sessions conducted and supervisory visits. Parenting questionnaires were administered using pencil and paper methods in a face-to-face interview at the participant's home or by phone. The average duration of the interview was 20 minutes for phone interviews and 30 minutes for face-to-face interviews.

Qualitative data collection

The qualitative research team comprised four male and four female RAs with a Masters in anthropology and experience in conducting in-depth interviews and focus group discussions, including in health settings. The RAs had no prior connections with the participants and were new to the field of early childhood development. In-depth interviews were conducted by both male and female members of the qualitative team. The focus group discussions were facilitated by the female members of the team only. The team was trained over two weeks by the qualitative lead, with support from the intervention lead, through a combination of inoffice training followed by field practice with stakeholders from areas not selected for the qualitative investigation. The interviews with the managers and inspectors were conducted at the sub-district hospital, their designated duty stations. The interviews with CHWs and the focus groups with mothers and grandmothers were conducted at their respective community clinics. The average duration of interviews with managers was 35 minutes, with inspectors 50 minutes, and with CHWs approximately 60 minutes. The focus group discussions averaged 50 minutes in duration. Interviewers made debrief notes on the main themes emerging after each in-depth interview and focus group discussion and these notes were used during weekly meetings with the qualitative lead to identify emerging themes and to promote quality control. All interviews were audiotaped and transcribed verbatim and then the transcripts were independently checked for accuracy against the audiotape.

Data analysis

Quantitative data was entered into SPSS and analysed using descriptive statistics.

Qualitative data was analysed manually using thematic analysis [29]. We used the individual topic guides to develop a list of key themes and subthemes for each type of respondent (CHW, inspector, manager, mother, grandmother) using a deductive approach. Additional codes were added inductively as new themes emerged from the data. We developed a codebook in which each code was described in detail along with examples of quotes that matched each code. To ensure intercoder reliability, the team of eight qualitative RAs engaged in a week-long training session with the lead qualitative researcher that involved discussion of the codes and hands-on coding exercises. Finally, two rounds of coding were conducted in which each of the transcripts were coded by two separate RAs. The lead qualitative researcher reviewed the coded transcripts, and any discrepancies were discussed and resolved within the team. Analyses were conducted separately for each type of respondent, and the results were combined in a final stage to identify commonalities and differences across respondents. All analyses were conducted with the transcripts in Bangla and once analyses were complete, key quotes were identified and translated to English.

Qualitative results

Engaging caregivers in the intervention.

We identified common themes across the different types of respondents relating to the enablers and barriers to caregiver attendance at sessions and engagement in the program (Table 5). An important enabler to caregivers' participation was the observable benefits to the children from attending the sessions and these benefits were observed by mothers, grandmothers and other family members. Another enabler was the efforts by the CHWs to inform the community about the importance of the program during their routine duties.

Conversely, a lack of understanding of the importance of the program within the community acted as a barrier to participation and mothers were prevented from attending sessions by prohibitions from their husband and mother-in-law and because of the demands of their household duties. Participants suggested that CHWs need to continue to raise awareness of the importance of the program among all family members during routine clinic and home visits. They also suggested that influential community members could be engaged to promote the program. This would include religious leaders like imams, schoolteachers, and community leaders, in addition to health staff.

Motivation of community health workers (CHWs)

CHWs, inspectors and managers were asked about their perspectives on the enablers and barriers to CHWs' motivation to engage with the program. The most commonly mentioned enablers were the CHWs receiving acknowledgement and praise for their active involvement in the program from their supervisors and the fact that the CHWs received positive feedback from mothers and observed positive changes in the participating children (Table 6). The inspectors reported that visits by district managers to observe program activities acted as a motivator for the CHWs.

The main barriers were resource constraints. CHWs were not provided with funds for phone calls or refreshments and some CHWs used their own personal funds to support program activities. Heavy workloads and insufficient staffing in some clinics reduced CHWs' motivation and some CHWs reported being demotivated due to the lack of support and supervision from their supervisor.

Ideas for improvement were common across all types of health staff. A commonly mentioned theme was providing additional training to CHWs to maintain their motivation. The training is highly valued as it is enjoyable, useful, and is also remunerated according to government daily rates. Providing awards and recognition to CHWs, including verbal praise, certificates and/or small prizes were suggested. CHWs, inspectors and managers acknowledged the importance of regular, supportive supervision and reported a need to strengthen the supervisory mechanisms. Finally, participants suggested including the SB3 program as a core part of the CHWs' role.

Supervision and monitoring

CHWs valued the visits from their supervisors and reported that the visits helped them to improve their skills in session delivery (Table 7). Supervisory visits were most likely to take place when the CHW and inspectors liaised together about the session schedule and/or planned the schedule together. Clinic staff reported that conducting the sessions together in a collaborative way was helpful and led to improved delivery. Inspectors and managers also reported on the importance of good communication among the various cadres of health staff, including integrating updates on the SB3 activities in the monthly meetings with CHWs, and inspectors sending regular reports on program activities to their manager so they can followup as necessary.

The main barrier to supervision and monitoring was reported to be the workload of the inspectors and the shortage of inspectors in some subdistricts due to vacant posts. The Family Planning Inspectors were reported to be less active in supervision than the Health Inspectors.

Inspectors reported that poor communication about the session schedule from some CHWs was an additional barrier.

Managers and inspectors suggested that additional training needs to be provided specifically focused on how to supervise and support the CHWs. Improved planning, better coordination between the Family Planning and Health sector staff and including the SB3 program as a standard and mandatory component of monthly reporting and monthly meetings were additional suggestions for improvement. Finally, given the difficulties faced in providing face-to-face supervision, some clinic staff and inspectors suggested using virtual supervision, by audio or video call to support the CHWs. Discussion

In this mixed-method process evaluation, we identified both strengths and weaknesses of the implementation of the SB3 program through the primary health care services in rural Bangladesh. Importantly, CHWs showed high compliance in conducting parenting sessions, actively promoted mothers' attendance at sessions through phone calls and during routine home visits, and were competent in using many of the required skills for session delivery.

Attendance as reported by mothers was good, and mothers were able to recall the majority of what they had learnt and reported playing with their child at home. The CHWs were less skilled in conducting activities aimed at promoting children's language and most mothers could not recall these activities. Other concerns were that the parenting sessions were often conducted with much larger groups than recommended, play materials were not always provided for mothers to take home, and there was limited supervision by the government supervisors. From qualitative interviews with a range of stakeholders, we investigated enablers and barriers relating to caregiver engagement, health worker motivation, and monitoring and supervision. The main enabling factors affecting caregiver engagement were the mothers' and the wider family's belief that the program was important for promoting children's development. Participants suggested that raising this awareness required additional efforts from both the CHWs themselves and from other influential persons within the community. In terms of CHW motivation, important influences included CHWs receiving appreciation for their work from their supervisors and from the community and receiving ongoing training and supervision. For monitoring and supervision, an important enabler was good communication and co-ordination at all levels of the health service. Many CHWs reported conducting sessions collaboratively which allowed them to support each other in implementation. Implementation barriers included insufficient staffing, insufficient resources, and staff workload which impacted many aspects of program implementation. Participants also reported that to ensure that the program is sustained, it needs to be further embedded into the health service as a mandatory component.

The positive findings of high compliance by CHWs, their efforts to promote mother attendance, and their competence in conducting sessions, alongside mothers' recall of most of the activities and their interest and engagement in the sessions, provide evidence for the acceptability and feasibility of the program by the frontline facilitators and beneficiaries.

Sessions were mostly conducted by the Community Clinic Health Provider (CHCP) who works full-time in the clinic and who have high level of education (Masters' degree), although other CHWs assisted at times. It is evident that the program has been adopted by the primary health care service by health staff at all levels. This relative success in integrating the SB3 program into an existing government service builds on best practices in designing, implementing and scaling interventions. For example, the SB3 program uses an evidencebased parenting program that has been carefully adapted through multiple interations for the context in which it operates [22] [23] [24] [25]. During the adaptation process, we identified the optimal caseload for CHWs, the optimal group size, and determined the frequency and duration of the sessions taking into consideration the perspectives of both CHWs and participating mothers.

The program uses a structured curriculum and structured, high-quality training focusing on demonstration and practice, features that have been identified as key to program success in prior studies [30] [31] [32]. The frontline facilitators are government CHWs, with stable employment, and who are respected and trusted by the local communities. Furthermore, the primary health care service has an existing organisational structure that can be leveraged to implement the program. Low staff turnover, respect from the community and an existing organizational structure promote the ongoing sustainment of interventions integrated into government systems [33].

The program also faces some implementation challenges. One key weakness is the low level of supervision by government inspectors. Supportive supervision with constructive feedback has been shown to increase the implementation quality of ECD parenting programs over time [34] [35]. High implementation quality is in turn associated with increased participant engagement and greater benefits to parenting practices and child development [36]. In previous trials in Bangladesh, when high quality supervision was provided by the research team, large benefits to parenting outcomes and to child development and behaviour were found [23] [24]. In this study, the low levels of supervision were reflected in some implementation weaknesses. Challenges in ensuring adequate monitoring and supervision has been reported in other studies and is especially difficult to achieve as interventions are scaled up [10, 32, 37]. In qualitative interviews, CHWs, supervisors and managers reported that ongoing supervision is important, not only for maintaining implementation quality, but also for promoting CHWs' motivation and engagement with the program. The importance of CHWs feeling appreciated and valued, and gaining respect for their work, has also been shown to be key to promoting facilitator compliance and motivation in other studies [31].

Another challenge was the inconsistency in loaning the play materials to mothers for home practice and the fact that mothers did not consistently return the toys. Provision of materials requires additional manpower, funding, timely distribution, storage facilities and regular stock-taking to ensure an adequate supply of materials. These challenges in making, distributing and storing materials have been reported in other studies [30] [31] and pose a significant logistical challenge to further scale-up and to sustainment within the government system.

The qualitative data also showed the importance of the demand side of the program.

While the supply side incorporates what is offered by the program (such as the curriculum, process of delivery, staffing), the demand side focusses on participant enrolment, engagement and retention in the intervention [38]. In our qualitative data, respondents reported that mothers' engagement was influenced not only by their own beliefs about the importance of the program, but also by the beliefs of other family members as mothers require permission from their husband and mother-in-law to attend the sessions. In previous trials of the program, we incorporated community awareness-raising activities to increase the demand for the program with the wider community, including the families of the beneficiaries [23] [24] [25].

These activities were omitted when moving towards scale due to their cost and complexity.

Lack of advocacy on the importance of ECD at the community level is a common weakness of large-scale ECD parenting programs and it is important to identify low-cost, alternative strategies to create demand and sustain engagement for these programs [39]. Fortunately, when mother-child dyads attend the sessions, the mothers themselves and other family members observe the benefits of the program which promotes further engagement as seen in this study and elsewhere [39].

The qualitative data also highlighted the need for further strengthening of the organisational systems through which the intervention was delivered. Although there is strong political will and government buy-in for SB3 program implementation, we identified gaps in communication and co-ordination between the different levels of health staff (at community, union, sub-district and district levels) and across staff from within the Directorate of Health Services and staff within the Directorate of Family Planning. It was evident that full integration had not been achieved as the SB3 program was not routinely included in meetings and in reporting mechanisms. Full integration requires that the program becomes a core part of the role of the health service.

In table 8, we show how we have used the findings from this mixed-method study to inform revisions to the intervention. This includes addressing the challenges relating to: 1) play materials and books, 2) health staff motivation, 3) caregiver engagement, 4) program monitoring and supervision, and 5) integration of the program into the government service. In terms of the play materials and books, to address the logistical challenges of providing these materials at scale, we now include toy making demonstrations in the parenting sessions and encourage mothers to make their own toys. In addition, the picture books and blocks are for sale at low cost in village shops nearby the community clinic. For health worker motivation, we have added motivational messages and strategies to the booster training sessions for both clinic health staff and their supervisors. To promote caregiver engagement, we are utilizing key community members such as imams and schoolteachers to raise awareness about the importance of early childhood development within the community. Health staff are also encouraged to explicitly point out the benefits of the program during each parenting session.

Finally, we are advocating for full integration of the program into the government health service including integrating the activities into the established reporting mechanisms, including the program as an agenda item in the scheduled monthly meetings, and including the program in the job descriptions of the health workers and inspectors. By addressing challenges across these different areas, we address the demand and supply sides of the program. The revisions to implementation aim to facilitate vertical scaling of the program within the health service and to increase the feasibility of sustainable horizontal scaling across districts.

The strengths of this research include the use of a mixed-method design that included quantitative and qualitative data. We used a stratified random sampling method to select the clinics for the observation and in-person interviews to reduce selection bias. In the qualitative evaluation, we gathered data from multiple stakeholders including the community (mothers and grandmothers), frontline health workers (community health workers), their supervisors, and health managers. This ensures that we are incorporating viewpoints from diverse perspectives. Furthermore, researchers collecting both quantitative and qualitative data had no previous contact with the participants and received high quality training in how to administer the measures.

There are also several limitations to the study. These limitations include the small number of observations of session quality; due to budget constraints, we were only able to observe one parenting session in sixty clinics. Furthermore, we only included 240 out of the 613 clinics in the initial sampling pool when randomly selecting clinics. This was because with only one field co-ordinator per district, there was insufficient time and resources to rate all clinics. This may reduce the representativeness of the sample. The CHWs were aware the observer would be attending, and it is possible that they were better prepared and delivered the session with higher quality when the observer was present. Social desirability bias may have influenced mothers' responses to the interviews. In addition, some interviews were conducted by phone, and it is possible that their responses were affected by the presence of other household members. However, no sensitive questions were asked. We collected each set of data at one time point only and hence we cannot track implementation over time, and we did not have resources to get feedback from stakeholders on the interpretation of the qualitative results. The qualitative team were not experienced in early childhood parenting interventions, and this may have affected their ability to probe effectively at times. However, they received rigorous initial training and ongoing support from the qualitative lead (RH) with support from SFM (a child development expert and the intervention lead). We were also unable to include fathers in our sample and so we have no information on fathers' perspectives of the program.

Primary health care services in rural Bangladesh are delivered through a network of community clinics. Clinics are staffed by three cadres of community health workers (CHWs): a Community Health Care Provider (CHCP) who works full time in the clinic, a Health Assistant (HA) and a Family Welfare Assistant (FWA), both of whom work 2-3 days a week in the clinic and 3-4 days a week in the community. CHCPs and HAs have Masters' degrees; most FWAs have completed high school. At the union, sub-district and district levels, the work of the CHCPs and HAs comes under the Directorate of Health Services, while the work of the FWAs comes under the Directorate of Family Planning, both under the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare. Therefore, there are two sets of government inspectors responsible for clinic health workers: Health Inspectors supervise the CHCPs and HAs, while Family Planning Inspectors supervise the FWAs. Similarly, at the sub-district and district levels, there are Medical Officers and Health and Family Planning Officers to oversee their respective activities.
