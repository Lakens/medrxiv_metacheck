How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. Abstract

Background. Historically, the Variola virus caused deadly smallpox epidemics in children.

In Copenhagen, transmission was halted by 1810 when ~90% of children were vaccinated following the introduction of a mandatory single-dose vaccine program. A 14-year honeymoon period followed, but in 1824 smallpox re-emerged as a milder disease of adults. We set out to study smallpox epidemics between 1824 and 1835 when all smallpox patients in Copenhagen were quarantined.

We analyze data from 3795 smallpox patients reported by the treating physicians between 1824 and 1835. The data include age, vaccination status, and outcome. We estimate age-specific case fatality ratios (CFR) and vaccine effectiveness (VE) against mortality.

Findings. For the four outbreaks while quarantine was mandatory (1824-1834), the CFR was ~1% among the vaccinated and ~25% among the unvaccinated, corresponding to a VE of ~95%. For the fifth outbreak in 1835, we found that CFR was low among the vaccinated but increased with age, which is evidence of waning vaccine protection. Almost no cases were over 40 years of age, suggesting lifelong immunity from smallpox infection.

Interpretation. As the unvaccinated CFR was high and comparable to the pre-vaccine era, the milder post-honeymoon smallpox disease cannot be explained by genetic changes in the virus. Rather, high coverage with an effective vaccine combined with waning vaccine protection in adulthood explains the changing face of smallpox. We demonstrates how high vaccine coverage can disguise an outbreak of highly pathogenic smallpox as the milder Variola minor.

INTRODUCTION

In the 18 th century, smallpox was a dreaded epidemic childhood disease all over the world, and Copenhagen was no exception. The disease killed 10-15% of all young children in the city which at the time was a growing population center 1, 2 , with 87,000 inhabitants around 1770 increasing to just over 100,000 by 1800. The dense population of the city made it especially vulnerable to deadly smallpox outbreaks. The enormous death toll and maiming pock marks pushed people to adopt the practice of inoculation during the 1700s. This was a risky practice in which a small amount of matter from human smallpox pustules was inoculated into the superficial layers of the skin to induce a hopefully mild illness and subsequent immunity.

Then, in 1796 Jenner developed a cowpox-based vaccine that did not carry these risks.

The Danish Professor Winsløw inoculated 80 children in 1801 and saw no serious adverse events. He then challenged two of the children with smallpox and both survived. 3 A royal vaccination committee was established in October that year and mass vaccination in Denmark began. During 1801-1810 a continually increasing proportion of the birth cohort was vaccinated. 4 The vaccine was in general well received. By 1810, Denmark implemented a vaccination program that was mandatory and free of charge. The mandate was enforced at confirmation age (13-15 years), by requiring evidence of immunity from natural smallpox or vaccination for church confirmation. Going forward, ~ 90% of children were vaccinated, and the mean age at vaccination dropped to ~1 year of age by the 1820s. 4 After 1810 smallpox disappeared from Denmark, and physicians wrote that the disease had been defeated. However, in 1824 a smallpox outbreak occurred, after 14 "honeymoon years" without the disease. The returning smallpox manifested as a milder disease affecting young adults. The return of smallpox had perhaps been anticipated by the Danish medical establishment, as this phenomenon had been observed in Edinburgh in 1818, 5 and in England in 1818 6 and 1819. 7 There was at the time widespread concern and debate about the reasons for this return of the disease, whether it was even smallpox, and whether the vaccine had failed. The etiology was debated as most cases were adults with a milder "modified" disease, characterized by a smaller number of lesions and low mortality. 8 The returning smallpox was considered a societal threat and to avoid further transmission in Copenhagen, all cases -mild and severe -were isolated at the Søkvæsthuset hospital in the city.

To investigate the effectiveness of the first smallpox vaccine against infection and death, we searched historical medical journals for studies of post-honeymoon smallpox outbreaks during 1824-1835. From May 1835 onward, the disease was no longer considered a societal threat, and efforts to isolate all cases ended, implying less complete data. We identified five Danish-language publications from the period by the physicians Møhl, Hoppe and Wendt. We also uncovered the patient records from Søkvæsthuset underlying the publications. Analyzing the smallpox data provided by the physicians with modern epidemiological methods we demonstrate that the disease was as deadly as in the pre-vaccination era among the unvaccinated, and that the vaccine was highly protective against mortality.

However, the protection of the single-dose childhood vaccination against smallpox infection waned after a decade or two, explaining the changing age distribution and milder manifestation in the vaccination era.

METHODS

1. Data on smallpox cases and deaths during 1824-1835 outbreaks

Patient records of Søkvæsthuset. 7 Søkvæsthuset recorded the name, admission date, address, age, and outcome of all patients. Vaccination status was not reported in the surviving version of the list. As both mild and severe smallpox cases were admitted to the hospital for quarantine, we assume these records include most smallpox cases in Copenhagen during 1824-1835. After July 1835, Søkvæsthuset closed because smallpox was no longer considered a societal threat. Afterwards only severe cases were admitted, with patients distributed across several city hospitals. 7 During 1824-1835, a total of 3795 smallpox patients were admitted. Of these, 186 died (CFR ~5%). We used these data to compute the age distribution of smallpox cases in the 1824-1835 period (Table 1).

Contemporary publications. We identified five publications that analyzed outbreak data from the epidemics during 1824-1835, and which had data on vaccination status. These include four papers written by the physicians Møhl and Hoppe, who attended the smallpox patients at Søkvæsthuset, and a book by Wendt. These publications include data from each of the five smallpox outbreaks in Copenhagen in 1824, 6 1825-1827 10 , 1828-30 11 , 1830-32 12 , and 1835 3 . The 1835 outbreak data are from Almindeligt Hospital for July-Dec 1935 and thus were collected after the isolation policy of milder cases was no longer in place.

Computing Case Fatality (CFR) and Vaccine Effectiveness (VE)

Using summary data from tables of data extracted from these publications for each of the five smallpox outbreaks during 1824-1835, we computed the CFRs by vaccine status and the vaccine effectiveness (VE) against mortality as an outcome.

For the 1835 outbreak Wendt only tabulated data from May 15-December when the policy of isolating mild cases was no longer in place. We used summary tables from the five publications by Møhl, Hoppe, and Wendt to compute CFRs for patients of different ages. CFR for vaccinated: CFRvax = deathsvax/casesvax CFR for unvaccinated: CFRunvax = deathsunvax/casesunvax Vaccine effectiveness: VE =1-(CFRvax/CFRunvax), with 95% confidence intervals.

Due to the VEs being relations between fractions where the fractions sometimes involve small numbers, the relative risks and confidence intervals were computed using Koopman's method. 10

RESULTS

Analysis of data from five smallpox outbreaks in Copenhagen during 1824-1835

After the introduction of the mandatory vaccination program in 1810 a "honeymoon period" free of smallpox followed. But then the disease returned to the city in December 1823 and caused five smaller, less deadly outbreaks between 1824-1835. 1 These were documented by the physicians Møhl, Hoppe and Wendt in five publications. 8, [10] [11] [12] 14 The 14-year-long "honeymoon period" ended when in December 1823, a 30-year-old worker who may have encountered smallpox on a ship in the Copenhagen harbor was hospitalized at Almindeligt Hospital for the care of the sick and poor. A hospital-based outbreak followed, as curious medical students wanting to see the rare illness fell ill, along with other patients at the hospital. Soon smallpox reached a poor house (an institution for paupers) and spread all over the city. To avoid further spreading all patients diagnosed with smallpox were admitted to a dedicated smallpox hospital, Søkvæsthuset. The physician Møhl was ordered to be quarantined at Søkvæsthuset in 1824 where he cared for the patients throughout this and the subsequent outbreak in 1825-1827. In 1825 he published an article about the 1824 outbreak where he had treated and studied all mild and severe smallpox cases in the city. He described the new and milder clinical presentation of "modified" smallpox and argued that it could be explained by waning vaccine protection 6 . In particular, he noted that very few vaccinated children below the age of six got smallpox, whereas smallpox infections became more common in older cohorts. He also noted that none of the patients were older than 40 years and suggested that this was due to lifelong protection from surviving smallpox in their childhood in the late 1700s. From this, he deduced that vaccine-induced immunity waned over time: He admitted 562 patients to the hospital and 29 of them died. 11 In 1830, Dr Møhl died for unrelated reasons and was replaced by Dr Hoppe who reported on the data Møhl had collected during the following 1828-1830 epidemic. 11 Later Hoppe reported on a fourth outbreak in 1832-1834. 11 A fifth outbreak occurred in 1835 and peaked in Nov that year. 3 For this outbreak we do not have the total number of smallpox cases but city statistics show that 434 persons died of the disease. 3 The only surviving data we could find were from Almindeligt Hospital and represented the 2 nd half of the 1835 epidemic after Kvæsthuset had closed. 3 As the mandatory isolation of patients with mild infections had ended by May 1835, the CFRs are here greater and less reliable than in previous outbreaks because some proportion of mild cases are not included. The data we analyzed from Almindeligt Hospital included 99 deaths (around 25% of all smallpox deaths in the city). We have included these data because this was the only outbreak for which double-stratified data by age and vaccination status was available to us, so that we could compute the CFR trends over time since childhood.

Case Fatality (CFR) and Vaccine effectiveness (VE)

For each of the five smallpox outbreaks during 1824-1835, we computed the CFR for vaccinated and unvaccinated cases using tabulated data in the five publications by Møhl, Hoppe and Wendt (Table 2). For example, from the 1824 outbreak data we could extract the numbers of vaccinated and unvaccinated persons, broken down by outcome (died or lived). From this we computed the CFR in the vaccinated patient group to be 0•4% (1/248).

For the unvaccinated cases the CFR was 25•7% (39/152). From these CFR estimates we computed the relative risk (RR) of dying among all smallpox cases and calculated the vaccine effectiveness against death (VE) to be 98%. We then repeated this calculation for each outbreak and found the same pattern: high CFR in unvaccinated patients and a high VE.

For the first four outbreaks we found profoundly different mortality risk in the vaccinated and unvaccinated. The CFR of the unvaccinated was ~25%, comparable to the high severity of smallpox in the pre-vaccination era, while CFR ~1% for the vaccinated. For the 1835 outbreak (where we only have data from July to December after the mandatory quarantine was lifted and only severe cases were hospitalized), the CFRs were somewhat higher.

The vaccine effectiveness remained high during 1824-1835, but declined gradually, from 98% to 89% over the 11-year study period. There was also a clear tendency for the number of cases in the vaccinated to increase with age (Table 1). This pattern of waning vaccine protection was not lost on Møhl and Hoppe, as shown by the above quote.

Age distribution of smallpox cases

The data in Table 1 show the age shift of smallpox from a childhood illness to a disease of adulthood. In the 1824 outbreak, 37% of the cases were in adults (Table 1). When stratifying by vaccination status, one sees an increasing number of adult vaccinated smallpox cases from age one to 23 years and none over 23 years of age. This is as expected, given that the earliest vaccinated cohort of children were born in 1801 and thus were 23 years old in 1824 (Figure 1). The increasing number of vaccinated cases with age is consistent with waning immunity in those who had been vaccinated long ago. Møhl commented already in his 1826 and 1828 works that those who had been vaccinated 6-20 years earlier could become infected, albeit with modified/milder form of illness. In contrast, the age distributions of unvaccinated smallpox cases were bimodal and highest among young children, likely because not all young children had been vaccinated by the age of two (Figure 1). A second peak of cases in young adults around 24 years of age is best explained by a "lost generation" born around 1800 --too early to have benefitted from the vaccination program and too late to have protection from surviving smallpox infection in childhood as the disease came under vaccine control (Figure 1).

Age variation in CFR and VE

Data from the 1835 outbreak 3 can be used to study the waning of vaccine protection over time. In Almindeligt Hospital, 98 smallpox patients died (47 vaccinated and 38 unvaccinated) out of 1165 admitted smallpox cases. This patient cohort accounted for ~25% of all smallpox deaths in Copenhagen during 1835, 3 and within the cohort ~90% of persons born after 1810 were vaccinated. 7 From a data table from Wendt 3 detailing data from 1042 cases with age, vaccine and outcome, we computed the case fatality and vaccine effectiveness (VE) for each ten-year age group. Across all ages, the CFR was 4•5% among the vaccinated and 41% among the unvaccinated (Table 3). Among the unvaccinated, the CFR was similar in all age groups (from 38% in the 40-49-year-olds up to 54% in those under 10). For the children less than 10 years old, the relative risk was not defined for the vaccinated group (zero deaths). For the unvaccinated, the CFR of those under 10 years old had very wide confidence intervals.

Therefore, we combined the age groups <20 years and estimated a VE of 95%. The VE was significantly lower for the 30-39 age group, demonstrating vaccine waning with age (time since childhood vaccination). The 40-49 age group had too few cases to obtain significant CFR and VE estimates. Across all the outbreaks, very few cases were older than 45 years. This we interpret as those born before 1800 being protected for life by immunity from natural infection in childhood. The 1835 CFRs are necessarily overestimated as many milder cases likely went unnoticed due to the abolition of mandatory isolation. Underestimation of the number of cases likely affected CFR estimates for the vaccinated more than the unvaccinated due to the typically milder cases in the vaccinated population.

This would have resulted in an underestimation of VE. However, this cannot explain the rising CFRs with age among the vaccinated, nor the fall in VE with age (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

Using unique historical reports by Danish physicians from smallpox outbreaks occurring during 1824-1835, 1, 6, 8, 9, 11 we investigated how the vaccine affected the CFR and age distribution of smallpox cases in Copenhagen when the disease returned after a 14-year honeymoon period between 1810 and 1824. We find that the vaccine almost totally prevented mortality (98% effectiveness against death in 1824, declining to 89% by 1835). Among the vaccinated, the risk of being infected increased with age, consistent with vaccine waning.

In a previous paper, we likewise used a dynamic model to show that vaccine waning may explain the age shift of smallpox after 1824 4 . Here we confirm this conclusion, this time based on direct study of vaccinated individuals and their time since vaccination. While after 1824 the disease was milder in the large majority of individuals who were vaccinated, the data showing a CFR of 25% among the unvaccinated cases clearly demonstrate that the virus was still as virulent as before 1810.

From our data from historical Copenhagen it is evident that in a well vaccinated population where the vast majority of cases are breakthrough infections in the vaccinated, even a highly virulent variola virus (later termed Variola major) would cause a mild outbreak, making it appear that the pathogen itself had become less virulent. Indeed, the physicians at the time entertained the possibility that the returning smallpox was in fact a new, inherently milder disease. While we have documented that this was not the case, starting in the early 20th century, physicians around the world noted that some outbreaks of smallpox had much lower CFR than others, and the responsible smallpox strain was eventually termed Variola minor (or Alastrim). Our work shows that it is impossible to distinguish outbreaks of inherently low virulence (V. minor) and V. major outbreaks unless the vaccination coverage in the population is taken into consideration. For some reason this is rarely the case in literature. While it is in some cases indisputable that less severe variants did exist, 15, 16 outbreaks were usually classified as one or the other based solely on population level CFR. 17 Our study follows the comprehensive analysis of smallpox in Sweden by Peter Sköld. 1 He also found that the vaccine was associated with a sharp reduction in smallpox mortality after the introduction of the vaccine, and that, after a "honeymoon period" without outbreaks, smallpox returned as a less deadly disease of adulthood. 6 Whereas the seminal study by Sköld was based only on mortality data, the Danish data from 1824-1835 are at recordlevel and contain information about all cases, age, vaccination status and outcome (death). This, and the contemporaneous treatment of smallpox as a societal threat so that all cases were isolated at a single hospital, allowed us to study the CFR of smallpox when it returned after the honeymoon period. Our Danish data confirm and strengthen his findings on the effect of the vaccine on smallpox epidemiology. In the present case, two thirds of smallpox patients were adults aged 20 or more by 1833-35, an even higher proportion than the one found in Sköld's mortality data.

Our present results likewise echo the observations made already in 1825 by Dr Møhl who noted the disappointment over the return of smallpox after the honeymoon period. Despite this, he described how the severity of smallpox was by then far less than before the vaccine was introduced. He proceeded to conclude that the vaccine protected against severe illness and mortality with protection waning after 6-20 years against illness and later against mortality. 9,11 He referred to British colleagues who had discussed a similar return of smallpox in London two years earlier, 11 noting much contemporary debate about the etiology of "modified smallpox", and even suggestions that this was a new disease. However, the strong link made by Møhl between being vaccinated and getting a "modified" course of smallpox made him conclude that the etiology of modified and natural smallpox was the same. Using modern statistical methods we here confirm his observation.

Our high CFR estimate for the unvaccinated is in apparent contradiction with Schneider and Davenport (2026) 2 who recently used smallpox mortality data from Sweden to conclude that the CFR of smallpox before vaccination was 8-10%. 2 As they estimate this by a comparison of population-wide smallpox mortality with the age-specific infection rates required to induce high immunity in adults, this estimate is in our opinion really an infection fatality rate (IFR). We agree that to determine how deadly the disease was in the general population before the vaccine this is the right approach to take, and we will conduct a similar study using the Danish data in a forthcoming paper.

In summary, we found that the vaccine was highly protective, but that this protection waned over time so that persons could again become infected with smallpox after a decade or so. The vaccine-induced protection, especially against infection but also increasingly against death, was significantly weakened in those vaccinated over 20 years earlier.

Our CFR analysis shows conclusively that unvaccinated persons who had not had natural smallpox in their childhood continued to suffer severe smallpox, similar in severity to the epidemics before the vaccine era. This shows that the virus itself had not changed after 1800. In addition to the drop in CFR, smallpox also changed from a disease of childhood to adulthood after the 1810-24 honeymoon period. We explain this age shift by two factors: firstly, the "lost generation" of young adults born before 1800 as described in the introduction. Secondly, the contribution of the waning of vaccine protection over time. We have previously used a mathematical model to explain the temporal epidemic dynamics and age patterns due to vaccine waning, 4 and here we repeat this result using statistical data analysis alone.

In our present study, we have shown that the introduction of the smallpox vaccine in Copenhagen in 1801 caused the age distribution of smallpox cases to shift and reduced its apparent severity, to the point where it was possible for contemporaries to suggest that it was no longer the same illness. However, we also show that smallpox returned in 1824 due to waning vaccine immunity and a susceptible "lost generation". The currently observed resurgence of some infectious diseases due to limited vaccine duration and unvaccinated segments of the population thus have a precedent dating back to the earliest vaccination program. More optimistically, the potential massive benefits of vaccination programs for public health were likewise already apparent at the time. As Hoppe said, "It is in no way impossible that we, by widespread and early immunization (…) could become -not free of smallpox, this is something we never should expect as susceptibility returns too early -but free from deaths due to smallpox."

-Hoppe, 1831

Hoppe and his colleagues would be pleased to know that smallpox was eventually eradicated by a massive, global WHO vaccination effort in 1979. This final victory occurred about a century after the mandatory vaccination program had ended the disease in Denmark.
