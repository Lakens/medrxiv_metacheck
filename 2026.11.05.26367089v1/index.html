To fix Exact P-Values

What we found

Show details

Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

What we found

We found 13 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Article highlights: Why did we undertake this study?

Recent international clinical guidelines have suggested that patients initially thought to have type 2 diabetes who progress to insulin within 3 years should have islet antibody testing, and that in this setting, positive islet autoantibodies will confirm classical type 1 diabetes. The guidelines are based on the mathematical interpretation of islet-autoantibody test performance and have not been directly tested.

What is the specific question(s) we wanted to answer?

Does a positive islet autoantibody test in people initially treated as having type 2 diabetes (without insulin) who progress to insulin within 3 years of diagnosis identify those with the genetic characteristics and C-peptide loss seen in classical type 1 diabetes?

What did we find?

In prospective analysis from diagnosis, we found that individuals initially treated as type 2 diabetes who progressed to insulin within 3 years of diagnosis and had one or more positive islet autoantibodies had similar genetic susceptibility to type 1 diabetes, and rate of C-peptide loss over 3 years, as individuals with classical type 1 diabetes. In contrast, the genetic susceptibility and rate of C-peptide loss in participants autoantibody positive without insulin requirement were largely similar to antibody-negative type 2 diabetes. Findings were similar in the analysis of two crosssectional studies.

What are the implications of our findings?

Our results support routine islet autoantibody testing in patients initially thought to have type 2 diabetes who require insulin within 3 years and suggest that individuals who test positive should be classified and managed as classical type 1 diabetes, in line with recent international guidelines.

Abstract

Objective: Early progression to insulin following initial management as type 2 diabetes may indicate missed type 1 diabetes. Recent guidelines advise isletautoantibody testing in all adults who progress to insulin within 3 years, though this has not been directly tested. We aimed to determine whether positive islet autoantibodies in this setting confirm the genetic characteristics and C-peptide progression of classical type 1 diabetes.

Research Design and Methods:

We compared genetic susceptibility to type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T1DGRS and T2DGRS) and prospectively assessed C-peptide loss over 3 years after diagnosis among islet-autoantibody-positive adults initially treated as having type 2 diabetes (without insulin) who progressed to insulin within 3 years (n=86), versus participants with classical type 1 (n=499) and type 2 (n=603) diabetes. Results were replicated in cross-sectional data from the DARE/GoDARTS studies (n=9466).

Results: Participants who progressed to insulin within 3 years and had positive islet autoantibodies had the genetic characteristics and C-peptide loss of classical type 1 diabetes: T1DGRS -0.04 (-0.27, 0.18) vs 0.00 (-0.09, 0.09) [p=0.71], T2DGRS -0.35 (-0.59, -0.11) vs -0.35 (-0.44, -0.26) [p=0.99] and annual change in urine C-peptide creatinine ratio -47% (-55%, -40%) vs -49% (-52%, -46%) [p=0.6] respectively. In contrast, autoantibody-positive participants without early insulin (n=51) had genetic and C-peptide characteristics consistent with type 2 diabetes. Findings were similar in cross-sectional cohorts.

Introduction

Diagnosing type 1 diabetes in adults can be challenging as features overlap with type 2 diabetes. Even highly specific features for type 1 diabetes may have limited predictive value due to the high prevalence of type 2 diabetes (1, 2). Consequently, a large proportion (~30-40%) of those developing classical type 1 diabetes as adults are misclassified and inadvertently diagnosed and treated as type 2 diabetes (3-5).

Islet autoantibodies are the primary tests for differentiating diabetes subtypes at diagnosis (6-9). However, these tests have imperfect specificity, and when autoimmune aetiology of diabetes is unlikely (low pre-test probability, such as in those with clinical features suggesting type 2 diabetes), a single positive autoantibody may be insufficiently specific to confirm type 1 diabetes (7, (10) (11) (12). In the population with positive islet autoantibodies there will therefore be those with true positive results (autoimmune aetiology diabetes) and false positive (or disease irrelevant) results (nonautoimmune (type 2) diabetes), which may explain the heterogeneous and overlapping phenotype of adults initially treated as type 2 diabetes who have positive islet autoantibodies, termed Latent Autoimmune Diabetes in Adults (LADA) (1, 10, 12).

Recent clinical guidelines from the American Diabetes Association (ADA) and European Association for the Study of Diabetes (EASD), as well as other organisations, have recommended that autoantibody tests be routinely performed in all patients with clinically suspected type 1 diabetes and in people initially treated as having type 2 diabetes who progress to insulin treatment within 3 years of diagnosis (8, 13, 14). The recommendation is based on the finding that rapid progression to insulin treatment is highly predictive of missed type 1 diabetes, and in this (high pretest probability) setting positive islet autoantibodies are mathematically likely to be highly predictive of autoimmune aetiology diabetes (2, 3, 10).

We hypothesise that one or more positive islet autoantibodies, assessed using modern high-specificity assays, will confirm the presence of autoimmune diabetes in adults initially treated as type 2 diabetes who progress to insulin treatment within 3 years of diabetes diagnosis. Therefore, this patient group will have the genetic and C-peptide progression characteristics of classical type 1 diabetes. In contrast, those without early insulin (a very low pre-test probability setting) will largely consist of false positive results and have characteristics similar to type 2 diabetes. We aimed to test this hypothesis in a prospective study of newly diagnosed adult-onset diabetes, with additional replication in cross-sectional cohorts.

Research Design and Methods

We compared C-peptide progression and genetic susceptibility to type 1 diabetes (T1DGRS) and type 2 diabetes (T2DGRS) in participants initially treated as type 2 diabetes (without insulin for over 4 weeks from diagnosis), followed prospectively for >3 years from diabetes diagnosis, and categorised according to islet autoantibody and progression to insulin status as follows: • progressing to insulin within 3 years of diabetes diagnosis with ≥1 positive islet autoantibody (I+A+; n=86) • progressing to insulin within 3 years of diagnosis with negative islet autoantibodies (I+A-; n=44) • not progressing to insulin within 3 years of diagnosis with a positive islet autoantibody (I-A+; n=51).

These participants were compared to type 1 and type 2 reference groups from the same prospective study: a type 1 diabetes reference group defined by a clinical diagnosis of type 1 diabetes at study recruitment, ≥1 positive islet autoantibody and treatment with insulin within four weeks of diagnosis (n=499), and a type 2 diabetes reference group defined by islet autoantibody negativity and no progression to insulin within 3 years of diabetes diagnosis (n=603).

Findings were replicated in a combined cross-sectional cohort from two unselected population studies: DARE and GoDARTS (n=9,466).

Prospective cohort

Adults (≥18 years) with newly diagnosed diabetes (≤12 months diabetes duration, excluding secondary or gestational diabetes) were recruited from 55 National Health Service (NHS) sites across the UK between August 2016 and February 2020 in the StartRight study (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03737799). Participants were recruited through routine clinical care appointments and record screening in primary care and self-referral after advertisements. Individuals were followed annually for 3 years. To ensure sufficient participants with late-onset type 1 diabetes, recruitment of those aged >50 was enriched for participants receiving insulin treatment at recruitment. A study flow diagram is presented in Supplementary Fig. 2s. Participants were excluded if they received insulin within 4 weeks, but did not have a clinical diagnosis of T1D or were islet autoantibody negative. Participants who did not progress to insulin treatment within 3 years of diagnosis were excluded if they had insufficient follow up (<36 months).

At recruitment, participants self-reported diabetes type and details of initial presentation, including initial treatment and where applicable timing of insulin initiation. A research nurse confirmed the diagnosis details based on medical notes and laboratory records. A non-fasted (within 1-5 h of a meal) blood sample was taken for analysis of C-peptide, islet autoantibodies (glutamic acid decarboxylase antibody [GADA], zinc transporter 8 [ZNT8A], islet antigen 2 [IA-2A]), and DNA extracted for the generation of type 1 and type 2 diabetes genetic risk scores (T1DGRS and T2DGRS). Baseline BMI was calculated from measured weight and height.

At recruitment and at each annual visit, participants provided a urine sample in boric acid preservative for measurement of the urine C-peptide-to-creatinine ratio (UCPCR) within 2 hours of completing a home meal (15, 16). After recruitment, participants were contacted annually to report their current treatment, any changes in treatment, and the date of each change. The research team obtained postrecruitment HbA1c results from healthcare records. At ≥3 years from recruitment (final visit), blood or capillary C-peptide was also assessed within 1-5 hours of a meal. In GoDARTS and DARE, clinical information was assessed by self-report and examination through an interview with a research nurse. A non-fasted blood sample was collected for C-peptide (DARE only), islet autoantibodies (DARE: GADA, IA-2A and ZNT8A; GoDARTS: GADA only), and genotyping as previously described (3, 17). Progression to insulin treatment was based on self-report in the DARE cohort, and assessment of linked electronic prescription records in GoDARTS.

Cross-sectional cohort

Laboratory analysis

All biochemical analyses other than HbA1c values were undertaken at the Royal Devon and Exeter Hospital academic blood sciences department in Exeter, UK. HbA1c results at recruitment were analysed at local NHS laboratories. GADA, IA-2A and ZNT8A were measured using the RSR ELISA assay (RSR, Cardiff, UK) using the DYNEX DS2 automated ELISA system (DYNEX Technologies, Worthing, U.K.). Islet autoantibodies were positive if the result was higher than the 97.5 th centile of 1,559 control subject population without diabetes; GADA ≥11 units/mL, IA-2A ≥7.5 units/mL, and ZNT8A ≥65 units/mL in those aged <30 years and ≥10 units/mL in those aged ≥30 years (18, 19). Exeter Hospital's academic blood science department achieved 99% specificity across all three islet autoantibodies, with 74% sensitivity for GADA and ZNT8A, and 72% for IA-2A, in the 2020 international islet autoantibody standardisation program.

The Roche Diagnostics E170 immunoanalyser (Mannheim, Germany) was used to measure plasma, capillary and urine C-peptide, with a limit of detection of 3.3 pmol/L for plasma and urine, and 200 pmol/L for capillary. Inter-and intra-assay coefficients of variation were <4.5% and <3.3%, respectively. Urine creatinine was analysed with the Jaffe method on the Roche P800 modular analyser for UCPCR.

Diabetes Genetic Risk Scores

We generated the T1DGRS from 67 single-nucleotide polymorphisms associated with type 1 diabetes, accounting for interactions among 18 HLA-DR-DQ haplotype combinations as previously reported (20).

LGC Genomics genotyped singlenucleotide polymorphisms (21). A T2DGRS was calculated using a previously validated risk score obtained from the PGS Catalog (PGS002720) (22). Standardised GRS are presented (T1DGRS standardised to type 1 diabetes controls and T2DGRS standardised to type 2 diabetes controls).

Reporting of Results to Participants and Clinicians

In the StartRight study, islet autoantibody results and brief advice (see Supplementary Figure 1s) were provided to participants and their treating clinicians after the baseline visit. Participants and treating clinicians were unaware of the baseline or follow-up C-peptide, or genetic results. The individual's treatment was left to the responsible clinician. In GoDARTS and DARE, autoantibody, C-peptide and genetic results were not fed back to participants.

Statistical Analysis

Mean type 1 and type 2 genetic risk scores and 95% CIs were presented for each insulin/antibody subgroup and compared to the reference type 1 and type 2 diabetes groups using t-tests.

Annual rate of loss of C-peptide (StartRight only), measured by UCPCR (assessed annually from recruitment to 3 years) was compared between subgroups (I+A+, I+A-, I-A+) and the two reference groups (type 1 diabetes and type 2 diabetes) using a mixed-effects model of UCPCR (23), with time since recruitment (in months, grouped into 6-month intervals and restricted to the first 36 months), subgroup and a subgroup*time interaction as fixed effects, and a random intercept and random slope for time for each individual. UCPCR data were natural log transformed (ln) for analysis due to skewness, allowing a linear fit, in line with previous studies (23) (24) (25) (26). Differences in slopes between the key subgroups (I+A+, I+A-, I-A+) and the two reference groups (type 1 diabetes and type 2 diabetes) were assessed by using the subgroup*time interaction terms in the models. Coefficients are presented as annual percentage change per year by back-transforming the fixed-effect coefficient to the original scale and multiplying by 100 [(exponential(12 x β-coefficient) -1)*100], where β is the monthly log scale slope. 95% confidence intervals were derived using the same equation on the log-scale confidence intervals. The half-life of UCPCR was determined from ln(0.5)/β, representing the time in months for UCPCR to decline by 50%.

Blood (plasma/capillary) C-peptide at recruitment and at the ≥3 year follow-up visit (measured on two occasions only) in StartRight is reported as median (IQR), and as the percentage below 200 pmol/L (severe insulin deficiency). The proportion of severe insulin deficiency was assessed using chi-squared. Slope for blood C-peptide (measured at baseline and final visit) was not assessed because the lower limit of the C-peptide assay when assessed by capillary sample is 200 pmol/L (76% [969/1,283] of those with follow up samples), making this less sensitive for determining slopes compared to UCPCR and biasing comparisons. C-peptide analysis in DARE reported median (IQR) C-peptide levels at recruitment, limited to those with diabetes duration >3 years at C-peptide assessment, and compared proportions <200 pmol/L (DARE only) using chi-squared.

Secondary analysis included limiting analysis to those progressing to insulin after six months of diabetes duration (in line with the widely used definition of LADA) (27, 28) and exploration of whether GAD titre and the number of islet autoantibodies (1 vs >1 positive autoantibody) were associated with genetic risk and C-peptide progression in antibody-positive participants with and without early progression to insulin using the same approaches described above. Consistent with a previous study, GAD titres were log-transformed and classified using a two-component Gaussian finite mixture model (11).

Results

Overall participant characteristics (prospective StartRight study) are presented in Table 1, and the flowchart for participant selection is shown in Supplementary Figure 2s (StartRight). At recruitment, when islet autoantibodies were measured, the median duration of diabetes was 4.9 months (IQR 2-9). The median diabetes duration at the latest follow-up (with blood C-peptide measurement) was 52 months (IQR 45-60). Of the individuals initially treated as having type 2 diabetes (without insulin), 130 progressed to insulin within 3 years from diagnosis (median (IQR) time to insulin initiation 6 months (2-15)), of which 66% (86/130) were islet autoantibodypositive (59% of these (51/86) were multiple antibody positive). 654 individuals did not progress to insulin within 3 years of diagnosis of whom 8% (51/654) were islet autoantibody-positive; 10% of these (5/51) were multiple antibody-positive.

Individuals with early progression to insulin and a positive islet autoantibody have the genetic characteristics of classical type 1 diabetes.

Restricting analysis to those progressing to insulin after 6 months did not meaningfully change findings.

Sensitivity analysis restricted to those progressing to insulin after 6 months of diagnosis (I+A+ n=43) did not alter findings (Supplementary Figure 3s), with similar genetic risk and UCPCR loss in the I+A+ subgroup, again comparable with T1D [p >0.3 for all]. Discussion

We have shown that positive islet autoantibodies are associated with the genetic and C-peptide progression characteristics of classical type 1 diabetes in individuals who progress to insulin rapidly (within 3 years of diabetes diagnosis), suggesting a diagnosis of type 1 diabetes is usually appropriate, in line with recent international guidelines. In contrast, most individuals who were islet autoantibody-positive but did not progress to insulin had genetic characteristics and C-peptide loss similar to classical autoantibody-negative type 2 diabetes. Findings were largely consistent in cross sectional data from those with longstanding clinically diagnosed type 2 diabetes.

A key strength of the StartRight study is the routine testing of islet autoantibodies at diagnosis and C-peptide in newly diagnosed adults with diabetes, with follow up for more than 3 years after diagnosis. The systematic collection of islet autoantibody status allows us to assess the utility of this test without biasing results by who was tested, and prospective study allows robust assessment of decline in C-peptide and progression to insulin. Another key strength is the inclusion of GRS, as clinicians are blinded to the result. Therefore, GRS can be used as an independent marker of diabetes type within a cohort, whereas clinical features will reflect the diagnosis to which a patient is assigned (29). Replication in a completely independent crosssectional cohort, assessed using the same islet autoantibody and C-peptide assays in the same laboratory, and the same assessment of genetic susceptibility, provides reassurance that the findings are robust.

A limitation of our analysis in the StartRight study is that autoantibody status was fed back to the treating clinician team, which may have influenced the decision to start insulin. However, this applies to all autoantibody positive participants, and if clinicians were starting insulin unnecessarily based on islet autoantibody results, we would expect this to bias results of the I+A+ groups towards a type 2 diabetes phenotype, resulting in (for example) slower loss of endogenous insulin secretion than classical type 1 diabetes in this group. In contrast, we found that this group did not differ from classical type 1 diabetes, suggesting this is unlikely to be the case. Importantly, many autoantibody-positive individuals never progressed to insulin, suggesting that clinicians predominantly relied on disease progression or other clinical features rather than autoantibody status alone to initiate insulin. Similar findings were observed in DARE/GoDARTS, which did not feedback autoantibody results. Further limitations include that the moderate numbers of islet autoantibodypositive individuals found among those initially treated as type 2 diabetes in prospective and cross-sectional cohorts mean confidence intervals are wide in subgroup analyses, and that autoantibodies in DARE/GoDARTS were limited to GADA and measured many years after diabetes diagnosis.

Our findings are consistent with previous research that has shown an association between insulin requirement and the phenotypic and/or genetic characteristics of type 1 diabetes in those diagnosed with type 2 diabetes who are autoantibody positive (30) (31) (32). For example, Radtke et al., using data from the HUNT study, showed that patients meeting LADA criteria, who did not require insulin at a median of 8.1 ± 0.9 years of follow-up, had similar C-peptide levels and other clinical characteristics to autoantibody-negative type 2 diabetes (32), which is consistent with our findings for autoantibody positive individuals without insulin requirement at 3 years. Our findings of little or no association between autoantibody titre and both Cpeptide progression, and type 1 diabetes genetic susceptibility are largely consistent with previous studies in clinically diagnosed type 1 diabetes (11, 33). However, they contrast with the majority of studies in the overall population of individuals with apparent type 2 diabetes, in which higher titres and numbers have been associated with a more type 1-like phenotype and genotype (27, 34). This difference, as we have previously reported, may be explained by Bayes' theorem (1, 10). In high-priorprobability settings (clinically diagnosed type 1 diabetes, or rapid progression to insulin) even a single positive autoantibody is likely to confirm autoimmune diabetes.

Here, factors that further increase test specificity (higher titre, more antibodies) will not meaningfully further increase test positive predictive value. In low-priorprobability settings (clinically suspected type 2 diabetes) a single positive autoantibody will be insufficiently specific to confirm autoimmune aetiology diabetes: and a mix of true and false positives is expected. Factors that increase test specificity (such as higher antibody titre or number) result in a test positive population with a higher proportion of true autoimmune diabetes (higher positive predictive value), and therefore a more type 1 diabetes like phenotype.

Clinical implications

Our results support recent ADA/EASD and UK type 1 diabetes immunotherapy consortium guidelines, suggesting autoantibody testing for all who progress to insulin within 3 years, even when they are thought to have type 2 diabetes, with a positive test (1 or more positive autoantibodies) usually confirming type 1 diabetes (8, 14).

Our results suggest that it is usually appropriate to treat this group of patients as classical type 1 diabetes, even if only one autoantibody is positive. These individuals will usually have a similar aetiology, loss of insulin secretion (and therefore treatment requirements) to autoantibody-positive adults who are recognised as having type 1 diabetes at diagnosis. In contrast, our results suggest that where patients initially thought to have type 2 diabetes are positive for a single autoantibody, the majority of those who do not require insulin within 3 years are likely to have nonautoimmune aetiology type 2 diabetes. However, most current guidelines consider all patients with a positive autoantibody to have a form of autoimmune type 1 diabetes, despite the imperfect specificity of these assays (35, 36). A caution in interpreting our findings, and a key area for further research, is that we do not have sufficient sample size to determine whether a 3-year cut-off, recommended in EASD/ADA guidelines and supported by previous research, is optimal (3, 8). Although in small numbers, our preliminary analysis suggests that the predictive value of positive autoantibodies will be high but (in the absence of other supporting evidence of type 1 diabetes) imperfect in those progressing to insulin within 2 and 3 years from diabetes diagnosis.

In conclusion, islet-autoantibody-positive individuals with early insulin requirement following initial treatment for type 2 diabetes have similar genotype and progression to classical type 1 diabetes. This supports recent international guidance that these patients should be diagnosed and treated as type 1 diabetes.
