Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 3 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Abstract

Mpox re-emerged as a global public health concern in 2022, and the Western Region of Ghana became the epicentre of the country's largest recorded outbreak in 2025. Ahead of a sub-national vaccination campaign launched in October 2025, no study had examined the determinants of mpox vaccine acceptance among peri-urban residents of the region using a theoretically grounded framework. This study determined the sociodemographic and Health Belief Model-based determinants of mpox vaccine acceptance among peri-urban residents of the Sekondi-Takoradi Metropolis, Western Region, Ghana. A community-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 580 adults in six purposively selected peri-urban areas and their surrounding communities between July and August 2025, before the vaccination campaign began. Vaccine acceptance was measured as a three-category outcome (accept, hesitant, refuse) and analysed using chisquare tests and multinomial logistic regression adjusted for sociodemographic characteristics, Health Belief Model constructs, and peri-urban area. Introduction Background

Mpox (formerly monkeypox) is a zoonotic disease caused by the monkeypox virus, a member of the Orthopoxvirus genus within the Poxviridae family, first identified in 1958 during an outbreak among laboratory monkeys, with the first human case documented in 1970 in the Democratic Republic of the Congo [1]. The virus is classified into two clades: Clade I, historically endemic to Central Africa and associated with greater disease severity, and Clade II, endemic to West Africa and generally associated with milder illness [1, 2]. Although mpox had circulated at low levels in endemic African countries for decades, the disease gained unprecedented global attention in May 2022, when Clade IIb strains began spreading across multiple non-endemic countries, prompting the World Health Organization (WHO) to declare a Public Health Emergency of International Concern in July 2022 [2, 3]. A second Public Health Emergency of International Concern was declared in August 2024 following the emergence and spread of the more transmissible Clade Ib strain in the Democratic Republic of the Congo and neighbouring countries [1]. By late 2025, the WHO had recorded tens of thousands of confirmed cases across the African region alone, with the Democratic Republic of Congo, Liberia, Ghana, Kenya, and Uganda reporting the highest recent case burdens [4].

Ghana has not been insulated from this resurgence. Following sporadic cases in late 2024 and early 2025, a substantial outbreak began in May 2025, with the Western Region rapidly emerging as the country's epicentre, at one point accounting for over 70% of national confirmed cases [5]. Within the Western Region, the Sekondi-Takoradi Metropolis recorded a sustained rise in suspected and confirmed cases through the third quarter of 2025, prompting the Ghana Health Service, in partnership with the WHO and the Ministry of Health, to launch a sub-national vaccination campaign targeting the most affected districts from October 2025 [5, 6]. Vaccination remains one of the most effective tools for interrupting mpox transmission and protecting at-risk populations [7], yet the success of any vaccination campaign depends fundamentally on population-level willingness to be vaccinated [8].

Vaccine hesitancy and acceptance as a global public health challenge

Vaccine hesitancy, defined as the delay in acceptance or refusal of vaccination despite the availability of vaccination services [9], has been identified as a persistent obstacle to mpox outbreak control worldwide, though its magnitude varies considerably by population and context. Among healthcare workers, studies have reported acceptance ranging from as low as 8.8% in the Czech Republic [10] to over 90% in China [11], while among men who have sex with men, a population prioritised for vaccination in several countries, acceptance has generally been higher, ranging from 81.5% in the Netherlands [12] to 86.2% in China [13]. Among general population samples, findings have been similarly heterogeneous: moderate acceptance has been reported in the Middle East [14] and Peru [15], while acceptance in Romania has remained persistently low despite a modest upward trend between 2022 and 2025 [16]. This variability underscores that mpox vaccine acceptance is not a fixed or universal quantity but is instead shaped by context-specific social, cultural, and epidemiological factors that must be understood locally rather than assumed from findings elsewhere.

Mpox vaccine acceptance in Ghana and West Africa

Empirical evidence on mpox vaccine acceptance within Ghana specifically remains limited and, where available, points to considerable hesitancy. Ghazy et al. [17], surveying a national online sample of Ghanaians in late 2022, found that only 46.1% intended to accept the mpox vaccine, with urban residence, prior refusal of the COVID-19 vaccine, and low vaccine confidence identified as key determinants. Using more recent cross-sectional data collected across four major Ghanaian cities, Braimah et al. [18] reported an even lower acceptance rate of approximately 32%, with prior vaccination history emerging as a critical predictor. Comparable studies from neighbouring Nigeria have similarly documented moderate hesitancy: Ghazy et al. [19] found low vaccine confidence and high perceived constraints among Nigerian healthcare workers, while Iliyasu et al. [20], in a mixed-methods study among the general public in Kano, found that only half of respondents were willing to accept the vaccine despite near-universal awareness of the disease. This body of evidence, while informative, shares three important limitations relative to the present context. First, existing Ghanaian studies were conducted online or in urban centres, predominantly using convenience or snowball sampling, and none has focused specifically on peri-urban populations. Second, all were conducted either before the 2025 outbreak or during its earliest phase, when mpox in Ghana remained a comparatively abstract, low-visibility threat rather than a lived, community-level emergency. Third, each measured vaccine acceptance as a binary outcome, collapsing hesitancy and outright refusal into a single category despite growing recognition that these represent conceptually distinct psychological states [18]. A broader literature on COVID-19 vaccine acceptance in Ghana, including work by Aram et al. [22], Tabong et al. [23], Aberese-Ako et al. [24], and Aninagyei et al. [25], the latter conducted in the Western Region itself, offers useful methodological precedent but cannot substitute for diseaseand context-specific evidence, given that determinants of vaccine acceptance are known to vary meaningfully across different vaccines and outbreak contexts [21].

Theoretical framework: The Health Belief Model

The Health Belief Model (HBM) is among the most widely applied theoretical frameworks in vaccine acceptance research, positing that health-protective behaviour is shaped by an individual's perceived susceptibility to a health threat, perceived severity of that threat, perceived benefits of the recommended action, perceived barriers to taking that action, and exposure to cues that prompt action, with self-efficacy incorporated in later extensions of the model [26]. The HBM has been applied directly to mpox vaccine acceptance in several international settings. Riad et al. [10] found that cues to action and perceived susceptibility were the strongest predictors of mpox vaccine acceptance among Czech healthcare workers, while Mohamed et al. [27], applying the full six-construct model among healthcare workers across three Gulf countries, identified perceived benefits and self-efficacy as the dominant predictors. Among Belarusian healthcare professionals, Riad et al. [28] similarly found that perceived benefits and cues to action most strongly predicted willingness to vaccinate.

Although the HBM has not previously been applied to mpox vaccine acceptance in Ghana, it has been validated for other health behaviours within the Ghanaian context, lending support to its theoretical appropriateness for this population. Tweneboah-Koduah [29] found that HBM constructs explained nearly 69% of the variance in breast cancer protective behaviours among Ghanaian women, Kyei-Gyamfi et al. [30] applied the model to condom use among internal migrant girls in Ghana, and Bawuah et al. [31] drew on HBM-informed prior work to explain cervical cancer screening uptake among Ghanaian women. This establishes a clear and consequential gap: no known study has yet applied the Health Belief Model to mpox vaccine acceptance within Ghana, despite the model's demonstrated relevance both to mpox vaccination behaviour internationally and to other health behaviours domestically.

Residence and vaccine acceptance

Place of residence has been repeatedly identified as a determinant of vaccine acceptance, though the direction of this association is inconsistent across settings. Some studies report higher hesitancy in urban areas: Cissé et al. [35] found significantly higher COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy in urban compared to rural areas of Burkina Faso, a pattern also observed across three successive waves of a national Chinese survey [32]. Others report the opposite pattern: in the United States, rural residents showed lower HBM-measured risk perception and lower intention to accept the mpox vaccine than urban residents [33], and rural adolescents showed significantly lower HPV vaccination uptake than their urban counterparts [34]. This inconsistency suggests that residence-based effects are context-dependent rather than universal and reinforces the recommendation of Oladapo et al. [36] that vaccine promotion strategies in low-and middle-income countries be deliberately tailored to peri-urban and semi-urban communities rather than assumed to mirror either fully urban or fully rural patterns. Periurban populations, which share some characteristics of both urban and rural settings while remaining distinct from each, thus represent a specific and, in the Ghanaian mpox context, entirely unstudied population.

Rationale and objectives

Despite the Western Region of Ghana emerging as the epicentre of the country's largest recorded mpox outbreak, and with a sub-national vaccination campaign about to be launched in the Sekondi-Takoradi Metropolis, no empirical study has examined the determinants of mpox vaccine acceptance among residents of this specific, heavily affected area. Existing Ghanaian evidence on mpox vaccine acceptance is drawn from national or urban online samples collected before or during the earliest phase of the outbreak, measures acceptance as a binary outcome that cannot distinguish hesitancy from outright refusal, and has not applied a theoretical framework capable of explaining why acceptance varies as it does. This leaves public health authorities in the Western Region without a locally grounded, theoretically informed evidence base to guide the forthcoming vaccination response, a gap this study addresses directly. This study offers three distinct contributions. First, by collecting data during an active, geographically concentrated outbreak, it provides a rare within-outbreak account of vaccine-related beliefs under conditions of real rather than hypothetical threat, addressing a temporal limitation of the existing Ghanaian literature. Second, by focusing specifically on peri-urban communities within the Sekondi-Takoradi Metropolis, it addresses a population that existing residence-based vaccine acceptance research, in Ghana and elsewhere, has not directly examined. Third, by applying the Health Belief Model and modeling acceptance as a threecategory outcome distinguishing acceptance, hesitancy, and refusal, it offers a more theoretically grounded and analytically precise account of vaccine decisionmaking than prior binary-outcome studies in this context have provided. The findings are intended to directly inform risk communication and vaccination strategy in the Western Region response, and to contribute reusable, contextspecific evidence for future mpox outbreak preparedness in similar peri-urban West African settings.

The general objective of this study was to determine the Health Belief Modelbased and sociodemographic determinants of mpox vaccine acceptance among peri-urban residents of the Sekondi-Takoradi Metropolis, Western Region of Ghana. The specific objectives were to (i) assess the level of knowledge of mpox and its transmission among peri-urban residents of the metropolis; (ii) determine the level of mpox vaccine acceptance; (iii) assess the Health Belief Model constructs (perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, perceived bene-fits, perceived barriers, and cues to action) regarding mpox vaccination; and (iv) determine the sociodemographic and Health Belief Model determinants independently associated with mpox vaccine acceptance.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This was a community-based, cross-sectional study conducted among adult residents of peri-urban communities within the Sekondi-Takoradi Metropolis (STM), Western Region, Ghana, between July and August 2025. Data collection took place during an active regional mpox outbreak and before the sub-national mpox vaccination campaign was launched in October 2025 [6]. The study examined mpox-related knowledge, Health Belief Model constructs, and stated willingness to accept the mpox vaccine; it did not assess actual vaccination uptake.

Study population and eligibility

The study population comprised adults aged 18 years and above who had lived permanently in a peri-urban community of the STM for more than one year. Peri-urban communities were identified using the Ghana Statistical Service classification. Visitors and non-residents, people who were too ill or otherwise unable to give informed consent, and individuals younger than 18 years were excluded.

Sample size determination

The minimum required sample size was estimated using the Cochran formula for proportions: n = Z²p(1-p)/d², where Z = 1.96 (95% confidence level), p = 0.50 (a conservative estimate, in the absence of prior community-based mpox vaccine acceptance data in Ghana), and d = 0.05, yielding a minimum of 385 participants. Allowing for a 10% non-response rate, the adjusted target was 424 participants. In total, 580 eligible adults were interviewed, and the response rate was 98%.

Sampling technique

A two-stage sampling approach was used. In the first stage, six peri-urban areas of the STM, all classified as peri-urban by the Ghana Statistical Service, were purposively selected: Kojokrom, Essikado, Adiembra, Diabene, Essipong, and Kansaworodo. The six areas were selected on the basis of the degree and pace of urban expansion within them and the size of their resident populations, so that, of all the peri-urban areas in the STM, those included were the ones where a vaccination campaign would reach the largest number of eligible people [37].

In the second stage, trained data collectors visited each selected area and the communities immediately surrounding it, and approached adults in their homes and in the immediate vicinity; eligible adults who were available and consented were interviewed. Because respondents at this stage were recruited on the basis of availability rather than random selection, this stage constituted convenience sampling. Each respondent was assigned to a peri-urban area using the GPS coordinates recorded in KoboCollect at the time of interview. The final sample comprised 183 respondents from Kojokrom, 127 from Essikado, 107 from Adiembra, 87 from Diabene, 46 from Essipong, and 30 from Kansaworodo (total n = 580; response rate 98%).

Theoretical framework

This study was guided by the Health Belief Model (HBM), operationalised across five constructs: perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, perceived benefits, perceived barriers, and cues to action. Self-efficacy, while part of the extended HBM, was not directly measured by this instrument and is not included as an operational construct in this study.

Data collection instrument

Data were collected using a structured, interviewer-administered questionnaire programmed in KoboCollect and completed electronically. The questionnaire comprised six sections: (1) sociodemographic characteristics; (2) knowledge of mpox and its transmission; (3) awareness of and exposure to information about the mpox vaccine; (4) HBM construct items; (5) vaccine acceptance and perceived barriers; and (6) factors that would increase willingness to vaccinate. Questions on transmission and on the vaccine were asked only of respondents who had heard of mpox. The questionnaire was pre-tested among 30 respondents in 10 communities outside the study area, and no changes were required before data collection. Data were collected by 24 trained data collectors, comprising community health volunteers, community health nurses, health promotion officers, and other public health officers, who were trained in the study objectives, the questionnaire, and informed consent procedures before fieldwork. Interviews were conducted in English, Fante, or Twi, according to the language each respondent understood best; data collectors fluent in these languages translated the questions orally, using standardised wording agreed during training.

Variable measurement

Outcome variable: Willingness to accept the mpox vaccine was measured with a single item and treated as a three-level categorical outcome: accept, hesitant ("not sure"), and refuse, consistent with the definition of the WHO Strategic Advisory Group of Experts Working Group on Vaccine Hesitancy, which treats hesitancy as encompassing delay or uncertainty as distinct from outright refusal.

Knowledge score: Among respondents who had heard of mpox, a composite transmission-knowledge score (range 0-4) was constructed by summing correctly identified transmission routes (skin-to-skin contact, sexual contact, contact with contaminated objects, consumption of bush meat); selection of the distractor item ("mosquito bite") was recorded separately as a misconception indicator. Scores were categorised as poor (0-1), moderate (2-3), or good (4). Respondents who had not heard of mpox (n = 85) were not asked these questions and were analysed as a separate category.

HBM constructs: Perceived susceptibility (whether respondents considered themselves at risk of contracting mpox), perceived severity (concern about becoming infected), and perceived benefits (belief that the vaccine can prevent the disease) were each measured with a single categorical item. Perceived barriers were measured with a single item on perceived vaccine safety (very safe, somewhat safe, don't know, not safe) and a count of endorsed concerns (side effects, lack of information, mistrust in vaccines, belief that mpox is not serious, and religious or cultural reasons; range 0-5). Cues to action were operationalised as a count of endorsed factors that would increase willingness to vaccinate (more information from health authorities, seeing others vaccinated, assurance of safety and effectiveness, and community leader endorsement; range 0-4).

Peri-urban area: Each respondent's peri-urban area (Kojokrom, Essikado, Adiembra, Diabene, Essipong, or Kansaworodo) was recorded as a six-category variable.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed in Python (version 3.13) using pandas (version 3.0), SciPy (version 1.18), and statsmodels (version 0.15); the analysis code is provided in S1 Code. Descriptive statistics are presented as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and means with standard deviations for continuous variables. Bivariate associations between candidate determinants and vaccine acceptance status were assessed using the chi-square test of independence; where more than 20% of expected cell frequencies were below 5, a Monte Carlo permutation test (10,000 permutations) was used in place of the asymptotic p-value. Multivariable analysis used multinomial logistic regression with acceptance as the reference outcome category. Predictors were specified a priori from the HBM and sociodemographic characteristics: sex, age (continuous, in years), education, occupation (student, employed, unemployed/retired), marital status (single, ever married), religion (Christian, non-Christian), perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, perceived benefits, perceived vaccine safety, barrier count, and cue count. Peri-urban area was included as a fixed effect to account for differences between areas and the clustering of respondents within them. Crude odds ratios (OR) were estimated from models containing each predictor alone, and adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) from the full model. Model fit was assessed with McFadden's pseudo-R² and the likelihood ratio test, and the contributions of perceived vaccine safety, peri-urban area, and transmission knowledge were assessed with likelihood ratio tests comparing nested models.

Multicollinearity was assessed with variance inflation factors. There were no missing data for variables in the regression model; transmission-knowledge items were skipped by design for respondents who had not heard of mpox. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of respondents

A total of 580 peri-urban residents of the Sekondi-Takoradi Metropolis completed the survey. The majority of respondents were female (406, 70.0%), with a mean age of 33.5 years (SD 12.6; range 18-75). Most respondents had attained secondary education (241, 41.6%) or tertiary education (205, 35.3%), while 134 (23.1%) had no formal education, primary education only, or Junior High School education. Students constituted the largest occupational group (192, 33.1%), followed by the self-employed (149, 25.7%) and government/public sector workers (143, 24.7%). The majority were single (343, 59.1%), and most identified as Christian (511, 88.1%), with 61 (10.5%) identifying as Muslim. Respondents were drawn from Kojokrom (183, 31.6%), Essikado (127, 21.9%), Adiembra (107, 18.4%), Diabene (87, 15.0%), Essipong (46, 7.9%), and Kansaworodo (30, 5.2%) (Table 1).

Knowledge and awareness of mpox and the mpox vaccine

Most respondents (495, 85.3%) reported having heard of mpox prior to the survey; the 85 who had not were not asked about transmission routes. Among the 495 aware respondents, the mean transmission-knowledge score was 2.8 out of 4 (SD 1.3), with 250 (50.5%) classified as having good knowledge, 126 (25.5%) moderate, and 119 (24.0%) poor. Notably, 141 aware respondents (28.5%) endorsed mosquito bites as a transmission route, a documented misconception, indicating that gaps in accurate knowledge persisted even among a broadly aware population. Of the 495 respondents aware of mpox, 349 (70.5%) knew a vaccine existed, 112 (22.6%) did not, and 34 (6.9%) were unsure.

Health Belief Model construct distributions

Across the sample, 429 respondents (74.0%) considered themselves at risk of contracting mpox (perceived susceptibility), and 454 (78.3%) reported being very concerned about infection (perceived severity). Regarding perceived benefits, 449 (77.4%) believed the vaccine could effectively prevent the disease, while 94 (16.2%) were unsure and 37 (6.4%) did not believe it was effective. For perceived barriers, belief in vaccine safety was generally high (381, 65.7% considered it "very safe"), though 34 (5.9%) considered it unsafe. The most frequently endorsed specific barriers were fear of side effects (459, 79.1%), mistrust in vaccines (367, 63.3%), and lack of information (330, 56.9%); religious or cultural objections were comparatively rare (65, 11.2%). On cues to action, more information from health authorities (459, 79.1%) and assurance of safety and effectiveness (436, 75.2%) were the factors respondents most frequently indicated would increase their willingness to vaccinate (Table 2).

Vaccine acceptance status

Overall, 439 respondents (75.7%) indicated they would accept the mpox vaccine if offered, 75 (12.9%) were hesitant ("not sure"), and 66 (11.4%) indicated they would refuse.

Bivariate determinants of vaccine acceptance

All sociodemographic characteristics examined were significantly associated with acceptance status (Table 1). Acceptance declined steadily with age, from 88.4% among 18-24-year-olds to 53.1% among those aged 45 and above (p < 0.001).

A clear educational gradient was evident, though non-linear: acceptance was lowest among respondents with no or only primary education (28.6%), rose sharply among those with Junior High School (84.7%) and Secondary education (90.5%), and was comparatively lower again among tertiary-educated respondents (65.9%) (p < 0.001). Occupation showed a similarly strong pattern, with students (94.3%) and private-sector workers (93.6%) showing the highest acceptance and the unemployed/retired group the lowest (28.6%) (p < 0.001). Acceptance also differed by peri-urban area, ranging from 55.1% in Adiembra to 95.6% in Kojokrom (p < 0.001).

All HBM construct items were significantly associated with acceptance status (Table 2). The steepest gradient was observed for perceived vaccine safety: acceptance ranged from 95.5% among those who considered the vaccine "very safe" to 14.7% among those who considered it "not safe," with refusal rising correspondingly from 2.4% to 58.8% (p < 0.001). A similarly steep gradient was observed for perceived benefits (89.5% acceptance among those believing the vaccine effective, versus 48.6% among those who did not, p < 0.001). Perceived susceptibility and perceived severity showed non-monotonic patterns: respondents at the extremes (either clearly at risk/concerned, or clearly not) showed higher acceptance than those in the intermediate "not sure"/"somewhat concerned" categories, suggesting that ambivalence itself, and not merely low risk perception, is associated with reduced acceptance. Knowledge was also associated with acceptance status (p < 0.001): acceptance was 87.6% among respondents with good knowledge, 76.2% and 73.9% among those with moderate and poor knowledge, and 42.4% among those who had not heard of mpox. The association persisted when the analysis was restricted to the 495 respondents who had heard of mpox (p = 0.007).

Discussion

Summary of key findings

This study found that three-quarters of peri-urban residents in the Sekondi-Takoradi Metropolis (75.7%) were willing to accept the mpox vaccine, with 12.9% hesitant and 11.4% refusing. Beliefs about the vaccine itself, namely its safety and its effectiveness, were the strongest determinants of acceptance: perceived safety was independently associated with both hesitancy and refusal, uncertainty about effectiveness with hesitancy, and belief in effectiveness with lower odds of refusal. Education and occupation were also independently associated with acceptance status, and acceptance differed substantially between peri-urban areas. Knowledge of mpox transmission was associated with acceptance in bivariate analysis but not after adjustment for vaccine beliefs and sociodemographic characteristics.

Vaccine acceptance in context

The acceptance rate observed here is notably higher than in earlier Ghanaian mpox studies. Ghazy et al. [17], surveying a national online sample in late 2022, found that only 46.1% of Ghanaians intended to accept the mpox vaccine, and Braimah et al. [18], using cross-sectional data from four major Ghanaian cities, reported an even lower acceptance rate of approximately 32%. A plausible explanation lies in timing and context. Data for the present study were collected in July-August 2025, during an escalating outbreak in which the Western Region had become the epicentre of Ghana's mpox cases, and before the sub-national vaccination campaign was launched in October 2025 [6]. Respondents were therefore living through a visible, community-level outbreak and were exposed to outbreak-related health messaging, including risk communication and health education, rather than responding to a hypothetical or distant threat. Direct proximity to a live outbreak plausibly heightened perceived susceptibility and severity in ways that earlier, pre-outbreak surveys could not capture, consistent with the Health Belief Model's premise that perceived threat is a precondition for protective action. This pattern also echoes Elsayed et al. [38], who found that recent, lived outbreak exposure shaped vaccine-relevant beliefs in Dar es Salaam more than background knowledge did. Because the data predate the campaign, these findings also serve as a pre-campaign baseline against which uptake during and after the campaign can be compared.

Health Belief Model constructs and acceptance

Perceived vaccine safety and perceived benefits were the dominant constructs in this study. Respondents who regarded the vaccine as very safe had 98% and 97% lower adjusted odds of hesitancy and refusal, respectively, than those who regarded it as unsafe, and fear of side effects was the most frequently endorsed concern (79.1%). The importance of perceived benefits is consistent with the broader HBM-vaccine literature. Riad et al. [28], studying mpox vaccine hesitancy among Belarusian healthcare professionals, identified perceived benefits as the construct most strongly correlated with willingness to vaccinate, and Mohamed et al. [27], applying the HBM to mpox vaccine acceptance among Gulf healthcare workers, found perceived benefits and self-efficacy to be the strongest predictors. Al-Metwali et al. [39] reached the same conclusion for COVID-19 vaccine acceptance in Iraq, where perceived benefit was among the most robust correlates identified. In the present study, the protective association between belief in vaccine effectiveness and refusal was attenuated once perceived safety was taken into account but remained significant (aOR 0.22, 95% CI 0.06-0.86). The two beliefs therefore overlap substantially, since most respondents who considered the vaccine very safe also considered it effective, yet each contributes independently.

Perceived susceptibility behaved differently. In bivariate analysis, respondents who considered themselves at risk were more likely to accept the vaccine, but after adjustment they had higher odds of refusal than those who did not consider themselves at risk (aOR 4.59, 95% CI 1.28-16.44). This reversal, together with the wide confidence interval, suggests that once beliefs about vaccine safety and effectiveness are held constant, perceived risk adds little independent motivation to be vaccinated, and the finding should be interpreted cautiously. A related pattern was reported by Wong et al. [40], who, in a populationbased HBM study of COVID-19 vaccine acceptance in Hong Kong, found that perceived susceptibility carried no significant association with acceptance once other constructs were considered; the authors attributed this to governmental recommendation and perceived benefit overriding personal risk calculus. The outbreak's visibility likely elevated baseline risk perception across nearly the entire sample (74.0% considered themselves at risk), leaving relatively little variance for susceptibility to explain once vaccine beliefs were accounted for.

The non-monotonic pattern observed for both susceptibility and severity, in which the intermediate "not sure" and "somewhat concerned" categories showed lower acceptance than either extreme, is a less commonly reported finding in this literature, though it aligns conceptually with treating hesitancy as a distinct psychological state rather than as a midpoint on a single continuum from acceptance to refusal, consistent with the working group definition adopted here and with this study's decision to model acceptance as three categories rather than collapsing to binary. It suggests that ambivalence about personal risk, not simply low perceived risk, is itself an obstacle to firm acceptance, a nuance that binary-outcome studies in this literature would not be able to detect.

The barrier and cue-to-action counts require careful interpretation. The barrier question was worded for respondents who were unwilling or unsure but was answered by all respondents, and accepters endorsed more concerns on average than hesitant or refusing respondents (mean 2.6, 1.7, and 2.0 concerns, respectively). After adjustment, the barrier count was not associated with refusal (aOR 1.28, 95% CI 0.74-2.21), while a higher count was associated with lower odds of hesitancy, which likely reflects accepters listing hypothetical concerns rather than barriers that deterred them. The cue-to-action count was not independently associated with either outcome once peri-urban area was taken into account. Descriptively, refusers named the fewest factors that would increase their willingness to vaccinate (mean 1.7, compared with 2.7 among accepters) and most often cited mistrust in vaccines (76%), consistent with firmer opposition that is less open to persuasion, whereas accepters and hesitant respondents pointed to concrete needs, namely more information from health authorities, assurance of safety and effectiveness, and visible peer uptake. These factors are better read as a guide to where targeted intervention could still move undecided individuals than as determinants in their own right, and the results caution against interpreting count-based HBM measures without multivariable adjustment.

Knowledge of mpox transmission was associated with acceptance in bivariate analysis: acceptance was 87.6% among respondents with good knowledge but 42.4% among those who had not heard of mpox. However, knowledge was not independently associated with acceptance once sociodemographic characteristics, vaccine beliefs, and area were taken into account (likelihood ratio test p = 0.76). This aligns with Elsayed et al. [38], who, examining knowledge, attitudes, and practices toward viral disease outbreaks including mpox in Tanzania, concluded that vaccine hesitancy was driven primarily by concerns about efficacy and fear of side effects rather than by overall knowledge deficits. Taken together, these findings suggest that awareness of the disease is a necessary foundation, since respondents who had never heard of mpox were the least willing to be vaccinated, but that transmission-route education alone is unlikely to be sufficient; beliefs about the vaccine itself appear to be the more direct intervention target.

Sociodemographic determinants

Education showed a distinctive non-linear relationship with acceptance: respondents with Junior High School or Secondary education showed markedly higher acceptance than either those with no/primary education or, notably, those with tertiary education. This is a departure from the assumption that acceptance rises monotonically with education, but it is not without precedent: higher educational attainment can be associated with greater exposure to competing or conflicting information sources and more independent critical evaluation of health claims, which does not uniformly translate into higher acceptance. The occupational gradient was more conventional: students and the formally employed showed substantially higher acceptance than the unemployed or retired, consistent with Bushi et al.'s [21] systematic review of malaria vaccine acceptance determinants across sub-Saharan Africa, which similarly identified employment status as a significant correlate of vaccine acceptance, plausibly reflecting differential access to health information, healthcare services, and institutional trust associated with formal engagement in education or work.

Christian respondents had higher adjusted odds of hesitancy than non-Christian respondents (aOR 4.09, 95% CI 1.03-16.26), with no corresponding difference in outright refusal. Descriptively, Muslim respondents were more polarised (a higher refusal rate but a lower hesitancy rate) than Christian respondents, who more often occupied the undecided middle category. Given that the non-Christian reference group was small (n = 69, predominantly Muslim) and the confidence interval wide, this pattern is better treated as a hypothesis for qualitative investigation into denomination-specific decision-making, of the kind explored by Aberese-Ako et al. [24] in their ethnographic study of community engagement and COVID-19 vaccine acceptance in Ghana, than as a settled conclusion.

Acceptance also differed substantially between peri-urban areas, from 55.1% in Adiembra to 95.6% in Kojokrom. Part of this difference reflects the composition of the samples: 61% of Kojokrom respondents were students, compared with 13-25% in the other areas. After adjustment for individual characteristics and vaccine beliefs, residents of Diabene, Essikado, Essipong, and Kansaworodo remained less likely than residents of Adiembra to be hesitant, and Essikado residents were also less likely to refuse. These residual differences may reflect local variation in outbreak exposure, health messaging, or community leadership, and suggest that vaccination outreach should be tailored to individual communities rather than applied uniformly across the metropolis.

Notably, age, sex, and marital status all showed strong, statistically significant associations with acceptance status in bivariate analysis, but none remained significant after adjustment for education, occupation, religion, and the HBM constructs. This indicates that their apparent influence operated indirectly, through their correlation with education and occupation, rather than as independent determinants, a distinction only visible because both crude and adjusted estimates were reported, and a reminder that single-variable descriptive comparisons common in much of the vaccine-acceptance literature can overstate the independent role of basic demographic characteristics.

Strengths and limitations

This study's principal strength is its timing and specificity: it captures vaccinerelated beliefs from a defined peri-urban population during an active, geographically concentrated outbreak and before vaccination began, using a theoretically grounded instrument and a three-category outcome that preserves the distinction between hesitancy and refusal, a distinction most binary-outcome studies in this literature cannot make. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the peri-urban areas were purposively selected and respondents were recruited from among available adults rather than by random selection; the sample overrepresents women (70.0%) and students (33.1%), the level of acceptance may therefore not reflect the wider peri-urban population, and the sample size calculation did not account for a design effect. Second, respondents were clustered within six areas; area was included as a fixed effect, but with so few areas, between-area variation could not be modelled as a random effect. Third, most HBM constructs were measured with single items, self-efficacy was not measured, and the barrier question was answered by all respondents although it was worded for those who were unwilling or unsure. Fourth, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; the associations identified here describe correlates of stated willingness at a single point in time rather than confirmed drivers of vaccination behaviour. Fifth, self-reported willingness may be subject to social desirability bias, particularly during an active outbreak with prominent public health messaging. Finally, generalisation to urban-core or rural populations elsewhere in the Western Region, or to Ghana more broadly, should be made cautiously.

Conclusions

This study found that three-quarters of peri-urban residents of the Sekondi-Takoradi Metropolis were willing to accept the mpox vaccine during an active regional outbreak, before the vaccination campaign began. Beliefs about the vaccine itself were the strongest determinants: perceived safety was independently associated with both hesitancy and refusal, and beliefs about its effectiveness with both outcomes. Education, occupation, and peri-urban area also independently shaped acceptance status, whereas knowledge of transmission, although associated with acceptance in bivariate analysis, was not independently associated with it once vaccine beliefs were considered. By modelling acceptance as three categories rather than collapsing it to a binary outcome, this study showed that hesitancy and outright refusal are not driven by identical factors: hesitancy was most closely tied to uncertainty about vaccine benefit, safety concerns, and lower education, while refusal was shaped by safety concerns, disbelief in vaccine effectiveness, and employment status.

These findings suggest that mpox vaccination strategies in similar peri-urban West African settings should prioritise clear, credible communication about vaccine safety and effectiveness, building on basic disease awareness rather than relying on it, and should be deliberately targeted toward unemployed and lesseducated residents, who showed disproportionately lower acceptance independent of their underlying beliefs, as well as toward areas with lower acceptance. Because data collection coincided with a live, geographically concentrated outbreak and preceded the vaccination campaign, these results offer a rare within-outbreak, pre-campaign baseline of how vaccine-related beliefs behave under real, rather than hypothetical, threat conditions. This evidence can inform the vaccination response in the Western Region and future outbreak preparedness planning across similar communities in Ghana and the wider sub-region.
