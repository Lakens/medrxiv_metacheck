Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

What we found

We found 12 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. Abstract

Ascending aortic surveillance relies on diameter thresholds, yet most dissections occur below them. Inflammation drives aortic wall remodeling independent of diameter.

Inflammation drives aortic wall remodeling independent of diameter. Inflammatory indices derived from routine blood counts, such as neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), red cell distribution width (RDW), and systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) have been show to predict adverse cardiovascular outcomes, but their association with ascending aortic growth is unclear.

We retrospectively studied adults with ≥2 computed tomography (CT) scans of the native ascending aorta ≥6 months apart at a single center (2000-2023), excluding those with aortitis or no complete blood count during follow-up. Markers were summarized as 95th percentile values per patient and per scan interval. Growth was defined as a ≥2mm increase in mid-ascending aortic diameter. Patient-level logistic regression and interval-level generalized estimating equation models were adjusted for age, sex, body mass index, baseline diameter, systolic blood pressure, diabetes, and heritable thoracic aortic disease. Among 3,262 patients, data were available in 1,882 for RDW, 1,471 for NLR, and 1,468 for SII; 192 (10.2%) met criteria for growth. Only RDW was independently associated with growth in the overall cohort (patient level: odds ratio [OR] 1.071, 95% CI 1.002-1.144, p=0.043; interval level: OR 1.075, 95% CI 1.012-1.141, p=0.019); NLR (p=0.080) and SII (p=0.164) were not. Among patients with guidelinedefined ascending aortic dilation (≥40 mm), all three markers were associated with interval growth (RDW OR 1.14, NLR OR 1.05, SII OR 1.01 per 100 units; all p≤0.014), whereas none were among aortas <40 mm; effect modification was significant for NLR (p=0.01).

Conclusions:

RDW was modestly associated with ascending aortic growth overall, and all three markers were associated with interval growth in patients with guideline-defined aortic dilation, suggesting that systemic inflammation may contribute to progression of aortic dilation. Prospective studies with more specific inflammatory markers are needed.

Clinical Perspective

Introduction

Ascending thoracic aortic aneurysms (aTAA) are managed primarily through serial imaging surveillance, in which absolute aortic diameter is measured to predict the risk of life-threatening complications. However, more than 90% of dissections occur in patients below these surgical thresholds, with a median pre-dissection diameter of approximately 40 mm. [1] [2] [3] [4] This well-described paradox exposes a fundamental gap in current size-based risk stratification and underscores the need for complementary markers of disease activity that extend beyond aortic diameter.

Aortic aneurysm formation and progression are driven at the cellular level by degeneration of the medial layer of the aortic wall. 1 Under pathological conditions, contractile vascular smooth muscle cells dedifferentiate into a synthetic phenotype, producing matrix metalloproteinases and proinflammatory factors that remodel the extracellular matrix and reduce structural integrity. 5, 6 This phenotypic switch can be induced by inflammatory cell infiltration and oxidative stress, and is accompanied by vascular inflammation independent of the degree of aortic dilation. 7 Because these processes precede and may drive measurable diameter change, circulating markers that reflect systemic inflammatory burden have been proposed as potential surrogates of active aortic remodeling.

Several inexpensive, widely available indices derived from the complete blood count (CBC) have emerged as markers of systemic inflammation. The neutrophil-tolymphocyte ratio (NLR) and the systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) have been linked to adverse cardiovascular outcomes and stroke risk. 8, 9 Red cell distribution width (RDW) has similarly emerged as a robust prognostic marker in cardiovascular disease, where elevated values are thought to reflect chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and cytokine-mediated impairment of erythropoiesis. [10] [11] [12] Despite the biological plausibility of a link between systemic inflammation and aortic remodeling, the value of these routinely collected markers for predicting growth of the ascending aorta during surveillance remains unclear.

In this study, we evaluated whether CBC-derived inflammatory markers are associated with interval growth of the ascending aorta among a large cohort of patients undergoing serial CT surveillance. We specifically aimed to determine whether these markers independently predict aortic growth at both the patient level and the individual surveillance-interval level, after adjustment for established demographic and clinical risk factors. We further examined whether these associations differed in patients with guideline-defined ascending aortic dilation, the population in whom imaging surveillance is recommended.

Methods

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request. Study participants were identified via institutional aortic research databases and electronic medical record (EMR) queries. Inclusion criteria comprised adult patients (≥18 years) with a minimum of two serial computed tomography (CT) scans of the ascending aorta, separated by at least six months, without prior surgical repair of the ascending aorta. Given that the average pre-dissection diameter is approximately 40 mm and risk is present below conventional aortic diameter boundaries, 2 no minimum dilation threshold was imposed. Patients were excluded if they had a known diagnosis of aortitis or if no complete blood count (CBC) data were available during the surveillance period. Heritable thoracic aortic disease (HTAD) was defined as the presence of any of the following: Marfan syndrome, Loeys-Dietz syndrome, vascular Ehlers-Danlos syndrome or non-syndromic pathogenic variants (e.g., ACTA2, MYH11, or MYLK)

Study Design and Population

Data Collection and Laboratory Integration

We extracted mid-ascending aortic (mid-AsAo) diameters from structured clinical radiology reports using an in-house text processing pipeline that has been employed in other related studies. 13 Of note, all aortic measurements were performed in a specialized 3D imaging lab, by trained image analysis technologists using standardized protocols with expert radiologist oversight. Implausible values and extreme interval changes suggestive of typographical errors were manually audited and corrected. EMR data were abstracted for baseline demographics, comorbidities, vital signs, and clinical outcomes. Three CBC-derived inflammatory markers were evaluated: red cell distribution width (RDW), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and systemic immune-inflammation index (SII). NLR was calculated as the absolute neutrophil count divided by the absolute lymphocyte count. SII was calculated as (platelet count × absolute neutrophil count) / absolute lymphocyte count. All absolute count values were normalized to K/µL prior to calculation. For SII and NLR computation, all components were required on the same calendar date.

Inflammatory marker values were summarized at two levels. At the patient level, each marker was summarized using the 95th percentile of all eligible CBC results obtained between the patient's first and last CT scans, resulting in one marker value per patient across the entire surveillance period. At the interval level, each marker was summarized using the 95th percentile of all eligible CBC results obtained between each pair of consecutive CT scans, resulting in one marker value per imaging interval.

Because the number of CBC draws varied across patients, each marker's 95th percentile was computed by averaging the two nearest results, using midpoint interpolation. Patients with only a single draw had that value used directly. This approach was selected to capture upper-end inflammatory burden over time while reducing sensitivity to isolated extreme values that may reflect acute illness or measurement variability. Time-weighted approaches were considered but the irregular CBC sampling made them less reliable.

Definitions of Aortic Growth

Aortic growth was treated as a binary variable for analysis. Growth was defined as an increase of ≥2 mm in mid-AsAo diameter during the surveillance interval, a threshold that was chosen because this degree of change exceeds typical inter-scan measurement variability. 14, 15 Intervals shorter than six months were excluded.

Statistical Analysis

Associations between inflammatory markers and aortic growth were evaluated at two analytical levels. At the patient level, univariable logistic regression was performed using the 95th percentile of each marker summarized across the entire surveillance window, with one observation per patient. At the interval level, logistic regression was performed using generalized estimating equations (GEE) analyzing each consecutive scan pair independently while accounting for within-patient correlation.

Multivariable logistic regression was then performed to assess the independent association between each marker and binary aortic growth, after adjustment for age, sex, body mass index (BMI), baseline mid-AsAo diameter, systolic blood pressure, diabetes mellitus, and HTAD. Results are reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).

To focus on the subgroup of patients with guideline-defined ascending aortic dilation, models were stratified at a baseline mid-AsAo diameter of 40 mm (<40 mm vs ≥40 mm), the threshold at which imaging surveillance is recommended, 1 and effect modification was tested with a marker-by-diameter-group interaction term. Sensitivity analyses (1) excluded intervals with extreme NLR values (>99th percentile) and (2) substituted the mean, median, and maximum for the 95th percentile of each marker.

GEE models used an exchangeable working correlation structure clustered by patient.

Continuous variables are reported as median (interquartile range [IQR]) and were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test; categorical variables are reported as n (%) and were compared using the chi-square test. A two-sided p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study Population

Of 3,262 patients with serial CT imaging of the native ascending aorta, CBC data during surveillance were available for RDW in 1,882, NLR in 1,471, and SII in 1,468.

The population included for analysis consisted of 1,882 patients, of whom 67.3% were male, with a median age of 63.1 years (IQR 53.4-70.9), median baseline mid-AsAo diameter of 40.0 mm (IQR 36.0-43.3), and median follow-up of 4.1 years (IQR 2.0-7.0).

Hypertension was present in 90.2% of patients and diabetes in 30.8%. Baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1. On multivariable logistic regression, RDW was independently associated with aortic growth at the patient level after adjustment for mid-AsAo diameter, systolic blood pressure, diabetes mellitus, HTAD, and demographics (OR=1.071, 95% CI=1.002-1.144, p=0.043; Figure 1). At the interval level, this association was confirmed on GEE logistic regression after the same adjustment (OR=1.075, 95% CI=1.012-1.141, p=0.019). NLR and SII were not independently associated with aortic growth on multivariable analysis at either level (NLR p=0.080; SII p=0.164; Figures S1 and S2).

Ascending Aortic Dilation Subgroup

We next evaluated whether the association between inflammatory markers and aortic growth differed between patients with and without guideline-defined ascending aortic dilation (≥40 mm vs <40 mm) at both the interval and patient levels (Table 2). At the interval level, none of the three markers were associated with growth among aortas <40 mm (RDW OR=1.02, 95% CI=0.94-1.11; NLR OR=1.01, 95% CI=0.99-1.02; SII OR=1.00, 95% CI=0.99-1.01). In contrast, all three markers were associated with growth among aortas ≥40 mm (RDW OR=1.14, 95% CI=1.04-1.24, p=0.004; NLR OR=1.05, 95% CI=1.02-1.07, p<0.001; SII OR=1.01, 95% CI=1.00-1.03, p=0.014). The interaction between baseline diameter and NLR was statistically significant (p=0.01).

For RDW and SII, the change in OR point estimates between non-dilated and dilated groups did not reach statistical significance (p=0.10 for both). At the patient level, this pattern was not reproduced. RDW was associated with growth among aortas <40 mm (OR=1.10, 95% CI=1.01-1.19) but not ≥40 mm, and no marker showed evidence of effect modification (all p for interaction >0.45).

Discussion

In this retrospective analysis of 1,882 patients undergoing serial CT surveillance of the mid-AsAo, comprising 2,824 discrete imaging intervals and 9,371 cumulative patient-years of follow-up, RDW was modestly but independently associated with aortic growth at both the patient level and the interval level. Neither NLR nor SII was associated with growth in the overall cohort at either level of analysis. However, among patients with guideline-defined ascending aortic dilation (≥40 mm), the population under clinical surveillance, all three markers were associated with interval growth, whereas none were among nondilated aortas. This concordance is notable because the markers capture partly distinct biology; RDW reflects inflammation-related disturbance of erythropoiesis accumulated over months, whereas NLR and SII reflect leukocyte and platelet responses. A consistent signal across erythrocyte-and leukocyte-based indices supports an inflammatory contribution to growth once aortic dilation is established. To our knowledge, this is among the largest longitudinal analyses of circulating inflammatory markers and ascending aortic growth to date. A further strength is that, instead of relying on a single value, we related the inflammatory burden within each surveillance interval to the aortic growth observed over that same interval, allowing inflammatory markers to be temporally aligned to the interval in which growth occurred.

Medial degeneration of the ascending aorta is a chronic process. Contractile vascular smooth muscle cells dedifferentiate toward a synthetic phenotype, increase matrix metalloproteinase and cytokine production, and degrade the elastic lamellae over months to years. [5] [6] [7] Consistent with this, murine models of Marfan-associated aneurysm have demonstrated that adventitial inflammation, characterized by progressive immune cell infiltration, tracks closely with aneurysm progression and precedes rupture. 16 Adaptive immunity may also contribute, as B cells accumulate in the adventitia of dilated human ascending aortas and loss or depletion of B cells attenuates aneurysm development in experimental models, further supporting a mechanistic link between chronic inflammatory activity and aortic wall remodeling. [17] [18] [19] A circulating marker that tracks this process must reflect sustained inflammatory activity. RDW quantifies the variation in circulating red cell volume and rises when erythropoiesis produces a more heterogeneous red cell population, with chronic inflammation as a driver of this heterogeneity. Sustained interleukin-6 signaling raises hepcidin, which sequesters iron within macrophages, 12, 20 while inflammatory cytokines blunt erythropoietin responsiveness and impair erythroid maturation, and the resulting anisocytosis accumulates across the erythrocyte lifespan of roughly 120 days. 11 RDW therefore behaves as a downstream marker of months of low-grade inflammation. Its independent association with ascending aortic growth supports the premise that chronic inflammatory activity contributes to aortic remodeling and disease progression. The stronger association observed among patients with aortic dilation is consistent with the expectation that chronic inflammatory pathways matter more once structural aortic disease is established and the wall is actively remodeling. The same reasoning explains why RDW carries prognostic weight across heart failure, 10 stable coronary artery disease, 21 stroke, 22 and abdominal aortic aneurysm. 23 RDW was the only marker of the described pathway available at scale in our data. Markers positioned upstream in this chain may reflect the inflammatory signal more directly. Interleukin-6 (IL-6) is an important mediator of the inflammatory response, while high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) is a downstream acute-phase marker influenced by IL-6 signaling. Each carries prior support in aortic disease, with a Creactive protein to IL-6 ratio tracking uncomplicated thoracic aortic aneurysm size 24 and elevated hs-CRP predicting incident abdominal aortic aneurysm in a prospective population cohort. 25 We examined these markers in our data. hs-CRP was available during surveillance in 58 patients and IL-6 in 3 patients. These data were too sparse and too selectively obtained to support meaningful inference across a heterogeneous surveillance population. Prospective sampling with a standardized inflammatory panel at fixed surveillance intervals would place these markers on equal footing with imaging and test the chronic-inflammation hypothesis on its own terms.

The null results for NLR and SII in the overall cohort are consistent with the same hypothesis. NLR derives from neutrophil and lymphocyte counts, and SII additionally incorporates platelets, all of which shift within hours in response to infection, trauma, corticosteroids, surgery, and physiologic stress. 26 A cohort sampled opportunistically across years captures many such episodes, and the variance they introduce reflects transient inflammation remote from medial degeneration. We summarized each marker by its 95th percentile across the interval, which captures peak inflammatory burden while remaining more stable than a single maximum value. This reduces the influence of any one extreme draw, yet the 95th percentile still samples the upper end of each marker's distribution. For NLR and SII, whose upper values are composed largely of transient elevations, this statistic may capture acute events more than sustained inflammatory tone. In patients with established dilation, in whom medial remodeling is presumably more active, sustained inflammatory activity may be large enough to emerge above this background variability, which may in part explain why NLR and SII were associated with growth only in this group. NLR and SII have previously been linked to all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in large cohorts, 8, 9 yet neither examined aortic growth. Two prior studies examined CBC-derived indices in thoracic aortic aneurysm. One cross-sectional study reported higher RDW-to-platelet and mean platelet volume ratios in aneurysm patients compared to non-dilated controls. 27 This is broadly consistent with a link between RDW and ascending aortic disease, though the study addressed a different question. Their study examined whether RDW is elevated in an already established disease, whereas our study examined whether absolute RDW values predict growth over longitudinal follow-up. A second study found that a preoperative NLR above 3.5 was significantly more common among patients with symptomatic or ruptured aTAA, suggesting that NLR may be associated with more advanced or acute aortic disease. 28 In the overall cohort, we found no association between NLR and aortic growth during longitudinal outpatient surveillance in an asymptomatic population, although NLR was associated with interval growth among patients with aortic dilation. This discrepancy is likely related to differences in patient population and clinical setting. That study measured NLR once, immediately before surgery, when levels may have been influenced by the acute presentation, whereas our study evaluated repeated NLR measurements collected over several years and examined their association with subsequent aortic growth. While NLR may have value as a marker of symptomatic or ruptured disease, our findings do not support its use as a standalone predictor of growth across all patients under surveillance, although it may carry signal in those with established dilation.

Limitations

Our study is not without limitations. First, our data are retrospective and from a single center, limiting generalizability. Second, the use of electronic medical records may not fully capture the comorbidities, inflammatory conditions, infections, medication changes, and interval procedures that influence the biomarkers in this study. Third, and relatedly, CBC data were not uniformly available, and patients with more laboratory data may have had more severe or chronic disease and greater health care contact. Fourth, growth was modeled as a binary rather than continuous outcome, precluding assessment of a dose-response relationship. This dichotomous definition was chosen deliberately, since clinical aortic diameter measurements carry substantial intrinsic variability, and annualizing modest changes over short surveillance intervals amplifies this error to produce spurious growth rates. Fifth, the dilation-stratified analyses were secondary, formal effect modification reached significance only for NLR, and because SII is derived from the same neutrophil and lymphocyte counts as NLR, these two indices do not provide fully independent evidence. Our findings, including the RDW association and the signal among dilated aortas, should therefore be considered hypothesis-generating and require validation in an independent, prospectively sampled cohort before clinical application.

Conclusions

In conclusion, among a large cohort of patients undergoing longitudinal CT surveillance of the ascending aorta, elevated RDW was modestly but independently associated with aortic growth, whereas NLR and SII showed no independent association in the overall cohort. Among patients with guideline-defined ascending aortic dilation, however, all three inflammatory markers were associated with interval growth, a concordant signal that supports a contribution of systemic inflammation to disease progression in the population undergoing surveillance. While the modest effect sizes limit the value of these markers for standalone risk stratification, these associations provide proof of concept that systemic inflammatory activity may contribute to ascending aortic remodeling and support prospective evaluation of more specific markers such as hs-CRP, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, and IL-6, particularly in patients with aortic dilation.
