To fix Exact P-Values

What we found

We found 1 imprecise p value and 1 p value reported as exactly zero out of 38 detected p values.

Show details

Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

P-Value Text
p > 0.05 However, these differences were not statistically significant (p > 0.05).

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

American Psychological Association (2020). Publication manual of the American Psychological Association, 7 edition. American Psychological Association.

P values are never exactly zero. A p value of .000 is a rounding artifact — the actual value is simply smaller than the reported precision. Very small p values should be reported as p < .001 rather than p = .000. A p = 0 can also be a model parameter, such as an ARIMA order; check that it is a p value.

What we found

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Chatbots offer scalable, accessible, and personalized support for physical activity (PA). This review synthesized evidence on their efficacy and examined potential moderators.

Eleven databases were searched for English-language published through January 27, 2026.

Eligibility was assessed using the PICOS framework. Data extraction was guided by research questions, mERA checklist, and PRISMA 2020. Risk of bias was evaluated using Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool and the NIH Quality Assessment Tool. Meta-analyses reported Hedges' g. Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots, Egger's regression, and trim-and-fill analyses.

Potential moderators were examined through subgroup analyses and meta-regression.

A total of 12,481 records were identified, of which 43 studies were included in the systematic review and 27 in the meta-analysis. Most studies included in the meta-analysis had some risk-ofbias concerns (16/27). Chatbot interventions produced a significant small-to-moderate postintervention effect on PA (g = 0.31, 95% CI [0.19, 0.44]; n = 24), whereas the follow-up effect was small and non-significant (g = 0.11, 95% CI [-0.01, 0.22]; n = 4). Effects varied across PA outcomes (g = 0.08-0.44), and several subgroup differences helped explain the significant heterogeneity. For daily steps, significant subgroup differences were observed by clinical status, while intervention duration (QM = 6.09, p = 0.048) and clinical status (QM = 5.69, p = 0.017) were significant moderators.

Chatbot interventions showed small-to-moderate post-intervention effects but limited follow-up effects. Few significant moderators were identified, underscoring the need for more rigorous RCTs.

Introduction

Physical activity (PA) is widely recognized as critical components of public health [1, 2] and has increasingly recognized as a leading modifiable exposure for controlling noncommunicable diseases [3]. However, the prevalence of physical inactivity has risen significantly, from 23.4% in 2000 to 31.3% in 2022, with current trends indicating that the global target of a 15% reduction by 2030 is unlikely to be met [4]. Therefore, scalable strategies are needed to reduce physical inactivity at the national level. Digital health interventions, characterized by interventions delivered via digital technologies such as smartphones, websites, text messaging, have substantial potential to improve access to PA over the past decades [5]. In recent years, research has grown in interest in chatbots for promoting PA [6] [7] [8], especially with the advent of ChatGPT (Chat Generative Pre-Trained Transformer) in 2022. A Chatbot, otherwise known as "conversational agent", is a computer program designed to respond to conversational or informational replies with verbal (audio-based chatbots) or written (text-based chatbots) messages [9]. These chatbots primarily functioned as health coaches or self-monitoring tools to promote PA [10].

Some existing meta-analytic evidence has demonstrated the moderate efficacy of chatbots for promoting PA [11, 12]. However, some research gaps exist. First, limited synthesized evidence compares generative AI chatbots with traditional machine learning-based or rule-based chatbots for promoting PA. Computer-based health counseling emerged in the late 1990s [13, 14], followed by early non-AI PA chatbots in the 2000s [15]. Since 2018, AI chatbots have become increasingly viable for PA support [10]. More recently, advances in generative AI since 2022 may further enhance chatbot-based PA interventions by reducing repetitive responses and increasing content novelty [16]. Therefore, updated evidence is needed to understand how advanced algorithms may influence chatbot performance in promoting PA. In addition to the evolution of chatbot algorithms, visual representations of chatbots, such as bodies and facial expressions, remain underexplored. These features may be important for enhancing user engagement during interactions. However, previous meta-analyses have not distinguished between chatbots with visual representations, such as embodied conversational agents, and chatbots without visual representations when examining intervention efficacy.

Regarding intervention characteristics, although intervention duration has been examined in previous meta-analyses [11, 12], follow-up effects have not been explored. From public health and clinical perspectives, sustained effects at follow-up are important indicators of intervention efficacy. With the increasing number of chatbot intervention studies in recent years, the evidence base has expanded, allowing us to examine the difference between follow-up effects and postintervention effects. Moreover, intervention efficacy may vary by comparison group. For example, previous studies suggested that non-visual AI chatbots may show different effects for promoting PA when compared with human coaches versus other chatbots with visual expression [10]. However, none of existing meta-analysis [11, 12] explored how the efficacy varies from different control groups, such as usual care, human adviser, and base chatbot. Understanding how results differ by control condition is important because the comparison group directly shapes how intervention efficacy should be interpreted. Distinguishing between true controls, such as placebo or no treatment, and active controls helps determine whether chatbots are effective compared with doing nothing or offer added benefits over existing alternatives. In addition, context is also an important determinant of health outcomes [17]. However, to our knowledge, limited evidence is available on whether the efficacy of chatbots for promoting PA varies across different intervention settings such as community setting, free-living setting, and home setting. Lastly, from the participant characteristics perspective, previous reviews have highlighted the importance of examining whether chatbot efficacy differs between healthy and clinical populations [10], which may help inform the use of chatbots in clinical populations.

However, there remains a lack of meta-analytic synthetic on the efficacy of chatbots among clinical status.

Therefore, this systematic review with meta-regression study aims to evaluate the efficacy of chatbot interventions on PA while also exploring potential moderators, including comparison group, multiple intervention, intervention duration, intervention setting, participants clinical status, assessment time point, chatbot output type, chatbot's use of visual expression, chatbot's algorithm type, as well as two groups of moderators including chatbot characteristics and intervention characteristics, through subgroup analyses and meta-regression.

Methodology Protocol and registration

The protocol for this systematic review and meta-analysis was prospectively registered on Open Science Framework [18] and the results are reported according to PRISMA guidelines.

There was no funding source for this study.

Search strategy

We searched 11 databases, including Web of Science, PubMed, CINAHL, MEDLINE, EMBASE, Scopus, APA PsycINFO, IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, arXiv, and medRxiv.

The search strategy primarily focused on titles and abstracts, using terms related to chatbots and PA (see Table S1). To ensure a comprehensive search, we identified relevant synonyms of physical activity and chatbot from previously published studies. Filters were applied to include English-language records published before January 27, 2026. Document types were limited to articles, early access articles, and proceedings papers. One reviewer was involved in the data searching stages (L.F.).

Selection process

The reference records were exported from each database in multiple formats (e.g., .ris, .nbib, and .enw) and then imported into Covidence platform for screening [19] S2. We conducted two stages of screening: title/abstract screening and full-text screening. At each stage, two reviewers (L.F. and D.S.) independently screened the records. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and consultation from a third reviewer (Y.B.). Interrater reliability was calculated using Cohen's kappa in Covidence [20]. The results indicated substantial agreement, with kappa values of 0.78 for title and abstract screening and 0.76 for full-text screening.

Data extraction and synthesis

The original extraction items were derived based on the research questions, the mobile health evidence reporting and assessment (mERA) checklist [21], and the Preferred reporting items for systematic review and meta-analysis protocols (PRISMA-P) 2020 [22]. Extracted information included publication characteristics, chatbot characteristics, outcomes of interest, population characteristics, intervention characteristics, as well as sample sizes, means, and standard deviations data of intervention and control group (see Table S3 for more information).

Included full-text articles were stored in Box folder, and data were extracted using a Microsoft Excel form. One reviewer (L.F.) conducted the primary extraction, while a second reviewer (D.S.) independently verified a random half of the articles. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion. A narrative approach was then used to systematically synthesize the evidence. All studies that met the review eligibility criteria were included in the narrative synthesis, and their study, outcome, participant, intervention, chatbot, characteristics were summarized in tables.

Studies were included in the meta-analysis only when sufficient quantitative data were available to calculate an effect size.

Risk of bias assessment

Two reviewers (L.F. and D.S.) independently assessed risk of bias. The review team was not blinded to the authors or journals during the review process. Disagreements were resolved through discussion between two reviewers. We used the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool [23] to assess randomized controlled trials (RCTs), including parallel RCTs, cluster RCTs, and crossover RCTs. The tool assesses five domains of bias: the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selection of the reported result. Minor adaptations were applied for cluster RCTs and cross-over RCTs according to the Cochrane guidance. We followed the signaling questions and algorithms provided in the Cochrane documentation to determine the risk of bias for each domain and the overall risk of bias for each study. Overall risk of bias was classified as low risk, some concerns, or high risk.

For single-group before-after studies, we used the NIH Quality Assessment Tool for pre-post studies with no control group [24, 25]. This tool primarily assesses internal validity using 12 evaluation items, such as whether the study objective was clearly stated, eligibility criteria were prespecified, etc. For each study, we calculated the percentage of applicable items rated "Yes."

Consistent with previous studies [26], studies were classified as low risk (>75%), some risk (50%-75%), or high risk (<50%). To provide comprehensive findings, no studies were excluded based on risk-of-bias or quality ratings. The overall risk of bias for each study was finally visualized using the robvis tool [27]. Green indicates low risk, yellow indicates some concerns, and red indicates high risk.

Outcomes of interest

Outcomes of interest were (1) daily steps; (2) moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA, minutes/week); (3) total physical activity minutes (any measure of low, moderate and/or vigorous physical activity reported as a duration), and other PA related outcomes if available.

Meta-analysis methods

The main meta-analysis included both RCTs and single-group pre-post studies. An additional RCTs-only meta-analysis was conducted as a sensitive analysis. In both the main and sensitive analyses, two time points were analyzed separately: post-intervention and follow-up. Data were visualized by outcome type in the forest plots. Standardized mean differences (SMDs; Hedges' g) were used as the effect measure to allow comparison across studies using different outcome scales. Hedges' g was selected because it provides a bias-corrected estimate of effect size, particularly for studies with small sample sizes. Standardized classifications were used to interpret the magnitude of effect sizes: 0.20 as small, 0.20 to 0.50 as medium, and greater than 0.50 as large [28]. A p-value of < .05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic. Tau-squared (τ²) was also reported to estimate between-study variance. The following cutoffs were used to interpret I²: 0% to 29% = no heterogeneity, 30% to 49% = moderate heterogeneity, 50% to 74% = substantial heterogeneity, and 75% to 100% = considerable heterogeneity [29]. All meta-analyses were conducted in R using the metagen function. We contacted study authors when sample size, means, and standard deviations were not reported in the article.

For publication bias analyses, effect sizes were aggregated within studies by outcome because multiple effect sizes derived from the same study are statistically dependent. This approach reduces potential violations of the independence assumption required for funnel plot interpretation, with each point in the funnel plot representing one outcome per study. Visual inspection of funnel plots and Egger's regression test for funnel plot asymmetry was conducted.

In addition, the trim-and-fill method was applied to estimate the potential impact of missing studies on the overall effect size. Publication bias analyses were conducted using the metabias function in R.

Subgroup analysis and Meta-regression methods

Random-effects subgroup meta-analyses using restricted maximum likelihood were conducted separately for each PA outcome. Meta-regression was then used to identify differences between specific subgroup categories. Univariable and multivariable meta-regression analyses were conducted separately using restricted maximum likelihood. Multilevel random-effects meta-regression models were fitted to account for between-study heterogeneity and within-study dependence among effect sizes, as some studies contributed more than one comparison or effect size. For each moderator, we conducted outcome-specific and all-outcome models. Outcomespecific models were fitted separately when a sufficient number of studies were available for a given outcome (>10 studies) [30]. The all-outcome models pooled all PA-related outcomes to increase statistical power and provide a more comprehensive assessment of PA behavior.

Analyses were performed in R using the meta package.

The examined moderators of subgroup meta-analysis and meta-regression included: (1) comparison group, including no intervention, usual care, human advisor, and text chatbot (base), (2) multiple intervention, categorized as chatbot only versus chatbot plus, (3) intervention duration, categorized as ≤6 weeks, >6 to <12 weeks, and ≥12 weeks, (4) intervention setting, including community, home, and free-living settings, (5) clinical status, categorized as clinical versus non-clinical, (6) assessment time point, categorized as mid-intervention, post-intervention, and follow-up, (7) chatbot output type, categorized as speech/voice, text, or mixed output, (8) use of visual expression, categorized as embodied conversational agent versus chatbot, and (9) algorithm type, categorized as non-AI, traditional AI, and generative AI. Categories within each moderator were included only if they were represented by more than one study. In addition, two groups of moderators were examined: chatbot characteristics, including output type, use of visual expression, and algorithm type; as well as intervention characteristics, including intervention duration, intervention setting, and clinical status.

Discussion

Overall, AI chatbot interventions demonstrated statistically significant small-to-moderate efficacy in promoting physical activity (PA) across different outcomes at post-intervention. This finding is consistent with previous meta-analyses of chatbot interventions for PA [11, 12]. The observed effects are also comparable to those reported in other digital health interventions for PA [48, 49] and a benchmark effect size (Cohen's d = 0.21) for PA interventions reported by Wright et al. (2021) [50]. However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because most studies included in the meta-analysis were rated as having some concerns regarding risk of bias. When different outcomes were considered, significant changes were observed only for walking minutes, total PA, and daily steps, with effect sizes decreasing in that order. Chatbots showed only a small, non-significant effect on MVPA. These findings differ from Singh et al. (2023) [11], who reported stronger effects for MVPA than for total PA and steps, but is consistent with Bi et al.

(2024) [51], who found stronger effects on steps than MVPA. One possible explanation is that walking minutes, total PA, and steps capture a broader range of movement, including lightintensity PA as well as MVPA. In contrast, increasing MVPA generally requires individuals to engage in activity at a higher intensity (>100 steps/minutes) [52], such as brisk walking or structured exercise, which may be more difficult to achieve and maintain. Therefore, chatbot interventions may be more effective in promoting overall movement or step accumulation than in increasing MVPA. When comparing walking, total PA, and step counts, the walking minutes and total PA outcomes included in this meta-analysis were primarily assessed using self-reported approach. In contrast, step counts were generally assessed objectively. It is well known that participants may overreport their PA levels when conducting self-report surveys. This issue may be more pronounced in digital intervention studies, where participants often cannot be blinded to the intervention conditions. Therefore, walking minutes and total PA showed greater improvement than step counts after the chatbot intervention. However, researchers should be cautious that the results for walking minutes were only from two non-RCTs [53, 54]. Moreover, RCTs-only sensitivity analysis showed a non-significant effect for total PA, with an effect size relatively like that observed for steps. This means more RCTs are needed to explore the efficacy on walking minutes and total PA.

A small, non-significant effect size was observed at follow-up assessments. This provides new synthesized evidence compared with previous meta-analyses [11, 12]. The small follow-up effect size is consistent with findings from other digital health interventions targeting PA, which reported a pooled effect of SMD = 0.17 (95% CI: 0.04-0.31) [48]. However, the follow-up effect should be interpreted with caution because it was based on only four studies. In the metaregression analysis including all PA outcomes, a similar pattern was observed, with follow-up effects being slightly smaller than post-intervention effects when compared with mid-intervention effects. However, assessment time did not significantly moderate the effects of chatbot interventions on PA outcomes. One potential concern regarding the meta-regression of assessment time is the uneven distribution of studies across assessment points, with most studies provided post-intervention evidence, whereas relatively few studies examined mid-intervention and follow-up effects. Therefore, we were unable to draw a firm conclusion about whether intervention effects are maintained over the long term after chatbot interventions. Future studies are encouraged to provide more evidence on follow-up effects. In addition, because of the limited number of studies evaluating follow-up outcomes, we were also unable to examine whether factors such as intervention duration, chatbot characteristics, and user engagement moderate the long-term maintenance of PA after chatbot interventions. For example, future studies could examine whether longer intervention duration helps maintain PA engagement after the intervention ends.

The exploratory subgroup analyses suggested that chatbot effects may differ by comparison group type, such as usual care, human advisor, or digital non-chatbot controls, particularly for total PA, exercise time, and all PA outcomes. However, in the meta-regression analyses including daily steps or all PA outcomes, comparison group type was not a statistically significant moderator. This discrepancy may reflect the difference between descriptive subgroup comparisons and formal moderator testing, especially after accounting for dependency among effect sizes. Therefore, although comparison group type may contribute to heterogeneity for some specific outcomes, it should not be interpreted as a robust or consistent moderator of chatbot intervention effects. In particular, chatbot interventions were associated with greater increases in daily steps compared with usual care in both the subgroup and meta-regression analyses. One possible explanation is that participants in the chatbot intervention groups often continued to receive usual care, with the chatbot added as an additional PA support component. Therefore, the observed effects may reflect the added benefit of chatbot-based support beyond traditional usual care. Finally, only one study compared chatbots with human advisors, [54] limiting conclusions about their relative effectiveness. Further comparative research is needed.

When intervention duration, participants' clinical status, and intervention setting were examined together as intervention characteristics, the combined model did not show a statistically significant moderating effect. Similarly, subgroup analyses suggested that the effects of chatbot interventions on daily steps did not differ by intervention duration, which is consistent with another meta-analysis comparing interventions lasting ≤6 weeks versus >6 weeks [11].

However, in the meta-regression analysis of daily step outcomes, intervention duration was a significant moderator. Specifically, interventions lasting 6-12 weeks had significantly greater effects on daily steps than those lasting ≤6 weeks, whereas interventions lasting >12 weeks showed smaller, non-significant effects compared with those lasting ≤6 weeks. The reduced effects observed in longer-duration interventions are consistent with Wang et al. (2025) [12], who found that chatbot interventions produced a modest but statistically significant increase in PA over shorter durations (≤8 weeks), but not over longer durations (>8 weeks). Although we did not have sufficient data to examine whether the association between engagement and PA outcomes changed over time, some studies found that higher engagement (i.e., usage measures) was associated with greater PA participation [41, 55]. Therefore, the lower effects observed after longer intervention durations may be partly explained by reduced adherence or engagement over time, which could weaken post-intervention effects. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because the number of studies was unevenly distributed across duration categories, with 50% of studies lasting less than 6 weeks. In addition, when all PA outcomes were included in the sensitivity meta-analysis, intervention duration was no longer a significant moderator. This may be because including multiple PA outcomes increased heterogeneity in measurement tools and units, thereby increasing uncertainty around the moderator effect. Future studies should provide stronger evidence on the moderating role of intervention duration across different outcomes, such as MVPA and total PA, and further examine how engagement patterns change over time and relate to post-intervention efficacy. Building on previous reviews [11, 12], we further found a significant moderating effects of clinical status on daily steps, but not on across all outcome meta-regression. Clinical participants showed a significantly greater increase in daily steps compared with non-clinical participants. This might be because participants in clinical samples may have greater perceived needs, clearer health goals, and stronger intuitive motivation, which could enhance responsiveness to chatbot-based support. However, the number of studies was unevenly distributed between clinical (n = 3) and non-clinical (n = 8) populations.

Additional studies are needed to confirm these findings. Furthermore, intervention setting was not a statistically significant moderator. However, the step-specific meta-regression suggested slightly smaller effects in free-living settings compared with home-based settings. In the metaregression including all PA outcomes, home and free-living settings showed non-significantly smaller effects than more structured community settings. These findings suggested that chatbot interventions may have stronger effects in more controlled or structured settings. Future research should further explore strategies to enhance intervention effects in free-living settings.

Regarding chatbot characteristics, the multivariate models including output type, use of visual expression, and algorithm type did not show statistically significant moderating effects. In addition, neither chatbot output type nor algorithm type significantly moderated intervention effects in either the step-specific or the all PA outcomes meta-regression, which is consistent with previous meta-analytic findings [11]. Some interesting patterns were observed. Only the meta-regression including all PA outcomes had sufficient data to examine differences among generative AI, traditional AI, and non-AI chatbots. The results showed that both traditional AI and generative AI chatbots had non-significantly larger effects than non-AI chatbots. Traditional AI chatbots also showed larger effects than generative AI chatbots relative to non-AI chatbots.

However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because only three studies used generative AI approaches, which may have limited statistical power. More evidence is needed to compare these approaches in chatbot interventions. Moreover, we did not find a statistically significant effect of visual representation, such as embodied conversational agents, compared with chatbots without visual representation. One possible explanation is that most chatbots with visual representation used non-AI algorithms, which may have limited the potential benefits of visual embodiment. Future studies should explore whether combining visual representation with AI algorithms can enhance the effectiveness of chatbots in promoting PA. We also examined different chatbot features and found several non-significant differences. For example, audiobased chatbots [35], action planning [56], humorous outputs [57], and visual cues [58] showed slightly larger effects than their comparison features, including text-based chatbots, coping planning, non-humorous outputs, and relational cues. These findings suggested that modifying a single chatbot feature may not be sufficient to produce substantial benefits. Future studies should test combinations of features to better understand how chatbot design can be optimized for promoting PA.

Contribution, limitations, and implications

This review used rigorous methods, including searches of 11 databases, multistage screening, risk-of-bias assessment, comprehensive data extraction, meta-analysis, and sensitivity analyses. It extended previous subgroup analyses [11, 12] by using meta-regression to formally test multiple participant-, intervention-, and chatbot-related moderators, although findings should be interpreted cautiously because of the limited number of studies. It also allows multiple moderators to be tested simultaneously. Second, this study provided preliminary evidence on the sustainability of chatbot effects by analyzing the follow-up effects. Third, analyses of intervention setting and clinical status suggested that chatbots may be useful across controlled and free-living settings and in clinical populations.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, only studies published in English were included, which may have introduced language bias. Second, the review deviated from the registered protocol by narrowing the outcomes to physical activity and expanding the planned moderators from five to 11. Conclusions

This meta-analysis found statistically significant small-to-moderate effects of chatbot interventions on PA. Several subgroup differences across PA outcomes helped explain heterogeneity. In the meta-regression analyses, intervention duration and participants' clinical status were significant moderators of daily steps. Future large-scale trials with rigorous designs are needed, along with additional studies to provide sufficient power to determine how participant, intervention, and chatbot characteristics influence the effectiveness of chatbots for PA promotion. For single-group studies, common concerns included lack of outcome assessor blinding and limited pre-post measurement, which may reduce confidence in the reliability of the intervention effects. Overall, the included studies were conducted across 12 countries or regions. The largest number of studies were conducted in the United States (n1 = 17/43; n2 = 12/27), followed by the Netherlands (n1 = 7/43; n2 = 4/27) and Australia (n1 = 5/43; n2 = 3/27). Publication years ranged from 2005 to 2026, with most studies published after 2020 (n1 = 31/43; n2 = 21/27). Dr. Timothy Bickmore's research group contributed the largest number of studies on embodied conversational agents and PA (n1 = 9/43; n2 = 7/27). The total sample size was 5,901 across all included studies and 4,794 among studies included in the meta-analysis. Most studies recruited fewer than 100 participants (n1 = 25/43; n2 = 14/27). Participant age ranged from 12.02 to 74 years across all studies and from 12.02 to 71.3 years among studies included in the meta-analysis.

In terms of intervention characteristics, most studies combined chatbots with other intervention components (n1 = 28/43; n2 = 21/27), such as activity trackers, additional app functions, usual care, daily web-based educational content, home blood pressure monitoring, physician visits, or prescribed medications. Intervention duration ranged from a single session to 48 weeks. Most interventions lasted 12 weeks or less. Specifically, 19 studies lasting less than 6 weeks (n2 = 8/27), 18 studies lasting 6 to 12 weeks (n2 = 11/27), and 6 studies lasting more than 12 weeks (n2 = 6/27). Interaction frequency varied across studies and included daily, weekly, biweekly, several times per week, and several times per day. Among studies that specified a predefined interaction duration, each interaction typically lasted between 5 and 15 minutes. Most studies were conducted in free-living settings without a specified physical location (n1 = 24/43; n2 = 15/27). The remaining studies were conducted at home (n1 = 11/43; n2 = 8/27), in laboratories (n1 = 3/43; n2 = 1/27), community centers (n1 = 2/43; n2 = 2/27), schools (n1 = 1/43; n2 = 1/27), workplaces (n1 = 1/43; n2 = 0/27), and primary care clinics (n1 = 1/43; n2 = 0/27). Among studies in free-living settings (n1 = 24; n2 = 15), smartphones were the most used platform for interacting with the chatbot (n1 = 16/24; n2 = 11/15). Most smartphone-based interventions used standalone apps, although a small number integrated chatbots into existing social platforms, such as Slack [41,42] and Facebook Messenger [43]. In contrast, among studies conducted in specified physical settings (n1 = 19; n2 = 12), computers were the most commonly used platform (n1 = 15/19; n2 = 7/12). Regarding chatbot characteristics, sixteen studies (n1 = 16/43) used chatbots that were graphically represented with a body or avatar and were categorized as embodied conversational agents (n2 = 8/27). Most embodied conversational agents used mixed text-and audio-based input/output modalities (n1 = 8/16; n2 = 6/8) and non-AI algorithms (n1 = 13/16; n2 = 8/8).

The study's conclusions are also limited by the current evidence base. Most studies included in the meta-analysis were rated as having some concerns regarding risk of bias. Most studies included predominantly female adults from nonclinical populations and evaluated text-based chatbots within multicomponent interventions. Voluntary recruitment may also have underrepresented underserved individuals and those with lower motivation or literacy, limiting generalizability. Although randomization supports causal interpretation of overall intervention effects, subgroup and meta-regression analyses rely on nonrandomized study-level characteristics. These analyses may identify potential sources of heterogeneity but cannot establish causal moderation. Evidence suggests that chatbots are promising tools for promoting PA in clinical and nonclinical populations. Future research should prioritize rigorous randomized controlled trials with larger samples (>100), longer interventions (>12 weeks), and follow-up assessments. More studies are also needed among adolescents, sex-diverse populations, and clinical groups. Researchers should also examine changes in behavioral and emotional engagement over time and their associations with post-intervention and follow-up intervention outcomes. Direct comparisons with human advisors and non-chatbot digital interventions are needed to establish the added value of chatbots. Such comparisons are necessary to ensure that claims regarding the added value of chatbots are supported by evidence rather than based primarily on theoretical assumptions. Future studies should also compare generative and traditional AI chatbots, evaluate models beyond GPT-based systems, and test combinations of design features, including avatarbased interfaces, empathy, dialogue flexibility, and humor.
