Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

Abstract

In September 2024, Hurricane Helene caused significant flooding in western North Carolina (NC), leading to concerns surrounding water quality, including the risk of acute gastrointestinal illness (AGI). The potential for flooding-related drinking water contamination is exacerbated for private well users, who comprised a large proportion of the population most affected by the hurricane. Here we investigated the impact of Hurricane Helene on AGI and determined if this impact was different in areas with greater proportions of residents using private wells. We used electronic health records from Truveta to identify inpatient and outpatient visits to hospitals for AGI in NC from 2016-2024 at the zip3-by-week level and classified exposure to flooding using satellitederived flood depth estimates. We conducted a controlled interrupted time series analysis to estimate the effect of the hurricane on the incidence of AGI among flooded zip3 units. From 2017 to 2024, there were approximately 548k AGI visits in NC across 20 zip3 units. Our findings show that the hurricane was associated with positive, insignificant changes in the incidence of AGI three weeks following the hurricane (incidence rate ratio: 1.11 [0.59, 2.07]), controlled for pre-hurricane trends and post-hurricane changes in lesser flooded regions. Results were consistent across several time windows and analyses examining sensitivity to geographic data masking, model specification, and study design. Our findings warrant re-evaluation at finer spatial and temporal scales and examination of other public health impacts of hurricanes, which are expected to increase in severity and frequency in the eastern United States due to climate change.

1. Introduction

Hurricanes are expected to increase in both frequency and intensity due to climate change (1).

These events have widespread impacts on health, including but not limited to physical injuries, waterborne diseases, and mental health issues (2). The disruption of health care services and public health systems, which need to shift from normal operations to emergency response, further compounds the impacts of these events (1, 3). The impact of hurricanes can be significant for drinking water systems, particularly individuals using private wells (4). Over 43 million United States (US) residents (13% of the population), many in rural or underserved areas, rely on private wells for drinking water (5). Because private wells are unregulated by the federal government, water contamination can often remain undetected unless well users conduct water testing independently.

From 1980-2024, North Carolina (NC) has experienced 33 tropical cyclones and flooding events with financial losses exceeding 1 billion USD. In September 2024, Hurricane Helene caused widespread destruction in rural, western NC and is considered the most devastating disaster in the region's history (6). Rainfall amounts ranged from 20-30 inches, and the state experienced severe flooding and landslides, resulting in 108 deaths (6, 7). Counties in declared disaster zones following the hurricane faced barriers to healthcare access and generally had greater proportions of individuals with disabilities, older adults, and mobile homeowners (8, 9). The 25 western counties of North Carolina that were most affected by Hurricane Helene are also home to many rural communities relying on private wells, which can have elevated risk of contamination and are not subject to routine monitoring (10). Across all of NC, roughly 25% of residents rely on private wells and only 6% of the predicted 1.6 million wells have been tested since February 2009 (11, 12), leading to significant knowledge gaps in water quality for many individuals even in the absence of extreme weather conditions.

Acute gastrointestinal illness (AGI) broadly describes enteric illness caused by Escherichia coli, Salmonella, Campylobacter, Yersinia enterocolitica, Cryptosporidium, Giardia, or other pathogens or non-infectious agents (11) and is clinically characterized by general abdominal cramping, diarrhea, and nausea (12). In the US, roughly 12-16.4 million cases of AGI attributed to public drinking water systems occur annually (13) (14) (15). Although the total burdens of drinkingwater associated AGI and other waterborne illnesses in countries with high access to safe drinking water are uncertain (16, 17), evidence suggests individuals who use private wells are at exacerbated risk in comparison to those receiving water from public water systems. For example, a study conducted in North Carolina found that, of drinking-water associated AGI emergency department visits, 99% were associated with private well contamination while only 1% were associated with public water systems, reflecting that the burden of drinking-water related AGI falls on private well users (18) . During extreme rainfall or flooding, pathogens can be transmitted through agricultural or stormwater runoff or sewer discharge, increasing waterborne disease risk (19) (20) (21). Studies globally have documented increases in the incidence of AGI following hurricanes, flooding, and heavy rainfall events (21) (22) (23) (24). In NC, prior studies have linked hurricane-associated flooding to increased rates of AGI emergency department visits following Hurricanes Florence and Matthew (20), and increased outpatient visits for waterborne diseases following Hurricane Floyd (25). At the time of this work, studies on the impacts of Hurricane Helene have documented impacts on mental and physical health, infrastructure, communication, and food security in surveys of affected residents (26, 27). However, the literature remains limited on adverse health outcomes in NC following Hurricane Helene and which populations were most affected, including residents who use private wells.

This study investigated the impacts of Hurricane Helene on the incidence of AGI and sought to determine whether these impacts were different in areas where more residents use private wells.

We used a newly available electronic health record (EHR) database with shorter delays in data availability than traditional government surveillance resources to provide a more expedient understanding of the impacts of the hurricane. We adopted a controlled interrupted time series design for our analyses, allowing us to estimate the effect of Hurricane Helene on the incidence of AGI in flooded regions, controlled for pre-hurricane trends and post-hurricane changes in control regions. Our study provides one of the first analyses of the public health implications of one of the deadliest disasters in NC's history and furthers the understanding of how extreme events like hurricanes can affect health in rural regions of the US.

2. Methods

>Methods

2.1 Study objectives

Our primary study objective was to investigate the impact of Hurricane Helene on the incidence of AGI in NC, US using EHR available from 2017-2024. All analyses were conducted at the zip3 and week (zip3-by-week) level due the spatial granularity available in the EHR data.

Here, zip3 units refer to the group comprising five-digit zip codes that share the same first three digits. We also assessed whether impacts of the hurricane were different in areas with greater proportions of residents using private wells. For comparison, we used a similar approach to investigate the impact of Hurricane Florence, which impacted NC in 2018 and has been examined previously using data from the NC Disease Event Tracking and Epidemiologic Collection Tool (NC DETECT) (11, 20).

2.2 Outcome data

We ascertained cases of AGI in NC at the zip3-by-week level through a data access agreement with Truveta, which provides access to de-identified EHR data on a shorter delay in data availability compared to traditional government data sources. Data from inpatient and outpatient visits were included. Truveta's data is collected from member healthcare organizations within the US. No data is included from immigration facilities, correctional facilities, military facilities, or Indian health facilities. EHR data for this study were extracted in January 2026 with a timeoptimized study profile to provide an appropriate level of temporal accuracy. Truveta's deidentification is attested to by a qualified expert in accordance with the Health Insurance and Portability Act (HIPAA) Privacy Rule. Truveta's deidentification process excludes patients in groups with a high risk of reidentification (e.g., patients with rare diseases, rare ethnic groups). In the time-optimized study profile, this results in data masking into coarser geographic regions, such as at the zip2 or state level. Additional details on Truveta's data model have been described in detail previously (28, 29).

Diagnosis codes from the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD 10)

and SNOMED CT were used to classify AGI, following approaches in prior literature on hurricanes, flooding, and AGI hospitalizations (18, (20) (21) (22). For the ICD 10, these included intestinal infectious diseases (A00-A09), unspecified non-infectious gastroenteritis and colitis (K52.3, K52.89, K52.9), vomiting (R11.10-R11.12), and diarrhea (R19.7). We included equivalent codes from SNOMED CT after consulting with Truveta staff experts (Supplemental Information [SI] Table S1 contains a list), as well as the hierarchical descendants (e.g., children or grandchildren) of all codes. For each zip3, we tabulated the weekly number of AGI visits from 2017-2024 for our analyses. Here, the zip3 locations correspond to where the healthcare interactions for AGI occurred. A total of 547,986 visits for AGI was observed without masking from 2017-2024 at the zip3 level, with an additional 328,834 (60%) that were geographically masked in zip2 or state-level groups.

2.3 Flooding exposure datasets

Satellite-derived flood depth estimates were obtained from the Global Flood Monitoring System (GFMS), which estimates flooding extents in three-hour increments at a 1/8 th degree resolution (30). Zip code tabulation area (ZCTA) boundaries were obtained from the 2020 U.S.

Census and aggregated to the zip3 spatial scale to match the spatial resolution of the outcome data (31). We calculated the zip3 area-weighted mean, median, and maximum percent of flooding over the period of the hurricane to compare inundation metrics (September 24, 2024 12:00AM to September 28, 2024 9:00PM) (6). Prior to conducting the statistical analysis, we defined a zip3 unit as exposed to flooding if, on average, >50% of the area was flooded over three-hour increments throughout the course of the hurricane (hereafter referred to as "exposed" and "control" units). We chose this definition because it allowed for acceptable balance between the exposed and control groups since all zip3 units experienced at least some flooding (see Figure S1).

Classification based on the median percent area flooded was also identical.

We calculated the total population in each year for each zip3 unit using the ZCTA-level American Community Survey 5-year estimates for 2017-2023 (32). When this analysis was ongoing, population estimates for 2024 were not yet available, so 2023 population estimates were carried forward for 2024. Exposure to Hurricane Florence was assessed using the Global Flood Observatory (GFO) (33).

The GFO was used because the GFMS did not have data for the time period during which Hurricane Florence took place (September 14-16, 2018) (34). For each flooding event, the GFO dataset includes start and end date, country, area, main cause, number of fatalities, number displaced, a severity score, a flood impact index, and a polygon boundary of the area that experienced flooding. We rasterized the polygon boundary to create a binary raster dataset identifying areas that experienced flooding in North Carolina due to Hurricane Florence (1 = flooded,0 = not flooded). We then calculated the area in each zip3 that experienced flooding and used the same exposure definition as Hurricane Helene (i.e., >50% of the zip3 unit experiencing flooding).

2.4 Covariates

Data on daily temperature (minimum, maximum, and mean), precipitation, and dewpoint were obtained from the Parameter-elevation Regressions on Independent Slopes Model dataset at the 800 meter resolution (35). Covariates were aggregated to the zip3-by-week level, where we calculated the weekly averages of the mean daily temperature, minimum temperature, maximum temperature, precipitation, and the weekly sum of total precipitation. We used dewpoint to calculate the average relative humidity per zip3 unit per week with the Magnus approximation (36). These covariates were used for exploratory data analysis to describe weather patterns in the weeks prior to the hurricane and were additionally considered as potential time-varying confounders in sensitivity analyses.

We used 2020 census-block level domestic water source estimates to obtain the percentage of private well users in each zip3 unit (37). In exploratory analyses, we compared sociodemographic characteristics between the exposed and control zip3 units using data from the American Community Survey 2024 5-year estimates and 2020 decennial US Census (32, 38). Variables included racial/ethnic compositional factors, health insurance coverage, number and percent of rural households, median annual income, and land area.

2.5 Statistical analyses

All primary analyses used a controlled interrupted time series (CITS) design to estimate the average change in the incidence rates of AGI in exposed zip3 units in the weeks following each hurricane compared to the incidence rate had the hurricane not occurred, controlled for changes in the control areas after the hurricane. This method compares each zip3 to itself over time, which controls for time-invariant zip3 characteristics by design. We fit separate CITS models for each hurricane. We defined three-week, five-week, and eight-week post-hurricane intervals as intercept shifts for both hurricanes. Following prior approaches (20), we removed visits for AGI in the eightweek period following other landfalling hurricanes in NC from the 2017-2024 time series to remove the influence of other major hurricanes in the time series (39, 40). For Hurricane Helene, this removed data during Hurricanes Isaiah (in 2020), Dorian (2019), and Florence (2018). Since we used AGI data from 2017-2024 and there were no landfalling hurricanes in NC in 2017, this did not impact the time series for Hurricane Florence.

Our primary models for Hurricanes Helene and Florence were of the following form: the 5-year ACS estimates. To account for moderate spatial autocorrelation observed in initial models (global Moran's I ranging from 0.17 to 0.21, p = 0.07 to 0.08), we included the number of AGI visits in neighboring zip3 units as a spatially lagged independent variable (shown as log(neighbors + 1) ). First order neighbors were identified using queen's contiguity and neighboring AGI visits were weighted by the number of neighbors for each zip3. In primary models, we accounted for overdispersion in AGI visits by assuming a quasi-Poisson error distribution. Our primary coefficient of interest was 𝛽 ( , an intercept shift which estimates the change in AGI incidence in exposed compared to control units in the weeks after the hurricane, controlled for pre-hurricane temporal trends.

We examined whether hurricane-associated changes in AGI incidence disproportionately affected areas with residents using private wells in two ways. We examined effect heterogeneity among zip3 units with greater proportions of residents on private wells by introducing product terms between the continuous proportion of private well users in each zip3, the pre-/post-indicator for the occurrence of the hurricane, and the inundation exposure indicator. We also reclassified the CITS model in terms of exposure and control zip3 units according to whether >30% of residents used private wells. We explored other, lower cut-points (such as 10% and 20%) with similar results, but we did not examine an indicator for whether > 50% of residents used private wells because only one zip3 would be classified as such (Figure S2). The SI section 1.1 contains additional methodological details for both sets of analyses.

2.6 Examining the impacts of geographic masking

Geographic masking in the EHR database resulted in AGI visits that could not be analyzed directly at the zip3 level and were aggregated into coarser spatial units, such as the zip2 or state level. For our analysis Hurricane Helene, we used a hierarchical allocation procedure to examine the sensitivity of our results to this masking procedure. This approach is comparable to a modelbased interpolation of aggregated spatial count data (41), where the allocation model here was fit only to the pre-hurricane time series.

We first generated a new time series with an additional grouping denoting the combination of zip3, week, and compatible, masked geographic regions (zip2 or state level). In our first modeling step, we modeled the incidence of AGI in the pre-hurricane period, accounting for temperature, humidity, and precipitation; year, month, and zip3 fixed-effects; and a variable representing the total number of weeks since the start of the time series. Second, for each combination of zip3, week, and compatible masked geographic unit (zip2-or state-level), we calculated allocation probabilities for the incidence of AGI by dividing the predicted zip3-by-week level visits by the total predicted visits in each compatible masked unit for the full time series. These allocation probabilities were multiplied by the total number of weekly visits for each masked geographic unit to allocate the aggregated visits for each zip3. In our third step, we accounted for uncertainty in the allocation estimates (which can also be fractional) by generating multiply imputed datasets (n = 20) where the allocated visits were drawn from a multinomial distribution with parameters obtained from step two (42). This approach preserves the total number of masked AGI visits in each imputed dataset. We then repeated the primary analyses and pooled the estimates and variances across these datasets using Rubin's rules (43). Additional method details and a model description are provided in the SI section 1.2.

2.7 Sensitivity analyses

For our analysis of Hurricane Helene, we conducted several analyses to examine the sensitivity of our results to choices related to model specification, assumptions for the post-hurricane period, and the CITS study design. We compared our primary models that included month fixed-effects to models with season fixed-effects. We separately considered weekly average mean, minimum, and maximum temperature and relative humidity as potential time-varying confounders. Across several years, we observed changes in the AGI visit time series in the October and November periods, which partially co-occurred with the eight-week post-Helene period and may reflect the influence of holidays (Figure S3). We therefore repeated our analysis using a three-week posthurricane period but excluding the subsequent five weeks from the time series.

As described previously, all zip3 units experienced at least some flooding during Hurricane Helene. We thus repeated our analyses with a non-controlled interrupted time series. These analyses examined whether the occurrence of the hurricane resulted in a significant average increase in the incidence of AGI compared to the incidence rate had the hurricane not occurred.

All analyses were conducted in R version 4.5.1 (44).

3. Results

We identified 547,986 AGI visits that occurred across 20 zip3 units from 2017-2024. Eight of 20 zip3 units (40%) were classified as exposed to flooding to Hurricane Helene, corresponding to >50% of the land area flooded over the course of the hurricane. Zip3 units that experienced greater flooding during Hurricane Helene comprised approximately 4 million residents (Table S2). These zip3 units comprised regions predominately in western NC and included both rural and urban areas such as Asheville and Wilmington, NC (Figure 1). Compared to control units, exposed zip3 units had greater proportions of non-Hispanic White residents; lower proportions of non-Hispanic Black, Asian, and American Indian and Alaskan Native residents; and higher median household income and population density (Table S2). We observed similar results in analyses examining sensitivity of the primary CITS models to differences in model specification and the post-Helene period (Table S3). For example, we observed 9-16% increases in the incidence of visits for AGI among exposed units in negative binomial models. Confidence intervals in these sensitivity analyses were similarly imprecise and included the null. In non-controlled ITS analyses, effect estimates were attenuated in comparison to the CITS models with confidence intervals including the null (e.g., IRR in the three-week interval: 1.00 95% CI [0.71, 1.37]; Table S4).

3.2 Analyses examining impacts on residents using private wells

Table S5 shows coefficient estimates from models including additional product terms to examine effect heterogeneity related to private well use and models with a redefined exposure classification based on the proportion of residents using private wells. We did not observe significant increases in the incidence of AGI among zip3 units with >30% residents using private wells compared to control units in the weeks following Hurricane Helene (Table S5). For example, in the three weeks following the hurricane, we observed an 8% decrease in the incidence of AGI visits among zip3 units with >30% residents using private wells (IRR: 0.92, 95% CI: 0.35, 2.10), compared to pre-hurricane trends and controlled for post-hurricane changes in the control units.

We also generally did not observe evidence of significant heterogeneity in the impact of Hurricane Helene on flooding units in association with the proportion of residents using private wells (Table S4). One exception was for the eight-week interval following the hurricane (IRR for the three-way product term: 0.98 [0.95, 1.00], p = 0.07). However, in this model, we did not observe significant shifts in AGI visits following the hurricane among any of the additional two-way product terms.

3.3 Hurricane Florence and the incidence of AGI

Table S6 shows coefficient estimates from models examining the impact of Hurricane Florence in September 2018 on the incidence of AGI visits in zip3 units exposed to hurricane-related flooding. Effect estimates here indicated increases of 6-11% among the exposed zip3 units at the three-, five-, and eight-week intervals following the hurricane. However, results were similarly imprecise, with wide 95% confidence intervals that included the null.

4. Discussion

Our study investigated the impacts of Hurricane Helene on the incidence of visits for AGI in NC at the zip3 and week level. Using a CITS study design, we observed 11-16% increases in the incidence for AGI visits among more heavily flooded zip3 units after the hurricane, albeit estimates were imprecise with wide confidence intervals including the null. We did not observe clear evidence that the hurricane disproportionately impacted areas with more residents using private wells. Results were consistent across several sensitivity analyses, and similar results were observed for Hurricane Florence, which made landfall in NC in 2018.

Prior literature on weather-related impacts on the incidence of AGI globally have primarily focused on heavy rainfall, temperature, and flooding, although fewer have focused specifically on hurricane-associated flooding (23). Among studies focused on heavy rainfall events, several have reported significant increases and outbreaks in diarrheal and waterborne diseases, including nationwide and regional studies situated in the US (45), United Kingdom (46), Canada (19, 47), Eswatini (48), Japan (49), and China (50). Prior reviews have found that a commonality among these outbreaks is the occurrence of heavy rainfall preceded by a dry period, a phenomenon in line with the "concentration-dilution hypothesis" (23, 24). Under this hypothesis, pathogens can accumulate during periods of less rain, while heavy rainfall can flush pathogens into surface water bodies. However, longer periods of heavy rainfall may dilute pathogen concentrations, reducing risk of waterborne diseases. In our study, we observed elevated rainfall prior to Hurricane Helene in several zip3 units (Figure S4), coinciding with prior storms earlier in September 2024 (51), which may partially explain our findings.

Studies on the impacts of prior hurricanes on AGI incidence in NC have typically relied on data from the North Carolina Disease Event Tracking and Epidemiologic Collection Tool (NC DETECT) (20) or Medicaid claims (25). For example, Quist et al., 2022 employed a similar CITS study design and observed an 11% increase in AGI emergency department visits after Hurricanes Matthew and Florence (20). A follow-up 2025 study examined the differential impacts of hurricane-associated flooding in areas with more concentrated animal feeding operations (CAFOs), finding increases AGI emergency department visits in zip5 units with more CAFOs following Hurricane Florence (11). In NC, CAFOs follow distinct geographical and social patterning, with more hog CAFOs in rural and eastern counties with higher proportions of people of color, concentrated poverty, and multiple other environmental hazards (e.g., landfills) (20, 52, 53).

Previous studies documenting significant increases in AGI following hurricanes in NC are supported by evidence that these storms result in elevated concentrations of various pathogens and contaminants in surface water bodies (54, 55) and microbial contamination of drinking water sources, particularly private wells (4,10). At the time of this work, studies on Hurricane Helene are scarce. However, a mixed-method survey indicated that residents in Southern Appalachia most frequently reported health conditions that suggested exposure to contaminated water and mold, including headaches; nose, eye, skin, and throat irritation or cough; and gastrointestinal issues (27).

Voluntary well testing following Hurricane Helene revealed that approximately 30% of private wells in western counties of NC tested positive for total coliform (10, 56), although these testing programs did not detail specific pathogen species. Aside from exposures to contaminated water and related health conditions, surveys of affected residents have documented widespread impacts on mental health, physical infrastructure, and food security following the hurricane (26, 27).

Our study was strengthened in its use of an EHR data source with a relatively short lag time, allowing us to study Hurricane Helene on a quicker timeline in comparison to traditional government data sources. We employed a quasi-experimental study design, which reduces confounding by time-invariant factors. The CITS framework further accounts for potential common shocks coincident with the hurricane that may have impacted both exposed and units.

Satellite-derived estimates were used to classify exposure groups for two hurricanes according to similar definitions. Additionally, we were able to examine almost 8 years of pre-Hurricane Helene AGI trends.

However, our study faced several limitations. Many individuals affected by AGI in rural communities may not seek health care resulting in undercounting and potential under-estimation of the effect of hurricanes on AGI. During extreme weather events, individuals across the state may also change healthcare seeking behaviors or experience disruptions in care.( 57) Although state coverage for Truveta in NC is high (>100% of the census population accounted for across encounters in NC), the data are limited in both spatial and temporal granularity. Prior studies on earlier hurricanes have reported similar effect sizes to those observed here that were significant at the 0.05 level, although with data available at finer spatial and temporal scales such as the zip5by-day level (20). Truveta's database also includes only data from participating member health systems, which may not be representative of all parts of the state. While we attempted to account for geographic data masking in the AGI visit data obtained from Truveta, we cannot account for unmeasured factors that contributed to masking, which may be a particular concern for lower counts observed in lesser populated areas. Finally, we were unable to account for population displacement or public health interventions (e.g., issuance of boil water notices) that occurred during and following the hurricane (58) and may have reduced exposure to contaminated water.

Conclusions

We observed positive, statistically insignificant increases in the incidence of AGI visits in the three-to eight-week window following Hurricane Helene. We used a novel EHR data source with a shorter lag time, although limitations in its spatial and temporal resolution suggest that future work focused on hurricane-associated AGI should examine these relationships at finer scales.

Although we did not observe clear evidence indicating that regions with greater proportions of residents using private wells were disproportionately impacted, future work should re-examine these relationships for confirmation because individuals using private wells may be uniquely susceptible to water quality contamination following hurricanes. Additionally, future work should examine the impact of public health interventions employed during hurricanes and whether this may mediate or modify observed relationships. Within quasi-experimental frameworks, comparisons of exposure classifications at different spatial scales would be valuable, particularly within rural areas where private wells are commonly used and may experience highly localized flooding-induced contamination. In the eastern US, the intensity and frequency of hurricanes are expected to increase as the climate changes (59). Thus, studies should continue to examine hurricane-associated water quality contamination and other health outcomes with robust study designs to examine both pre-and post-hurricane data.
