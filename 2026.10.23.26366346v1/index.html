To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 6 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Semantic similarity features derived from language embeddings have been proposed as markers of schizophrenia spectrum disorders (SSD), but prior studies have reported inconsistent directions of effect and limited evidence regarding their relationship to specific dimensions of thought and language disturbance. We systematically evaluated how language model architecture and preprocessing strategy influence semantic similarity signals in SSD.

Methods: Speech samples from 228 participants (152 SSD, 76 healthy volunteers) were analyzed using six embedding models: GloVe, LSA, Word2Vec, RoBERTa, GPT-2, and Llama. Semantic similarity features were computed across three preprocessing levels (verbatim, disfluency-removed, and disfluencyremoved with stopword filtering) using k-interword, moving-window, and sentence-to-sentence comparisons, summarized with various statistical descriptors. Features were compared between SSD and healthy volunteers and examined for associations with dimensions of thought and language disturbance, including inefficiency, incoherence, and impaired expressivity.

Results: SSD was predominantly associated with increased semantic similarity across models and comparison strategies. Model performance varied by preprocessing. RoBERTa performed best on minimally processed transcripts, yielding the greatest number of diagnosis-associated features, whereas GPT-2 achieved the largest diagnostic effect size after maximal preprocessing. GPT-2 showed the strongest signal for inefficiency and incoherence, while RoBERTa yielded the greatest number of associations with impaired expressivity.

Conclusions: SSD diagnosis and its dimensions of thought and language abnormalities are characterized primarily by greater semantic proximity in open-ended speech. Optimal preprocessing depends on model architecture and the clinical dimension of interest, highlighting the importance of model-and phenotype-specific analytic choices in computational studies of thought and language disturbance in SSD.

1. Introduction

Schizophrenia spectrum disorders (SSD) are marked by alterations in underlying meaningmaking processes manifested in speech (1). Phenomenologically, alterations are often described along two dimensions: 1. Disorganization, in which the global or local structure of meaning is disrupted, leading to incoherent contents or tangential transitions (2,3); 2. Impoverishment, in which the effective semantic space is constricted, due to reduced lexical variety and repetitive or perseverative content (4).

Increasingly, emerging natural language processing (NLP) methods have been implemented to objectively quantify and track symptom severity including language disturbances in SSD (5) (6) (7) (8).

A leading NLP approach examines distances in meaning between linguistic units by leveraging semantic embeddings, which numerically represent the meaning content of linguistic units such as words and sentences and enable their comparison across a speech sample (9). Embeddings are extracted from language models which encode each linguistic unit as a vector in a multidimensional semantic space, allowing semantic relationships to be quantified geometrically, for example through cosine similarity.

Language models can be broadly divided into two categories: static vs. contextual. The first comprises static, or non-contextual, models, which assign each linguistic unit within a pre-determined dictionary a fixed representation independent of its surrounding context, such as Global Vectors for Word Representation (GloVe) (10), Latent Semantic Analysis (LSA) (11), and Word to Vector (Word2Vec) (12).

The second type of language models comprises contextual models, which generate representations that depend on the linguistic environment of each unit, incorporating either preceding words only (unidirectional models, e.g., Generative Pre-trained Transformer 2, GPT-2 (13), and Large Language Model Meta AI, Llama ( 14)) or both preceding and following words (bidirectional models, e.g., Robustly Optimized Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers Pretraining Approach, RoBERTa (15).

Numerical semantic representations extracted from language models could be then compared using variety of comparison strategies each generating a semantic similarity metric. Comparisons may be made at different levels of language organization for example between words (word-to-word similarity) or sentences (sentence-to-sentence similarity). Similarity may be captured between linguistic units at various intervals (e.g., comparing adjacent words or sentences, or those occurring at certain distance from each other), or within a block of word sequences (e.g., extracting similarities within a moving window of certain number of words). Finally, each semantic similarity metric may be described using different statistical approaches such as mean, median, minimum, maximum, etc., of semantic similarities (9).

Alteration in semantic similarity features has been associated with psychotic disorders including SSD. Earlier studies conceptualized decreased semantic similarity as a proxy for loosening of association, as occurs in disorganization phenotypes (16). Elvevåg et al. reported decreased semantic similarity in oneword associations, animal fluency task, and comparisons between participants' responses and original questions in open-ended interviews, hence, coining the term coherence for cosine semantic similarity metrics (17). Similarly, decreased semantic similarity was observed in psychosis between adjacent sentences (sentence-to-sentence) and with respect to an artificial stimulus description (sentence-toreference), ostensibly capturing incoherence and tangentiality (18). Other studies have reported reduced semantic similarity during animal fluency in patients with SSD (19), particularly among those exhibiting phenotypes of disorganization such as derailment or tangentiality (20). This pattern, however, was not replicated in a later study conducted by our team (21). Finally, decreased semantic similarity was predictive of conversion to psychosis in longitudinal clinical high risk (CHR) cohorts (22, 23).

An opposing line of research has supported the inverse pattern, linking increased semantic similarity to psychosis (24). In this vein, increased mean semantic similarity, including higher average similarity across all word pairs and within moving windows of ten words during picture-description, was observed in first-episode psychosis compared to healthy controls (25). A subsequent study, reported higher word-to-word semantic similarity in picture description in psychosis despite no significant difference in word diversity or content specificity to stimuli, suggesting more constrained word selection from tightly related semantic clusters (26). With further replications of increased semantic similarity in psychosis (27, 28), this phenomenon was interpreted as the reflection of narrowing of lexical-conceptual semantic space (29, 30), challenging the earlier notion of semantic similarity as primarily an index of coherence (31).

Going beyond diagnostic differences, the relationship between semantic similarity and the dimensions of thought and language disturbance is not well understood. Despite conceptual elegance of interpreting decreased semantic similarity as a measure of decreased coherence of thought and language (17) (18) (19), there is stronger evidence pointing instead to a relationship between greater semantic similarity and impoverishment. Palominos and colleagues reported an association between greater alogia based on the Scale for the Assessment of Negative Symptoms (SANS) and word-to-word average and maximum semantic similarity using BERT model and minimum semantic similarity using FastText as well as negative correlation with maximum semantic similarity based on FastText (30). No word-to-word semantic similarity metric correlated with positive formal thought disorder based on the Scale for the Assessment of Positive Symptoms (SAPS). This result was consistent with the hypothesis that impoverishment involves narrowing of the effective semantic space as measured by contextual and noncontextual models (32). Other studies investigated indirect relationships between semantic similarity measures and formal thought disorder per their contribution to predictive models. Delal et al. found that semantic similarity was strongly predictive of impoverishment per Thought and Language Index (TLI) in relapse prediction Bayesian models, without finding such relationship with TLI disorganization (24). In a recent study by Ciampelli et al., a semantic similarity factor score showed poor performance in item-level symptom severity prediction: in the Dutch sample, semantic similarity factor failed to be significant across all mixed-effect models to predict The Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) items including conceptual disorganization. Similarly, word and sentence similarity factors showed no predictive signal for conceptual disorganization per Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale (BPRS) in the English sample (33).

Model selection, preprocessing approach, and feature curation introduce additional complexity in utilizing semantic similarity features for the detection of SSD. Language models have different intrinsic architecture that affects semantic representations obtained from them. Tokenization and preprocessing decisions may preserve, enhance or remove clinically relevant signals. Comparison strategies, as described above, can also influence the direction and magnitude of observed effects. To date, no study has compared how these factors jointly influence the use of semantic similarity measures to identify SSD and characterize dimensions of thought and language disturbance associated with SSD. Here, we sought to address this gap by systematically evaluating semantic similarity features derived from six language models across three levels of preprocessing and multiple comparison methods in a large sample of 228 participants. Our aims were: (1) to determine the optimal level of pre-processing for each language model in distinguishing SSD from healthy volunteers (HV), (2) to confirm the direction of diagnostic effects across semantic similarity metrics, and (3) to examine how these features relate to the dimensions of language disturbance described by impaired expressivity, inefficiency, and incoherence factors based on select items from The Scale for the Assessment of Thought, Language, and Communication (TLC) and SANS (3).

2. Methods

>Methods

2.1. Participants and Recruitment

Participants were recruited from the outpatient and inpatient services at Zucker Hillside Hospital (Queens, NY) as part of three previously published studies, including Remora (n = 81), LPOP (n = 66), and ACES (n = 81). Previous studies have examined relationships between wide range of NLP metrics and clinical endpoints such as symptom severity, longitudinal illness trajectory and social cognition (5, 8, 34, 35); the present study presents a new systematic evaluation of semantic similarities. The sample comprised 76 healthy volunteers and 152 individuals with schizophrenia spectrum disorders. All participants provided written informed consent. Study procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Feinstein Institutes for Medical Research.

2.2. Assessment and Diagnosis

Clinical diagnoses were established using standardized diagnostic interviews aligned with DSM-5 criteria. To evaluate dimensions of language disturbance, trained raters assessed 18 items from the Thought, Language, and Communication (TLC) scale (36) and 2 additional items from the Scale for the Assessment of Negative Symptoms (SANS) (37) that capture relevant aspects. Ratings were based on participants' whole interviews, which included speech tasks as well as clinical assessments (BPRS, etc. write them here). Item ratings were used to compute three dimensions of language disturbance using previously published factor loadings with demonstrated cross-diagnostic validity: impaired expressivity, incoherence, and inefficiency, (3).

2.3. Speech Samples

Speech samples were elicited from each participant using open-ended prompts (e.g., "Tell me about yourself," "How have you been recently?") that were used for this study. Other tasks included picture description, phonemic and categorical fluency, etc., which were not utilized here. Recordings were transcribed verbatim with intuitive punctuations to define sentence boundaries. To protect participant privacy, prior to analysis, specific personal references were removed using manually annotated tags to replace personal identifiers with substitutes of the same function (e.g., "My sister Maria…" → "My sister Jennifer…"). Non-speech verbalizations, such as symbolic placeholders for noises, laughter, unintelligible segments, and partial words, were also excluded from the transcripts. Final transcripts comprised turnlevel dialogues in response to each individual prompt with speaker tags identifying the participant and the interviewer. Participants responded to one (N = 6) or two open-ended prompts (N = 222); when two transcripts (each in response to one of the above-mentioned open-ended prompts) were available for a participant, the average value of downstream semantic similarity features were used.

2.4. Pre-processing Levels

To compare varying degrees of textual pre-processing, we analyzed each transcript at three levels of pre-processing, each reflecting incremental removal of non-content words (Figure 1A). 1) Level 1 (Verbatim): Verbatim transcripts cleaned of non-verbal content as described above, but inclusive of disfluencies and filler words.

2) Level 2 (Disfluency-removed): Level 1 transcripts with additional removal of speech disfluencies, including filled pauses and repetitions.

3) Level 3 (Disfluency-removed and Stopword-filtered): Outputs from the embedding extraction applied to Level 2 were further filtered to remove stop words (i.e., non-content words, namely stop words, such as auxiliary verbs and prepositions). This sequencing allowed non-content words to provide contextual information to content-word embeddings prior to their elimination but removes them from the similarity calculation.

2.5. Embedding Extraction

Verbatim and disfluency-removed samples (Levels 1 and 2) underwent token-level embedding extraction using six pre-trained language models (Table 1): three non-contextual (GloVe, LSA, Word2Vec) and three contextual (GPT-2, RoBERTa, Llama). Models were selected based on their previous appearance in semantic similarity literature as well as their architectural variety. Of note, later versions of GPT are not open-weight models. Hence, GPT-2 was used in this study. For non-contextual models, interviewer speech was excluded and each word from the participant's transcript was presented to the model independently. The corresponding embedding was recorded when existing in model's corpus and skipped otherwise. For contextual models, full two-party conversations (participant and interviewer) were provided to the model to preserve discourse context. Token-level embeddings were extracted using each model's native tokenizer. Tokens were mapped back to their originating words, and for words split into multiple tokens, the mean of the token embeddings was computed to yield a single word-level vector.

After embedding extraction, interviewer turns were removed, retaining participant embeddings that benefited from the conversational context. Transcripts which exceeded the token limit of each model in length were parsed into overlapping segments and presented to the model separately. We avoided breaking sentences in the middle while keeping each segment with maximum allowed tokens. For tokens in the overlapping regions, the average embedding value was calculated.

2.6. Similarity Metrics

Word-level embeddings derived from participant speech at each of the three pre-processing level (1.

Verbatim; 2. Disfluency-removed; and 3. Disfluency-removed and stopword-filtered), and each model (GloVe, LSA, Word2Vec, GPT-2, RoBERTa, and Llama) were used to compute the following similarity comparisons. Percentile and median-based statistics were chosen over mean, absolute minima and maxima to decrease sensitivity to outliers. 1) K-interword distances were generated by computing the cosine similarity between each word and the word occurring after k intervening words, for k = 0 to k=10. For example, the k-interword distance with k=0 compares a word to its immediate successor, whereas k=6 compares each word to the seventh successive word with six intervening words. For each transcript, the 5 th percentile (minimum), 95 th percentile (maximum), 50 th percentile (median), and interquartile range of all kinterword comparisons were calculated.

2) Moving-window similarity was computed with window widths of 5 and 10 words and a stride of one word. Within each window we computed the minimum, maximum, median, and interquartile range of the pairwise cosine similarities, and these statistics were subsequently averaged across all windows to produce sample-level measures.

3) Sentence embeddings were obtained by mean-pooling all word embeddings within each sentence at the given pre-processing level per standard practice in the field (38). Cosine similarity was then computed between consecutive sentences to assess first-order coherence and between sentences separated by one intervening sentence to assess second-order coherence. For each sample, the minimum, maximum, median, and interquartile range of these sentence-to-sentence similarity scores were calculated. This procedure yielded 68 unique similarity metrics computed for each sample across three preprocessing levels and six embedding models, resulting in 1,224 similarity features per original sample (68 × 3 × 6; Figure 1B). For participants with two transcripts (N=222 participants), each feature was averaged across the two transcripts to obtain a single participant-level value. A complete list of semantic similarity features is provided in the appendix (Table S-1).

2.7. Statistical Analysis

Similarity features (n = 1,224) were compared between SSD and HV groups using the Mann-Whitney U test (39), with effect sizes summarized as Cohen's d (d). The Mann-Whitney U test was chosen because it is robust to non-normally distributed data. To distinguish which pre-processing level optimizes group-level effect per model, we counted and reported number of significant features and maximum absolute effect size for each model-pre-processing level pair. Subsequently, for the dimensional analyses, we utilized the level of pre-processing at which each model yielded the highest number of significant features and largest effect sizes. For the dimensional analyses, we examined associations between features and latent dimensions of thought and language disturbance within the SSD group (3): impaired expressivity, incoherence, and inefficiency. Spearman's rank correlation (ρ) was reported.

Overall processing pipeline is schematically demonstrated in Figure 1. All statistical and NLP analyses, and data visualizations were conducted in Python 3 using pandas, SciPy, seaborn, and spaCy libraries (40-43).

3. Results

>Results

3.1. Participant Characteristics

Participants included 228 individuals (Table 2-A), comprising 152 participants with schizophrenia spectrum disorders (SSD) and 76 healthy volunteers (HV). The SSD and HV groups did not differ significantly in age (SSD: 27.9 ± 7.4 years; HV: 29.0 ± 6.8 years; p = 0.30) but differed in sex distribution (p = 0.02), with a higher proportion of males in the SSD group (64%) than in the HV group (46%). Race distribution did not differ significantly between groups (p = 0.14); across the full sample, 37% of participants identified as African American, 33% as Caucasian, 14% as Asian, and 16% as other race. As expected, clinical ratings of thought and language disturbance were significantly higher in the SSD group than in HVs, including TLC total score (SSD: 17 ± 15; HV: 2 ± 3; p < 0.001) and all three latent language-disturbance factors: inefficiency, incoherence, and impaired expressivity (all p < 0.001)

3.2. Speech Sample Description

Altogether, 450 documents were available from 228 participants, each corresponding to one of two open-ended prompts described above (see Table 2-B). Eight samples containing fewer than 10 words were excluded from analysis (7 from individuals with SSD and 1 from an HV participant). On average, samples comprised 145 words across 12 utterances. The SSD and HV groups did not differ significantly in the number of words per sample (SSD: 143; HV: 149; p = 0.69), number of utterances (both 12; p = 0.91), number of stopwords (SSD: 80; HV: 79; p = 0.88), or repetition rate (SSD: 1.38% of total words; HV: 1.16% of total words; p = 0.33). Healthy volunteers used filled pauses more frequently than participants with SSD (HV: 6.53% of total words vs. SSD: 5.43% of total words) with statistical significance (p = 0.04).

3.3. Effect of Pre-processing on Semantic Similarity Group Effects

The effect of pre-processing on the group effects of semantic similarity features in terms of number of features reaching p < 0.05 the maximum observed effect size (d) is illustrated in Figure 2. As non-content words were progressively removed, models showed distinct trends in terms of their maximum effect size and total number of significant features. GPT-2 exhibited an incremental performance increase; RoBERTa, GloVe, and Word2Vec showed decreased performance; and LSA and Llama were comparatively insensitive to pre-processing. For GPT-2, further pre-processing particularly enhanced signal between SSD diagnosis and increased minimum and median semantic similarity in k-interword, moving-window, and sentence-to-sentence similarity comparisons. For Word2Vec same minimum and median semantic similarity signal diminished with pre-processing but only in word and window comparisons. For RoBERTa and GloVe, no generalizable pattern was observed (Table -S2).

RoBERTa at pre-processing level 1 outperformed all other model-pre-processing combinations in the number of significant features, with 67 features. GPT-2 at pre-processing level 3 achieved the highest effect size of 0.82 among all comparisons. The best pre-processing level for each model was selected for further analysis. They included GloVe at pre-processing level 1, LSA at pre-processing level 1, Word2Vec at pre-processing level 1, RoBERTa at pre-processing level 1, GPT-2 at pre-processing level 3, and Llama at pre-processing level 2. Comprehensive statistics for all comparisons (68 features × six models × three pre-processing levels) are provided in the supplement (Table S-2).

3.4. Association with Diagnosis across Language Models

Feature-wide association of SSD diagnosis with semantic similarity metrics derived from the six models at their optimized level of pre-processing are illustrated in Figure 3. Selected features including even k-words are included here for better visualization (See Figure 1-S for all comparisons). Overall, SSD diagnosis was associated with increased semantic similarity across models, pre-processing approaches and statistical metrics, with the exception of inter-quartile range comparisons.

Within k-interword analyses, GPT-2 outperformed other combinations, showing consistently strong associations between SSD and increased minimum and median interword similarities across all lags k0-k10 (d = 0.46-0.82). RoBERTa produced comparatively stronger signals with increased maximum interword similarity being associated with SSD (d = 0.46-0.69). Llama yielded weaker effects overall, with gradual improvement at more distant lags and its highest effect size observed for increased maximum similarity at k-10 (d = 0.36). Non-contextual models also showed multiple significant associations with SSD diagnosis, but without a consistent pattern across interword distance or statistical summary; notably, Word2Vec achieved the highest effect size among non-contextual models for increased minimum interword similarity at the 4-word interval (d = 0.78). Among moving window comparisons, effect sizes were generally stable within each model across window size (five vs. 10 words) and statistical approach. GPT-2 was the best performing combination, with multiple features at both moving-window sizes reaching d ≈ 0.8. Llama was the weakest, showing minimal improvement with larger windows (from d ≈ 0.12-0.18 to d ≈ 0.21-0.22). RoBERTa, LSA, GloVe, and Word2Vec produced features with peak effect sizes in d ≈ 0.4-0.6 range.

For first-and second-order sentence-to-sentence similarity, none of the non-contextual models (LSA, Glove, Word2Vec) yielded significant associations with SSD diagnosis. In contrast, all three contextual models (GPT-2, RoBERTa, and Llama) did demonstrate significant group effects, where SSD was associated with increased minimum, median, and maximum sentence-to-sentence similarity and decreased interquartile range.

3.5. Association with Dimensions of Thought and Language Disturbance

Figure 4 presents feature-wide correlations between similarity metrics from each model and three language disturbance factors, namely, inefficiency (Figure 4-A), and incoherence (Figure 4-B), and impaired expressivity (Figure 4-C). Overall, all models contributed features with the strongest performance in ρ = 0.2-0.35 range, with distinct patterns in the number of significant correlations across combination as described below. GPT-2 produced the greatest number of significant correlations with the inefficiency factor.

Greater inefficiency was associated with increased similarity across k-interword, moving-window, and sentence-to-sentence metrics. Increase in maximum first-order sentence-to-sentence similarity, as well as median and maximum second-order similarity, were correlated with greater inefficiency across all six models.

GPT-2 also outperformed other models in correlation with incoherence. More incoherent speech was associated with increased minimum and median similarity in multiple k-interword and movingwindow comparisons and decreased average interquartile range in 5-and 10-word windows.

RoBERTa yielded the greatest number of significant correlations with impaired expressivity, characterized by increased minimum, median, and maximum similarity and decreased interquartile range with k-interword and moving-window metrics tracking with severity. Higher median and maximum sentence-to-sentence similarities were correlated with lower impaired expressivity in RoBERTa, GloVe and Word2Vec. Few sporadic significant associations observed between impaired expressivity and semantic similarity features extracted from other language models.

4. Discussion

In this study, we systematically examined how semantic similarity features derived from multiple embedding models and preprocessing levels relate to SSD diagnosis and to dimensions of thought and language disturbance. Three main findings emerged: 1. SSD diagnosis and dimensions of thought and language disturbance were generally associated with increased semantic similarity across models and comparisons; 2. Different dimensions of thought and language disturbance had distinct semantic similarity fingerprints; and 3. Language models exhibited varying behavior in response to preprocessing of the transcribed text.

A central finding of this study was that the existence of SSD was associated with higher semantic similarity and lower dispersion across models and comparison approaches: average, minimum and maximum semantic similarity is SSD was broadly increased with respect to healthy controls across word, window, and sentence-level analyses, and this increase was commonly accompanied by reduction in semantic variability as reflected in decreased inter-quartile range. This pattern aligns closely with recent work interpreting elevated similarity as evidence of a constricted semantic space (29, 32). In addition, as increased semantic similarity found to be associated with both positive and negative dimensions of thought disorder, our results indicate that this computational metric is relevant not only to impoverishment in SSD but also to disorganization. Prior studies indicated that increased semantic similarity in SSD is a product of word choice that occurs from tightly clustered semantic spaces, rather than being merely a result of insufficient diversity, i.e., using fewer instances of words to build discourse (26). Taken together, our findings indicate that disorganization is not always the result of co-occurrence of semantically unrelated words or sentences in speech; rather, it may also result from juxtaposition of semantically similar words or sentences in incoherent or inefficient manners. In addition to future informing efforts to develop objective speech and language markers for psychosis, these findings may also guide future work in synthetic linguistic data representing formal thought disorder using generative language models (44).

In the present data, increased sentence-level similarity was more prominently related to inefficient speech (e.g., tangentiality and circumstantiality), compared to incoherence. This finding is in line with the previous conceptualization of inefficiency as disruption of global meaning intent that is carried between sentences, in contrast to incoherence, which reflects disruption of local meaning that occurs within sentences (3). This association with increased similarity may reflect the fact that rather than semantic divergence (18), inefficient speech often remains semantically close across sentences because it elaborates redundantly, circles around a topic, or fails to advance discourse efficiently. In contrast, incoherence may be better captured by shorter-range metrics, such as local interword or moving-window comparisons, where disruptions or over-constraint in immediate semantic transitions are more visible. More broadly, these findings support the idea that different dimensions of thought and language disturbance in SSD may operate at different linguistic scales and units, and that embedding-based measures should be interpreted in relation to the level of discourse they quantify.

Preprocessing had substantial differential effects on the relationship between downstream semantic similarity measurements and clinical measures. RoBERTa showed the strongest overall performance on minimally processed transcripts, whereas GPT-2 performed best after the most extensive preprocessing. This suggests that, in embedding methodology, depending on language model selection, pre-processing might preserve or attenuate linguistic signals relevant to clinical outcomes. For RoBERTa, minimally processed speech may retain discourse scaffolding carried by function words, repetitions, and disfluencies that remain clinically informative, particularly for impoverishment-related or expressive deficits. By contrast, GPT-2 may benefit from removal of non-content words because this sharpens sequential relations among content-bearing words, thereby enhancing signals relevant to disorganization. This different pattern could also be explained in terms of models' intrinsic architecture: In general, RoBERTa's bidirectional text processing might be robust to repetitions, disfluencies, etc., whereas, GPT-2 is trained and optimized to process text unidirectionally to predict the next word, as such, unstructured, conversational "noise" might disrupt its prediction flow and requires aggressive cleaning. Finally, this finding is also consistent with our previous hypothesis about semantic localization of language disturbance in psychosis, namely that disorganization-related signals are more concentrated in the semantic core of speech (i.e., information related to events and their participants), whereas impaired expressivity is more diffusely distributed across core and peripheral structure (i.e., information related to setting, manner, etc.) (5, 45). Several limitations should be noted. Although we compared six embedding models, we did not include representations from some newer or more advanced models and model variants. For example, at the time of this study, token embeddings were only readily extractable from GPT-2 among OpenAI models; also, more powerful models were not necessarily open source or open weight for us to safely extract model internal representations. However, our results indicated that despite the overall superiority of more advanced models, older non-contextual language models were still able to generate features with effect sizes comparable to those of large language models despite not directly encoding the syntactic information in the text or discourse context from the interviewer. Moreover, the models tested in our work are relatively light and cost-efficient to deploy and reproduce, requiring much less compute compared to the more advanced large language models. In addition, our analyses were based on open-ended interview speech, and the findings may not generalize directly to more constrained elicitation tasks such as picture description or verbal fluency. To ensure methodological consistency across contextual and non-contextual models, we constructed sentence-level embeddings by mean-pooling the embeddings of their constituent words. However, this approach does not fully leverage the capabilities of advanced contextual models, which can generate sentence-level representations informed by the broader conversational context. Future studies may explore such context-aware embeddings to better capture semantic relationships across sentences. Finally, the observed relationships with language-disturbance factors were modest in magnitude, indicating that semantic similarity captures only certain aspects of clinically rated language abnormality. Other aspects may be better characterized by complementary metrics such as perplexity (46) or alternative computational approaches such as semantic graph representation of discourse structure (47) or measures of referential coherence (48). Integrating these measures may provide a more comprehensive characterization of language disturbance in SSD.

5. Conclusion

Semantic similarity alteration in SSD was characterized predominantly by higher similarity and reduced variability across models, feature types, and pre-processing approaches. RoBERTa performed best on minimally processed transcripts and was most informative for impaired expressivity, whereas GPT-2 performed best after maximal preprocessing and was most informative for inefficiency and incoherence, shedding practical insights on the utility and selection of language models in future SSD research. These findings suggest that negative formal thought disorder may be better captured with minimal preprocessing with bidirectional approach, which is sensitive to broader speech context, whereas disorganization-related signals may emerge more clearly after stronger preprocessing with unidirectional approach agnostic to downstream context. Furthermore, inefficiency was more prominently associated with sentence-level similarity, whereas incoherence was more closely related to shorter-range interword and moving-window comparisons, suggesting that these dimensions of language disturbance may preferentially reflect disruptions in global versus local semantic organization, respectively.
