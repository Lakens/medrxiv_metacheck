How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Abstract

Background: Proper adoption of AI in emergency medicine necessitate thorough and rigorous appraisal. Classic benchmarks, which rely heavily on basic question-and-answer formats, though dominating the benchmarking field of LLM usage in medicine, have proven inadequate in assessing the full spectrum of skills incumbent upon an agent in healthcare.

Methods: We propose an intricate benchmarking scheme in which the skill set required of an emergency physician is tested and quantified. Our benchmarking structure is 3-fold: skills such as history taking, diagnostic accuracy, treatment plan, and empathy are evaluated by an emergency medicine expert, while the same expertise is scored by an intern from the perspective of a patient, through what we call a simulated emergency room visit. A comprehensive written examination is also utilized to measure general emergency medicine knowledge.

Results: Eight LLMs were benchmarked against each other and in contrast to nine emergency medical doctors. Our findings indicate that the AI models significantly outperformed human physicians across several key domains, including history taking, investigation, diagnostic accuracy, and treatment planning. Furthermore, the models demonstrated superior performance in comprehensibility, credibility, and empathy. When comparing AI models against each other, performance advantages were marginal during simulated emergency room visits and were mostly pronounced during the written examination.

Conclusion: AI models demonstrate a clear advantage over human physicians across a diverse range of emergency medicine competencies. The proposed benchmarking scheme provides a comprehensive metric for a thorough evaluation of medical skill set, furthering validation, adaptation, and utilization of AI in emergency medicine.

Introduction

Background Diagnostic error, i.e., a clinician's oversight of a diagnosis or failure to reach a correct one in a timely manner, is a pervasive global occurrence and among the prime causes of preventable harm in the healthcare system [1, 2].

In emergency medicine (EM), rapid and accurate information gathering, decision-making and treatment planning are of paramount importance. However, the heuristics of emergency medical doctors are constantly under disruptive pressure from the high-stakes nature of EM; doctors have to make multitudinous life-saving decisions under time pressures and in disorderly situations. These factors, among others, hinder the diagnostic skills of clinicians, prompting research in methods tackling these factors and facilitating accurate diagnosis in EM [3].

Alongside the explosive advancements of Artificial Intelligence (AI) and the widespread utilization in different fields, it has been incorporated in EM as well. Specific sub-classes of AI, e.g., Large Language Models (LLMs), are suited for tasks such as gathering information and clinical decision-making. By integrating LLM assistance, one could subdue the eroding factors of working in EM, culminating in the reduction of diagnostic errors [3, 4]. LLMs can streamline history and information taking from the patient, which speeds up the process and reduces the cognitive load and the chance of cognitive fatigue [5, 6]. They can also support the clinician through the decision-making process, increasing accuracy and patient throughput, decreasing the chance of cognitive bias, and overall, reducing the probability of a diagnostic mistake [7, 8]. Moreover, LLMs are shown to outperform physicians in diagnostic reasoning depending on the context, making them ideal to be part of an AI-based support system [9]. Importance LLM utilization in EM requires thoughtful design, meticulous validation, and careful attention to ethical and practical considerations; hasty adoption of LLMs could ironically result in an increase in errors in ED, endangering patients' health, diminishing trust in AI, and therefore hampering the global adoption of such tools in EDs [3, 10].

Innumerable research projects have been dedicated to evaluating AI applicability and safety in the medical context. Since LLMs' medical theoretical knowledge forms the foundation for their medical competence, initial efforts focused on compiling multiple-choice medical questions [11] [12] [13] [14]. On top of evaluating medical knowledge, attempts to appraise clinical reasoning and decision-making skills were made through benchmarking datasets [15] [16] [17]. There are disputes about these types of benchmarks, however, arguing that simple question-and-answer approaches don't represent the clinical utility of AI completely, nor do the more complex and synthetic computational simulations of clinical practice [18, 19]. Validating and implementing AI as a safe and accepted EM tool necessitates thoughtful design and thorough investigation schemes, by involving EM clinicians and patients as stakeholders in the whole benchmarking process [3].

Goals of This Investigation

In this paper, we propose an extensive evaluation process to measure an LLM's medical capabilities in the ED. Our benchmarking approach is based on simulating EM; by putting an LLM in simulated emergency scenarios as an EM clinician, not only can the medical knowledge and diagnostic proficiencies be analyzed, but we can also properly evaluate the other necessary skills, such as historytaking or empathy [20]. To contrast against the common benchmarking approaches, we also design a written exam to evaluate EM knowledge and ascertain the competency of LLMs and EM doctors.

Overall, our work provides a comprehensive insight into different types of LLMs' performance capabilities for the ED (Fig 1).

Methods

Study Design

To establish a baseline to compare the LLMs against, we employed nine EM physicians with different levels of expertise: three residents in early years of residency, three last-year residents, and three emergency medicine specialists. The selected physicians were compared against eight different LLMs, namely ChatGPT 5.6 Sol, Claude 4.8 Opus, DeepSeek R1, DeepSeek V4 Pro, Gemini 3.1 Pro, O3, Qwen 3.7 Max (Fast), and Qwen 3.7 Max (Thinking), all accessed in June 2026.

To achieve an adequate emulation, we utilized real emergency cases that have occurred in the ED. Making use of the book "100 Cases in Emergency Medicine and Critical Care" [21], we randomly 1 chose thirty emergency cases, three cases for EM simulation (one per clinician in the three-person groups of residents and specialists) and twenty-seven for the written exam.

Written Exam

The twenty-seven randomly choses cases, each followed by two to three questions, were provided to the eight LLMs, and their corresponding answers were retrieved. For testing the human doctors, each clinician was tested with 9 out of 27 cases, in a way that each group (residents, last-year residents, and specialists) would be tested with all 27 cases in the exam questionnaire. Written answers from clinicians were also retrieved. Both the answers of the LLMs and clinicians were given to the emergency medicine specialist expert to score on aspects such as diagnosis, investigation, and treatment (Fig 1A).

EM Simulation

For each physician in the experiment, we recruited an intern to represent an emulated patient in an ED case. After being informed and instructed about the case, each intern, acting as an emergency patient, was interviewed by one human physician and eight different LLMs. All the interviews were in Farsi language and performed through a mediator, so that the interns wouldn't know if they were being interviewed by a human doctor or AI. All the clinicians, interns, and LLMs were instructed beforehand on how the experiment was performed and what was expected from them. After each interview, the intern was provided with a feedback form to rate the interview from different aspects, such as diagnosis, concern reduction, or treatment plan, on a 5-point Likert scale. In addition, the written records of the interviews were given to an emergency medicine specialist expert to rate the performance of clinicians and LLMs on different criteria, namely history taking, diagnostic accuracy, treatment plan, and the level of empathy. Names were removed from the interview records; hence, the expert was blinded to the identity of the interviewer, human or LLM (Fig 1B).

Data Analysis

All the gathered data in this work is in the format of a 5-point Likert scale, which means that in this work we deal with ordinal data. Therefore, we generally utilized nonparametric methods for processing and analyzing the data. We used the Mann-Whitney U test to compare Likert scores from two different groups. For comparing more than two populations, we employed the Kruskal-Wallis test. To perform post-hoc power analysis for the aforementioned tests, we used Monte Carlo simulation to estimate the empirical power of Mann-Whitney U and Kruskal-Wallis tests. To reduce the dimensions and extract the underlying factors from the multi-dimensional Likert data, we used factor analysis based on 1 Using NumPy library in Python polychoric correlation. Detailed explanation about the methods, used packages, the software and the code that performed the analysis is provided in the supplements (S1).

Results

We used the following nomenclature to distinguish the results of different types:

Simulated Doctor Visit refers to results that are acquired from EM simulation, resulting from the EM expert scoring the interview records. II)

Simulated Patient Experience designates the results from the feedback form received from interns who were acting as simulated patients in EM simulation. III)

Written Exam is the result of the EM expert scoring the written answers of clinicians and AI to the written exam.

Firstly, results from LLMs and physicians were plotted separately as horizontal stacked bar charts, also known as Likert plots (Fig 2). Scores from each category, e.g., history taking, empathy, etc., were compared between the human physicians and the LLMs using a directional Mann-Whitney U test, testing whether LLMs' scores are significantly higher than the physicians', and the resulted p values are reported in the central column of Figure 2.

As is evident from the charts and the results of the statistical tests, LLMs scored predominantly higher in almost all categories. In every single category the written exam, LLMs scored significantly higher than physicians, with the p values suggesting an extremely significant difference, i.e., p < 0.001, marked by three asterisks (Fig 2C), and post-hoc power analysis estimating empirical power at ~1. The same can be said about values from the simulated doctor visit, with p values pointing toward a highly significant advantage, i.e.,0.001 ≤ p < 0.01 marked by two asterisks, or an extreme advantage for LLMs (Fig 2A), with estimated empirical power of ~0.97. The only group of results in which LLMs didn't score significantly higher in all categories was simulated patients' opinions; while LLM results were extremely significant in some categories, such as diagnosis or concern reduction, they were only slightly significant (0.01 ≤ p < 0.05, marked by one asterisk) in others, such as communication or comprehensibility (Fig 2B), and the estimated empirical power was ~0.83.

When acquiring simulated patients' feedback after each visit, we queried about their opinion regarding the similarity of the doctor, human or AI, to a human. Patients graded this similarity on a 5-point Likert scale, with 1 meaning a very artificial and 'robotic' interaction and 5 referring to a very natural and human-like interaction. Since LLMs were instructed not to reveal that they are AI, and the patients were blinded to the identity of their visiting doctors, this score worked as a Turing test [22]. Ironically, from patients' point of view, the only category with non-significant difference between human physicians and LLMs was human similarity (Fig 2B).

For a deep, granular look into the performance of each LLM and comparing them against each other, it is possible to plot the Likert scores for every single LLM in each category separately (Supp. Fig 1A). However, having multiple groups of results, each one with multiple subgroups and categories, represents a complex data structure which makes it difficult to properly quantify the differences among LLMs. Using machine learning and data mining approaches, we aimed to simplify the data structure, extract the relevant information, and calculate the performance differences. Firstly, we used factor analysis to extract the underlying factors in the data, so that data dimensions are reduced while relevant information is retained. Scree plots show that there is one underlying factor in simulated doctor visit scores, one in simulated patient experience scores, and three in written exam resulting data (Fig S3).

We used factor analysis to extract the underlying factors in doctor visit and patient experience data sets.

As for written exam data, we averaged over diagnosis, investigation, and treatment categories to have three interpretable dimensions of data. We then used linear regression modeling on this 5-dimensional data, 5-dimensions being the dependent variables and the type of LLM used as independent or explanatory variable. As is common in the field, we used the performance of ChatGPT in our experiment as the reference and compared the performance of other LLMs against it. In the fitted linear regression models, each LLM in our work is represented by one term in the models, and the corresponding coefficients are plotted in a forest plot and an accompanying table (Fig 3). Each dot in the forest plot represents the average performance difference of an LLM compared to ChatGPT on a Likert scale, and the whiskers around the dots show the extent of the 95% confidence interval. For example, the value for Gemini in the simulated doctor visit model is -0.78, which means Gemini scored, on average, 0.78 worse than ChatGPT in simulated doctor visit grades.

Comprehensive benchmarks have been implemented to evaluate AI in the context of EM. They range from simple approaches that assess a singular skill to holistic frameworks that quantify the competence of AI from multiple angles [11, 25]. Most usually took the same approach: compiling a compendium of questions and tasks, aiming to make each dataset larger than the state of the art, with the goal of covering as many necessary EM skills as possible while circumventing challenges such as data leakage [26, 27]. We took a different approach, however; rather than separately and explicitly testing AI for each specific skill, we aimed to simulate EM and place AI in the simulation. This way, all necessary skills are intrinsically tested, and the results more accurately reflect real-world performance.

The results demonstrated a clear advantage for the tested AI compared to the human physicians. LLMs scored significantly higher in skills such as history taking, investigation, diagnosis, treatment plan, comprehensibility, credibility, concern reduction, and empathy. Unlike the clear contrast between the performances of LLMs and human physicians, the medical expertise among different LLMs was not distinguishable as clearly. In general, some AI models scored higher than others, but the differences were minor and non-significant. The only clear distinction was observed in the written exam, in which ChatGPT, O3, Claude, and DeepSeek V4 performed better than Qwen and Gemini, and DeepSeek R1 performed almost universally worse than others.

Findings were consistent between EM simulation and written exam results. However, there were evaluation aspects that only became evident in the simulation scenarios. Interview length was a variable factor for instance: on average, Gemini reached a diagnosis after asking 6 questions, Claude after around 9, and ChatGPT or O3 after about 12 questions. Since there were no significant differences among LLMs in their history taking and diagnosis scores (Fig S1A-I), the higher interview pace suggests a nimbler conduct. In the high-stakes, chaotic environment of EM, agile performance is a great asset. Additionally, quicker diagnosis signifies far fewer tokens spent. At scale, such disparity can translate to differences in cost and the required resources.

Since the medical competence benchmarking was done in Farsi, it simultaneously operated as a linguistic bias test. English is the most common language in LLM training, resulting in a bias toward this language [28] [29] [30]. Analyzing how an AI handles a less common language provides a window into how language bias can affect the interaction quality of LLMs. In our experiments, LLMs handled Farsi communication well for the most part, as reflected in the high comprehensibility scores by the simulated patients (Fig 2). It seemed that most LLMs performed in their original language (English or Chinese, depending on the origin of the LLM), and translated the message in either the last or first stage. Therefore, the quality of the conversation was dependent on the quality of the translation. In rare cases, LLMs produced calques, reducing the quality of the translation. For example, in one instance, in an attempt to provide reassurance, Claude literally translated the term "you are in good hands" to Farsi, which became meaningless and resulted in patient confusion.

Linguistic bias can be classified as a sub-category of cultural bias. On a broader scale, training a language model predominantly in one language and attempting to interact with humans in another can hinder the quality of communication due to the inherent cultural discrepancy between the languages.

An example of such bias was the frequent attempts by one of the LLMs to reassure or empathize, which made a simulated patient feel uneasy and doubtful. He was used to interacting with direct and to-thepoint doctors, and his interaction with the LLM felt dishonest and insincere. This sentiment was supported by some others during our experiments, leading us to believe that developing one model and using translation to utilize it in different countries might result in impaired communications. If the aim is to deploy a medical AI system on a commercial scale that can properly interact with the patients, adaptations based on the target country are needed.

Since their inception, LLMs have been marred by hallucinations. With each new iteration of an LLM, measures have been taken to reduce the rate of hallucinations [31]. Considering all the different LLMs that have been thoroughly tested in the medical context, this work provided an apt testing environment for hallucination occurrence. During the patient interviews in the course of the EM simulation, no hallucinations were detected. We speculate this is because most of the LLMs used in this work are rather new, and have modern mitigation strategies to prevent hallucinations. The same was true during the written exam benchmarking, with the exception of Claude 4.8 Opus; when the questions in the written exam were given to Claude all together, it answered the first few, then started hallucinating new questions answering its own made-up questions. Multiple tries resulted in the same outcome, forcing us to divide the written exam into smaller batches of questions and gather the answers batch-wise. This shows that while the hallucination rate has been reduced drastically in newer LLMs, they still occur. In medicine, these occurrences can have drastic consequences, necessitating further improvement in hallucination prevention.

In this work, we have demonstrated how novel benchmarking approaches can provide better insights into AI performance in distinctive applications. The added insight comes at a price, however, warranting extra time and resources compared to automated Q&A-based methods. A potential countermeasure for the costly steps would be to partially automate the framework. Admittedly, major ethical, legal, and regulatory challenges for the fully automated benchmarks persist [1]. Yet, approaches such as humanin-the-loop artificial intelligence solutions can mitigate these challenges by balancing automation efficiency with human oversight and improve accuracy beyond unassisted human or AI performance alone [32]. Predictive models can also circumvent some steps which are time-and resource-costly. A solution to use publicly available benchmarking results to predict AI competence in EM is explored in the supplements (S4 and Fig S2).

AI advancements were astonishing in the past few years. In this work, AI could outperform human physicians in almost every metric (Fig 2). Even in a metric such as the human similarity, AI performed quite well, indicating that patients found AI and human doctors indistinguishable. The widespread utilization of such competent medical tools might be enticing to the stakeholders. However, any mishap tracing back to impetuous AI employment can have devastating repercussions for the AI acclimation in medicine [10]. Though still limited, negative perception is growing against AI in medicine, due to the usage of imperfect AI programs by some U.S. health insurers and the subsequent sinister effect on patients' medical care [33, 34].
