How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Abstract

Background. Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a common complication of diabetes, and in Bangladesh laboratory testing for it is not always available. Questionnaire answers could help decide whom to test first, but small machine-learning studies often report optimistic accuracy.

We analysed a public dataset of 400 adults with at least 10 years of diabetes attending a tertiary hospital in Dhaka, 185 with a hospital diagnosis of CKD. A pre-specified ridge logistic regression using 20 non-invasive features was developed and internally validated with nested cross-validation (five folds, ten repeats), compared with lasso, random forest and gradient boosting, and assessed for discrimination, calibration and net benefit. Simulations examined case-enriched sampling and single train/test splits.

The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was 0.75 (95% confidence interval 0.70-0.80; optimism-corrected 0.76). Flexible algorithms were not detectably better (differences -0.005 to +0.006). Calibration within the sample was good (slope 1.06, 0.79-1.33), and testing the highest-scoring 30% of patients would have found 48% of CKD cases. The sample was smaller than the 883 patients recommended. Urinary infection, body-mass index, insulin use, diabetes duration and pain-killer use were positively associated with CKD and diet adherence negatively; regular walking and hypertension showed directions opposite to clinical expectation, consistent with features recorded after diagnosis. The yearly history added no reliable information. In simulation, case-enriched sampling preserved the AUC but overstated absolute risk (mean predicted risk 0.37 at a true prevalence of 0.10). Over 300 random 80/20 splits of the same data, the test AUC ranged from 0.61 to 0.87; reporting the best of ten splits would overstate it by about 0.07.

Conclusions.

Questionnaire features moderately distinguished patients with an existing CKD diagnosis. Whether they identify patients before diagnosis requires external validation in patients assessed before CKD status is known. The study was not registered.

Introduction

Diabetes affected an estimated 13.9 million adults in Bangladesh in 2024, 13.2% of the adult population, and about two in five of them were undiagnosed [1]. Diabetes is the leading cause of chronic kidney disease (CKD) worldwide [2], CKD is common in South Asia [3], and reduced kidney function is frequent among patients with type 2 diabetes attending a tertiary diabetes hospital in Dhaka [4]. Guidelines recommend at least annual testing of serum creatinine and urinary albumin in people with diabetes [5], but in low-resource settings these tests are not always available or affordable, and many patients are tested late. A tool that ranks patients by their likelihood of CKD from information that can be collected in a conversation could help clinics decide whom to test first. Simple questionnaire-based scores have been developed for this purpose in other populations [6].

Machine-learning studies of CKD often report very high accuracy, but they are typically based on small datasets of a few hundred patients, such as the widely used public dataset of 400 records [7], and are rarely validated in clinical settings or in diverse populations [8]. Across clinical prediction research, studies using machine learning are frequently at high risk of bias [9], flexible algorithms seldom outperform logistic regression [10], and leakage between training and test data inflates reported performance [11]. Small samples add a further problem: estimates from a single train/test split carry wide error bars [12], models developed on too few patients are unstable [13], and minimum sample sizes depend on the number of candidate predictors, the outcome prevalence and the anticipated model fit rather than on a fixed number of events per variable [14, 15].

A public dataset collected at BIRDEM General Hospital in Dhaka records 20 non-invasive features, covering demographics, diabetes history and treatment, comorbidities, urinary symptoms, medication and lifestyle, for 400 adults with long-standing diabetes, 185 of whom had CKD [16, 17]. These features can be collected without laboratory tests, but the dataset also has properties that shape what any model trained on it can show: it is small, it contains far more patients with CKD than a clinic population would, and the patients were interviewed once, after many of them had been diagnosed.

The aims of this study were therefore threefold. First, to develop and internally validate a prespecified penalised logistic regression model that classifies CKD status from these 20 features, to compare it with more flexible algorithms, and to quantify its discrimination, calibration and, as an illustration, how it would rank patients for laboratory testing, with their uncertainty. Second, to examine which features carry information and whether their associations agree with clinical knowledge. Third, to quantify two methodological issues that affect small clinical datasets in general: how case-enriched sampling changes predicted probabilities, and how much a single train/test split can mislead at this sample size. Two secondary questions asked whether the pattern of diabetes management and the ten-year yearly history add information beyond the current profile.

Materials and methods

Study design and reporting

This is a secondary analysis of a published, de-identified dataset. We developed and internally validated a model that classifies CKD status in adults with long-standing diabetes from 20 non-invasive features, and we used the same data to examine how the sampling design and the evaluation procedure affect the evidence such models can provide. Reporting follows the TRIPOD+AI statement [18]; the completed checklist is provided in S1 Checklist.

The analysis plan, including the primary model, the primary metric and all secondary questions, was written into the analysis code (protocol version 1.0) and fixed before the final analysis was run; any later change would have been recorded in a deviation log, which remained empty.

Exploratory analyses of the same dataset had been carried out before the plan was fixed, so the plan is not a prospectively registered protocol. The study was not registered, and the protocol file is available with the code. Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct or reporting of this analysis.

Data source and participants

The dataset was collected at BIRDEM General Hospital, a tertiary diabetes hospital in Dhaka, Bangladesh, and is described in detail elsewhere [16, 17]. In brief, adults who had lived with diabetes for at least 10 years and held a complete diabetes record book were interviewed with a structured questionnaire during routine hospital visits between March and June 2024. The questionnaire was developed with a supervising nephrologist and piloted twice before use, and answers were cross-checked against each patient's record book. Each patient contributes 10 consecutive yearly records, and the last year of each record is the collection year. We analysed version 2 of the public dataset (SHA-256 fingerprint 9cef0eb4…3618, verified at every run).

Before analysis, the code checked the structural properties that the analysis relies on: 400 patients with exactly 10 records each, no missing values, diabetes duration and age increasing by one year per record, and a CKD indicator that never reverts from 1 to 0.

Outcome

The outcome was a diagnosis of CKD at any point in the 10-year record, as documented in each patient's hospital-issued diabetes record book, in which physicians record CKD status on the basis of standard laboratory tests at regular check-ups. The laboratory values themselves are not part of the dataset, so CKD stage could not be analysed [16]. The dataset codes the diagnosis as a yearly indicator that changes from 0 to 1 in the year of diagnosis and remains 1 afterwards; no patient had CKD in the first year of the record.

Predictors

The 20 candidate predictors were: gender, job exertion level (normal, intermediate, heavy), family history of diabetes, diabetes duration, age, body-mass index, adherence to the suggested diet, oral diabetes medicine, insulin, hypertension, heart disease, sufficient sleep (7-9 h per night), sufficient water intake (more than 2 L per day), smoking, zarda or betel leaf use, regular walking (at least 30 min per day), urinating properly, urinary infection or other urological issue, regular pain-killer use, and daily calorie intake (definitions and coding in Table A in S1 Appendix). Hypertension was recorded as present when the record book showed persistently high blood pressure (at least 140/90 mmHg) treated with antihypertensive medication; the drug class was not recorded. Calorie intake was estimated from the reported typical diet using standard dietary portion values [16]. Height, weight, average weight and average age were not used, because body-mass index is derived from height and weight and the averaged columns summarise the whole record. All 20 features were collected in the same interview and were treated alike in the analysis. Because CKD status was documented in the hospital records that were consulted during the interview, predictor assessment was not blinded to the outcome.

Predictor values were taken from the first year of the record, when every patient was free of a CKD diagnosis. This choice does not make the analysis prospective. Several features are recorded as one value for the whole record (gender, family history, body-mass index, which is averaged over the 10 years, and calorie intake), urinary infection is a history over the 10-year period, job exertion describes the working life, and hypertension reflects blood pressure over the record. Most of the remaining features rarely change from year to year. The predictor values therefore largely describe patients as recorded at the collection interview, which for most patients with CKD took place after the diagnosis. We accordingly treat the task as classification of current CKD status and interpret associations as cross-sectional.

Binary features were coded 1 (present) and 0 (absent), job exertion as an ordinal score from 1 to 3, and continuous features on their original scales; all features were standardised inside the modelling pipeline using the training data only. There were no missing values, because complete records were an inclusion criterion.

Models

The primary model, pre-specified because it is a regression model suited to small samples, was ridge-penalised (L2) logistic regression [19]. Its regularisation strength (inverse penalty C in {0.01, 0.03, 0.1, 0.3, 1, 3}) was chosen by 3-fold cross-validation on the training data, minimising log-loss so that tuning rewards well-calibrated probabilities as well as ranking.

Comparators were lasso (L1) logistic regression [20] (C in {0.03, 0.1, 0.3, 1, 3}), a random forest [21] (200 trees; minimum leaf size 3 or 10; 50% of features or the square root of the number of features per split) and histogram-based gradient boosting [22] (150 iterations, L2 regularisation 1.0, minimum leaf size 15; maximum depth 2 or 3; learning rate 0.03 or 0.08). Two baselines bounded the comparison: a prevalence-only model, which assigns every patient the training prevalence, and a ridge model with age and diabetes duration only. Search grids were kept small on purpose, because large searches mainly fit noise at this sample size. No model was selected on the evaluation data; the comparators were evaluated to show whether more flexible algorithms changed the conclusions. No resampling or reweighting for class imbalance was used, because the two classes were of similar size. The model output is a probability; no classification threshold was chosen, and tertiles of the predicted probability were used only to describe observed CKD proportions.

Internal validation

Performance was estimated by nested cross-validation [23]. The outer loop was stratified 5-fold cross-validation repeated 10 times (50 test folds of 80 patients, each with 320 training patients); the inner loop was the 3-fold tuning step above, run on the outer training data only. The analysis table had one row per patient, so no patient appeared in training and test data at the same time. Each patient's out-of-fold predictions were averaged over the 10 repeats, and these pooled out-offold predictions were used for all summaries of discrimination, calibration and clinical utility.

The primary metric was the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), with a 95% confidence interval from a patient-level percentile bootstrap of the pooled out-of-fold predictions (2000 resamples). We also report the fold-to-fold standard deviation of the AUC, the area under the precision-recall curve, the Brier score and log-loss. Differences in AUC between the primary model and each comparator were computed fold by fold on identical folds and summarised with the Nadeau-Bengio corrected resampled t-interval [24], which accounts for the overlap of training sets in repeated cross-validation; p-values across comparators were adjusted with the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure [25].

Two further analyses checked the primary estimate. First, the optimism of the apparent AUC of the final model was estimated with Harrell's bootstrap [26] (300 resamples), repeating the complete modelling strategy, including tuning, in every resample. Second, a label-permutation test [27] compared the cross-validated AUC (5-fold, regularisation fixed at the final value, mean over three fold assignments) with its distribution under 500 random permutations of the outcome, with p = (1 + b)/(1 + m), where b is the number of permutations reaching the observed AUC and m the number of permutations [28].

Calibration and clinical utility

Calibration [29] was assessed with the calibration slope (ideal 1) and calibration-in-the-large (ideal 0), each with a 95% patient-level bootstrap interval (1000 resamples), and with a calibration plot showing a locally weighted (LOWESS) curve [30] and observed proportions in six quantile groups with Wilson intervals [31]. To express performance in terms of patients, we divided the pooled out-of-fold scores into tertiles (lower, middle and higher risk) and report the observed CKD proportion in each. In an illustrative testing-prioritisation scenario, we computed the share of all CKD cases found if only the highest-scoring 10% to 60% of patients were sent for laboratory testing, with bootstrap intervals, and the lift relative to testing the same number of patients at random. Decision curve analysis [32] compared the net benefit of testing according to the model with testing everyone or no one over threshold probabilities from 0.05 to 0.90. All probabilities refer to the study sample, in which 46% of patients had CKD.

Sample size

The sample size was fixed by the available data. Its adequacy for developing a 20-parameter model was assessed retrospectively with the criteria of Riley and colleagues [14]: expected shrinkage of at least 0.90, an optimism of at most 0.05 in the Nagelkerke R², and estimation of the overall outcome proportion within ±0.05. The anticipated Cox-Snell R² was taken from the cross-validated predictions of the primary model, a deliberately conservative choice. The implementation was checked against the pmsampsize package. Events per variable are reported for orientation only.

Predictor effects and plausibility audit

The final ridge model was fitted to all 400 patients with the regularisation strength selected by cross-validation, and its full equation is reported with the results. Its coefficients are reported as odds ratios per one standard deviation of each predictor, so that binary and continuous predictors share one scale, with 95% percentile intervals and the proportion of resamples with the same sign (sign agreement) from 2000 bootstrap refits. Permutation importance, the mean fall in AUC when one predictor is permuted, was computed on the held-out folds of the primary analysis (five permutations per fold). For a plausibility audit, the expected direction of association of each predictor with CKD was specified in the analysis code before the model was fitted, based on clinical knowledge (Table A in S1 Appendix). An effect was classified as supported when its interval excluded 1, and supported effects were compared with the expected direction. For readers who need inference on individual coefficients, an unpenalised multivariable logistic regression with Wald confidence intervals and Benjamini-Hochberg-adjusted p-values is also reported.

Subgroups and sensitivity analyses

To check whether performance differed between groups of patients, discrimination (AUC with bootstrap interval) and calibration (ratio of observed to expected cases) were examined within subgroups of gender, age tertile, body-mass index category (below 18.5, 18.5-22.9, 23-24.9 and at least 25 kg/m², the cut-offs proposed for Asian populations [33]) and diabetes duration at the start of the record (1 year, 2-7 years, 8 years or more). Subgroups with fewer than 20 patients were not analysed.

Three sensitivity analyses repeated the nested cross-validation: after removing patients with an outlying value of age, diabetes duration, body-mass index or calorie intake (Tukey's 1.5 × interquartile range rule [34]); with cubic spline terms (four knots) for the four continuous predictors, to check the linearity assumption; and with a different random assignment of folds. A feature-group ablation evaluated four clinical domains (demographics and body; diabetes history and treatment; comorbidity, urinary symptoms and pain-killer use; lifestyle and substance use) alone and by leaving each out of the full model, with the regularisation strength fixed at the final value and the same 50 folds.

Secondary questions

Diabetes management pattern. Diet adherence, oral medicine and insulin together describe how a patient's diabetes was managed, so they are not independent. We described the CKD proportion in each of the eight possible combinations (Wilson intervals; Monte Carlo permutation test of the chi-square statistic, 10,000 permutations) and tested whether seven pattern indicators, or three pairwise interaction terms, improved the cross-validated AUC beyond the three separate indicators (ridge model with fixed regularisation, same folds, Nadeau-Bengio intervals). We also compared how often the pattern changed during years 1 to 4 between patients later diagnosed with CKD and patients never diagnosed, restricted to patients still free of CKD at year 4 (Fisher's exact test).

Sampling design.

To describe when diagnoses fall within the record, we compared the share of first diagnoses in the final (collection) year with the 1 in 9 expected if diagnoses were spread evenly over years 2 to 10 (exact binomial test), and tested whether the remaining diagnoses were spread evenly over years 2 to 9 (chi-square test). Because a sample with 46% CKD is enriched for cases, we examined the consequences in a semi-synthetic simulation anchored to the cohort [36, 37]. Predictor rows were resampled from the 400 patients, and outcomes were generated from the fitted ridge model with the intercept set to give a population prevalence of 10%, 20% or 30%. Models were trained either on a case-enriched sample of the cohort's composition (185 cases and 215 controls) or on a random sample of 400, and the case-enriched models were also evaluated after prior correction, which adds log[π/(1-π)] -log[ȳ/(1-ȳ)] to the predicted logodds, where π is the population prevalence and ȳ the sample proportion [37]. Each design was replicated 300 times per prevalence and evaluated on 20,000 independent simulated patients (AUC, mean predicted risk, calibration-in-the-large and slope).

Reliability of single train/test splits. Many published models are evaluated on one random split.

We quantified the consequences in four ways [12]. (a) The ridge, random forest and gradient boosting models, with fixed hyper-parameters, were evaluated on 300 random stratified 80/20 splits of the cohort; the expected result of reporting the best of k random seeds was obtained by resampling the observed distribution (5000 draws). (b) In a simulation with 10 standard-normal predictors, an outcome prevalence of 46% and a known true AUC of 0.65, 0.75 or 0.85, 100 datasets were generated for each sample size from 100 to 1600. In each dataset we compared the estimate from a single 80/20 split, and the best of 3, 5 or 10 splits, with the estimate from 5-fold cross-validation repeated three times; errors were measured against the true AUC of the evaluated logistic regression model(s) on 8000 independent simulated patients. (c) A learning curve repeated 5-fold cross-validation of the ridge model on stratified subsamples of 100 to 400 patients. (d) The split experiment was repeated with logistic regression on seven public clinical datasets from OpenML [38].

Discussion

In 400 adults with long-standing diabetes attending a tertiary hospital in Dhaka, 20 non-invasive questionnaire features classified CKD status with an AUC of 0.75 (95% CI 0.70-0.80). The signal was real, robust to the choice of algorithm and to sensitivity analyses, and well calibrated within the sample, and ranking patients by the model would have found about half of the CKD cases among the highest-scoring 30%. The evidence is limited in three ways that the analysis makes explicit: the sample was less than half the size recommended for a model of this complexity, the features largely describe patients after diagnosis, and the case-enriched design means that the predicted probabilities do not apply to an ordinary clinic population without recalibration.

An AUC of 0.75 means that a randomly chosen patient with CKD received a higher score than a randomly chosen patient without CKD three times out of four. That is too weak for diagnosis but could be useful for deciding whom to test first where laboratory capacity is limited, which is the intended use of such a tool. Two conditions apply. First, the probabilities refer to a sample in which 46% of patients had CKD; our simulation shows that a model trained on such a sample would overstate risk in a population with 10% to 30% prevalence, and that the bias can be removed only if the target prevalence is known or the model is recalibrated locally [29, 37].

Second, studies that compare cases identified at diagnosis with clearly unaffected controls tend to overestimate diagnostic accuracy [43]. Our simulation cannot capture this, because it assumes that the features relate to CKD in the same way in the sample and the population, so the AUC itself may be lower in consecutive patients.

The predictor associations should be read as cross-sectional. Most features were recorded as one value for the whole record or rarely changed, and for two thirds of the patients with CKD the diagnosis fell in the year of the interview. Several associations are therefore plausibly consequences of CKD or of its management rather than risk factors. Insulin use is a clear example: oral agents such as metformin are restricted at low kidney function, so patients are often moved to insulin as CKD develops [5]. Regular walking, which was associated with higher odds of CKD, is recommended to patients with diabetes and CKD [5], and patients may have reported the advice they had received after diagnosis. The positive association with regular painkiller use agrees with the known nephrotoxicity of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs [44], but the direction of the relation cannot be established here.

Hypertension, a recognised cause of CKD progression, was associated with lower odds of CKD.

Two explanations are plausible and cannot be distinguished with these data. In the dataset, hypertension means sustained high blood pressure treated with medication, and angiotensin II receptor blockers and angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, which slow the decline of kidney function in diabetes [45, 46], are widely prescribed in Dhaka [47, 48]. Treated hypertensive patients may therefore have been partly protected, although treatment more readily explains a weaker association than a reversed one. Second, eligibility required at least 10 years of diabetes and current attendance at the hospital; hypertensive patients whose kidney disease progressed fastest may have left this population through dialysis, transfer or death before they could be recruited, a form of selection bias that can reverse associations [49]. Because the drug class was not recorded, neither explanation can be tested. For these reasons the model should not be used to give advice about behaviour or treatment.

The ten-year yearly records added little. Most features hardly changed within patients, the gain from trajectory summaries was small and inconsistent across landmarks, and flexible models did not extract more from the same inputs. Studies that attribute added value to longitudinal modelling of such data should show it against a snapshot baseline evaluated on the same folds.

Similarly, the diabetes management pattern was strongly associated with CKD, but the three separate indicators already carried this information.

The single-split experiments have implications beyond this dataset. Splitting the same 400 patients at random gave test AUCs from 0.61 to 0.87, and selecting the best of ten splits would have inflated the result by about 0.07. The simulation shows that error bars of this size are expected at a few hundred patients whatever the true accuracy, consistent with earlier work [12, 13], and the public datasets show the same pattern. Published CKD models trained on small datasets and evaluated on a single split [8] should therefore be interpreted with caution. This dataset is no exception: one analysis treated its 4,000 patient-years as independent observations, split them at random and reported an accuracy of 0.94 [50]. Because about 90% of patient-years carry no CKD label, always predicting no CKD would score 0.90, and records of the same patient fall on both sides of such a split. Repeated, nested cross-validation with confidence intervals, a pre-specified primary model, and reporting of all analyses, as recommended by TRIPOD+AI [18], are inexpensive safeguards.

The strengths of this study are a pre-specified primary model and analysis plan, evaluation by nested repeated cross-validation at the patient level, confidence intervals for every performance measure, assessment of calibration and clinical utility, an explicit audit of the plausibility of the associations, simulations that separate design effects from features of the data, and public data and code that reproduce every number. The limitations are substantial. The data come from one tertiary centre and include only patients with at least 10 years of diabetes and a complete record book, who are likely to differ from the wider diabetic population. The sample was smaller than recommended, so the coefficients may be unstable even with penalisation. There was no external validation. Predictors were collected at a single structured interview and cross-checked against the record books, but lifestyle items relied on patient report, CKD status was visible to interviewers in the record books, several features were recorded as values for the whole record, and antihypertensive drug classes were not recorded. The model needs all 20 inputs and has no procedure for missing answers. Finally, the findings on subgroups were imprecise.

Before any practical use, the model would need external validation in consecutive patients with diabetes who complete the questionnaire before their CKD status is known, recalibration to the prevalence of the setting, and evaluation of its effect on testing decisions.

Conclusions

Twenty non-invasive questionnaire features carried real but moderate information about CKD status in adults with long-standing diabetes in Bangladesh. The estimate is uncertain, the associations are cross-sectional, and two of them contradict clinical expectation, so the model is not ready for clinical use. At this sample size, single train/test splits and case-enriched samples can produce misleading accuracy and probabilities; repeated nested cross-validation with confidence intervals, together with external validation and recalibration, is needed before such models are used to prioritise testing.
