Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

INTRODUCTION.

White matter hyperintensities (WMH) are among the most common neuroimaging findings in older adults and are associated with cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD), cognitive decline, stroke, and dementia. Although WMH are often interpreted as markers of vascular brain injury, their biological substrates are heterogeneous and may reflect contributions from small vessel injury, neurodegeneration, and other vascular and metabolic processes (Wardlaw et al., 2015; Smith and Beaudin, 2018; Vemuri et al., 2022; Maillard et al., 2024). Accordingly, WMH are increasingly quantified as a continuous marker of cumulative white matter injury across population-based aging studies, vascular cohorts, and studies of Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (AD/ADRD) (Garnier-Crussard et al., 2023; DeCarli et al., 2025). Reliable automated segmentation is essential for these applications because manual delineation is labor-intensive, subject to inter-rater variability, and impractical for large, multisite imaging datasets.

The broader use of WMH as a cross-disease imaging biomarker, however, presents a substantial translational challenge because models are often applied to populations that differ from their development cohorts in both clinical characteristics and MRI acquisition protocols.

Contemporary neuroimaging studies frequently combine participants with different disease profiles and lesion burdens whose scans were acquired on different scanners and with different FLAIR protocols, including conventional thick-slice 2D and approximately isotropic 3D sequences. Segmentation performance can vary across these conditions, particularly in participants with low WMH burden or small punctate lesions, and protocol-dependent errors may introduce systematic differences in estimated WMH volume (Heinen et al., 2019; Hotz et al., 2022; Kuijf et al., 2019; Tubi et al., 2020; Torres-Simon et al., 2024). Methods developed within a particular disease spectrum or acquisition setting may therefore perform less consistently when both the population and imaging protocol differ from their development distributions (Caligiuri et al., 2015; Balakrishnan et al., 2021; Rahmani et al., 2024). This mismatch complicates pooled analyses and may force investigators to use different algorithms across sub-cohorts, exclude incompatible acquisitions, or deploy a single method beyond the setting in which it was validated.

Recent deep-learning methods have substantially advanced automated WMH segmentation.

SHIVA-WMH has demonstrated sensitivity to early WMH in vascular and aging cohorts, segcsvdWMH was developed using heterogeneous datasets to quantify WMH in CSVD and related clinical populations, and WMH-SynthSeg was designed to support segmentation across heterogeneous clinical MRI data (Gibson et al., 2024; Laso et al., 2024; Tsuchida et al., 2024).

These methods address important dimensions of population or acquisition heterogeneity.

However, evidence remains limited for a single empirically trained model designed and evaluated across the intersection of neurodegenerative, vascular, and population-based cohorts and both conventional 2D and isotropic 3D FLAIR acquisitions. Moreover, relatively few studies have determined whether performance is maintained across lesion burden and lesion size, tested transfer to an unseen acquisition configuration, or examined whether algorithm-dependent differences in WMH quantification alter downstream clinical information (Tubi et al., 2020; Gesierich et al., 2025). Addressing this intersectional gap is necessary to establish a consistent WMH measure for large, heterogeneous neuroimaging studies.

In this study, we used the self-configuring nnU-Net framework (Isensee et al., 2021) to develop a single WMH segmentation model using three complementary cohorts: the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI), representing the neurodegenerative disease spectrum; the Washington University White Matter Ischemic-Hypoxia study (WWISH), enriched for CSVD and contributing paired 2D and 3D FLAIR acquisitions; and UK Biobank (UKB), representing population-based aging. Together, these cohorts encompass substantial variation in WMH burden, lesion morphology, disease context, and FLAIR acquisitions. We addressed four questions: 1) whether the model provides accurate and consistent overall segmentation across cohorts and acquisition types relative to contemporary benchmark methods; 2) whether it maintains sensitivity to small lesions and low WMH burden; 3) whether the model maintains its performance on an independent set of ADNI 3D FLAIR scans not used for model development or tuning; and 4) whether its WMH volume estimates provide incremental information about incident dementia beyond established clinical predictors. We hypothesized that deliberately incorporating heterogeneity in disease context, lesion burden, and acquisition into model development would produce a single deployable model with consistent segmentation performance across the evaluated imaging settings and clinically informative WMH estimates.

METHODS

Participants and imaging datasets

We assembled a multi-cohort imaging dataset designed to reflect the heterogeneity of populations and FLAIR acquisitions encountered in aging and cerebrovascular research.

Participants were drawn from three cohorts: the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI), representing an AD-focused neurodegenerative cohort; the Washington University White Matter Ischemic-Hypoxia study (WWISH), representing a CSVD-enriched cohort; and the UK Biobank (UKB), representing population-based aging. Together, these cohorts encompassed a broad range of WMH burden, lesion morphology, clinical context, and 2D and 3D FLAIR acquisition protocols.

The model-development dataset comprised 460 FLAIR scans from 330 unique participants. ADNI contributed one 2D FLAIR scan from each of 100 participants spanning cognitively unimpaired, mild cognitive impairment, and dementia groups. WWISH contributed same-visit paired 2D and 3D FLAIR scans from 130 participants with clinically defined CSVD, for a total of 260 scans. UKB contributed one 3D FLAIR scan from each of 100 community-dwelling participants, generally characterized by lower WMH burden.

The dataset was partitioned using participant-level grouping, such that each participant was assigned exclusively to the training, validation, or internal test set. For WWISH participants, paired 2D and 3D FLAIR scans were assigned together to the same partition, preventing subjectlevel information leakage across partitions. The internal held-out test set comprised 92 scans from 66 unique participants: 20 ADNI participants with one 2D FLAIR scan each, 26 WWISH participants with paired 2D and 3D FLAIR scans, and 20 UKB participants with one 3D FLAIR scan each. The remaining 368 scans from 264 participants formed the model-development set used for training and validation. Within the development set, participant-level grouping was maintained throughout model fitting and validation so that both WWISH acquisitions from a given participant remained in the same subset (training or validation). External validation was performed in an independent ADNI3 test cohort of 25 nonoverlapping participants with 3D FLAIR imaging that was entirely withheld from model development and tuning.

Demographic and clinical characteristics of ADNI and WWISH participants and the distributions of WMH volume across all three development cohorts are summarized in Table 1. Linked demographic and clinical data were not available for this UKB subset. Cohort-specific inclusion criteria are described in the Supplementary Methods, and FLAIR acquisition parameters are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

WMH reference segmentation

Reference WMH masks were generated using a standardized semiautomated labeling strategy.

An existing automated WMH segmentation method (Phuah et al., 2022) was first applied to each FLAIR image to provide an initial candidate mask. A trained rater then reviewed and manually refined each mask using threshold-guided and voxel-wise editing to correct false-positive regions, recover missed lesions, and improve lesion-boundary delineation. Final masks underwent review and adjudication by an experienced neurologist. For WWISH participants, reference masks were generated separately for the 2D and 3D FLAIR acquisitions. Consequently, each acquisition was evaluated against its acquisition-specific reference segmentation.

Image preprocessing

All images underwent a standardized preprocessing pipeline to reduce nonbiological variability across cohorts and acquisition types, adapted from a previously described approach (Phuah et al., 2022). T1-weighted images were first resampled to 1-mm isotropic resolution. T1-weighted and FLAIR images were then skull-stripped using FreeSurfer SynthStrip (Hoopes et al., 2022) and corrected for intensity inhomogeneity using N4-bias field correction in ANTs (Tustison et al., 2010). Both 2D and 3D FLAIR images were registered to their corresponding resampled T1weighted images using affine registration in FSL-FLIRT (Jenkinson et al., 2002). The resulting transformation was applied to resample each FLAIR image and its WMH reference mask onto the 1-mm isotropic T1-weighted image grid. Within the T1-derived SynthStrip brain mask, T1weighted and registered FLAIR intensities were normalized by subtracting the mean and dividing by three times the standard deviation, then clipped to [-1,1]. The same workflow was applied across cohorts and acquisition types. Figure 1 summarizes the preprocessing, model development and evaluation workflow.

Model architecture and training

We implemented a single 3D nnU-Net model for WMH segmentation (Isensee et al., 2021). The network received two input channels: the preprocessed FLAIR image and the coregistered T1weighted image. nnU-Net automatically configures network architecture, patch size, batch size, and other training parameters according to the properties of the input dataset. The resulting model used an encoder-decoder architecture with skip connections, instance normalization, and leaky rectified linear unit activations.

Both native 2D and native 3D FLAIR acquisitions were represented as 3D image volumes after preprocessing and resampling, and coregistration to the T1-weighted image. The same network was therefore applied to both acquisition types; separate 2D-and 3D-specific networks were not trained. This design was intended to produce a single model deployable across the range of FLAIR protocols represented in the development data.

A central feature of the training strategy was the deliberate integration of three complementary cohorts spanning neurodegenerative disease, CSVD, and population-based aging, together with both 2D and 3D FLAIR acquisitions. This design exposed a single model to variation in clinical context, WMH burden and morphology, and FLAIR acquisition during training, rather than developing separate disease-or sequence-specific models. Training patches were sampled from the preprocessed FLAIR and T1-weighted images and corresponding reference masks and underwent the standard nnU-Net on-the-fly spatial and intensity augmentations. The model was optimized using a composite Dice and cross-entropy loss to address the substantial class imbalance between WMH and non-WMH voxels.

The model development set was randomly divided at the participant level into a training set ( 294scans from 211 participants) and a validation set (74 scans from 53 participants), with paired WWISH 2D and 3D scans from the same participant assigned to the same subset. A single model was trained on the training set, and the validation set was used to monitor training and select the final checkpoint. At inference, the model's softmax outputs was binarized using a probability threshold of 0.5. The internal test set and ADNI3 3D set remained excluded from all model fitting and model-selection procedures.

Benchmark algorithms

The proposed model was benchmarked against three contemporary automated WMH segmentation methods: SHIVA-WMH (Tsuchida et al., 2024), segcsvdWMH (Gibson et al., 2024), and FreeSurfer WMH-SynthSeg (Laso et al., 2024). These methods were selected to represent recently developed approaches intended for vascular, heterogeneous clinical, or largescale imaging applications. Each benchmark method was run using its recommended configuration and decision threshold: 0.5 for SHIVA-WMH, 0.35 for segcsvdWMH, and the discrete WMH label provided by WMH-SynthSeg. Each method received its recommended inputs: FLAIR and T1-weighted for SHIVA-WMH, and FLAIR for both segcsvdWMH and WMH-SynthSeg. Input preprocessing required by each method was applied accordingly.

Algorithm outputs were subsequently resampled and aligned to the corresponding referencemask space to permit evaluation using common voxel-and lesion-level definitions. All methods were evaluated on the same internal test and ADNI3 validation acquisitions. No benchmark method was retrained or fine-tuned using the internal test or ADNI3 data.

Segmentation performance

Segmentation performance was evaluated at the voxel, volume, and lesion levels. The Dice similarity coefficient between the predicted and reference WMH masks was the primary voxellevel metric. Volumetric performance was evaluated using signed and absolute differences between predicted and reference total WMH volumes. Agreement across the range of WMH burden was further examined using Bland-Altman analysis. For lesion-level evaluation, spatially connected WMH components were identified separately in the predicted and reference masks. A reference lesion was considered detected if it shared at least one voxel with a predicted lesion (Ganiler et al., 2014). For each scan, we calculated the proportion of reference lesions detected, lesion-level Dice, and the number of false-positive predicted lesions. Lesions were stratified by reference volume into four prespecified categories: very small, 15-30 voxels; small, 31-100 voxels; medium, 101-250 voxels; and large, > 250 voxels. Because all masks were represented at 1-mm isotropic resolution, one voxel corresponded to 1mm 3.

Performance was examined overall and within prespecified strata defined by cohort, native FLAIR acquisition type, reference WMH burden, and lesion size. WMH burden was categorized as low, < 5 mL; moderate, 5-10 mL; or high, > 10 mL. Acquisition-specific analyses included all 2D scans, all 3D scans, and cohort-by-acquisition subgroups comprising ADNI 2D, WWISH 2D, WWISH 3D, UKB 3D, and external ADNI3 3D. As secondary analysis, we assessed direct agreement between predicted WMH volumes from same-visit paired 2D and 3D FLAIR scans in 26 WWISH participants from the held-out internal test set. The same analyses were applied to the paired expert reference volumes (Supplementary Table S5).

Cognitive outcome analysis

We evaluated the downstream clinical validity of WMH volume estimates by examining their associations with incident dementia among cognitively unimpaired participants (global Clinical Dementia Rating [CDR] score = 0; Morris et al., 1997) from ADNI and the Knight Alzheimer Disease Research Center (Knight ADRC) cohort. Participants were eligible if they had a baseline FLAIR scan and a CDR assessment within 180 days of imaging. Incident dementia was defined as progression to a global CDR of ≥1 during follow-up. Both cohorts included baseline 2D and 3D FLAIR scans; ADNI training participants were excluded to prevent participant-level overlap.

For each segmentation method, total WMH volume was calculated as the number of WMHlabelled voxels multiplied by voxel volume. Separate logistic regression models evaluated whether WMH volume derived from each algorithm provided incremental information about incident dementia beyond age, sex, years of education, APOE 𝜀4 carrier status, and cohort. A clinical-only model containing these covariates served as the reference; each algorithm-specific model additionally included standardized WMH volume. Associations were summarized as odds ratios per 1-SD increase in WMH volume, with 95% confidence intervals, and Wald p-values.

Incremental model fit was assessed using likelihood-ratio tests comparing each WMHaugmented model with the clinical-only model. Because the sample included both 2D and 3D FLAIR, and methods differed in acquisition-dependent volume offsets, a sensitivity analysis additionally adjusted both the clinical and WMH-augmented models for FLAIR acquisition type. Discrimination was assessed using 10-fold cross-validated area under the receiver operating characteristic (AUC) curve. The same 10-fold splits were used for all algorithm-specific models, ensuring that cross-validated AUCs were evaluated in identical held-out participants. Confidence intervals were estimated by bootstrap resampling. Permutation testing with randomly permuted outcome labels was used to assess the significance of ΔAUC relative to the clinical model.

Statistical analysis

Segmentation metrics were summarized at the scan level. Differences among segmentation methods were evaluated using the Friedman test, followed by paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests comparing the proposed model with each benchmark method. These analyses were conducted in the overall internal test set and within prespecified acquisition, cohort-by-acquisition, WMH burden, and lesion size strata. For each metric, Wilcoxon p-values were adjusted jointly across all strata and all three comparator comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery-rate (FDR) procedure. The overall internal test set included paired 2D and 3D acquisitions from 26 WWISH participants and therefore contained repeated observations from these participants.

Performance was consequently also evaluated separately by acquisition and cohort-byacquisition stratum, within which each participant contributed one scan. The pooled scan-level analyses did not explicitly account for within-participant correlation between paired WWISH acquisitions; the acquisition-specific analyses were therefore used to assess the robustness of the pooled findings. Cross-acquisition volumetric agreement was assessed using absolute-agreement intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) with 95% confidence intervals and Bland-Altman analysis. Paired differences were defined as predicted 3D minus 2D WMH volume; mean bias and 95% limits of agreement (mean bias ±1.96 SD of the paired differences) were reported. All analyses were performed in Python version 3.13.5 using NumPy version 2.1.3; Pandas version 2.2.3; SciPy version 1.15.3; statsmodels version 0.14.4.

RESULTS

Cohort and acquisition characteristics

By design, the development cohorts differed in WMH burden, vascular risk profile, and FLAIR acquisition (Table 1). Mean reference WMH volume was lowest in UKB (3.6 mL; 3D FLAIR only) and higher in ADNI (15.5 mL; 2D FLAIR only) and WWISH, which also had the highest prevalence of hypertension (75.4%), diabetes (27.7%), and prior stroke or TIA (41.5%). In WWISH, the same 130 participants had a mean reference volume of 16.1 mL on 2D FLAIR and 11.2 mL on 3D FLAIR, illustrating that measured WMH volume depends on acquisition even when masks are expert-refined. Characteristics of the incident dementia analysis sample are summarized in Table 3.

Internal performance and benchmarking

In the internal held-out test set (92 scans from 66 participants), the proposed model achieved a mean Dice of 0.73 (SD 0.10; median 0.72, IQR 0.67-0.81). Mean Dice was 0.50 for SHIVA-WMH, 0.57 for segcsvdWMH, and 0.29 for WMH-SynthSeg (Friedman p < 0.001; FDR-adjusted Wilcoxon p ≤ 5.3 × 10 -16 for each comparison with the proposed model; Table 2). The proposed model also had the smallest volumetric error (median absolute difference 0.96 mL [IQR 0.36-2.12] vs. 2.17-3.36 mL for comparators) and the smallest mean bias (-0.86 mL [SD 3.11] vs. -1.83 to -3.90 mL; Supplementary Table S2). Bland-Altman analysis showed narrower limits of agreement for the proposed model than for the comparators, with modest underestimation in a small number of high-burden 2D scans (Supplementary Figure S1).

Segmentation performance across FLAIR acquisitions and cohorts

The proposed model achieved similar segmentation accuracy across acquisition types, with mean Dice of 0.75 (SD 0.09) on 2D FLAIR and 0.71 (SD 0.12) on 3D FLAIR (Table 2; Figure 2D).

The paired WWISH acquisitions provided a within-participant assessment of acquisition sensitivity. In the same 26 participants, the proposed model had a mean Dice of 0.76 on 2D and 0.74 on 3D FLAIR, with mean volume bias relative to acquisition-specific reference masks was -1.64 mL and +0.13 mL, respectively (Supplementary Table S2). Comparator biases varied more between acquisitions. segcsvdWMH underestimated volume more on 2D than on 3D FLAIR (-5.73 vs. -1.06 mL); WMH-SynthSeg substantially underestimated volume on 2D but showed near-zero bias on 3D FLAIR (-9.81 vs. +0.11 mL); and SHIVA-WMH shifted from underestimation on 2D to overestimation on 3D FLAIR (-3.58 vs. +2.46 mL). Thus, the between-acquisition difference in mean bias was 1.77 mL for the proposed model, compared with 4.67-9.92 mL for the comparators. A similar pattern was observed across the full internal test set (Supplementary Table S2).

In a secondary analysis, predicted WMH volumes were compared directly between paired 2D and 3D FLAIR scans, independently of the reference masks. The proposed model yielded lower volumes on 3D than on 2D FLAIR (mean difference, -4.57 mL), whereas SHIVA-WMH showed a smaller mean difference (-0.30 mL). Complete agreement statistics, including a separate analysis of paired expert reference volumes, are reported in Supplementary Table S5.

Across cohorts, mean Dice for the proposed model was 0.73 in ADNI 2D, 0.76 in WWISH 2D, 0.74 in WWISH 3D, and 0.67 in UKB 3D, and it exceeded each comparator in every cohort-byacquisition stratum (FDR-adjusted p ≤ 2.7 × 10 -5 ; Table 2; Supplementary Figure S2). The lower Dice in UKB is consistent with its low lesion burden, because small absolute errors weigh more heavily on Dice when total WMH volume is small; volumetric error in UKB was the smallest of any cohort (median absolute difference 0.35 mL; Supplementary Table S2).

Performance in low-burden scans and small lesions

Advantages were largest at the low end of the WMH spectrum. Across fixed reference-volume categories, the proposed model's mean Dice was 0.66 in low-burden (<5 mL), 0.73 in moderateburden (5-10 mL), and 0.80 in high-burden (>10 mL) scans (Figure 2A; Supplementary Table S3). In low-burden scans, comparator Dice ranged from 0.19 (WMH-SynthSeg) to 0.54 (segcsvdWMH), and the proposed model exceeded each comparator in all three burden categories (FDR-adjusted p < 0.001).

At the lesion level, the proposed model detected 77% of very small (15-30 voxels) and 87% of small (31-100 voxels) reference lesions, compared with 61-63% for SHIVA-WMH, 44-62% for segcsvdWMH, and 14-20% for WMH-SynthSeg (Figure 2B; Supplementary Table S4).

Lesion-wise Dice was also higher in every size category (FDR-adjusted p < 0.001; Figure 2C).

Higher sensitivity was not achieved by over-segmentation. The proposed model produced fewer false-positive lesions per scan than SHIVA-WMH in every size category (e.g., 0.71 vs. 1.49 very small lesions). segcsvdWMH produced slightly fewer very small false-positive lesions (0.32 per scan), but missed 2.7 times as many very small reference lesions (7.07 vs. 2.62 false negatives per scan). Gains extended to medium lesions (101-250 voxels), for which detection was 94% for the proposed model versus 33-63% for comparators, and to large lesions (>250 voxels; 99% vs.

79-88%).

The small-lesion advantage was largest on 2D FLAIR (Supplementary Figure S2B). On 3D FLAIR, SHIVA-WMH, which was developed with isotropic 3D FLAIR to detect punctate lesions, achieved very-small-lesion Dice comparable to that of the proposed model (0.58 vs. 0.64; FDR-adjusted p = 0.66), but with approximately seven times as many very small falsepositive lesions per scan (1.98 vs. 0.28; Supplementary Table S4).

External validation in ADNI3

In the independent ADNI3 3D FLAIR set (n = 25), the proposed model achieved a mean Dice of 0.70 (SD 0.14; median 0.75, IQR 0.58-0.80) without retraining or threshold adjustment, comparable to its internal 3D FLAIR performance (Table 2; Figure 3A). Mean Dice was 0.60 for SHIVA-WMH, 0.68 for segcsvdWMH, and 0.42 for WMH-SynthSeg, and the proposed model exceeded each comparator (FDR-adjusted p = 3.2 × 10 -5 , 1.6 × 10 -3 , and 2.7 × 10 -7 , respectively). segcsvdWMH was the closest competitor in this set (median absolute difference 1.83 vs. 1.59 mL for the proposed model; Supplementary Table S2), whereas the proposed model modestly overestimated volume (mean bias +1.49 mL). In low-burden ADNI3 scans (<5 mL; n = 10), Dice was similar for the proposed model and segcsvdWMH (0.56 vs. 0.57; FDRadjusted p = 0.49) and higher than for WMH-SynthSeg (0.32; p = 0.009; Supplementary Table S3). The proposed model detected 86% of very small and small lesions and 99-100% of medium and large lesions (Figure 3B; Supplementary Table S4). Detection advantages were most consistent relative to WMH-SynthSeg; SHIVA-WMH and segcsvdWMH detected small lesions at similar rates in this 3D set, although SHIVA-WMH generated more very small false-positive lesions (5.04 vs. 0.84 per scan). Representative segmentations across the range of WMH burden are shown in Figure 3C.

WMH volume and incident dementia

Among 1,174 cognitively unimpaired participants from ADNI and the Knight ADRC, 42 progressed to dementia over a median follow-up of 5.9 years (Table 3). After adjustment for age, sex, education, APOE ε4 carrier status, and cohort, higher WMH volume from the proposed model was associated with incident dementia (OR per SD of WMH volume 1.40, 95% CI 1.11-1.77) and improved model fit over the clinical model (likelihood-ratio χ2 = 6.90, p = 0.009; Table 3; Figure 4). Cross-validated AUC increased from 0.683 for the clinical model to 0.712 (95% CI 0.62-0.79; ΔAUC = 0.029, permutation p < 0.001). WMH volumes from WMH-SynthSeg (OR 1.30, 95% CI 1.02-1.67) and SHIVA-WMH (OR 1.27, 95% CI 1.01-1.59) showed weaker associations, and neither significantly improved model fit for incidence dementia beyond clinical covariates (likelihood-ratio p = 0.052 and 0.059; ΔAUC = 0.015 and 0.011). segcsvdWMH-derived volume was not associated with incident dementia (OR 1.07, 95% CI 0.81-1.41; likelihood-ratio p = 0.65; ΔAUC = -0.005). After additional adjustment for FLAIR acquisition type, WMH volume from the proposed model remained associated with incident dementia (OR per SD 1.34, 95% CI 1.05-1.70; likelihood-ratio p = 0.027; Supplemental Table S6).

DISCUSSION

We developed a single nnU-Net WMH segmentation model trained across neurodegenerative, CSVD-enriched, and population-based cohorts and across conventional 2D and isotropic 3D FLAIR. Four findings support its use as a common segmentation approach in multi-cohort studies. First, the model outperformed three contemporary tools at the voxel, volume, and lesion levels in a held-out internal test set. Second, accuracy was similar on 2D and 3D FLAIR, and volume bias relative to acquisition-specific references varied less between paired acquisitions than comparator biases. Third, its advantage was largest where WMH measurement is most difficult: in low-burden scans and for small lesions. Fourth, performance transferred to an unseen ADNI3 3D FLAIR protocol, and model-derived volumes added information about incident dementia beyond clinical covariates. The principal contribution is therefore not the nnU-Net architecture itself, but the development and validation of a single model across heterogeneous disease contexts and acquisitions, together with evidence that algorithm-dependent differences in WMH quantification can affect downstream clinical inference.

Acquisition-related segmentation bias is an important consideration in multi-cohort studies.

Differences in error between 2D and 3D FLAIR can complicate comparisons when acquisition type varies by cohort, site, or study phase. The same-visit paired WWISH scans allowed us to assess these differences within participants. The proposed model's mean volume bias relative to acquisition-specific references differed by less than 2 mL between acquisitions, compared with approximately 5-10 mL for the comparators; SHIVA-WMH also showed reversals in bias direction. These findings are consistent with Hotz et al. (2022), who reported different algorithm biases on 2D and 3D FLAIR relative to acquisition-specific manual references. Expert reference volumes themselves were, on average, 6.3 mL larger on 2D than on 3D FLAIR, and the proposed model's volumes differed in the same direction. Thus, similar accuracy against acquisitionspecific references does not establish interchangeability of 2D-and 3D-derived volumes.

The largest gains were in low-burden scans and small lesions, where existing tools differed most from the reference. This range matters because many population-based and preclinical samples, such as UKB, have total WMH volumes below 5 mL, where measurement error is large relative to between-person variation. The proposed model's higher sensitivity did not come at the cost of more false-positive lesions than SHIVA-WMH, and the low false-positive count of segcsvdWMH reflected conservative segmentation that missed many small lesions. SHIVA-WMH, which was developed with isotropic 3D FLAIR to detect punctate lesions (Tsuchida et al., 2024), matched the proposed model's very-small-lesion overlap on 3D FLAIR but not on 2D FLAIR, consistent with the importance of training-data acquisition for small-lesion detection.

More reliable detection at low burden may increase the usefulness of WMH as an early marker in cognitively unimpaired and population samples, although this requires direct testing in longitudinal data.

These comparisons should be interpreted in light of each comparator's design. segcsvdWMH was trained on heterogeneous 2D and 3D FLAIR from CSVD-related clinical cohorts (Gibson et al., 2024); SHIVA-WMH was trained on isotropic 3D FLAIR from young adults combined with the MICCAI WMH Segmentation Challenge data (Tsuchida et al., 2024); and WMH-SynthSeg was designed to be contrast-and resolution-agnostic across clinical and low-field MRI rather than to maximize accuracy on research-grade FLAIR (Laso et al., 2024). Input requirements also differed: our proposed model and SHIVA-WMH used both FLAIR and T1-weighted images, whereas segcsvdWMH and WMH-SynthSeg were run using FLAIR images alone. Comparator Dice values in our data were lower than those reported in their development studies. This likely reflects, in part, differences between our reference labeling convention and theirs, in addition to differences in populations and protocols. Because the proposed model was trained on masks generated with the same convention used for evaluation, voxel-level benchmarking alone may favor it. The incident dementia analysis does not depend on reference masks and therefore provides a complementary, label-independent comparison. The proposed model also exceeded segcsvdWMH within WWISH on both 2D and 3D FLAIR, although the margin was smaller on 3D (0.74 vs. 0.66), and segcsvdWMH was the closest competitor in ADNI3, with similar volumetric error to the proposed model.

Algorithm choice also influenced downstream clinical inference. All four methods were applied to the same participants and evaluated using the same clinical covariates, yet only WMH volume from the proposed model significantly improved model fit for incident dementia. Volumes from SHIVA-WMH and WMH-SynthSeg showed weaker associations, and segcsvdWMH-derived volume showed no association. These results extend prior evidence that WMH-cognition associations depend on the segmentation algorithm (Tubi et al., 2020). However, absolute AUC gains were modest. These findings provide complementary evidence of the clinical relevance of the proposed model's WMH estimates; they do not establish a validated clinical prediction tool.

Its stronger performance in low-burden scans and small lesions may contribute to the observed associations, although this explanation requires direct testing. The dementia analysis combined 2D and 3D FLAIR scans from ADNI and Knight ADRC; additional adjustment for acquisition type did not change the association for the proposed model.

For multi-cohort studies, these results support applying a single segmentation model across the evaluated disease contexts and acquisitions, reducing the need to switch algorithms between cohorts. This removes algorithm choice as one source of methodological variation, but does not eliminate acquisition-related differences in WMH volume. Acquisition type should therefore remain part of the analytical design, and comparable segmentation accuracy should not be taken to imply that unadjusted 2D-and 3D-derived volumes can be pooled interchangeably. Future work could evaluate whether incorporating paired acquisitions during training improves crossacquisition volume consistency while preserving segmentation accuracy.

This study has several strengths, including deliberately heterogeneous training data, same-visit paired 2D and 3D acquisitions, head-to-head benchmarking at the voxel, volume, and lesion levels, evaluation in an unseen acquisition protocol, and a label-independent clinical validation.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, although the cohorts were diverse, they do not capture the full range of scanners, field strengths, acquisition protocols, and populations encountered in practice. Second, reference masks were initialized using an automated method and refined by a single rater with neurologist adjudication. Inter-rater Dice agreement, previously assessed in a subset, was 0.72 (95% CI, 0.67-0.81; Phuah et al., 2022), comparable to the model's mean Dice in the internal test set (0.73), suggesting that model-reference agreement approached the level of agreement between human raters. However, reliability was not reassessed here, and systematic labeling conventions may be shared by training and evaluation.

Third, the external ADNI3 test set included distinct participants and acquisition protocol but originated from the same consortium as part of the development data, and its size was modest (n = 25), although comparable to or larger than the labeled external test sets used to validate SHIVA-WMH (n = 11) and segcsvdWMH (n = 10); fully independent validation in larger cohorts remains necessary. Fourth, paired WWISH acquisitions contributed correlated observations to pooled analyses, although results were consistent in acquisition-specific strata in which each participant contributed one scan. Fifth, reference masks were defined separately for each acquisition, allowing assessment of segmentation accuracy but not determination of which acquisition more accurately measured the underlying lesion burden. Direct cross-acquisition agreement was also assessed in only 26 participants from a single cohort. Sixth, the model requires both FLAIR and a coregistered T1-weighted image, which may limit its use in datasets without T1-weighted imaging or with poor FLAIR-T1 registration. It was also evaluated crosssectionally; its accuracy for measuring longitudinal change and its regional performance remain to be established. Finally, clinical validation was limited to progression on the global CDR in a modest sample.

Pooled and longitudinal WMH studies combine cohorts that differ in disease composition and FLAIR protocol, creating the potential for acquisition-related measurement differences to be mistaken for biological differences between cohorts, sites, or study phases. Our findings show that algorithm choice matters: segmentation methods differed in acquisition-dependent bias and in the clinical information their WMH estimates provided. A single model trained across disease contexts and both 2D and 3D FLAIR achieved similar segmentation accuracy on both acquisitions and more stable volume bias relative to acquisition-specific references than the comparator tools. These results support using a common segmentation approach across heterogeneous cohorts, reducing the need to exclude scans or switch algorithms solely because of acquisition type. Public release of the model will facilitate its use in multi-cohort aging, vascular, and neurodegenerative studies, including studies that span protocol transitions.
