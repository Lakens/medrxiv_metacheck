Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Full text

Abstract

Hip fractures are among the most serious consequences of osteoporosis, yet many individuals who sustain hip fractures do not meet the diagnostic threshold based on areal bone mineral density (aBMD). Finite element (FE)-based mechanical descriptors may improve hip fracture prediction beyond aBMD.

This study investigated whether FE-based mechanical descriptors derived from dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) improve hip fracture discrimination beyond aBMD in the Japanese Population-Based Osteoporosis (JPOS) cohort.

The study included 1,636 women, of whom 85 sustained hip fractures during 20 years of follow-up. Subject-specific three-dimensional femoral models were reconstructed from DXA images using statistical shape and appearance modelling, and nonlinear FE analyses were performed to derive mechanical descriptors under simulated sideways-fall loading. Predictive performance was evaluated using discrimination, model fit, reclassification, and hazard ratio analyses.

Over two-thirds of women who experienced hip fracture were not classified as osteoporotic based on aBMD. Several FE-based descriptors significantly improved fracture prediction relative to total hip and femoral neck aBMD across the evaluation frameworks. The best-performing descriptor, combining femoral strength with residual energy (𝐹 𝑈 + 𝑊 𝑅 ), achieved a significantly higher AUROC of 0.797 compared with 0.763 (p = 0.026) and 0.757 (p = 0.040) for total hip and femoral neck aBMD, respectively, and the highest hazard ratio (3.577 per SD). The same descriptors consistently performed best across all evaluation methods.

These findings demonstrate that FE-based descriptors provide meaningful information beyond aBMD and may improve identification of women at elevated hip fracture risk.

Introduction

Osteoporosis is a chronic condition characterized by low bone mass and reduced bone microstructural integrity, increasing skeletal fragility and fracture risk with aging [1], with hip fractures imposing the greatest socioeconomic burden among fragility fractures [2]. Fracture prevention requires identifying individuals at high risk, typically through areal bone mineral density (aBMD) measured by dualenergy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), with T-scores indicating deviations from healthy young adults. However, more than 50% of incident hip fractures occur in women without osteoporosis (aBMD Tscore > -2.5) [3]. Consequently, reliance on aBMD alone for fracture risk prediction does not adequately identify individuals at increased risk, as the mechanical competence of the proximal femur depends not only on bone mineral density but also on a broader range of organ-and subject-specific characteristics. Hip fracture risk is associated with reduced femoral strength, influenced by aging, aBMD, bone geometry, cortical and trabecular microarchitecture and anisotropy, and the magnitude and direction of mechanical loading. Accordingly, image-based, subject-specific finite element models (FEM) have been used to estimate femoral strength and assess fracture resistance [4] [5] [6]. While initial studies reported no added predictive value of femoral strength over aBMD [7, 8], more recent studies have demonstrated the superiority of FEM-derived measures [5, 9]. However, most of these approaches rely on quantitative computed tomography (QCT) [4, 5], which involves higher radiation exposure and costs, limiting its routine use in osteoporosis screening and longitudinal fracture risk assessment [10]. DXA is routinely used for osteoporosis screening due to its low radiation exposure and cost. Several studies have applied DXA-based 2D FEM to predict femoral strength and fracture risk in clinical cohorts [11] [12] [13]. Although these models capture aspects of femoral mechanical behavior [11], their performance in osteoporosis-related fracture classification remains limited [13]. A key limitation is the lack of volumetric information, preventing accurate representation of out-of-plane variations in femoral geometry and bone mineral density. The field subsequently explored DXA-based 2D-to-3D reconstruction techniques for developing 3D FEM [14, 15]. Grassi et al. used statistical shape and appearance modeling (SSAM) to reconstruct subject-specific 3D femoral anatomy from DXA images [16] and perform FE simulations [6, 17]. Using this approach, studies in the MrOS male cohort [6] and OSTPRE female cohort [17] demonstrated that FEM-derived bone strength, normalized to body weight and evaluated across 10 side-fall loading configurations, significantly improved hip fracture discrimination compared with TH aBMD and FN aBMD, respectively, adjusted for BMI. Despite these improvements, clinical implementation remains limited by computational costs [5], the need for greater automation [18], and the absence of regulatory approval [6]. Furthermore, most approaches rely primarily on bone strength, typically defined as the maximum reaction force, or for Grassi's model [6], evaluated under an external load model for side-fall. Incorporating additional FE-based descriptors of deformability and mechanical energy absorption could provide complementary information on bone fragility and improve fracture discrimination [19]. The 3D-Shaper® software (v2.14, 3D-Shaper Medical, Barcelona, Spain) uses a computationally efficient SSAM-based approach to reconstruct subject-specific 3D femoral geometry and volumetric bone mineral density distributions from DXA images [14]. The software is commercially available and has received regulatory clearance in several territories, including the European Union, United States, Japan, and Brazil. A 3D-DXA-based FE pipeline incorporating 3D-Shaper was developed to estimate femoral mechanical response under subject-specific loading [20], with femoral strength estimates evaluated against QCT-based models [20, 21]. Retrospective case-control studies in two Spanish cohorts demonstrated that FE-based mechanical descriptors provide complementary information to BMD for hip fracture discrimination [19, 22]. More recently, a prospective study in the Geneva Retirees Cohort [23] reported comparable associations of femoral strength and TH aBMD with major osteoporotic fractures (MOF), while a strength threshold of 2600 N improved fracture risk classification compared with TH aBMD-based thresholds. Notably, 27% of women with MOF who were not classified as osteoporotic by aBMD exhibited reduced femoral strength, supporting its complementary value in fracture risk assessment. To date, the Geneva Retirees Cohort is the only prospective study [23] evaluating the aforementioned 3D-DXA-based FEA [20], highlighting the need for further validation in larger and more diverse populations with more hip fracture events. Accordingly, the present study applies our previously developed nonlinear 3D-DXA-based FEM [20] to the prospective, population-based Japanese Population-Based Osteoporosis (JPOS) cohort to evaluate the predictive value of FE-based mechanical descriptors for hip fracture risk. These descriptors, introduced in our previous work [19], integrate femoral geometry, bone density distribution, mechanical properties, and tissue-dependent structural characteristics, including measures of accumulated plastic strain up to femoral strength. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate whether FE-based mechanical descriptors derived from standard hip DXA scans outperform aBMD for hip fracture prediction in a prospective, population-based cohort of women.

Materials and Methods

Study cohort and DXA data

The study population was derived from the Japanese Population-Based Osteoporosis (JPOS) study, as previously described [24]. Briefly, women aged 15-79 years were randomly sampled in 5-year age strata from seven regions across Japan, five of which were included in the JPOS Cohort Study. The cohort comprised 1,636 women, including 85 who subsequently sustained a hip fracture. Participants completed the baseline assessment and were followed for up to 20 years through up to five surveys.

2D-to-3D reconstruction

3D-Shaper software was used to reconstruct subject-specific 3D models of the proximal femur from standard hip DXA scans [14]. The method uses an SSAM derived from a QCT database, with femoral shape and volumetric density variability represented by principal component analysis. Through iterative 3D-2D registration, shape, density, and pose parameters are optimized to match the modelderived projection to the DXA image, reconstructing both cortical and trabecular compartments. The resulting measurements include integral vBMD (Int.vBMD, mg/cm³): mean vBMD across cortical and trabecular compartments in the total hip (TH) region; trabecular vBMD (Trab.vBMD, mg/cm³): mean vBMD within the TH trabecular compartment; and cortical surface BMD (Cort.sBMD, mg/cm²): calculated as cortical vBMD multiplied by cortical thickness and averaged over the TH region. These measurements have been validated against QCT [14, 21, 25].

Finite element modeling

The previously developed 3D-DXA-based FE framework [19, 20] was used to simulate femoral mechanical behavior under sideways fall loading. Subject-specific FE models were generated by morphing a generic mesh of linear eight-node hexahedral elements to the 3D-Shaper-reconstructed femoral geometry using thin-plate spline (TPS) morphing, preserving cortical and trabecular compartments. The reconstructed bone density distribution was mapped onto the mesh by linear interpolation. Compartment-specific material properties were assigned using established densityelasticity relationships [19], converting 3D-DXA-derived vBMD to ash density and then to apparent density to calculate Young's modulus. A constant Poisson's ratio of 0.3 was assumed. Nonlinear behavior was modeled using an elastic-perfectly plastic constitutive model with density-dependent, tension-and compression-specific yield properties for cortical and trabecular bone [19]. Boundary conditions reproduced an experimental lateral fall configuration [11], and widely adopted in FE-based femoral strength analyses [6], were applied in the FE simulations. The distal shaft was fully constrained, while the greater trochanter was constrained only along the fall direction. Displacement was applied to the femoral head through rigid-body constraints until the reaction force decreased with increasing displacement, indicating a surrogate marker of mechanical failure. All models were simulated with 10° adduction and 15° internal rotation to represent a typical sideways fall in the available experimental setups [26]. The FE pipeline is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Mechanical descriptors

The detailed methodology, mechanical definitions, and clinical relevance of the FE-based descriptors have been described in our previous study [19]. Mechanical descriptors were extracted from the nonlinear FE force-displacement response to characterize femoral strength, deformability, and energy absorption (Supplementary Fig. A1). Primary nonlinear descriptors included strength (𝐹 𝑈 ) (maximum reaction force), displacement at failure (𝐷 𝑈 ), and nonlinear energy to failure (𝑊 𝑈 ) [19]. An optimized piecewise-linear analysis [19] was applied to derive elastic descriptors (linear elastic force ( 𝐹 𝐿𝐸 ), linear elastic displacement ( 𝐷 𝐿𝐸 ), and linear elastic energy ( 𝑊 𝐿𝐸 )) and post-yield nonlinear deformation ( 𝐷 1 ). A loading-unloading analysis was additionally performed on the nonlinear FE models to quantify the mechanical energy dissipated through accumulated plastic deformation, from which three key descriptors were derived: residual displacement (𝐷 𝑅 ), dissipated energy (𝑊 𝐷 ), and residual (elastic) energy (𝑊 𝑅 ). To assess the complementary contributions of deformation and energy absorption to fracture discrimination, strength ( 𝐹 𝑈 ) and linear elastic force ( 𝐹 𝐿𝐸 ) were separately combined with deformation-and energy-based descriptors. All individual and combined descriptors (Supplementary Table B1) were evaluated for hip fracture discrimination in the full cohort.

Statistical analysis

Detailed statistical methods are provided in Supplementary C and are briefly summarized here. The predictive performance of the 3D-DXA-based FE mechanical descriptors (Supplementary Table B1) was evaluated through group comparisons, fracture discrimination, and survival analyses (Supplementary Fig. C1). Differences between fracture and control subjects were assessed using unpaired t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests, according to data distribution. Fracture discrimination was evaluated using logistic regression and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC). Multivariable models combining mechanical descriptors were assessed using leave-oneout cross-validation, and AUROC differences were compared using the DeLong test. Cox proportional hazards models were used to incorporate time-to-fracture information and censoring. Model discrimination was assessed using Harrell's C-index [27], with paired differences (ΔC) evaluated against reference predictors using two-sided nonparametric bootstrap resampling. Model fit was compared using Akaike's Information Criterion (AIC) and differences in AIC (ΔAIC) [28]. The additional predictive value of FE-based descriptors beyond TH or FN aBMD was assessed using categorical net reclassification improvement (NRI), integrated discrimination improvement (IDI), and continuous NRI (cNRI) [29, 30]. Risk categories were established using NHANES III reference data [31], and reclassification analyses were performed over the full available follow-up period. Associations with hip fracture risk were quantified using hazard ratios (HRs) from ridge-penalized Cox models [32]. Predictors were standardized, and HRs were reported per one standard deviation (SD) change. The ridge penalty parameter (λ) was optimized using 10-fold cross-validation, with the largest optimal λ applied across all models. Nonparametric bootstrap resampling was used to estimate 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and p-values for reclassification and HR analyses. All analyses were performed using MATLAB R2021a (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA).

Results

In this study, 67.06% (57 subjects) of women with hip fracture were classified as non-osteoporotic based on TH aBMD, whereas only 29.41% (25 subjects) were classified as osteoporotic using FN aBMD. Baseline characteristics and mechanical descriptors of the study cohort, stratified by hip fracture status, are presented in Table 1. Participants with hip fractures were significantly older and shorter than controls, with no significant differences in weight or BMI. TH and FN aBMD, all 3D-DXAderived parameters, and FE-based descriptors differed significantly between groups (p < 0.001). Controls exhibited higher force-and energy-related descriptors (𝐹 𝑈 , 𝑊 𝑈 , 𝐹 𝐿𝐸 , 𝑊 𝐿𝐸 , 𝑊 𝐷 , and 𝑊 𝑅 ) and lower deformation-related descriptors (𝐷 𝑈 , 𝐷 𝐿𝐸 , 𝐷 1 , and 𝐷 𝑅 ), indicating greater load-bearing capacity and energy absorption with reduced deformation. AUROC, Harrell's C-index, and AIC were evaluated for all FE-based descriptors (Supplementary Table B1), with complete results provided in Supplementary Table D1.

Table 2 summarizes the selected FE-based descriptors showing significant improvements in discrimination or ΔAIC ≥ 10 relative to TH or FN aBMD, together with the results for aBMD and 3D-DXA-derived parameters.TH and FN aBMD showed similar AUROC values (0.763 and 0.757, respectively). Among 3D-DXAderived parameters, Trab.vBMD achieved the highest AUROC (0.784), although none significantly outperformed aBMD. In contrast, 𝐹 𝑈 + 𝑊 𝑅 (AUROC = 0.797) and 𝐹 𝐿𝐸 + 𝑊 𝐿𝐸 (AUROC = 0.794) significantly outperformed both TH and FN aBMD (all P < 0.05), while 𝐹 𝐿𝐸 + 𝐷 𝐿𝐸 and 𝐹 𝐿𝐸 + 𝑊 𝑅 showed significant improvements over TH aBMD only. Harrell's C-index showed consistent findings. 𝐹 𝑈 + 𝑊 𝑅 (0.818), 𝐹 𝐿𝐸 + 𝑊 𝐿𝐸 (0.815), and 𝐹 𝐿𝐸 + 𝑊 𝑅 (0.817) significantly outperformed both TH and FN aBMD (0.788 and 0.782, respectively; all P < 0.05). 𝐹 𝐿𝐸 + 𝐷 𝐿𝐸 also improved discrimination relative to TH aBMD (P = 0.022). AIC further supported improved model fit for these four FE-based combinations (1106.013-1114.183 vs. 1133.778 for TH aBMD; ΔAIC > 10), with similarly favorable AIC values for Trab.vBMD and Int.vBMD. Net reclassification improvement (NRI) was evaluated for 3D-DXA-derived parameters and FEbased descriptors using TH and FN aBMD as reference models (Table 3), with complete results provided in Supplementary Table D2.

Using FN aBMD as the reference model, several parameters demonstrated significant improvements in risk reclassification. Among the 3D-DXA-based measurements, only Trab.vBMD showed a statistically significant improvement versus FN aBMD (NRI = 0.159; 95% CI, 0.024-0.311; P = 0.022), whereas Int.vBMD and Cort.sBMD were not statistically significant. Notably, multiple FE-based descriptors yielded significant improvements in reclassification performance relative to FN aBMD. In particular, 𝐹 𝑈 + 𝑊 𝑅 showed the highest NRI (0.202; 95% CI, 0.075-0.333; P < 0.001), followed by 𝐹 𝐿𝐸 + 𝐷 𝐿𝐸 (P = 0.002), 𝐹 𝐿𝐸 + 𝑊 𝐿𝐸 (P = 0.001), and 𝐹 𝐿𝐸 + 𝑊 𝑅 (P = 0.002), indicating improved classification of individuals into appropriate risk categories when combined with FN aBMD.

In comparison, when TH aBMD was used as the reference model, improvements were more moderate. FE-based descriptors such as 𝐹 𝑈 + 𝑊 𝑅 (P = 0.053) and 𝐹 𝐿𝐸 + 𝑊 𝐿𝐸 (P = 0.060) showed positive NRI values with borderline statistical significance, suggesting a consistent trend toward improved risk classification relative to TH aBMD. To complement the NRI analysis (Table 3), IDI and cNRI were evaluated to further assess predictive performance and risk reclassification beyond aBMD, without reliance on predefined risk categories. Complete results are provided in Supplementary Table D3. FE-based combined descriptors consistently showed positive IDI and cNRI values, with significant improvements relative to both TH and FN aBMD. Among these, F U + W R demonstrated the highest IDI (0.036 and 0.042) and cNRI (0.570 and 0.459) relative to TH and FN aBMD, respectively (all P < 0.001). Similar improvements were observed for the other selected FE-based combinations, supporting their added predictive value beyond aBMD. HRs for the selected FE-based descriptors (Table 2) are presented in Table 4, with complete results in Supplementary Table D4. TH and FN aBMD were significantly associated with hip fracture risk (HR = 2.696 and 2.746, respectively; both P < 0.001). All 3D-DXA-derived parameters also showed significant associations, with Trab.vBMD yielding the highest HR (3.268; 95% CI: 2.702-4.035). Among FE-based descriptors, 𝐹 𝑈 + 𝑊 𝑅 (HR = 3.577; 95% CI: 2.821-4.597) and 𝐹 𝐿𝐸 + 𝑊 𝑅 (HR = 3.541; 95% CI: 2.797-4.515) showed the highest HRs, followed by 𝐹 𝐿𝐸 + 𝑊 𝐿𝐸 (HR = 3.330; 95% CI: 2.623-4.281; all P < 0.001). For these FE-based descriptors, the 95% confidence intervals were shifted toward higher HR ranges compared with both TH aBMD and FN aBMD, while still overlapping with those of aBMD, further supporting their stronger association with fracture risk. The ROC curves for the FE-based descriptors (𝐹 𝑈 + 𝑊 𝑅 and 𝐹 𝐿𝐸 + 𝑊 𝐿𝐸 ), which showed the highest AUROC values with statistically significant improvement compared with both TH aBMD and FN aBMD, are presented in Fig. 2.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate that global FE-based mechanical descriptors derived from standard hip DXA scans outperform aBMD in hip fracture prediction within a prospective, population-based cohort of women. Notably, TH and FN aBMD already showed relatively high discrimination (AUROC = 0.763 and 0.757, respectively) compared with previous studies [6, 17, 19], making further improvements more challenging. Nevertheless, more than twothirds of women who experienced hip fractures were not classified as osteoporotic based on TH aBMD, with only a minority meeting the FN aBMD osteoporosis threshold. These findings highlight the limitations of relying on aBMD alone and suggest that FE-based mechanical descriptors may help identify women with mechanically vulnerable proximal femurs despite non-osteoporotic aBMD values. FE-based descriptors showed consistent improvements across complementary statistical analyses, including discrimination (AUROC and Harrell's C-index), model fit (AIC), reclassification (NRI, IDI, and cNRI), and risk association (HR). In particular, the combined descriptors 𝐹 𝑈 + 𝑊 𝑅 , 𝐹 𝐿𝐸 + 𝐷 𝐿𝐸 , 𝐹 𝐿𝐸 + 𝑊 𝐿𝐸 , and 𝐹 𝐿𝐸 + 𝑊 𝑅 demonstrated improved performance relative to aBMD across several metrics (Tables 2 3 4), whereas 3D-DXA-derived parameters generally showed comparable discrimination to aBMD. By combining strength with deformation and energy-related measures, these FE-based descriptors characterize complementary aspects of femoral mechanical behavior under subject-specific 3D geometry, density distribution, and simulated side-fall loading that are not captured by aBMD alone. Previous QCT-based FE studies by Orwoll et al. [7], Amin et al. [8], and Kopperdahl et al. [33] reported fracture discrimination comparable to aBMD using femoral strength and load-to-strength ratios. In contrast, Adams et al. [4] demonstrated improved fracture prediction using FE-derived strength within the Biomechanical Computed Tomography (BCT) framework, while Keaveny et al. [34] reported further improvements by combining strength with clinical variables. Building on these findings, the present study extends FE-based fracture prediction to a DXA-based framework by combining strength with deformation-and energy-related descriptors to characterize femoral mechanical behavior beyond strength alone. Moreover, the higher cost and radiation exposure associated with QCT support the development of more accessible DXA-based FE approaches. Previous studies using DXA-based 2D FE models reported marginal or inconsistent improvements over aBMD in fracture risk prediction using femoral strength, load-to-strength ratios, or Bone Strain Index [13, 35]. Their planar representation limits the characterization of subject-specific 3D femoral geometry, loading conditions, and mechanical response. In contrast, the present 3D-DXA-based FE approach demonstrated statistically significant improvements over aBMD across several measures of predictive performance. Previous 3D-DXA-based FE studies by Grassi et al. [6, 17] demonstrated improved fracture discrimination using weight-normalized femoral strength under multiple simulated side-fall configurations in the MrOS and OSTPRE cohorts, particularly relative to FN aBMD in OSTPRE. However, these studies employed linear elastic FE models with a surface strain-based failure criterion [36], whereas the present study incorporates nonlinear material behavior to characterize the volumetric inelastic response of the femur. Although differences between cohorts prevent direct comparisons of predictive performance, previous studies have demonstrated improved fracture discrimination using nonlinear FE models [5, 19]. Furthermore, while previous 3D-DXA-based FE studies primarily focused on femoral strength, our earlier study [19] demonstrated the additional discriminative value of nonlinear energy-related descriptors, which is further supported by the present findings in an independent prospective cohort.

Another relevant 3D-DXA-based FE study by Gugler et al. [23] investigated fracture prediction in the prospective Geneva Retirees Cohort, evaluating femoral strength under simulated fall conditions alongside 3D-DXA-derived parameters. Consistent with our findings, femoral strength showed a fracture risk association comparable to TH aBMD, whereas Trab.vBMD showed the strongest association among the evaluated FE strength and 3D-DXA-derived parameters, in agreement with Iki et al. [37] in the JPOS cohort. Gugler et al. [23] also reported numerically higher Harrell's C-index values for FE strength and vBMD than for aBMD, without statistically significant differences, consistent with our findings for Int.vBMD and Trab.vBMD (Table 2). However, methodological and cohort-related differences should be considered. The voxel-based FE approach of Gugler et al. [23] does not explicitly distinguish cortical and trabecular compartments [38], whereas our compartmentspecific structural mesh represents their distinct morphology and mechanical properties. Furthermore, their cohort included only five hip fractures, with a shorter follow-up period, compared with 85 hip fractures and up to 20 years of follow-up in the present study. Gugler et al. [23] also reported that strength-based thresholds improved fracture identification but increased the classification of nonfracture individuals as high risk, reducing specificity. These findings suggest the need to investigate thresholds combining strength with deformation-and energy-related descriptors. The present prospective results, together with our previous retrospective study [19] (64 fractures and 64 controls), provide a basis for evaluating such thresholds in future studies. From a computational perspective, the proposed framework requires approximately 1.5 min for 3D reconstruction (Intel Core i7-4790K CPU) and 10 min for FE simulation (8 CPU cores) per subject, supporting its potential clinical applicability. Despite its advantages, the proposed approach has several limitations. The relatively limited number of hip fractures (85 cases among 1,636 women) may affect the statistical power to detect improvements over aBMD [37]. Internal cross-validation, bootstrap resampling, and penalized regression were used to reduce overfitting and improve model robustness. Although the findings are consistent with our previous independent Spanish case-control study [19], validation in larger and more diverse cohorts is needed, particularly given the inclusion of Japanese women only and potential population-related differences in femoral geometry and density distribution. Furthermore, the elasticplastic constitutive model approximates bone failure mechanisms, and more advanced macroscopic or multiscale failure models [39] [40] [41] warrant investigation. Finally, global FE-based descriptors were evaluated, as fracture location was unavailable. Although local descriptors may improve site-specific fracture prediction [22], global descriptors offer greater computational efficiency and robustness.

Conclusion

This is the first study demonstrating that global mechanical descriptors obtained from standard hip DXA scans outperform aBMD in predicting incident hip fractures in a population-based, prospectively followed cohort of women. By incorporating mechanical descriptors beyond bone density alone, these descriptors may provide a more comprehensive characterization of skeletal fragility. These findings highlight the potential of integrating 3D-DXA and FE modelling into existing DXA-based clinical workflows to enhance fracture risk assessment.
