Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Patients with Multiple Long-Term Conditions (MLTCs), also known as multimorbidity, often present clinical and operational challenges in emergency departments (EDs), yet how their care pathways deviate from expected workflows remains underexplored. This study introduces a process-aware analytical framework that quantifies and interprets ED process deviations by integrating multi-perspective conformance checking with explainable machine learning. Using routinely collected Scottish ED data between 2017-2023, we derived an empirical benchmark pathway. We introduced a new metric, the deviation score (DS), which captures both structural misalignments and breaches of the four-hour ED standard, a national service target defined as completion of ED care within four hours of arrival. Compared with non-MLTC attendances, MLTC patients exhibited longer and more complex pathways, higher DS values, and lower conformance to the benchmark model. The framework enabled interpretable SHAP analyses, revealing that deviation severity among MLTC attendances was primarily associated with triage acuity, year of attendance, activity queue length (number of patients queuing for the same care activity), and referral source, with additional contributions from age, arrival time, All data from EHR and national registries were linked and de-identified by the DataLoch service (Edinburgh, United Kingdom) and analysed within their Secure Data Environment.

I. INTRODUCTION

M ULTIPLE LONG-TERM CONDITIONS (MLTCs), also known as multimorbidity, refers to the coexistence of two or more chronic health conditions in the same individual [1]. It is estimated that approximately one in three people globally live with multimorbidity [2] [3], [4]. Prevalence increases significantly with age, from just 0.9% in those aged 0-19 to 68.2% among individuals aged 80 and above [5]. People with MLTC are considered clinically complex due to the need for simultaneous management of chronic illnesses that may interact in unpredictable and compounding ways. These challenges are often exacerbated by polypharmacy, socioeconomic vulnerability, and the high burden of self-management [6], [7], [8]. This multifaceted complexity puts pressure on care systems that are traditionally structured around single-disease protocols and episodic treatment models. [6].

Emergency departments (EDs), primarily designed for acute, emergency care [1], [3], often struggle to accommodate the complex needs of MLTC patients [9], [10], [4]. Across the United Kingdom, ED performance is monitored using an access standard, a national time-based target for patients to complete ED care within four hours. This standard influences how patient flow is managed through the system. The tension between this time-driven performance target and the complex clinical needs of patients with MLTC has the potential to distort ED care and result in fragmented delivery to identify the key clinical and operational drivers that shape deviation severity. Box styles: bold for main stages, solid for feature groups or outputs, and dashed for derived effects. [9]. Transitions of care, such as movements between locations or providers, are often slower, less consistent, and more prone to failure [10]. The associated operational challenges may result in process deviations, defined as discrepancies between the expected or recommended pathway based on clinical guidelines or protocols and actual care [8]. Such deviations may involve skipped steps due to time pressures or perceived redundancy, or additional steps introduced in response to complexity or procedural gaps [11]. Whereas some deviations are adaptive and patient-centred [12], [13], others introduce potentially harmful variation that undermines care quality, increases inefficiency, and elevates the risk of adverse outcomes [14], [8], [15]. A growing body of research highlights that process deviations may accumulate without detection, leading to unresolved clinical problems [16] and increased likelihood of repeat ED attendance [17].

Despite its importance, most prior studies have examined deviation indirectly through its consequences, such as delays, overcrowding or long stays [12], rather than by analysing how the care pathway itself diverges from expectations. To study deviation at the level of the process, researchers have increasingly turned to process mining. Process mining reconstructs real-world care pathways from routine data and this approach has been applied to investigate the timeliness of care, inefficiencies in patient flow, and key bottlenecks in ED processes [18] [19] [20]. Such process-mining analyses demonstrate the value of identifying where deviations and delays arise within the ED process. However, process mining alone cannot explain why certain patients are more likely to experience deviation from expected pathways.

Explainable machine learning improves transparency by enabling interpretation of predictive models. A widely used post-hoc interpretability method is SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), which quantifies feature contributions and helps identify clinical or operational drivers of prolonged stays [21], admission risk [22], and extended length of stay [23]. Whereas explainable machine learning can identify factors associated with deviation risk, it does not capture the underlying structure or timing of processes and therefore cannot detect divergence at the process level.

To address the limits of process mining in identifying the clinical and operational factors associated with deviation, and the limits of SHAP in quantifying divergence at the process level, we introduce a new process-aware framework for detecting, measuring, and interpreting the magnitude of process deviations in ED care for patients with MLTC, as illustrated in Fig. 1. We summarise the major contributions of this paper as the following: • The proposed framework integrates multi-perspective conformance checking with explainable machine learn-ing, enabling both process-level and feature-level understanding of deviation.

• A new deviation metric, Deviation Score (DS). DS is derived through multi-perspective conformance checking, capturing both control-flow misalignments and breaches of the four-hour ED turnaround performance standard.

• The novel results of a systematic analysis of ED process deviation among patients with MLTC. Our work quantifies how far an attendance diverges from the expected care pathway and explains why deviation happens by identifying the clinical, operational, and temporal, factors associated with deviation.

• Evidence that the deviation experienced by MLTC patients follows systematic and clinically plausible patterns. Our work offers new insights into how MLTC interacts with clinical urgency and system-level pressures to influence variability in care.

II. RELATED WORK

II. RELATED WORK

A. Multiple Long-Term Conditions in Emergency Care

Recent large-scale cohort studies have quantified the burden of MLTC in ED settings. McParland et al. [24] demonstrated that MLTC is linked to both increased ED use and higher mortality. Blayney et al. [12] found a strong association between MLTC and a range of adverse outcomes, including 30-day mortality, re-attendance, and prolonged ED stays. Yordanov et al. [25] argued that ED systems require significant adaptation to meet the needs of MLTC patients, emphasising the importance of pathway-level understanding. Wartelle et al. [26] used cluster analysis to identify distinct multimorbidity profiles among frequent ED users, revealing heterogeneous patterns of service utilization that have implications for targeted interventions. Other work has examined contextual factors influencing ED use, including social determinants [27] and area-level deprivation [28], highlighting inequities in access and outcomes.

Whereas these studies advance understanding of the demand, outcomes, and systemic challenges posed by managing patients with MLTC in EDs, they mainly focus on highlevel utilisation metrics [12], outcome disparities [27], [28], and specific intervention points such as triage or discharge planning [10]. Far fewer studies have examined the sequence, timing, and interactions of care activities for this cohort, and none have systematically identified or interpreted process deviations. Moreover, few studies have explored the underlying factors that contribute to such deviations [29]. The lack of pathway-level and deviation-focused evidence limits understanding of process variation in ED care for patients with MLTC.

B. Process Mining in Emergency Care

Process mining extracts insights from event logs to help analyse, understand, evaluate, and improve real-world processes [30]. It includes three main paths: process discovery (deriving a process model from logs); conformance checking (comparing actual execution against a reference model to detect deviations); and process enhancement (enriching discovered models with performance, resource, or organisational perspectives). Initially developed for business process optimisation, process mining has gained significant traction in healthcare, where it is used to analyse patient pathways, detect inefficiencies, and support evidence-based redesign [31], [32].

In emergency care, most studies emphasize the discovery of control-flow and performance analysis. For example, Duma et al. [33] applied process mining to reconstruct patient flow through the emergency department, identifying bottlenecks and recurrent patterns that contribute to overcrowding. Rojas et al. [34] applied process mining for performance analysis, identifying recurrent loops that extended the length of stay in the ED. Conformance checking has demonstrated potential value in other areas such as inpatient care pathways [35], [36], yet remains comparatively underutilised in ED research. When applied, it typically examines structural (e.g., control-flow) or temporal deviations in isolation. For instance, Cho et al. [20] proposed a set of performance indicators for assessing emergency room processes using process mining. Erdogan et al. [18] conducted one of the few studies that incorporates multiple perspectives, using a goal-driven approach that combines control-flow alignment with performance metrics such as throughput times to identify process bottlenecks.

So, even though prior studies demonstrate the value of conformance checking from a multi-perspective view, there remains a gap in formally quantifying deviation severity from both the expected care pathway and time-based performance targets in ED settings.

C. Process Mining with Explainable Machine Learning in Emergency Care

Explainable Machine Learning refers to methods that make the predictions of machine learning models understandable to humans, either by using inherently interpretable models or by applying post-hoc techniques such as Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) [37]. In healthcare, explainable ML is increasingly valued for translating model outputs into clinically meaningful insights, enhancing trust and usability among practitioners [38]. Existing literature has explored the intersection of explainable ML and process mining. Nannini et al. [37] synthesised approaches for embedding interpretability across the process mining pipeline. Mehdiyev et al. [39] categorized post-hoc and intrinsic techniques for enhancing transparency in process-based predictive models. Whereas these works establish conceptual pathways for integrating process-level analytics with model interpretability, applications in ED settings remain sparse.

In the ED setting, explainable ML has primarily been applied to outcome prediction tasks, with limited attention to process-level analysis. These applications typically focus on identifying feature contributions to specific outcomes,such as prolonged length of stay or admission risk, without examining the underlying care pathways or structural deviations in clinical workflows. For instance, Sulaiman [23] used rule-based explanations to identify operational bottlenecks associated with prolonged length of stay. Arnaud [22] applied SHAP to highlight the key drivers of predicted care trajectories.

Although such approaches strengthen model transparency at the feature level, they do not incorporate formal processoriented perspectives.

Our work addresses this gap by integrating explainable ML with multi-perspective conformance checking to provide a unified view of both feature-level and process-level deviations, enabling a more comprehensive understanding of deviation severity in ED care for patients with MLTC.

III. METHODS

III. METHODS

A. Datasets and Study Population

In this study, data were sourced from DataLoch, the Trusted Research Environment for analysis of linked, de-identified health records from approximately 1M residents in South East Scotland. Linked data was provided from electronic health records in both primary (GP) and hospital care, specifically: • Demographic information including age, sex, ethnicity, and socioeconomic deprivation.

• ED attendance and discharge data including activities such as bed request time.

• Hospital inpatient data to understand who required admission from ED.

• National administrative data from the Scottish Morbidity Record for hospital attendances to understand MLTC status of individual patients using ICD-10 coding applied to a subset of 156 CALIBER phenotype condition codelists [40].

• GP data to understand MLTC using Read codes applied to the same subset of 156 CALIBER phenotype condition codelists [40]. These phenotype definitions and associated code lists were applied without modification.

• Laboratory results including time-stamped data on request time. We included all emergency department attendances presenting to the Royal Infirmary of Edinburgh during the study period between 1st January 2017 to 24th April 2023. In line with the focus of study on characterising pathway variation, each episode of care was treated as an independent case, even when multiple episodes were attributed to the same individual.

B. Data Pre-processing

Activities of interest include attendance, triage, blood test, bed request, discharge, and inpatient admission. Each activity was recorded as a single timestamped event, without information on the staff member, role, or organisational unit responsible for performing it. Attendances with incomplete, inconsistent, or implausible temporal information were excluded, including temporal data quality issues such as admission events recorded before ED arrival, bed requests logged after inpatient admission and episodes with missing discharge timestamps. ED stays extending beyond 48 hours and durations between ED discharge and hospital admission that exceeded a statistically defined outlier threshold (above the 99th percentile) were also excluded.

Health conditions present up to but not including the date of each ED attendance were identified using diagnoses recorded in both primary care and hospital care. At each attendance, MLTC status was assigned by counting the number of unique chronic condition phenotypes recorded before the visit. Attendances were classified as MLTC when two or more chronic condition phenotypes were present, whereas those with a single or no conditions were labelled as non-MLTC.

With the final analysis cohort established, we constructed the event log as follows. We defined the finite set of activity labels as A = {A, T, B, R, D, I}, corresponding to Attendance (A), Triage (T), Blood Test (B), Bed Request (R), Discharge (D), and Inpatient Admission (I), and let T denote the set of timestamps. An event is an ordered pair e = (a, t) ∈ A × T , where a is an activity and t is its occurrence time. A trace for case c is a finite, time-ordered sequence σ c = ⟨e 1 , e 2 , . . . , e nc ⟩, where e nc denotes the number of recorded events for case c, and with non-decreasing timestamps t1 ≤ t2 ≤ • • • ≤ t nc . The event log is the multiset L = {{σ c | c ∈ C}}, where C denotes the set of all ED attendances included in the study. Each case c ∈ C is characterised using attributes drawn from three domains: clinical and care-related, patient sociodemographic, and temporal and operational context. The clinical and care-related domain includes referralSource(c), indicating the origin of presentation (e.g., self-referred, GP referral, ambulance). The patient sociodemographic domain includes triageCategory(c), a nine-level acuity classification assigned by a triage nurse early in the ED attendance. sex(c); ethnicity(c); SIMDScore(c) derived from the Scottish Index of Multiple Deprivation [41]; nLTC(c), the number of Long-Term Conditions (LTCs) identified before the ED visit; and ageGroups(c), grouped into ordered age bands (0-17, 18-30, 31-64, 65-79, 80+). The temporal and operational domain comprises activity-level attributes: arrivalShift(a), indicating the shift during which the patient arrived (e.g., day, evening, or night), weekdayWeekend(a), season(a), year(a), and activityQueueLength(a), which is calculated as the number of patients concurrently performing activity a in the ED. These activity-level features are aggregated to the case level for downstream modelling: the median across activities is used for activityQueueLength(a), whereas the first observed value is used for the remaining activity-level attributes.

C. Benchmark Process Model

The benchmark process model represents the common ED care pathway, defined through empirical frequency analysis of observed event sequences and guided by clinical expertise. The model captures the most common low-complexity trajectory observed in the data, namely Attendance → Triage → Discharge. Activities such as blood tests, bed requests, or inpatient admissions were excluded from the optimised model and, when present, were treated as deviations during conformance analysis.

The benchmark pathway is specified as a Petri net [42] M = (P, T, F, m 0 , m f ), shown in Fig. 2, where: P = {p 0 , p 1 , p 2 , p 3 , p 4 , p 5 } is the set of places, represented graphically as circles, each denoting a state or condition between activities (e.g., after Attendance but before Triage). T = T obs ∪ T τ is the set of transitions, where T obs = {A, T, D} are observable clinical activities (drawn as labelled rectangles) and T τ = {τ 1 , τ² } are silent transitions (drawn as solid black rectangles) introduced to ensure structural soundness and flexible alignment. F ⊆ (P ×T )∪(T ×P ) is the flow relation. m 0 is the initial marking with m 0 (p 0 ) = 1 and m 0 (p i ) = 0 for all i ̸ = 0, representing the entry of a single case trace into the process. m f is the final marking designating completion of the case when a token (representing the progression of a case through the model) reaches p 5.

D. Multi-Perspective Conformance Checking

Multi-perspective conformance checking was applied to quantify process deviations by jointly assessing (i) structural adherence to a benchmark ED pathway and (ii) compliance with the ED 4-hour standard. This approach ensured that both control-flow and time-related requirements were incorporated into the analysis.

1) Structural Conformance:

For each case trace c ∈ C, structural conformance is assessed using the alignment-based replay algorithm in PM4Py [43], an open-source Python library for process mining. The algorithm aligns the observed sequence σ c to the benchmark model M by minimizing an alignment cost. A uniform unit cost function is adopted, whereby synchronous moves (when an observed event matches a corresponding model transition) incur zero cost, while both log-only moves (events observed in the trace but not enabled in the model) and model-only moves (model transitions not observed in the trace) incur equal unit penalties.

Whereas the alignment cost C align (c) quantifies the absolute degree of deviation for case c, it depends on trace length and is therefore not directly comparable across cases. To obtain a normalized and interpretable measure, we compute the standard alignment-based fitness score [44], defined as: where: • C align (c) denotes the optimal alignment cost of case c, and • C worst (c) denotes the worst-case alignment cost under the specified cost function, representing the maximum possible deviation for that trace.

The resulting score f (c) ∈ [0, 1] expresses the degree of structural conformance of case c to the benchmark pathway. A value of f (c) = 1 indicates that the trace σ c can be replayed on M without any deviations, representing perfect conformance, whereas f (c) = 0 corresponds to the limiting case where every move incurs penalties. Intermediate values f (c) ∈ (0, 1) capture partial conformance, quantifying the proportion of behaviour in σ c that is structurally consistent with the normative model. At the cohort level, conformance can also be summarised by the fitness-based conformance rate, defined as the proportion of cases with f (c) = 1, that is, traces that align perfectly with the benchmark pathway.

2) Temporal Conformance: Temporal conformance is assessed relative to the ED 4-hour operational standard, with threshold T max = 240 minutes. For each case c ∈ C, the observed duration is defined as D(c) = t end (c) -t start (c) where t start (c) corresponds to the timestamp of the A (Attendance) event and t end (c) corresponds to the timestamp of the D (Discharge) event in the trace σ c . These timestamps represent the recorded start and end of the ED episode and therefore define the interval over which temporal conformance is evaluated.

To quantify deviation beyond the 4-hour standard, we define the temporal penalty TP(c) as where D 95 denotes the 95th percentile of observed durations across the full study population, serving to normalise extreme values and constrain the penalty within [0, 1].

A value of TP(c) = 0 indicates perfect temporal compliance with the NHS 4-hour standard, TP(c) = 1 represents the worst observed overruns (beyond the 95th percentile), and intermediate values capture the relative degree of temporal deviation.

3) Multi-Perspective Deviation Metric:

To capture deviations that arise either from pathway misalignment or from breaching the 4-hour ED standard, we define the unified deviation metric, Deviation Score (DS), that integrates both structural and temporal perspectives. For each case c, the deviation metric is defined as where f (c) denotes the fitness score, TP(c) is the temporal penalty, and α ∈ [0, 1] is a weighting parameter that balances the relative contribution of structural versus temporal deviation.

We set α = 0.5, thereby assigning equal weight to structural and temporal perspectives. This symmetric weighting was adopted as a transparent and neutral baseline in the absence of established theoretical or empirical guidance favouring one dimension of deviation over the other in ED conformance assessment. Similar balanced weighting strategies have been employed in related domains, such as process trace clustering where activity sequences and temporal attributes are jointly considered, symmetric weighting has been reported to yield more stable and interpretable results [45]. The metric is normalised to the interval [0, 1], where DS(c) = 0 denotes no deviation in both structural and temporal dimensions, and DS(c) = 1 corresponds to the most extreme observed deviation, i.e., maximal misalignment combined with maximum temporal overrun. Intermediate values represent partial deviation, reflecting the relative contribution of structural and temporal departures from the benchmark pathway. For cohort-level reporting, two global measures are used: (i) the deviation-based conformance rate, defined as the proportion of cases with DS(c) = 0, and (ii) the distribution of DS(c), summarised by its median and interquartile range (Q1-Q3).

E. Feature Selection

To explore which case-level attributes were most strongly associated with variation in the DS, we performed a correlation analysis using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient alongside Mutual Information. Spearman's rho captures monotonic relationships between each feature and the continuous DS, whereas Mutual Information identifies non-linear or nonmonotonic associations that correlation alone may miss. This exploratory analysis was not used to remove variables. Rather, it provided an initial assessment of which features exhibited meaningful relationships with deviation severity, as well as whether any strong dependencies existed between features.

Because the full feature set comprised only twelve variables across clinical, sociodemographic, and operational domains and each variable carries interpretive relevance for understanding variation in ED pathways, all features were retained for downstream modelling. This approach avoids premature feature exclusion and ensures that the Random Forest models introduced in Section III-F are able to capture vary subtle, context-dependent relationships among features.

To assess multicollinearity among predictors, we also reported Variance Inflation Factors (VIFs) after encoding and scaling all features included in the regression model. This diagnostic step was used to assess whether any variables exhibited strong linear dependence on others. A threshold of VIF > 5 was prespecified to indicate strong linear dependence. No variables exceeded this threshold, and therefore no features were excluded.

F. Deviation Modelling

Deviation severity was modelled as a continuous outcome using a Random Forest (RF) regressor, with the unified DS as the target variable. For each case c, the input vector x(c) comprised the full set of clinical, sociodemographic, and operational attributes described in Section III-B. This modelling framework captures nonlinear relationships and interactions without imposing parametric assumptions about how features relate to deviation severity. Model performance was quantified using Mean Absolute Error (MAE), which reflects the average prediction error on the 0-1 deviation scale, and the coefficient of determination (R² ), which measures the proportion of variance in deviation severity explained by the model. All performance metrics were estimated using 10-fold cross-validation to ensure that MAE and R² reflected out-ofsample predictive behaviour.

Because temporal and system-level factors may influence ED workflow dynamics, we further examined the sensitivity of model performance and feature rankings to different representations of the Year attribute. Three variants of the RF model were therefore trained: one using year as a numeric variable, one using a clustered encoding distinguishing pre-pandemic (2017-2019), pandemic (2020-2021), and postpandemic (2021-2023) periods, and one excluding the year variable altogether. This sensitivity analysis allowed us to evaluate how temporal structure contributed to deviation severity and whether alternative encodings of year altered the relative importance of other predictors.

To interpret the RF regression models and ensure transparency, we applied SHAP values computed using the Tree-SHAP algorithm. SHAP provides an additive decomposition of each model prediction into contributions from individual features. For a given case c with feature vector x(c), the model output can be expressed as where g(•) is the model's decision function, ϕ 0 is the baseline (expected model output over the training set), and each SHAP value ϕ j (c) represents the marginal contribution of feature j to the prediction for case c, computed as the average effect of adding j across all possible subsets of features.

Triage category (TC) was the dominant predictor, with higher-acuity cases (e.g., Immediate Resuscitation (Code 1)) consistently associated with increased deviation scores. Year (clustered) also showed a clear pattern, where attendances occurring in later pandemic and post-pandemic periods were associated with increased deviation when compared with earlier years. Operational pressure, as captured by median activity queue length at time of attendance, was another key driver, with higher queue lengths consistently associated with higher predicted deviation. Referral source contributed substantially to prediction but, being nominal, does not display a colour gradient in the dot plot; its category-specific effects are shown in Fig. 6. Although all patients in the cohort had at least two long-term conditions, variation in the number of LTCs still accounted for meaningful differences in predicted deviation. V. DISCUSSION

This study introduced a novel, interpretable, and data-driven framework to quantify deviation at the process-level and explain it through its clinical and operational drivers in ED care for patients with MLTC. The proposed framework integrates multi-perspective conformance checking and explainable machine learning. The multi-perspective conformance checking captures both control-flow misalignments and breaches of the 4-hour ED standard. A new metric named deviation score was introduced through this approach, providing a unified measure of deviation severity. SHAP explainability was applied to identify and interpret the key clinical, sociodemographic, and operational factors associated with higher deviation severity.

A. Process-Level Validation of MLTC Complexity

The differences observed in Section IV between MLTC and non-MLTC attendances are consistent with well-established clinical and operational challenges in emergency care. MLTC patients were older and presented with higher triage acuity, increasing the likelihood of serious underlying pathology [25]. This greater degree of clinical complexity [10] leads to more urgent patterns of presentation, evident in the higher rates of ambulance arrival, unscheduled referrals, and assignment to higher-acuity triage categories. The higher use of blood tests, bed requests, and inpatient admissions in the MLTC cohort aligns with evidence that multimorbidity naturally requires broader investigation, more time for assessment and treatment, and more frequent specialist involvement [12]. Each added investigation or handover introduces delay, contributing to the longer inter-activity intervals observed in MLTC workflows and consistent with existing studies showing that greater clinical complexity slows progression in the ED settings [20]. The longer total ED durations and higher proportion of MLTC attendances not meeting the 4-hour target reflect the cumulative effect of diagnostic complexity compounded by operational pressures. Our conformance results provide a process-level validation of these differences. Lower conformance rates and higher deviation scores among MLTC attendances indicate more frequent structural and temporal divergences from the optimised pathway. Our metric, the DS, captures clinically plausible and literature-consistent differences between MLTC and non-MLTC cohorts.

B. Correlation Analysis and Model Reliability

The correlation findings provide an initial view of how clinical, operational, and sociodemographic attributes relate to deviation severity, with the strongest associations aligning with well-established drivers of ED workflow complexity and delay [20], [12], [25]. A moderate correlation was observed between age and number of LTCs, consistent with the expected age-related rise in MLTC burden [10]. VIF analysis confirmed that multicolinearity was not a concern among the predictors included in the final model. However, differences between the correlation analysis and the model-based SHAP explanations are expected, as these methods capture fundamentally different aspects of the data. Spearman's rho and mutual information quantify only bivariate associations, while the Random Forest model learns multivariate, nonlinear, and interaction effects across the full feature set.

Our findings suggest that the RF regression achieved acceptable performance for modelling deviation severity. As the aim of this study is explanatory rather than predictive, the coefficient of determination provides the most meaningful indication of model adequacy [46]. In complex clinical modelling tasks, the obtained R² values are considered significant, supporting the reliability of the SHAP-based interpretations. The MAE values (< 0.10 on a normalised scale) indicate that average prediction errors are small in absolute terms, reinforcing the model's suitability for interpretation even though absolute accuracy is not the primary objective. The sensitivity analysis results indicate that the clustered pattern effectively captured temporal variation, likely reflecting the operational and systemic shifts associated with the COVID-19 pandemic.

C. Feature-Level Insights into Deviation

SHAP results identified triage acuity, year, activity queue length, and referral source as key drivers of deviation in the MLTC cohort, capturing both clinical urgency and systemlevel pressures known to increase ED workflow complexity [20], [10]. Age group, arrival shift, and number of LTCs also contributed meaningfully, reflecting known challenges in caring for complex patients. Older adults often require nuanced assessment, and overnight arrivals may be treated by less experienced staff with more distant senior supervision. Whereas all modelled attendances came from the MLTC cohort, those with the highest number of conditions likely needed more care coordination, increasing deviation risk. In contrast, sociodemographic factors like SIMD, sex, and ethnicity had minimal impact, consistent with UK evidence that their influence fades when clinical and operational needs are considered [24].

Both queue length and MLTC showed clear threshold effects, where their contributions to deviation severity shifted from negative to strongly positive. This pattern reflects a non-linear and amplifying impact on process disruption once these factors exceed certain levels. Existing literature supports this observation, showing that ED workflows often remain stable under manageable complexity or demand but become fragile when care demands go beyond system capacity or coordination becomes overly intricate [7], [9], [10], [12], [29], [47]. Our analysis suggests a shift in the system adaptation since COVID, with greater deviation in performance postpandemic for apparently similar patient burden. This could reflect system changes outside of ED control, where access to hospital beds to allow flow of patients out of ED may be constrained by delayed hospital discharges.

Triage category, year, arrival shift, and number of LTCs represent a consistent directional trend, where higher levels within each domain associated with greater contributions to deviation severity. This likely reflects rising clinical and operational complexity, as urgent cases, post-pandemic constraints, and overnight arrivals introduce challenges that push care beyond routine protocols [47]. Similarly, patients with greater multimorbidity often require tailored management, increasing the likelihood of deviation when system resources are stretched.

Interestingly, patients in moderate-acuity categories (such as urgent (Code 3), standard (Code 4) and not urgent (Code 5)) referred via emergency ambulance or specialist services, as well as those aged 65 and above, showed the highest deviation contributions. These patterns may reflect presentations that initially appear manageable but involve underlying complexity or diagnostic uncertainty [48]. In older adults, subtle symptoms or atypical deterioration can complicate triage and care planning [25]. Notably, among patients aged 80 and over, those with fewer recorded conditions contributed more to deviation than their more MLTC peers. This may reflect appropriate clinical escalation in seemingly lower-risk cases, or conversely reduced prioritisation of these apparently low-risk patients resulting in delays to ED discharge.

D. Limitations and Future Work

This initial study provides a systematic structural and temporal assessment of ED process deviations based on routinely available operational data, whereas identifying opportunities for methodological refinement and extension. (i) The conformance assessment used a fixed weighting parameter (α = 0.5) to balance structural and temporal components when computing DS. This symmetric weighting was intentionally adopted as a baseline, avoiding data-driven optimisation that could compromise interpretability in this initial methodological study. Although equal weighting aligns with practices in related domains [45], its contextual appropriateness in the ED setting has not been empirically calibrated. Future research should explore how different values of α affect deviation patterns and their interpretability, potentially informing a more evidence-driven approach to weighting. (ii) Multimorbidity was defined using a simple definition of two or more long-term conditions, which may not fully capture the clinical heterogeneity within the MLTC cohort. Future work should adopt more granular characterisations, such as distinguishing between low and high condition counts or physical-only versus physical-mental health combinations. This would enable a richer examination of how deviation patterns vary across different MLTC profiles.

E. Significance and Implications

The current study's findings have important implications towards understanding and addressing the drivers of care pathway deviations for people with MLTC presenting to the ED. In particular, the identified threshold effects highlight critical tipping points where care becomes more fragile and variable. Our findings provide an evidence-based foundation for supporting targeted system adjustments, such as optimising staffing patterns during peak queue times, to reduce deviation risk and improve care consistency. Our proposed framework offers a systematic approach to quantifying how care pathways diverge from expected standards across both process and temporal dimensions, supporting future efforts to evaluate and optimise ED care for people with MLTC. This work forms part of a broader research effort to enhance how urgent care systems respond to the needs of people with MLTC through the application of process-aware analytical methods.

VI. CONCLUSION

The present study proposed a process-aware analytical framework to quantify deviation at the process level and explain process deviation in emergency care for people with MLTC. By integrating multi-perspective conformance checking with explainable machine learning, the framework enables both process-level and feature-level insights into deviation patterns. Central to this is the introduction of the deviation score (DS), a new metric that captures structural misalignments and time-based breaches in a unified, interpretable form. Our current findings evidence that attendances with MLTC are characterised by longer and more complex pathways, higher DS, and substantially lower conformance than those without MLTC. Among MLTC patients, deviation severity was primarily driven by triage acuity, year, activity queue length, and referral source. Older age, overnight arrival, and higher multimorbidity burden were also associated with increased deviation severity. Older patients or referral via emergency or specialist routes with moderate-acuity level may deviate more due to hidden complexity in presentations that appear initially stable. Importantly, the uncovered threshold effects in queue length and multimorbidity further reveal critical tipping points beyond which care becomes harder to standardise.

In future work, refining the weighting scheme used in DS and adoption of more nuanced multimorbidity definitions will enable deeper insights into deviation patterns across the MLTC cohort. More broadly, our study highlights the value of combining process-and feature-level perspectives to improve understanding of ED care for people with MLTC.
