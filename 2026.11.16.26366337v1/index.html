Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

p<0.20 Bivariable multilevel logistic regression analyses were first fitted for each explanatory variable, and variables with p<0.20 were considered for multivariable analysis; variables judged epidemiologically important from previous literature were also retained irrespective of bivariable significance to avoid exclusive reliance on statistical screening.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Introduction

The postpartum period remains one of the most dangerous yet neglected phases of the maternal and newborn continuum of care, and the first 24 hours after childbirth carry the highest risk of severe morbidity and death for both women and newborns [1] [2] [3]. Global progress in reducing maternal mortality remains off track for Sustainable Development Goal 3.1, while haemorrhage, hypertensive disorders, sepsis, and other direct obstetric causes continue to drive a large share of preventable deaths [4]. Most maternal deaths from haemorrhage and sepsis occur during the postpartum period, and nearly half of all postpartum maternal deaths occur on the first day after birth [1, 4].

WHO recommendations on maternal and newborn care for a positive postpartum experience emphasize that postpartum care should extend beyond survival and contact coverage to the quality of care, including appropriate maternal and newborn assessment, discharge readiness, counselling, and continuity of care after leaving the facility [5]. Recent guideline syntheses reinforce these recommendations [6]. Yet measurement of this phase remains underdeveloped: a scoping review of postpartum care quality measures found no indicators for WHO-recommended criteria to be assessed before discharge, highlighting a major blind spot in routine monitoring [7]. For that reason, failure to assess women before discharge is better understood as a gap in intrafacility quality of care than as a simple problem of postpartum service utilization.

Evidence from low-and middle-income countries indicates that substantial gaps remain in the quality and adequacy of postpartum care despite increasing institutional delivery. Recent pooled analyses of DHS data reported that only 41.4% of women received quality maternal postpartum care, while studies from sub-Saharan Africa documented persistently low coverage of adequate postpartum care and considerable regional inequalities [8, 9]. However, these studies focused on postpartum care utilization or adequacy rather than whether women received an essential maternal assessment before discharge after facility birth.

Ethiopia similarly illustrates the gap between contact with maternity services and receipt of effective postpartum care. Despite progress in expanding maternal health services, maternal mortality remains high, with the most recent internationally comparable estimate placing Ethiopia's maternal mortality ratio at 267 per 100,000 live births in 2020, a 72% decline since 2000, but still well above the SDG 3.1 target of 70 per 100,000 by 2030 [10]. Previous national and subnational studies have consistently documented low and inequitable postpartum care utilization and identified factors such as urban residence, maternal education, household wealth, media exposure, antenatal care attendance, facility delivery, caesarean birth, and delivery complications as important determinants [6, 11]. However, these studies still primarily assessed postpartum care utilization after childbirth or within recommended postpartum timeframes rather than whether women who delivered in health facilities received an essential maternal assessment before discharge [11]. This gap is important because women may reach health facilities for childbirth yet leave without receiving a basic maternal clinical assessment during the period of greatest vulnerability following delivery [12]. Therefore, missed maternal postpartum assessment before discharge represents an important indicator of effective facility-based care rather than postpartum service utilization alone. To the best of the available evidence, this study is among the first nationally representative analyses using the 2024-2025 Ethiopia Demographic and Health Survey to examine this outcome in Ethiopia. Although household surveys cannot directly measure facility processes, factors such as staffing, workload, adherence to discharge procedures, provider accountability, and quality-improvement practices may influence whether assessments are completed after delivery [3]. By applying a multilevel framework, this study provides new evidence on individual, obstetric, facilityrelated, and contextual factors associated with missed maternal postpartum assessment within a nationally weighted sample [8].

Understanding this gap has important implications for evaluating maternal health progress. High institutional delivery coverage may overestimate improvements in effective coverage and continuity of care if women are discharged without essential postpartum assessment [13]. Evidence on the prevalence and predictors of missed assessment can help identify vulnerable groups, reveal points of failure within the maternal care pathway, and inform strategies to improve facility-based postpartum care, strengthen the maternal continuum of care, advance universal health coverage, and support progress toward SDG 3 in Ethiopia [5, 9, 14]. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the prevalence and predictors of missed maternal postpartum assessment before discharge among women who delivered in Ethiopian health facilities using nationally representative 2024-2025 Ethiopia Demographic and Health Survey data.

Method and Materials

Study Settings

Ethiopia, located in the Horn of Africa, has an estimated population of about 135 million people in 2025, with most of the population living in rural areas [15]. The country is administratively structured into 12 regional states and two city administrations, which are further divided into zones, woredas, and kebeles. Health services are delivered through a decentralized three-tier health system that includes primary health care units, general hospitals, and specialized referral hospitals. Maternal and newborn health services are provided at different levels of this system, with facility-based childbirth and postpartum care forming key components of the national strategy to reduce maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality.

Data Source, Study Population and Sampling Technique

This study analyzed data from the 2024-2025 Ethiopia Demographic and Health Survey (EDHS), a nationally representative survey conducted from August 1, 2024, to April 14, 2025. The EDHS used a two-stage stratified cluster sampling design. First, 805 enumeration areas (281 urban and 524 rural) were selected from the national sampling frame. Then, households were selected systematically within each cluster. Detailed descriptions of the sampling frame, survey procedures, and data collection methods are available in the 2024-2025 EDHS final report.

The study used data from the Individual Recode (IR) file. The source population included women aged 15-49 years interviewed in the survey who had a recent live birth during the reference period. The study population consisted of women whose most recent live birth occurred in a health facility. Because the outcome variable captured whether a woman received a postpartum assessment before discharge, only institutional births were eligible for inclusion in the analysis. Women with incomplete data on the outcome variable or relevant covariates were excluded. Accordingly, the unit of analysis was women aged 15-49 years with a recent facility-based live birth.

Study Variables and Measurements

Selection of explanatory variables was informed by previous literature, theoretical relevance, and availability within the EDHS dataset.

Outcome Variable

The outcome variable was missed postpartum health assessment before discharge among women who gave birth in a health facility. It was categorized as a binary variable: "Yes" for women who did not receive a postpartum health assessment before discharge, coded as 1, and "No" for women who received a postpartum health assessment before discharge, coded as 0.

Independent Variables

Sociodemographic variables: included maternal age, maternal education, household wealth index, parity, marital status, health insurance status, and perceived birth size (Figure 1).

Obstetric variables and facility-level variables: included mode of delivery, birth attendant type, pregnancy complication status, and number of antenatal care visits. Mode of delivery was categorized as vaginal or caesarean birth. Birth attendant type was grouped as doctor, nurse, midwife, and other health professionals, which include health extension workers and health officers. Pregnancy complication status was categorized as at least one complication versus none. Antenatal care attendance was categorized as no ANC visit, 1-3 visits, 4-7 visits, and ≥8 visits. Type of place of delivery was categorized as government hospital, government health centre/post, and private/NGO facility. Geographic variables: Geographic variables included region of residence, place of residence, and perceived distance to a health facility.

Data Management and Statistical Analysis

Data were extracted from the 2024-2025 Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey (EDHS), and all data management, recoding, and statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 17. The analysis was restricted to women who gave birth in a health facility, consistent with the study focus on missed postpartum assessment before discharge. The analysis was restricted to 3,645 women who gave birth in a health facility. Sampling weights were subsequently applied to obtain nationally representative estimates. Variables were identified from the EDHS individual recode file, recoded according to the EDHS recode manual, and organized to reflect the study framework. Derived variables were generated where appropriate by recoding and categorizing EDHS variables according to the study objectives. Data completeness was assessed before analysis, and records with missing information for the outcome variable were excluded using a complete-case approach.

Sampling weights were applied throughout the analysis to account for unequal selection probabilities, nonresponse, and the complex survey design, thereby producing nationally interpretable estimates. The survey design variables, including sampling weights, primary sampling units (PSUs), and stratification variables, were incorporated in all descriptive and regression analyses. Because women were nested within enumeration areas, a multilevel mixed-effects logistic regression model was used to account for within-cluster correlation and between-cluster heterogeneity in missed postpartum assessment before discharge. Bivariable multilevel logistic regression analyses were first fitted for each explanatory variable, and variables with p<0.20 were considered for multivariable analysis; variables judged epidemiologically important from previous literature were also retained irrespective of bivariable significance to avoid exclusive reliance on statistical screening. Four sequential models were specified. Model 0 was an empty model containing only the random intercept. Model I included obstetric and facility-level variables. Model II added sociodemographic variables to Model I. Model III, the final model, added geographic variables to Model II. Because these models were nested, model comparison was based on log-likelihood, deviance, and Wald χ² statistics, and the model with the best overall fit was selected as the final model. Multicollinearity among explanatory variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor, and a mean VIF value of 1.4 indicates the absence of problematic multicollinearity. Fixed effects were reported as adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals, and statistical significance was declared at p<0.05. Random effects were evaluated using the cluster-level variance, intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC), median odds ratio (MOR), and proportional change in variance (PCV) to quantify between-cluster variability and the extent to which successive models explained that variability.

Results

Of the 3,645 women included in the analysis, the majority delivered at government health centers or posts (58.9%), followed by government hospitals (36.9%) and private/NGO facilities (4.2%). Most women delivered vaginally (87.0%), while 13.0% underwent caesarean section. Nearly half (46.2%) were attended by a midwife, followed by nurses (30.0%), doctors (21.8%), and other health professionals (21.8%).

Slightly over half of the women (51.3%) reported no pregnancy complications, while 48.8% experienced at least one complication. Most women (58.1%) did not have health insurance, compared with 41.9% who were insured. Regarding antenatal care (ANC) attendance, 41.3% had 4-7 visits, 29.0% had ≥8 visits, 20.5% had 1-3 visits, and 9.2% had no ANC visits.

Regarding maternal education, 40.0% had primary education, 36.2% had secondary or higher education, and 23.7% had no formal education. By household wealth, the largest proportion of women were in the richest quintile (33.1%), followed by richer (26.4%), middle (17.5%), poorer (13.6%), and poorest (9.4%) quintiles.

Most participants were aged 20-29 years (54.9%), followed by 30-39 years (35.9%), with smaller proportions aged ≤19 years (4.1%) and 40-49 years (5.2%). By parity, over half were multiparous (54.3%), while 27.8% were primiparous and 17.9% were grand multiparous. The vast majority of women were currently married (93.4%), with small proportions formerly married (5.6%) or never married (1.0%).

Regarding perceived baby birth size, nearly half (47.3%) perceived their baby as average size, 20.0% as larger than average, 14.0% as very large, 12.6% as very small, and 6.2% as smaller than average. Slightly more than half of the women resided in rural areas (56.1%) compared with urban areas (43.9%). Most women did not consider distance to a health facility a big problem (69.2%), while 30.8% did.

Geographically, the largest proportions of women were from Oromia (34.1%) and Amhara (28.8%), followed by Addis Ababa (7.2%), South Ethiopia (6.3%), and Tigray (6.6%), with the remaining regions each accounting for less than 6% of the sample (Table 1).

Postpartum Health Assessment Before Discharge Status in Ethiopia

A total of 3,645 women who delivered at a health facility were included in the analysis. Overall, 40.1% (95% CI: 37.0-43.3%) of women did not receive a postpartum health assessment before discharge (Figure 2).

Determinants of Postpartum Health Assessment Before Discharge

Random Effects Analysis

The null model revealed substantial between-cluster variation in missed postpartum assessment before discharge, with a cluster-level variance of 0.945 and an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 22.3%, indicating that approximately one-fifth of the total variability in the outcome was attributable to differences between clusters rather than individual-level characteristics. This finding justified the use of a multilevel modelling approach.

Introducing obstetric and facility-level variables (Model I) reduced the cluster variance to 0.900 and the ICC to 21.5%, corresponding to a proportional change in variance (PCV) of 4.8% relative to the null model, indicating that these factors explained a modest share of the observed clustering. The subsequent addition of sociodemographic variables (Model II) did not yield a further sequential reduction in cluster-level variance; instead, the cluster variance rose to 0.941 and the ICC to 22.2%, values close to those of the null model, corresponding to a net PCV of only 0.4% relative to the null model. This pattern suggests that sociodemographic characteristics accounted for little additional between-cluster heterogeneity once obstetric and facility-level factors were held constant, and may partly offset the variance reduction achieved in Model I, potentially reflecting compositional overlap between sociodemographic characteristics and obstetric or facility-level factors already captured in the model. Fixed-effects model fit nonetheless continued to improve at this stage, with the log pseudolikelihood increasing from -2,261.38 (Model I) to -2,228.86 (Model II) and the Wald χ² statistic rising from 117.48 to 143.93, indicating that sociodemographic variables contributed to explaining individual-level variation in the outcome despite their limited contribution to cluster-level heterogeneity.

The final model (Model III), which additionally incorporated geographic variables, produced the largest reduction in unexplained between-cluster variation, lowering the cluster variance to 0.811 and the ICC to 19.8% (PCV = 14.2% relative to the null model). Consistent with this trend, the median odds ratio (MOR) declined from 2.53 in the null model to 2.47 in Model I, rose to 2.52 in Model II, and declined further to 2.36 in the final model -a pattern that mirrors the PCV and ICC trends and indicates that geographic context, rather than individual sociodemographic characteristics, accounted for the majority of the explainable betweencluster heterogeneity in missed postpartum assessment. Model fit improved progressively and significantly across the full model-building sequence, with the log pseudolikelihood increasing from -2,435.08 (null model) to -2,202.67 (Model III) and the Wald χ² statistic rising to 220.70 in the final model, confirming that each successive model provided a significantly better fit to the data than the null model. Based on these comparative fit statistics, Model III -incorporating obstetric, facility-level, sociodemographic, and geographic variables -was selected as the final model for identifying factors associated with missed postpartum assessment before discharge (Table 2).

In Ethiopia, earlier nationally representative analyses reported very low maternal postpartum 321 care coverage within two days of birth, including 17% in the 2016 EDHS-based analysis 322 and 6.9% in another DHS analysis using a broader denominator of recent births [16, 18, 323 19]. Among women delivering in facilities, one Ethiopian multilevel study reported that 42% received postpartum care within two days, and also noted that most facility-born women did not receive proper postpartum care and that over two-thirds of vaginal births were discharged within 24 hours [9]. At the subnational level, quality-adjusted newborn postpartum care coverage in Ethiopia was only 9% in a 2016 EDHS analysis, and a later study using the 2019 mini-EDHS found a large gap between newborn postpartum contact (34.8%) and effective coverage (10.4%) [20, 21]. Taken together, these findings suggest that the present estimate is not directly comparable with prior Ethiopian postpartum utilization metrics, but it is consistent with a broader pattern in which service contact and effective postpartum care content remain substantially misaligned [20, 21].

This study therefore makes a distinct contribution by moving beyond conventional postpartum care utilization indicators to examine whether women actually received an assessment before discharge, which is better interpreted as an indicator of intrafacility maternal care quality than of service contact alone [5]. Unlike previous Ethiopian studies that focused on postpartum care attendance after childbirth or within two days of birth, this analysis isolates a critical but under-measured transition point: whether facility birth culminated in a documented maternal assessment before the woman left care [9, 17]. That distinction is important because WHO guidance places explicit emphasis on discharge readiness, quality of care, and assessment before women and newborns leave the facility, while recent measurement reviews have shown that several recommended pre-discharge criteria remain poorly captured in routine monitoring systems [7]. Methodologically, the use of the 2024-2025 EDHS, a nationally weighted sample, and a multilevel mixed-effects model incorporating individual, obstetric, facility, and regional factors strengthens the study's novelty and policy relevance for contemporary Ethiopia [17].

Comparable evidence from sub-Saharan Africa also suggests that the level observed here is concerning. A multi-country DHS analysis cited in the Ghana facility study estimated that only 66.6% of women across 33 sub-Saharan African countries received a pre-discharge check by a health professional, implying that roughly one-third did not, with marked crosscountry variation from 26.6% in Eswatini to 94.4% in Burkina Faso [22]. In a broader metaanalysis across 36 sub-Saharan African countries, pooled postpartum care utilization was 52.5%, while Ethiopia was among the lower-coverage settings in the regional comparison [9]. A 2025 systematic review across LMICs similarly estimated pooled postpartum care utilization at 48.9%, again indicating that postpartum care remains less consistently delivered than antenatal or delivery care in many settings [23]. Even so, these cross-country figures should be interpreted cautiously because most refer to postpartum care utilization or postpartum checks, not specifically to missed maternal assessment before discharge, which reinforces the novelty of the present estimate rather than allowing direct one-to-one comparison [7, 22].

The lower odds of missed assessment among women delivering in private or NGO facilities than in government hospitals likely indicate differences in care processes rather than ownership alone. A possible explanation is that private and NGO facilities may have lower caseloads, shorter queues, or more tightly supervised discharge procedures, whereas government hospitals may face heavier referral burdens and more complex workflows; however, this interpretation should remain cautious because ownership may also proxy urban location, case-mix, or socioeconomic selection that were not fully measured. This interpretation is broadly consistent with Ethiopian evidence showing that private facilities were associated with higher-quality PNC than public facilities in national DHS-based analysis, although that study assessed composite PNC quality rather than maternal assessment before discharge [17].

The strong association with caesarean birth is also consistent with prior Ethiopian and sub-Saharan literature on postpartum care use [9, 16, 24]. One plausible explanation is that women undergoing caesarean section remain in facilities longer and have more postoperative reviews, which creates more opportunities for formal postpartum assessment before discharge, rather than the operation itself intrinsically improving care quality [9]. By contrast, women with uncomplicated vaginal births may be discharged rapidly and treated as lower risk, despite evidence that major postpartum complications can still emerge after apparently normal delivery [25]. The implication is that universal postpartum assessment is needed, rather than a model in which formal review is triggered mainly by surgical delivery or overt complications.

The finding that births attended by other health professionals (extension workers or health officers) had higher odds of missed assessment than doctor-attended births should likewise be interpreted as a signal of how care is organised, not of professional worth. A plausible explanation is that gaps in handover, documentation, or role clarity around who is responsible for the discharge assessment may contribute to missed checks when care pathways are fragmented. This aligns with broader evidence that quality failures in postpartum care often reflect weakly specified processes of assessment and transition, and that women in sub-Saharan Africa frequently report compromised quality, poor communication, and limited accountability in facility maternity care [26].

Women reporting pregnancy complications were less likely to leave without assessment, which appears to reflect risk-responsive clinical attention. However, the converse finding is equally important: women without recognised complications may be systematically overlooked, even though severe postpartum morbidity can evolve rapidly after an uncomplicated labour. This again supports a universal approach to immediate postpartum assessment, with risk stratification used to determine intensity of follow-up rather than whether assessment occurs at all [5].

The graded association between ANC attendance and lower odds of missed postpartum assessment is one of the most policy-relevant findings. Prior Ethiopian studies consistently found that four or more ANC visits were associated with higher postpartum care use, and national and subnational analyses have similarly linked ANC to postpartum care utilization and quality-adjusted care along the continuum [18, 21, 23, 27]. One plausible explanation is that ANC increases counselling, preparedness, familiarity with facilities, and women's expectations regarding postpartum checks, but the current data do not directly test these mechanisms [21]. Even so, the consistency of this pattern supports using ANC not only to promote facility delivery, but also to prepare women for immediate postpartum assessment before discharge [22, 28]. The residual regional variation after adjustment indicates that missed postpartum assessment is also shaped by subnational health-system differences. This is consistent with Ethiopian evidence showing substantial geographic clustering and regional inequalities in postpartum care use, maternal continuum-of-care dropout, and quality-adjusted newborn postpartum care, with Somali repeatedly emerging as a higher-risk region in prior work [18, 20, 29]. These differences may reflect variation in readiness, workforce distribution, programme implementation strength, and fragility, but those pathways were not directly measured in the current survey and should therefore be interpreted as plausible system-level explanations rather than established causal mechanisms [30].

A notable implication of the null associations for several socioeconomic variables after adjustment is that, once women have already reached a facility for childbirth, the dominant failures may shift from access barriers to within-facility quality gaps. This interpretation is compatible with broader Ethiopian and LMIC evidence showing that social disadvantage remains strongly associated with postpartum care utilization overall, while effective coverage analyses increasingly show that contact does not ensure receipt of essential care content [20, 23].

Strength and Limitation

This study has several important strengths. First, it used a nationally weighted sample of 3,645 facility births from the 2024-2025 Ethiopia Demographic and Health Survey, which enhances the national representativeness and contemporary policy relevance of the findings. Second, the analysis focused on an underexamined but programmatically important outcome: whether women received a postpartum assessment before discharge after institutional birth, thereby capturing an aspect of intrafacility quality of care rather than service contact alone. Third, the use of a multilevel mixed-effects model accounted for both individual-and cluster-level heterogeneity and incorporated survey weighting, clustering, and stratification, which improved the robustness of the estimates and strengthened the validity of the inferences drawn at national level.

Several limitations should also be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the cross-sectional nature of the survey precludes causal inference, and the observed associations should therefore be interpreted as correlational rather than causal. Second, the outcome measure captured only whether any postpartum assessment occurred before discharge and did not provide information on the timing, completeness, technical content, or adherence to standard discharge criteria, which limits conclusions about the quality of the assessment itself. Third, as a secondary analysis of survey data, the study was unable to account for potentially important facility-level determinants, including staffing levels, caseload, provider workload, supervision, and local discharge practices, all of which may influence whether essential postpartum checks are completed before women leave the facility

Recommendation

These findings support a more operational set of recommendations than increasing institutional delivery alone. Ethiopia should consider making documented maternal postpartum assessment before discharge a routine standard for all facility births, supported by standardized discharge checklists, postpartum observation protocols for both vaginal and caesarean births, and explicit alignment with WHO postpartum care recommendations on quality, discharge readiness, and continuity of care. Given the association with facility type and provider cadre, government hospitals in particular may benefit from competency-based in-service training, clearer task allocation, stronger provider accountability, and routine auditand-feedback systems that verify whether essential discharge assessments were completed and recorded. At the health information level, inclusion of pre-discharge postpartum assessment indicators in Ethiopia's HMIS and electronic discharge documentation could improve routine measurement, especially given the documented gaps in existing postpartum quality metrics and the need for better monitoring of provision-of-care processes. Regional heterogeneity also argues for targeted quality-improvement support in high-burden settings, particularly underserved regions such as Somali, rather than reliance on uniform national strategies. Finally, because ANC attendance was strongly associated with lower odds of missed assessment, immediate postpartum assessment counselling and discharge expectations should be integrated explicitly into routine ANC communication so that women know a facility birth should end not simply with delivery, but with a completed maternal check before going home.
