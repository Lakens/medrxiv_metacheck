Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Objective: To describe global patterns of resting heart rate (RHR) in population subgroups and over time.

Methods: Observational data from WHO STEP surveys (n=363,462 participants from 76 countries, 2002-2020) and the South Asia Biobank (SAB, n=203,524 participants from four South Asian countries, 2018-2023). RHR was measured in triplicate while seated using a blood pressure cuff. Height and weight were measured using anthropometric techniques. Physical activity was self-reported and accelerometry-measured (SAB subsample, n=22,183). We describe RHR distributions by geographical region, temporality, sex, age, BMI, and physical activity.

Utility of RHR to explain accelerometer-based physical activity was examined by regression.

Results: Country-level median RHR ranged 65-80 bpm, higher in women than men. Age-related RHR patterns diverged markedly by sex; men showing a curvilinear association with higher RHR in midlife, and women showing a consistent decline with age across most regions.

Compared to normal-weight, obese participants had higher RHR [WHO: (men: +4.4 bpm, women: +0.9 bpm, SAB: (men: +3.1 bpm, women: +1.0 bpm)]. Participants meeting physical activity recommendations had lower RHR [WHO: (men -1.6 bpm, women: -0.9 bpm), SAB: (men: -2.1 bpm, women: -0.5 bpm)] compared to insufficiently active.

In SAB (field-work spanning whole-calendar year), seasonal variations were present (0.6-0.9 bpm higher RHR in June-August compared to the Sept-Nov period), most pronounced further from the equator. In 21 WHO countries with repeat survey data, RHR was stable over time in women but declined in men (-0.4 bpm/year). Self-reported activity explained 2-14% (women: 2-3%, men: 10-14%) of the variance in device-measured physical activity, and RHR added 1-3% incrementally.

Conclusions: RHR varies globally, exhibits different age-related patterns by sex, and is higher in population subgroups with greater non-communicable disease burden. RHR is easy to measure and may have utility for global health surveillance.

INTRODUCTION

Cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) is a strong predictor of morbidity and mortality (1). Low CRF is linked to obesity which affects over 1 billion people globally (2). Despite both fitness and obesity being modifiable risk factors, there is currently no global health surveillance system in place to monitor fitness, with levels being only sporadically described in population studies (3).

A small proportion of studies have described CRF in European populations, Latin America, Africa, and South Asia (4) (5) (6) (7). In Africa, different ethnic populations in Kenya, South Africa, and Tanzania exhibit large differences in CRF (7) (8) (9). Of the limited research available, it has been shown that CRF tends to be higher in men than women and declines with age (9) (10) (11). South Asian men living in the UK have lower CRF compared to age-and BMI-matched white men (12).

The gold-standard measurement of CRF is maximal oxygen consumption (VO2max) during an incremental exercise test to exhaustion. The feasibility of this assessment is, however, challenging in most situations, making it difficult to incorporate into surveillance systems.

Therefore, alternative methods are typically deployed, including heart rate response to submaximal exercise tests (13) but most cohort studies do not include dynamic exercise testing.

The most feasible indicator of CRF is resting heart rate (RHR) which is widely measured across national surveys, cohort studies and clinical settings; RHR has been shown to be inversely correlated with CRF (14). Additionally, individuals with low CRF who have metabolic disease or are at an elevated risk of metabolic disease have significantly higher RHR compared to healthy individuals (15, 16). RHR is also positively associated with cancer and CVD-related mortality (17, 18).

To gain insights into the global variation of RHR and its potential for health surveillance, we describe the global epidemiology of RHR, with an enhanced emphasis on South Asia that has relatively high levels of NCDs. Specifically, we integrate data from the WHO STEP surveys which collected RHR data from 76 countries globally, with the South Asia Biobank (SAB) which is a multicenter study focused on chronic diseases in four South Asian countries with additional objective measures of lung function and device-based physical activity (19, 20). This dual-dataset approach provides a broad global overview while offering fine-grained insights into South Asia, allowing for comparisons of RHR levels between populations and assessing its potential utility for routine surveillance of cardiorespiratory health.

METHODS

Study Populations

WHO STEP Surveys

The WHO STEPS surveys, first administered in 2002, follow the WHO STEP-wise approach to noncommunicable disease risk factor surveillance (19). This analysis includes data on representative samples of 363,462 individuals aged 18 and above from 76 countries (100 unique surveys) across 7 world regions. In total, 21 countries conducted repeat surveys (Micronesia: four surveys; Benin: three, the remaining 19: two each). Step 1 of the survey is questionnairebased and includes demographic variables and behavioral information on diet, physical activity, smoking, alcohol consumption, and disease status. Step 2 focuses on physical measurements, including anthropometry, resting blood pressure and heart rate.

South Asia Biobank

SAB is a large-scale multi-center cohort study investigating determinants of chronic diseases in South Asian populations (20). Between 2018 and 2023, the SAB study recruited 205,356 adults aged 18 years and above, from 564 surveillance sites across four countries or five test regions (Bangladesh, North India, South India, Pakistan, and Sri Lanka). At the baseline visit, participants attended local study assessment centers in the fasting state, for a single visit, during which they completed a structured assessment that was standardized across all settings. Data include self-report (demographics, diet, physical activity, smoking, alcohol, disease status), physical measurements (anthropometry, RHR, blood pressure, lung function), and device-based physical activity measures (accelerometry) in a subsample.

Measurements

Resting Heart Rate

RHR was measured as the pulse rate during seated rest when blood pressure was assessed using automated sphygmomanometers. Measurements were performed in triplicate in Step 2 in WHO STEPS and during the baseline clinical visit in SAB. Values below 30 bpm and above 150 bpm were discarded as invalid. The minimum pulse rate across valid measures for each individual was used as RHR in the present analyses to reflect the most rested state. We also conducted a sensitivity analysis using the median value.

Stratifying variables

RHR was described in different population subgroups: Sex (male, female), age group (18-24, 25-44, 45-64, 65+), BMI groups (underweight [<18.5, normal [18.5-24.9], overweight [25-29.9], obese[30+]), self-reported moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) as assessed by the global physical activity questionnaire (meeting/not meeting 150 min/week MVPA guideline), dietary intake (consume five or more servings of fruits and vegetables daily/ does not consume five or more servings of fruits and vegetables daily/ missing), smoking status (smoker/nonsmoker), alcohol status (drinker/non-drinker), marital status (single/married or cohabiting/divorced or separated/widowed), occupation (government/non-government employee/self-employed/ homemaker/unemployed/ Student/Retired/Non-paid/Missing), and education (no formal schooling, less than primary school completed, primary school completed, secondary school completed, college, postgraduate, missing). Levels for some covariates (e.g., occupation and education) differed between datasets due to survey-specific categorisations but were harmonised for analysis where possible.

In WHO STEPS, countries were also aggregated into regions (Sub-Saharan Africa, Central and East Asia, East Europe and West Asia, Latin and South America, Middle East North Africa, Small Island Nations, South and Southeast Asia) to describe broader place variation. Time (era) was parameterised as survey year centered on 2010. Income was parameterised using countryspecific classification (low, lower-middle, upper-middle, high).

In SAB, test region (Pakistan, North India, South India, Sri Lanka, and Bangladesh) was used to explore place differences. Time was expressed as calendar year centered on 2021 (mid-survey year) to capture any secular trends over the short time span of this fieldwork, whereas seasonal variation within year was captured as month of year. Personal income was parameterised in country-specific quartiles. Prevalent metabolic disease status (yes/no) was concatenated from clinical interview questions of hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, and type 2 diabetes. Fat mass was derived from bioelectric impedance and expressed relative to height squared as fat mass index (FMI) to provide an alternative adiposity measure to BMI. Physical activity was also measured objectively with a wrist-worn accelerometer in a subsample (n=22,183); MVPA was defined as wrist acceleration above 125 mg, aggregated in 5-sec analysis epochs and expressed as average weekly MVPA duration (min/week). Lung function, another proxy indicator of cardiorespiratory fitness, was measured by spirometry. Forced vital capacity (FVC) and forced expiratory volume (FEV1) were measured in triplicate, and the highest value across reproducible trials (within 0.15L of the second-best value) was retained per participant. FEV/FVC ratio was derived from these and categorised as high (<0.5), medium (0.5 to 0.7), and low (>0.7) obstruction to distinguish levels of airflow obstruction (21).

Statistical Analysis

SAB and WHO data were analysed separately.

Regional differences

Sex-stratified RHR percentiles (5 th , 25 th , 50 th , 75 th , 95 th ) were calculated and visualized using boxplots, violin plots, and line plots, stratified by population subgroup. The association with age was modelled using cubic splines with three degrees of freedom, fitted as a three-way interaction with sex and world region. In SAB, the same specification was applied but with test region to examine patterns between SAB test regions. Place was examined by country and world region.

The BMI-RHR association was examined ecologically to assess if RHR may rank countries by this NCD risk factor.

To align WHO and SAB results, common analytical methods were applied where possible, with extensions for more detailed information in SAB. To examine influence of survey approach, RHR distributions were compared in countries (Bangladesh and Sri Lanka) which had data from both WHO and SAB using medians (with percentiles), violin plots, boxplots, and Wilcoxon ranksum tests, stratified by sex, age group, and survey.

Population subgroups

BMI was examined both categorically (underweight, normal, overweight, obese) within the multilevel models described below, and continuously. For the continuous analysis, cubic splines were fitted separately within each sex, adjusting for country (WHO) or test region (SAB). For SAB, additional associations of RHR with FMI, lung function categories and device-based physical activity tertiles were explored. Potential regional heterogeneity in associations was explored by region-stratified analyses.

Time-related variation in RHR

In WHO STEPS, population median RHR trends over time (survey year centered on 2010) were visualized via line plots in the 19 countries with repeat surveys; a subset regression model (countries with repeats) specifically assessed secular trends. In SAB, seasonal variation in RHR was examined by test region using both cosinor models and time-of-year categories (Sept-Nov, Dec-Feb, Mar-May, June-Aug), with adjustment for calendar year (centered on 2021) to capture any short-term secular patterns.

Multivariable regression analyses

We examined RHR patterns in relation to all above-considered explanatory variables in sexstratified regression models. We used generalized linear multilevel models with a Gamma distribution and log link mutually adjusted for all explanatory variables, levels specified as individual and country to account for clustering by country. In WHO, analyses were weighted by population size. As SAB is a community-based study not necessarily designed for national representativeness, unweighted analyses were used.

Utility of resting heart rate to routine physical activity surveillance

In SAB, crude linear regression models evaluated RHR as a potential additional predictor of device-based physical activity, comparing models with GPAQ alone and RHR + GPAQ. For completeness, we repeated this evaluation swapping GPAQ as the dependent variable and treating device-based MVPA as predictor. Model performance was evaluated via R² values. Analyses were stratified by sex and age group (18-44 and 45+ years) and additionally by country, to explore RHR's complementary value to self-reported or device-based physical activity measures in population subgroups.

RESULTS

A total of 363,462 and 203,524 participants are included in the present analysis for WHO and SAB, respectively (flowchart, supplementary figure S1). In WHO STEPS, included participants were slightly older, less obese, more likely to be married, and less likely to smoke compared to participants excluded due to missing data. In SAB, included participants were more obese, more likely to be married or cohabiting, and exhibited higher smoking rates (men only) than excluded participants (Supplementary material S1).

Women had higher RHR (WHO: 76 bpm, SAB: 79) than men (WHO: 72 bpm, SAB: 70).

Expanded results are in the appendix (Supplementary Material S2). Sex-stratified multivariable regression analyses were conducted for each dataset (Tables 1 and 2). Using the median RHR instead of the minimum RHR of 3 readings did not materially change associations for any of the analyses (data not shown).

Age-related patterns and geographic variation

Across geographical regions, RHR exhibited a curvilinear relationship with age in men, rising through early and middle adulthood before declining in older age groups, whereas women showed a consistent decline in RHR with older age (Figure 1). The age at which RHR peaked in men varied by region, but the curvilinear shape was broadly consistent. This sex divergence was evident at the region level but not always at the country level (Figure S2), where betweencountry variation obscured the pattern. In SAB, the divergence was different in the pooled panel where men showed a rise but then a decline in older ages whereas women showed progressively lower RHR across older age groups. Age remained independently associated with RHR after mutual adjustment for all covariates in both datasets (Tables 1 and 2).

Associations with age varied by SAB test region (Supplementary Figure S3). Men in India showed curvilinear patterns resembling those in WHO regions, with a midlife peak followed by decline. Men in Bangladesh and Sri Lanka showed broadly monotonic declines, and men in Pakistan showed a plateau through midlife before declining. In women, patterns were largely monotonic across all SAB regions, consistent with the WHO regional findings.

The lowest median RHR values were observed in the Small Island Nations region (men: 68 bpm; women: 72 bpm) and the highest in MENA and Sub-Saharan Africa (men: 80 bpm; women: 86 bpm), with some evidence of multimodality; however country-level distributions were not multimodal (Figure S4a). Within SAB, Pakistan had the highest median RHR (men: 80 bpm; women: 86 bpm) and Bangladesh the lowest for both sexes (men: 64 bpm, women: 75 bpm), with intermediate values across the remaining test regions (Figure S4b). In the two countries with data from both surveys (Bangladesh and Sri Lanka), men generally had lower RHR in SAB than in WHO STEPS. Significant differences were found across all age and sex strata (Figure S5). RHR distributions and density plots stratified by age group, sex, and region are shown in Supplementary Figure S6.

Weight status

The relationship between BMI and RHR was non-linear in most populations. In WHO, cubic spline models fitted separately by sex showed a RHR nadir at a BMI of 20.7 kg/m 2 in men and 22.2 kg/m 2 in women; 26.6% and 31.9% of the sample are below these BMI thresholds, respectively. In SAB, RHR nadirs at BMI of 21.0 kg/m 2 in men and 23.5 kg/m 2 in women were observed, with 29.3% and 36.9% of the sample, respectively, falling below these (Figure 2).

Region-stratified splines showed largely similar results across WHO regions and SAB test regions (Supplementary Figure S7). The category-based multilevel models were broadly consistent with this pattern showing higher RHR in underweight, overweight, and obese participants compared to normal-weight in both datasets (Tables 1 and 2). The individual-level association with BMI was not apparent at the ecological level where ranking countries by median BMI revealed no clear gradient in median RHR, including when stratified by age group and sex (Figure S2). In SAB, however, test center-level RHR was positively associated with center-level BMI in both men and women (Supplementary Figure S8a) and this was also observed when using FMI as the measure of adiposity (Supplementary Figure S8b).

Physical activity

Not meeting physical activity guidelines was associated with higher RHR in both sexes (WHO and SAB) (Tables 1 and 2). RHR was also inversely associated with device-measured physical activity in the SAB accelerometry subsample (Supplementary Figure S9).

Lung function and disease status (SAB)

Better lung function, an alternative indicator of cardiorespiratory fitness, was associated with lower RHR (Supplementary Figure S9), whereas prevalent disease (hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, type 2 diabetes) was associated with higher RHR in both sexes (Table 2).

Socio-economic status

Unemployment was associated with higher RHR in women but not men in WHO STEPS, whereas in SAB, it was associated with higher RHR in both men and women (Tables 1 and 2).

Agricultural workers, cleaners, and unemployed participants generally had the lowest RHR. Widowed status was associated with higher RHR in men but not in women in WHO STEPS. In SAB, however, widowed women, but not men, had lower RHR.

Diet, smoking, and alcohol

RHR did not differ by fruit and vegetable intake (consuming at least 5 servings of fruits and vegetables a day). In SAB, smoking was associated with lower RHR (more so in women).

Alcohol consumption was associated with higher RHR in men in both datasets, with the strongest association observed in frequent drinkers in SAB. In women, associations were slightly lower in both datasets (Tables 1 and 2).

Variation across time

In 19 WHO countries with repeat survey data, RHR was stable or slightly declining, with a modest decrease only in men (-0.4 bpm per year) (Figure 3a). In SAB, RHR was higher mid-year (Table 2, Figure 3b), in both categorical (by month) and continuous (cosinor) analyses. Seasonal fluctuations were more pronounced for test regions further from the equator. Seasonal variation findings were supported by categorical analyses (Table 2).

Contribution of resting heart rate to routine physical activity surveillance

To explore if RHR could add to the estimation of physical activity, we modeled how much variance in MVPA RHR could explain in SAB. Overall, RHR was a modest (negative) predictor of MVPA (Supplementary Table S1, S2). In the model with both RHR and GPAQ-based MVPA, both terms were predictors of device-based MVPA.

DISCUSSION

Main Findings

This study integrates WHO STEPS surveys and the South Asia Biobank to describe global and regional distributions of RHR and evaluate its potential utility in non-communicable disease surveillance. Age-related patterns diverged by sex, with RHR increasing and then declining with age in a curvilinear pattern in men across most regions, while in women RHR was steadily declining with age. Higher BMI and insufficient physical activity were universally associated with elevated RHR. Repeat cross-sectional survey results indicated long-term temporal stability with modest declines in men but seasonal variation was observed in non-equatorial regions. RHR added to the prediction of device-based physical activity, over and above that of self-reported physical activity. As RHR is already widely available in large population surveys and an easy-tomeasure vital sign, our results suggest RHR may have utility in routine global health surveillance.

Across the lifespan

In most regions, men exhibited a curvilinear age-RHR pattern, with higher RHR in midlife and lower values at older ages, whereas women showed a consistent decline. The rise in RHR in men to a midlife peak followed by decline mirrors patterns in two large wearable cohorts, 92,457 Fitbit users in the US and 4.8 million Fitbit users geolocated across 217 countries and territories (22, 23). RHR patterns in women are less consistent. The US cohort showed a midlife peak while the global cohort showed a plateau or steady decline (22, 23). Our findings extend these observations to population-based samples from low-and middle-income settings that are underrepresented in the literature.

The male curvilinear pattern likely reflects the competing physiological processes across the lifespan. Age-related reductions in parasympathetic tone elevate RHR through midlife, while selective survival may produce an apparent decline in the oldest age groups, given the established association between elevated RHR and cardiovascular mortality (18, 24, 25). The more linear decline observed in women may reflect sex-specific autonomic regulation, potentially mediated by hormonal influences on cardiovascular control (26). These divergent patterns suggest that sex differences in RHR reflect differences in cardiovascular ageing which may have implications for potential reference standards.

Within SAB, the association between RHR and age varied substantially across test regions in men. Only Sri Lankan men showed a monotonic decline but the relationship was inverse Ushaped in other countries. Women across all test regions showed monotonic declines, consistent with the WHO regional findings. As cardiometabolic disease patterns also vary across South Asia, in India for example diabetes prevalence ranges from 4.8% to 26.4% between states (27), healthy volunteer bias may also contribute to observed associations with varying response patterns in different test regions within SAB (20).

Associations with modifiable factors

Higher BMI was associated with elevated RHR across both datasets, in both sexes, across all regions. In addition, insufficient physical activity was associated with higher RHR in both datasets, including when assessed by accelerometry in the SAB subsample, an assessment of physical behaviour that relates more strongly to health outcomes than self-reported behaviour (14). Prevalent metabolic disease (hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, and type 2 diabetes) was associated with higher RHR in SAB participants, consistent with prior evidence that RHR is associated with cardiometabolic risk (15, 16). Taken together, these associations suggest that RHR captures meaningful biological information across diverse populations and measurement contexts.

Temporality

In SAB, RHR was higher in June-August and this variation was more pronounced in test regions further from the equator. Similar results were observed in the largest study of RHR using wearables where summer-winter differences were 0.55 bpm in the Northern and 0.74 bpm in the Southern hemisphere (23). However, that study showed a peak in local winter, whereas SAB RHR peaked in summer. This may reflect heat stress or influence of time of day of readings (day vs night) (28). With that said, studies observing RHR differences across the year are inconsistent.

Vukosavljević et al. found RHR to rise in unusually hot and cold temperatures while another study in Burkina Faso found no association between heat exposure and nocturnal heart rate (23, 29). Irrespective of mechanism, an unbiased annual estimate of RHR requires repeat sampling across the year or statistical correction for season. WHO STEPS fieldwork is not not usually conducted to achieve full seasonal coverage, and date of measurement was also missing in most WHO data, hindering quantification of seasonal variation in this set (30).

In the WHO data with repeat surveys over longer time frames, RHR was broadly stable over time, with only a modest decline in men. This decline in men contrasts with recent literature documenting declining cardiorespiratory fitness globally that suggest the opposite (31). It is possible that differences in measurement protocols and participant selection across survey waves may partially explain this phenomenon (32, 33). For example, participants in later survey years may be more or less rested than participants in earlier survey years due to different circumstances in the home where the measurement took place, and such biases over time could mask true changes in population RHR levels. Confirming genuine secular trends in RHR requires standardized measurement conditions that do not yet exist uniformly across national surveys, and it could be debated if survey designs have captured the true resting state of participants and consistently so over time (14). Although RHR is relatively robust to routine stressors shifting by less than 2% after hard training, it rises by around 6% during illness (34). As personal wearables with continuous heart rate monitoring capabilities are becoming more common, such data could support our findings with more robust measurements of the true resting heart rate level, for example during sleep, which is also the estimate of RHR that correlates more closely with cardiorespiratory fitness levels (14).

Implications for Global Health Surveillance

The utility of RHR as a global surveillance tool relies on its accessibility and its biological validity. RHR is routinely captured alongside blood pressure in surveys like WHO STEPS, requires no additional equipment or specialist training, and is feasible in low-resource settings where VO2max testing is not (14). RHR is altered with physical training and is prospectively associated with health outcomes (18, 35). Elevated RHR in high-risk regions, particularly in Pakistan where both RHR and adiposity were highest, underscores its utility. These patterns identify subpopulations at elevated metabolic disease risk where NCD burden is high and fitness data are scarce (12, 36).

We also demonstrate the complementary value of RHR to self-reported physical activity which further strengthens the case for its use in routine surveillance. This was most evident in men.

Device-measured activity in women was found to be poorly explained by all examined predictors in our study. This is possibly due to wrist-worn accelerometers detecting upper-body and domestic tasks that impose less sustained whole-body movement and cardiovascular load (37).

The growing affordability of commercial wearables that continuously measure both physical activity and heart rate opens additional opportunities for personalized CVD risk stratification and community-based screening (38,39). Currently, however, most users are concentrated in highincome populations, with limited coverage in South Asia and Africa where NCD burden is high (23, 40). Whilst wearable ownership is steadily increasing, surveys such as WHO STEPS and SAB are feasible avenues for capturing objective health indicators in populations underrepresented in the field.

Strengths and Limitations

The strengths of this study include its large sample and global coverage. The use of population weighting enhances generalizability. Repeated cross-sectional surveys provides valuable insights into population-level time trends in RHR for routine health surveillance. Limitations include the cross-sectional observational design that limits causal inference, and differences between measurement protocols that may introduce heterogeneity and impact comparability. Studies using follow-up data and linkage to health outcomes in non-Western populations are needed to assert associations and strengthen causal interpretations.

What is already known on this topic

• Cardiorespiratory fitness is strongly associated with CVD and mortality but remains understudied globally, particularly in non-Western populations.

• Resting heart rate is a feasible proxy indicator for cardiorespiratory fitness, routinely captured in national surveys, but its global epidemiology and surveillance potential have not been systematically described.

What this study adds

• Globally, RHR generally ranges between 65 and 80 bpm • Men and women show divergent age-related RHR trajectories, with a curvilinear pattern in men • Higher BMI, insufficient physical activity, and prevalent metabolic disease are consistently associated with elevated RHR • RHR can improve the prediction of objectively measured physical activity, over and above that of self-report • RHR offers a low-cost, accessible tool for fitness surveillance in low-resource settings.
