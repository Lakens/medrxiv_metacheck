Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Preclinical studies implicate NLRP3 inflammasome activation, which drives release of interleukin-1β and interleukin-18, in vascular inflammation and atherosclerosis, and selective NLRP3 inhibitors are now in clinical development. However, whether lowering NLRP3 activity reduces coronary artery disease (CAD) risk in humans is unknown. We used human genetics to estimate this effect and its mediating pathways.

A genetic NLRP3 activity score was derived by combining the effects of eight cis-NLRP3 variants on NLRP3 expression and three inflammatory biomarkers: C-reactive protein, glycoprotein acetylation, and neutrophil count. The score was validated against circulating interleukin-1β and interleukin-18. Drugtarget Mendelian randomization was used to estimate its association with CAD, cardiometabolic traits and other diseases studied in NLRP3 inhibitor trials. Mediation by cardiometabolic risk factors was quantified using the product-of-coefficients method.

A lower genetically proxied NLRP3 activity score was associated with lower circulating interleukin-1β and interleukin-18, but with higher CAD risk (OR = 1.21, 95% CI 1.02-1.45, P = 0.032). It was also associated with higher systolic blood pressure, apolipoprotein B levels, and type 2 diabetes risk, which together mediated an estimated 87% of the association with CAD. The residual direct association with CAD was attenuated (OR = 1.03, 95% CI 0.85-1.24, P = 0.79). In contrast, a lower score was nominally associated with lower risk of gout, pericarditis, and Parkinson's disease.

Lifelong lower genetically proxied NLRP3 activity was associated with higher CAD risk, largely mediated by adverse cardiometabolic effects, with no evidence of protection after accounting for them. These findings do not provide genetic support for lowering NLRP3 activity to reduce CAD risk.

Introduction

Despite optimal management of established cardiovascular risk factors, including low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and blood pressure, patients with established coronary artery disease (CAD) remain at considerable risk of recurrent cardiovascular events. Persistent vascular inflammation, reflected by elevated high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP) levels, is recognized as a major contributor to this residual cardiovascular risk 1 . The CANTOS trial provided clinical proof of concept that selective inhibition of interleukin-1β (IL-1β), a pro-inflammatory cytokine, reduces recurrent cardiovascular events independently of lipid lowering 2 . Trials of the broad anti-inflammatory drug colchicine have yielded mixed results. Colchicine reduced cardiovascular events after recent myocardial infarction in COLCOT 3 and among patients with chronic coronary disease in LoDoCo2 4 , but not after acute myocardial infarction in CLEAR-SYNERGY 5 or after ischaemic stroke in CONVINCE 6 and CHANCE-3 7 . Recently, the phase 3 ZEUS trial found no reduction in cardiovascular events with interleukin-6 (IL-6) inhibition in patients with atherosclerotic disease, chronic kidney disease, and elevated hsCRP 8 . Together, these findings suggest that cardiovascular benefit depends on both the inflammatory pathway targeted and the clinical context, rather than on inflammation lowering per se.

One inflammatory pathway that has gained considerable attention is centred on NLRP3. NLRP3 is the sensor protein of the NLRP3 inflammasome, a multiprotein complex comprising NLRP3, the adaptor ASC, and caspase-1, whose activation promotes the maturation and release of IL-1β and interleukin-18 (IL-18) 9 . These cytokines promote downstream inflammatory signalling pathways, including IL-6 signalling, which drives the acute-phase response characterized by elevated inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and glycoprotein acetylation (GlycA), a composite measure of glycosylated acute-phase proteins 10, 11 . Concurrently, NLRP3 inflammasome activation increases the production of circulating myeloid cells, predominantly neutrophils 12.

In preclinical models of atherosclerosis, genetic deficiency or pharmacological inhibition of NLRP3 has been reported to reduce vascular inflammation and plaque burden 13, 14 . Together with the evidence linking NLRP3 inflammasome-related cytokines to vascular inflammation, these findings have motivated the development of small-molecule inhibitors that act directly on the NLRP3 sensor, several of which have entered clinical testing 15 . However, no phase 3 trial has yet established whether selective NLRP3 inhibition reduces cardiovascular events in humans.

In the absence of large randomized clinical trials, drug-target Mendelian randomization (MR) can provide genetic evidence on whether a target causally influences disease risk, using naturally occurring variation near drug-target genes to proxy differences in target activity. This approach has precedent in cardiovascular drug development. For example, genetic variation in PCSK9 supported LDL-C lowering through PCSK9 inhibition as a strategy expected to reduce cardiovascular risk 16 . Conversely, genetic proxies of lower lipoprotein-associated phospholipase A2 (Lp-PLA2) activity were not associated with lower coronary heart disease risk, consistent with the lack of benefit observed for pharmacological Lp-PLA2 inhibition 17.

Human genetic evidence on NLRP3 is limited. A study based on a single variant associated with NLRP3 expression in blood linked higher NLRP3 activity to higher cardiovascular mortality 18 . In this study, we combined the effects of multiple variants on NLRP3 expression with their effects on downstream inflammatory biomarkers into an NLRP3 activity score, validated it against IL-1β and IL-18, and used drug-target MR to test its association with CAD, the pathways involved, and diseases for which NLRP3 inhibitors have been, or are being, evaluated.

Methods

Study design

We used drug-target MR to test whether genetically proxied NLRP3 activity, represented by an NLRP3 activity score, was associated with CAD risk (Figure 1). We constructed the score from eight cisacting variants at the NLRP3 locus, using their independent associations with NLRP3 expression and well-powered circulating inflammatory biomarkers downstream of NLRP3 inflammasome activation (Supplementary Information). We validated this score against canonical NLRP3 inflammasome effector cytokines IL-1β and IL-18 before estimating its association with CAD. We further assessed mediation by cardiometabolic risk factors and explored additional diseases for which selective NLRP3 inhibitors have been or are being evaluated in clinical trials.

Data sources

Across analyses, we prioritized large publicly available genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary statistics from European-ancestry participants to maximize statistical power while maintaining ancestry consistency with the instrument-construction datasets. Analyses included men and women combined, because sex-specific summary statistics were not available for most datasets and would have reduced statistical power.

Datasets for instrument construction and validation

Instrument construction used GWAS summary statistics for circulating CRP levels, GlycA concentrations, neutrophil count, and whole-blood NLRP3 expression. Summary statistics for CRP levels were obtained from a UK Biobank and CHARGE meta-analysis including 575,531 European-ancestry participants 19 . Summary statistics for GlycA concentrations were obtained from 434,646 Europeanancestry UK Biobank participants, with GlycA quantified using the Nightingale Health nuclear magnetic resonance metabolomics platform 20 . Neutrophil count summary statistics were obtained from 519,288 European-ancestry participants of a multi-ancestry blood-cell trait meta-analysis 21 . Whole-blood NLRP3 expression quantitative trait locus data were obtained from 4,732 European-ancestry INTERVAL participants, with expression measured by RNA sequencing 22 . For instrument validation, we used plasma protein GWAS summary statistics from the full UK Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project cohort, comprising up to 51,637 predominantly European-ancestry participants per protein profiled using the Olink Explore platform, to maximize statistical power and variant coverage 23 . IL-1β and IL-18 were available only in this smaller proteomic dataset and were therefore used to validate the score rather than to construct it. Further details are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Datasets for disease outcomes

The primary disease outcome was CAD, for which we combined four European-ancestry datasets: a CAD GWAS meta-analysis including 181,522 cases and 984,168 controls 24 , coronary atherosclerosis in the Million Veteran Program (MVP), including 124,302 cases and 300,039 controls 25 , coronary atherosclerosis in All of Us, including 19,823 cases and 150,340 controls 26 , and coronary atherosclerosis in FinnGen (release 12), including 63,307 cases and 416,171 controls 27 . Variant-CAD association estimates were combined by fixed-effect inverse-variance weighted meta-analysis, and the resulting metaanalysed summary statistics served as the primary CAD outcome for MR. As a measure of subclinical atherosclerosis, we obtained summary statistics for imaging-defined atherosclerosis from the Swedish CArdioPulmonary bioImage Study (SCAPIS), comprising coronary artery calcification (n = 26,000), coronary plaque burden quantified as the segment involvement score on CT angiography (n = 24,811), and ultrasound-assessed carotid plaque (n = 26,807), which have no sample overlap with the datasets used for instrument construction or with the CAD datasets 28 . Further details are provided in Supplementary Table 2. We additionally obtained European-ancestry summary statistics for 15 diseases for which selective NLRP3 inhibitors have been, or are being, evaluated in clinical trials. Six were taken from MVP 25 , and the others from GWAS of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis 29 , asthma 30 , COVID-19 31 , knee osteoarthritis 32 , Parkinson's disease 33 , pericarditis 34 , rheumatoid arthritis 35 , type 2 diabetes 36 , and ulcerative colitis 37 . Further details are provided in Supplementary Table 3.

Datasets for cardiometabolic and lifestyle traits

We used publicly available European-ancestry GWAS summary statistics for cardiometabolic and lifestyle traits relevant to CAD risk, including lipid traits 20, 38, 39 , blood pressure 40 , body mass index 41 , type 2 diabetes 36 , smoking 42 , and alcohol consumption 42 . These traits were screened for association with the NLRP3 activity score, and associated traits (P < 0.05) were considered as candidate mediators. Further details are provided in Supplementary Table 4.

Genetic instrument construction

To construct a genetic proxy for NLRP3 activity, we used whole-blood NLRP3 expression and three well-powered inflammatory traits that are elevated downstream of NLRP3 inflammasome activation and have strong statistical evidence of genetic association at the NLRP3 locus: circulating CRP and GlycA levels, and blood neutrophil count (Supplementary Information). For each trait, variants within ±150 kb of NLRP3 were clumped for linkage disequilibrium (LD) at r² < 0.1 using 11,863 European-ancestry whole-genome sequences from INTERVAL as the LD reference panel. We merged the clumped signals across traits and retained variants associated at genome-wide significance (P < 5×10⁻⁸) with at least two traits, yielding eight variants for instrument construction (Supplementary Figure 1). To combine the four traits into a single exposure, we applied principal component analysis (PCA) to the centred and scaled trait-specific genetic effect estimates and used the first principal component loadings as data-driven trait weights. The resulting NLRP3 activity score was scaled to CRP, such that a one-unit decrease corresponds approximately to a one standard deviation decrease in CRP. Standard errors were estimated using the delta method 43 . Instrument strength was summarized by the F-statistic for the eight variants jointly, while accounting for their residual LD 44 . As a methodological positive control, we applied the same procedure at the IL1RN locus to construct a genetically proxied interleukin-1 receptor antagonist (IL1Ra) activity score and tested it against plasma IL1Ra concentration and against gout and rheumatoid arthritis.

Colocalization analyses

To evaluate whether inflammatory biomarker associations at the NLRP3 locus share a causal variant, we performed multi-trait colocalization using HyPrColoc 45 (version 0.0.2) across whole-blood NLRP3 expression, circulating CRP levels, GlycA concentrations, and blood neutrophil count, using variants within ±200 kb of NLRP3. To isolate the association cluster within the NLRP3 gene from the stronger peak upstream of it, we conditioned the NLRP3 expression summary statistics on that peak's lead variant (rs6689545) using GCTA-COJO 46 (version 1.94.1) with the INTERVAL reference panel. The conditioned expression and unconditioned biomarker summary statistics were then used for colocalization. HyPrColoc was run with default variant-specific priors (prior.1 = 1×10 -4 , prior.c = 0.02), and a posterior probability > 0.8 that the traits share a causal variant was interpreted as strong evidence of colocalization. To test for colocalizing signals elsewhere in the region, we repeated HyPrColoc, with the same inputs and priors, in the flanking regions from 200 kb to 1 Mb upstream and downstream of NLRP3.

Mendelian randomization analyses

We used two-sample MR to estimate associations of the NLRP3 activity score with circulating biomarkers, cardiometabolic traits, and disease outcomes. All estimates were oriented to represent lower genetically proxied NLRP3 activity, mirroring pharmacological inhibition. The primary estimator was random-effects inverse-variance weighted (IVW) MR. Weighted median MR was used as a robustness analysis and the MR-Egger intercept was used to test whether variant effects on the outcome were proportional to their effects on the exposure. MR-Egger was not used as a causal estimator, as its instrument strength independent of direct effect (InSIDE) assumption is not well suited to variants within a single gene region 47 . IVW and MR-Egger accounted for residual LD using the INTERVAL reference panel. An instrument variant missing from an outcome dataset was replaced by the variant in highest LD with it in the same panel (r² ≥ 0.9 within 50 kb), where one was available. For CAD, we additionally performed leave-one-out analyses, and repeated instrument selection and score construction at relaxed LD clumping thresholds (r² 0.2-0.6). We also repeated the biomarker, cytokine and CAD analyses using a conventional instrument based on NLRP3 expression alone (Supplementary Information). Estimates are reported as betas for continuous outcomes, odds ratios (ORs) for binary outcomes, and proportional odds ratios for ordinal outcomes, with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Analyses used the MendelianRandomization 44 (version 0.10.0) and MVMR 48 (version 0.4.2) R packages.

Evaluation of the NLRP3 activity score

We first assessed internal consistency by estimating associations of a lower NLRP3 activity score with the downstream inflammatory biomarkers used for score construction: circulating CRP levels, GlycA concentrations, and blood neutrophil count. We then evaluated circulating IL-1β, IL-18, and IL-6 levels as external biological readouts. IL-1β and IL-18 were selected because they are canonical cytokine products of NLRP3 inflammasome activation, while IL-6 was included as a downstream cytokine linking interleukin-1 signalling to the acute-phase response [9] [10] [11].

Mediation analysis

To investigate whether the association between a lower genetically proxied NLRP3 activity score and higher CAD risk could be explained by established cardiovascular risk factors, we performed mediation analyses for systolic blood pressure (SBP), apolipoprotein B (ApoB), and type 2 diabetes (T2D). These traits were associated with the NLRP3 activity score in the cardiometabolic and lifestyle trait screen and are established risk factors for CAD 49 . ApoB was chosen as the single lipid mediator 50 , and SBP was preferred to the correlated diastolic blood pressure (Supplementary Information). We used the product-ofcoefficients method to estimate indirect effects 51 , multiplying the MR effect of the score on each mediator by the mutually adjusted effect of that mediator on CAD from multivariable MR (MVMR) 48 . MVMR used genome-wide significant variants for SBP, ApoB and T2D from their source GWAS 20, 36, 40 , and instrument strength was assessed with the conditional F-statistic 48 (Supplementary Information). All CAD effects came from the meta-analysis of the four CAD datasets, and all mediation analyses used the logodds scale. The joint indirect effect was the sum of the three indirect effects, the proportion mediated was the joint indirect effect divided by the total effect, and the residual direct effect was the total effect minus the joint indirect effect. Uncertainty in the joint indirect effect was estimated with the multivariate delta method 52 , and the 95% CI of the proportion mediated by Monte Carlo simulation.

NLRP3 predicted loss-of-function analysis

To test whether rare NLRP3 predicted loss-of-function (pLOF) variants were associated with circulating inflammatory readouts, we performed gene-based burden tests for CRP levels, GlycA concentrations, and blood neutrophil count among UK Biobank participants with whole exome sequencing (WES) data and available biomarker measurements. Burden tests were performed using SAIGE-GENE+ (version 1.5.0) 53 . Effect estimates are per unit of the weighted burden score. pLOF variants were defined as frameshift, stop-gained, splice donor, or splice acceptor variants using variant consequences reported in gnomAD (version 4.1) 54 . Analyses were restricted to individuals of genetically inferred European ancestry 55 . Analogous pLOF burden analyses were performed for all protein-coding genes within ±110 kb of NLRP3. Phenotype processing is described in the Supplementary Information.

NLRP3 gain-of-function analysis

Pathogenic gain-of-function variants in NLRP3 were obtained from the INFEVERS registry 56 and Cosson et al. 57 . Carriers were identified among UK Biobank participants with WES data and available biomarker measurements. Only one of these gain-of-function variants was observed in UK Biobank, corresponding to GRCh38 chr1:247424765 C>T (p.Ala439Val). All three carriers were heterozygous. Due to the limited number of carriers, CRP levels, GlycA concentrations, and blood neutrophil counts were reported as percentiles relative to non-carriers. Phenotype processing is described in the Supplementary Information.

Proteome-wide association and over-representation analyses

To characterize broader circulating protein changes associated with lower genetically proxied NLRP3 activity, we performed two-sample MR using plasma protein GWAS summary statistics from the UK Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project 23 . Associations were estimated using IVW MR with LD correction, as described above. Proteins were considered significant using a Bonferroni correction based on the effective number of independent protein tests 58 , yielding a threshold of P < 2.7×10 -5 . Significant proteins were tested for enrichment of Gene Ontology Biological Process terms separately by direction of association. Details of the effective test correction and over-representation analysis are provided in the Supplementary Information.

Exploratory analyses across diseases under investigation for NLRP3 inhibition

Disease outcomes for exploratory MR analyses were selected based on diseases for which selective NLRP3 inhibitors have been, or are being, evaluated in registered clinical trials, as identified from a structured search of four clinical trial registries conducted on 28 April 2026 (see Supplementary Information for full details). Identified diseases were included where publicly available GWAS summary statistics of sufficient sample size were available. The included exploratory outcomes and corresponding GWAS sources are provided in Supplementary Table 3. As these analyses were exploratory, nominal Pvalues are reported without adjustment for multiple testing.

Software and statistical significance

Results

Genetic associations with inflammatory biomarkers at the NLRP3 locus

Using publicly available GWAS summary statistics (Supplementary Table 1), we identified associations of variants at the NLRP3 locus with whole-blood NLRP3 expression (rs58546652, P = 1.04×10 -58 , n = 4,732), circulating CRP levels (rs12239046, P = 1.34×10 -87 , n = 575,531), GlycA concentrations (rs56188865, P = 4.7×10 -28 , n = 434,646), and blood neutrophil count (rs56188865, P = 1.93×10 -43 , n = 519,288) (Figure 2A). Multi-trait colocalization across NLRP3 expression and the three circulating inflammatory biomarkers showed strong evidence of a shared causal variant between the four traits (posterior probability = 0.97). No additional colocalizing signals were observed in the flanking regions between 200 kb and 1 Mb on either side of NLRP3 (posterior probability < 0.13), indicating that the inflammatory biomarker associations shared across traits in this region are centred on NLRP3 (Supplementary Figure 2). Together, these findings support a coherent NLRP3-linked inflammatory signal at the locus and identify variants suitable for genetic instrument construction.

Genetic instrument selection and validation

Because neither NLRP3 expression nor any single circulating biomarker specifically measures NLRP3 activity, we constructed a composite genetic exposure combining whole-blood NLRP3 expression with downstream circulating inflammatory readouts (Supplementary Information). We identified genetic signals associated with at least two of the four traits (P < 5×10 -8 ). From these signals, we selected eight approximately independent variants (r 2 < 0.1, Supplementary Figure 1). We then applied PCA to their effect estimates across the four traits. The first principal component explained 96.8% of the variation across the traits' genetic associations. We used the weights derived from this component to combine these associations into the NLRP3 activity score (Figure 2B, Supplementary Table 5), which served as the exposure in subsequent MR analyses. The eight variants explained 8.3% of the variance in NLRP3 expression, 0.11% in CRP, 0.046% in GlycA, and 0.064% in neutrophil count, and the joint F-statistic for the score was 92.0, indicating a strong instrument.

To evaluate the NLRP3 activity score, we used two-sample MR to estimate its associations with downstream biomarkers. We first confirmed that a lower genetically proxied NLRP3 activity score was associated with lower CRP, GlycA, and neutrophil count, consistent with the score's construction (Figure 2C). We then tested associations with NLRP3-related cytokines that were not used in score construction. A lower genetically proxied NLRP3 activity score was associated with lower circulating IL-1β (β = -0.25, 95% CI -0.49 to -0.004, P = 0.046), IL-18 (β = -0.52, 95% CI -0.79 to -0.25, P = 0.00016) and IL-6 (β = -0.72, 95% CI -0.95 to -0.48, P = 2.8×10 -9 ). Effect estimates were directionally consistent across IVW and weighted median estimators (Supplementary Table 6). Together, these findings support the NLRP3 activity score as capturing the expected downstream inflammatory consequences of lower NLRP3 activity.

Association of the NLRP3 activity score with coronary artery disease risk

We performed two-sample MR to estimate the association between the genetically proxied NLRP3 activity score and CAD risk using a fixed-effect meta-analysis of four independent European-ancestry GWAS datasets, comprising 388,954 cases and 1,850,718 controls. Despite being associated with lower inflammatory biomarkers, a lower NLRP3 activity score was associated with higher CAD risk (OR = 1.21, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.45, P = 0.032) (Figure 3A). Estimates were directionally concordant across all four contributing datasets and were preserved under the weighted median estimator (OR = 1.23, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.42, P = 0.005). The MR-Egger intercept differed from zero (intercept = 0.009, 95% CI 0.002 to 0.016, P = 0.018), indicating that the variants' effects on CAD are not fully proportional to their effect on the NLRP3 activity score (Supplementary Table 7).

We next asked whether the inverse association was also observed for imaging-defined atherosclerosis in SCAPIS, an independent population-based imaging cohort. A lower genetically proxied NLRP3 activity score was associated with higher coronary artery calcification (β = 0.31, 95% CI 0.02 to 0.60, P = 0.034) and higher coronary plaque burden, measured as the segment involvement score on CT angiography (proportional odds ratio = 2.55, 95% CI 1.20 to 5.41, P = 0.015), with a directionally consistent but weaker association for carotid plaque (proportional odds ratio = 1.88, 95% CI 0.96 to 3.68, P = 0.066) (Figure 3B). The higher CAD risk associated with a lower NLRP3 activity score is therefore mirrored in imaging-defined atherosclerotic burden.

Mediation of the coronary artery disease association by cardiometabolic risk factors

To investigate potential mechanisms underlying the higher CAD risk, we examined associations of the NLRP3 activity score with established cardiometabolic risk factors 49, 59 . A lower genetically proxied NLRP3 activity score was associated with several adverse cardiometabolic traits, including higher ApoB levels (β = 0.14, 95% CI 0.06 to 0.22, P = 0.00078), higher SBP (β = 0.11, 95% CI 0.05 to 0.16, P = 0.00017), and higher T2D risk (OR = 1.26, 95% CI 1.09 to 1.46, P = 0.002), but showed no association with body mass index, smoking, or alcohol consumption (Figure 3C, Supplementary Table 8). To quantify how much of the association with higher CAD risk could be attributed to these cardiometabolic pathways, we performed mediation analysis using ApoB, SBP, and T2D as mediators, with each mediator-to-CAD effect estimated in mutually adjusted MVMR (Figure 3D). Conditional F-statistics were 15.4 for ApoB, 36.7 for SBP, and 37.2 for T2D, indicating strong instruments. Together, the mediators accounted for approximately 87% (95% CI 34%-459%) of the total association between lower genetically proxied NLRP3 activity score and higher CAD risk, with the largest indirect effect attributable to SBP (OR = 1.08, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.13) and smaller contributions from ApoB (OR = 1.05, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.08) and T2D (OR = 1.04, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.07) (Supplementary Table 9). Sequentially accounting for SBP, ApoB, and T2D attenuated the association, leaving a residual association that was no longer statistically significant (OR = 1.03, 95% CI 0.85 to 1.24, P = 0.79) (Figure 3E, Supplementary Table 9). Most of the higher CAD risk associated with a lower NLRP3 activity score was therefore attributable to adverse cardiometabolic effects, with little evidence of a residual direct effect.

Further validation of the NLRP3 activity score and robustness of the coronary artery disease association

We next examined whether the NLRP3 activity score and its association with CAD could be artefacts of the instrument rather than reflect NLRP3 biology. To test whether the inflammatory signal at the NLRP3 locus is driven by NLRP3 itself, we examined rare coding variants in the UK Biobank. Carriers of pLOF variants in NLRP3 had significantly lower CRP (β = -0.023, 95% CI -0.032 to -0.015, P = 9.4×10 -8 ), GlycA (β = -0.018, 95% CI -0.027 to -0.010, P = 2.8×10 -5 ), and neutrophil counts (β = -0.012, 95% CI -0.021 to -0.0036, P = 5.7×10 -3 ) than non-carriers (Figure 4A). Conversely, although carrier numbers were small, the three carriers of the gain-of-function variant had markedly elevated CRP and higher GlycA concentrations and neutrophil counts (Figure 4B), consistent with enhanced NLRP3 inflammasome activity. pLOF burden tests of the other protein-coding genes within ±110 kb of NLRP3 showed no associations with these biomarkers (Supplementary Figure 3), supporting NLRP3 as the gene driving the inflammatory biomarker associations at this locus.

As a methodological positive control, we asked whether our activity score MR approach could recover known drug-target biology at the IL1RN locus. IL1RN encodes IL1Ra, an endogenous inhibitor of interleukin-1 signalling 60 , and recombinant IL1Ra (anakinra) is approved for rheumatoid arthritis (RA) 60 and effective in acute gout flares 61 . We therefore constructed an IL1Ra activity score from IL1RN expression, CRP, GlycA and neutrophil count (Figure 4C). Because IL1Ra is directly measurable in plasma, we could test the score against the protein whose activity it is meant to proxy. A higher genetically proxied IL1Ra activity score was associated with higher circulating IL1Ra protein concentration (β = 4.79, 95% CI 3.34 to 6.24, P = 1×10 -10 ). Furthermore, consistent with the therapeutic effects of anakinra, a higher IL1Ra activity score was also associated with lower risk of RA (OR = 0.22, 95% CI 0.11 to 0.41, P = 3×10 -6 ) and gout (OR = 0.33, 95% CI 0.23 to 0.46, P = 3×10 -10 ) (Figure 4D, Supplementary Table 10).

To characterize the broader inflammatory pathways associated with the NLRP3 activity score, we performed proteome-wide MR. A lower genetically proxied NLRP3 activity score was associated with lower levels of 27 and higher levels of seven circulating proteins after multiple-testing correction (Supplementary Table 11). Over-representation analysis of the 27 proteins with lower levels showed enrichment for acute-phase and complement-activation pathways (Figure 4E), consistent with the hepatic acute-phase response downstream of NLRP3-driven IL-1β and IL-6 signalling. In contrast, the seven proteins with higher levels were enriched only for amino-acid metabolic processes, driven by three proteins (Supplementary Table 12).

Finally, we tested whether the CAD association depended on how the instrument was constructed. The association remained significant across LD clumping thresholds from r² = 0.1 to 0.6 (ORs 1.16-1.22, P ≤ 0.032) and was directionally consistent in leave-one-out analyses (Figure 4F, Supplementary Figure 4, Supplementary Table 13). A conventional instrument based on NLRP3 expression alone (12 variants, Fstatistic = 65.0) gave a CAD estimate in the same direction (OR = 1.027 per SD lower expression, 95% CI 0.998 to 1.056, P = 0.070), but the alleles associated with lower NLRP3 expression were not always associated with lower inflammatory biomarkers (Cochran's Q P ≤ 2×10⁻¹³), and this instrument was not significantly associated with IL-1β or IL-18 (P ≥ 0.11). The CAD estimate was larger when NLRP3 expression was proxied by the eight variants used in the NLRP3 activity score (OR = 1.044 per SD, 95% CI 1.003 to 1.087), consistent with the CAD association being carried by the variants whose expression effects are accompanied by lower inflammation (Supplementary Information, Supplementary Table 13).

Collectively, these findings identify NLRP3 as the gene underlying the inflammatory signal at this locus and show that the CAD association is consistent across sensitivity analyses.

Genetic associations of the NLRP3 activity score across diseases under investigation for NLRP3 inhibition

Beyond CAD, we explored whether the NLRP3 activity score was associated with 15 other diseases for which NLRP3 inhibitors have been, or currently are, under clinical investigation (Supplementary Table 14). A lower genetically proxied NLRP3 activity score was nominally associated with lower risk of gout (OR = 0.64, 95% CI 0.47 to 0.87, P = 0.0052), pericarditis (OR = 0.22, 95% CI 0.06 to 0.72, P = 0.012), and Parkinson's disease (PD) (OR = 0.48, 95% CI 0.24 to 0.95, P = 0.036) (Figure 5). Type 2 diabetes was the one indication where lower genetically proxied NLRP3 activity was associated with higher risk, and this estimate is reported above as a mediator of the CAD association. No clear associations were observed for the other indications examined (Supplementary Table 15). Together, these exploratory findings provide human genetic evidence consistent with potential benefit of lower NLRP3 activity in selected inflammatory indications.

Discussion

In this drug-target MR study, we investigated whether lower genetically proxied NLRP3 activity is associated with CAD risk. Because NLRP3 activity cannot be measured directly in large population datasets 62 , we constructed a composite genetic score combining NLRP3 expression with downstream inflammatory biomarkers. A lower genetically proxied NLRP3 activity score was associated with lower circulating IL-1β, IL-18, and IL-6, consistent with the expected downstream effects of lower NLRP3 activity. Despite these lower inflammatory markers, a lower NLRP3 activity score was associated with higher CAD risk, and with greater coronary artery calcification and coronary plaque burden. Mediation analyses suggested that this association was largely accounted for by adverse cardiometabolic effects, including higher SBP, higher levels of proatherogenic lipids and ApoB, and greater T2D risk, with little evidence of a residual direct association after accounting for these pathways. In contrast, a lower NLRP3 activity score was nominally associated with lower risk of gout, pericarditis and Parkinson's disease.

Our findings are surprising in light of the CANTOS trial, where canakinumab reduced recurrent cardiovascular events through inhibition of IL-1β, a downstream effector of NLRP3 inflammasome activation 2, 63 . Post hoc analyses of CANTOS showed that canakinumab lowered IL-6 but not IL-18, and that residual levels of both cytokines predicted recurrent events 64 . On this basis, NLRP3 inhibition was proposed to offer greater benefit than IL-1β blockade alone, because it would lower IL-18 in addition to IL-1β, and direct IL-6 inhibition was proposed as an alternative strategy 64 . This latter strategy did not reduce cardiovascular events in the ZEUS trial among patients with cardiovascular and chronic kidney disease 8 but is still being tested after acute myocardial infarction (ARTEMIS, NCT06118281). In our analysis, a lower NLRP3 activity score was associated with lower IL-1β, IL-18 and IL-6, yet with higher CAD risk, suggesting that the cardiovascular consequences of lower NLRP3 activity cannot be inferred from its anti-inflammatory effects alone. Experimental evidence is also mixed, as NLRP3 deficiency reduced atherosclerosis in one mouse model 14 , but not in another 65.

A potential explanation for the higher CAD risk is the adverse cardiometabolic profile associated with lower genetically proxied NLRP3 activity. The ApoB association is consistent with evidence from genetic proxies of IL-1α/β inhibition, which were associated with lower IL-6 and CRP but higher levels of proatherogenic lipids and higher coronary heart disease risk 66 . In RA, IL-6 receptor blockade and TNF inhibition can also raise lipids 67 . The T2D association contrasts with the rationale for testing NLRP3 inhibitors in T2D, but the experimental evidence behind it is mixed. NLRP3 deficiency protected mice against obesity-induced insulin resistance 68 , whereas other studies found no protection 69 or worse fatty liver disease 70, 71 . IL-18 offers one possible explanation, as lower NLRP3 activity was associated with lower IL-18 and its experimental loss impairs insulin sensitivity and energy homeostasis 72 . The SBP association similarly contrasts with experimental evidence that NLRP3 inhibition lowers blood pressure and renal damage in hypertension 73 . The cardiometabolic effects associated with lifelong lower NLRP3 activity are therefore only partly anticipated by existing experimental and pharmacological evidence. Trials of NLRP3 inhibitors should monitor blood pressure, lipid, and glycaemic effects.

Although our findings do not support lowering NLRP3 activity to reduce CAD risk in the general population, benefit may still exist in selected subgroups with inflammasome-driven cardiovascular risk. Clonal haematopoiesis provides one such setting, with experimental and human data implicating NLRP3dependent IL-1β signalling in clonal haematopoiesis-associated cardiovascular disease 74 . In an exploratory genomic substudy of CANTOS, participants harbouring TET2 variants showed a greater reduction in major adverse cardiovascular events when treated with canakinumab 75.

Our findings differ from those of Schunk et al. 18 . Using a single intronic variant (rs10754555) associated with NLRP3 expression, they reported that higher NLRP3 activity was associated with higher CAD prevalence among people younger than 60 years of age and with higher cardiovascular mortality across primary-and secondary-prevention cohorts. The variant was associated with higher NLRP3 expression and inflammasome activation in functional experiments, but not with lipid parameters. By contrast, our NLRP3 activity score integrates information across eight variants that tag rs10754555 only weakly, with a maximum r² of 0.26 in the INTERVAL reference panel, and was additionally associated with cardiometabolic traits. Because these cardiometabolic associations act on CAD in the opposite direction to the inflammatory associations, they may explain the discordance. The age-and metaboliccontext dependence reported by Schunk et al. further suggests that the cardiovascular consequences of NLRP3-related genetic variation may depend on the population studied.

Beyond CAD, the associations with gout and pericarditis are consistent with established NLRP3-IL-1 biology. Monosodium urate crystals are canonical NLRP3 activators in gout 76 . In recurrent pericarditis, IL-1 blockade is effective 77 but relies on expensive injectable biologics. An oral NLRP3 inhibitor has entered phase 2 testing for this indication (NCT06836232), and our estimate lends genetic support to that approach. The PD association contrasts with a recent genetic study that used expression-based instruments for NLRP3 inflammasome-related genes and found no evidence for a role of the NLRP3 inflammasome in PD risk 78 . Our score integrates multiple variants at the NLRP3 locus with downstream inflammatory readouts, which may contribute to the differing findings. Overall, these exploratory disease associations are consistent with the biological relevance of the NLRP3 activity score beyond CAD but require replication.

We acknowledge that our study has limitations. Although cis-restriction reduces the likelihood of horizontal pleiotropy, it cannot eliminate it, because variants may influence several biological processes through mechanisms not fully captured by the intended exposure 79 . The cardiometabolic associations could therefore reflect horizontal rather than vertical pleiotropy, a possibility consistent with the non-zero MR-Egger intercept for CAD at the primary clumping threshold (r² = 0.1). However, if the variants affected SBP, ApoB, or T2D through pathways other than NLRP3 activity, their effects on these traits would generally not be proportional to their effects on the NLRP3 activity score. MR-Egger regression showed no such departure from proportionality for any of the three traits, or for CAD at relaxed clumping thresholds (Egger intercept P > 0.12). In addition, rare loss-of-function and gain-of-function variants in NLRP3 affected the inflammatory biomarkers in the expected directions, and no neighbouring gene showed comparable associations, supporting NLRP3 as the causal gene at this locus. Bias from participant overlap is also possible, as several exposure and outcome datasets include UK Biobank participants, although with strong instruments (F = 92) the bias is expected to be minimal 80 . Another limitation is that genetic variants capture small, lifelong differences in NLRP3 activity, whereas pharmacological inhibition produces a larger perturbation over a defined treatment period. The adverse cardiometabolic effects observed here may not fully develop within that period, so trials could find less excess CAD risk than our genetic estimates suggest 81 . Our estimates also apply to a general population, whereas clinical studies of direct NLRP3 inhibition in CAD have focused on patients with established coronary artery disease, in whom the cardiometabolic pathways implicated here are aggressively treated with lipidlowering and antihypertensive therapy. Statistical power is a further consideration. The residual direct effect on CAD, after accounting for SBP, ApoB, and T2D, was close to the null, but its confidence interval remains compatible with a modest effect in either direction. Relatedly, for several disease outcomes, the available case numbers may have been insufficient to detect modest causal effects, and null findings should therefore be interpreted with caution. Our instrument is also derived from inflammatory biomarkers measured in peripheral blood and may not reflect NLRP3 activity in disease-relevant tissues such as the coronary arteries or the brain. Establishing these tissue-specific effects will require complementary evidence, including tissue-specific molecular data and pharmacodynamic biomarker measurements in clinical studies. Finally, the NLRP3 activity score and all disease outcome analyses were based on European-ancestry data, which limits generalizability.

In conclusion, a lower genetically proxied NLRP3 activity score was associated with higher CAD risk, with mediation analyses suggesting that this association was largely accounted for by adverse cardiometabolic effects. These findings reflect lifelong genetic variation in the general population and do not preclude benefit from pharmacological inhibition in selected patients with inflammasome-driven cardiovascular risk. Choice of traits for the NLRP3 activity score

No assay of NLRP3 inflammasome activity is available at population scale 62 . We therefore combined variant effects on NLRP3 expression, which anchors the score to the target gene, with their effects on downstream inflammatory traits. We did not use expression alone because inflammasome assembly depends on post-translational signals in addition to transcriptional priming, so NLRP3 mRNA levels may not reflect inflammasome output 9 . Consistent with this, alleles that lower NLRP3 expression differ in whether they lower downstream inflammatory traits (see below).

The most proximal circulating readouts of NLRP3 inflammasome activation are IL-1β and IL-18. Further downstream are IL-6 and the acute-phase response, reflected in CRP, GlycA and fibrinogen, together with increased granulopoiesis, reflected in neutrophil count [9] [10] [11] [12] . GlycA, a composite NMR signal from several glycosylated acute-phase proteins including α1-acid glycoprotein, haptoglobin and α1antitrypsin 11 , extends the acute-phase readout beyond CRP. Of these readouts, CRP, GlycA and neutrophil count have by far the largest GWAS (more than 430,000 participants each [19] [20] [21] ), and each shows genomewide significant associations at the NLRP3 locus. We therefore used them to construct the score. While IL-1β and IL-18 more directly reflect inflammasome activation, they and IL-6 were measured in only about 50,000 participants in the UK Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project 23 , giving far less power to detect the small effects of individual NLRP3 variants. Holding them out of construction instead allowed them to serve as an independent test of whether the score captures inflammasome output.

CRP, GlycA and neutrophil count are not specific to the NLRP3 inflammasome. Specificity was instead addressed through the design: instruments were restricted to cis variants at NLRP3, variants associated with the expression of other genes were excluded, and we tested whether these traits shared a causal variant with NLRP3 expression and were shifted by rare loss-and gain-of-function variants in NLRP3 but not by loss-of-function variants in neighbouring genes.

Construction of the NLRP3 and IL1Ra activity scores

To construct the NLRP3 activity score, we used genetic associations for four traits related to NLRP3 inflammasome activation: whole-blood NLRP3 expression, circulating CRP and GlycA levels, and blood neutrophil count. For each trait, variants within ±150 kb of NLRP3 were identified and LD-clumped using PLINK2 82 (version 2.00a6) with a 250 kb clumping window, a genome-wide significance threshold of P<5 ×10 -8 , and a primary clumping threshold of r 2 <0.1 . The LD reference panel comprised 11, 863 European-ancestry whole-genome sequences from INTERVAL. Only single-nucleotide variants were used, and variants whose effect-allele frequency in the panel differed from the neutrophil-count GWAS frequency by more than 0.05 were excluded from the reference panel. Because standard clumping may select different lead variants for different traits even when these tag the same underlying signal, we next identified variants in high LD with each trait-specific lead variant using r 2 >0.95 within a 40 kb window.

These high-LD variant sets were treated as signal blocks, and blocks were merged across traits if they shared at least one variant. Merged blocks represented in at least two of the four traits were retained for score construction, of which there were ten. For each retained block, we selected a single representative variant, prioritizing variants available across all four datasets and then choosing the variant with the lowest CRP association P-value. Where the representative variant was absent from one or more datasets, we substituted a proxy within 50 kb, provided r² was at least 0.9. Two representatives were still missing from at least one dataset after this substitution and were excluded, leaving eight variants for construction of the NLRP3 activity score (Supplementary Figure 1). We further required instruments to be specific to the target gene. Using INTERVAL whole-blood expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) summary statistics, a variant was excluded if it was associated (P < 1×10⁻³) with expression of another gene tested in cis, and this association persisted (P < 1×10⁻³) after conditioning on that gene's lead eQTL. This increased the specificity of the instruments for their target gene.

We centred and scaled the trait-specific effect estimates to have mean zero and unit variance across variants before applying PCA to the resulting variant-by-trait matrix. Standard errors for β j NLRP 3 score were estimated using the delta method 43 . For each variant, we defined a variance-covariance matrix for the trait-specific effect estimates. Because the CRP, GlycA, and neutrophil count GWAS may include overlapping participants, we used a conservative approach and assumed perfectly correlated sampling errors across these biomarker traits. This assumption inflates standard errors and therefore yields conservative inference. As NLRP3 expression was measured in a distinct cohort, expression quantitative trait loci effect estimates were assumed to be independent of the biomarker effect estimates.

The same procedure was applied at the IL1RN locus to construct a genetically proxied interleukin-1 receptor antagonist (IL1Ra) activity score, using IL1RN expression and the same blood-based inflammatory traits.

The expression-only instrument was constructed with the same selection pipeline, using INTERVAL whole-blood NLRP3 expression as the only readout. Of its 12 variants, four are also NLRP3 activity score instruments and a further two are in LD with NLRP3 activity score signals (r² > 0.9). The expressionlowering alleles at rs72771965 and rs4925645 were associated with higher rather than lower CRP and neutrophil count. The instrument therefore averages over variants with opposing downstream effects, which is reflected in highly significant between-variant heterogeneity for all three inflammatory biomarkers (Cochran's Q P ≤ 2×10⁻¹³). We also estimated the CAD association using the effects of the eight NLRP3 activity score variants on NLRP3 expression alone.

Mediation analysis

ApoB was selected as the lipid mediator because it reflects the concentration of atherogenic ApoBcontaining lipoprotein particles and provides a parsimonious measure of lipid-related cardiovascular risk, avoiding joint modelling of highly correlated lipid fractions 50 . SBP was preferred to diastolic blood pressure, with which it is highly correlated, because it showed stronger evidence of association with the NLRP3 activity score. The MVMR instruments were selected independently of the cis-acting NLRP3 variants used to instrument the activity score. For MVMR, variants were clumped within each trait and then again across traits (r² < 0.001, 10 Mb window, INTERVAL reference panel), so that the combined instrument set of 1,265 variants is approximately independent. Multiplicative random effects were applied to account for heterogeneity across the mediator instruments. Because the mediator GWAS draw on partly overlapping samples, the covariance between their variant effect estimates was estimated from zscores at variants that were null for both traits (P > 0.05), using one variant from each 100 kb window across the genome so that the variants were approximately independent 83.

NLRP3 rare variant phenotype processing

For NLRP3 pLOF burden analyses and gain-of-function carrier analyses, CRP levels and GlycA concentrations were adjusted for age, age 2 , sex, age × sex, age 2 × sex, assessment centre, genotyping array, and the first 20 genetic principal components before inverse-rank normalization. GlycA concentrations were additionally adjusted for technical variation using the ukbnmr package (version 3.3.1) 84 . Blood neutrophil count was adjusted following the blood-cell trait processing framework described by Astle et al., including adjustment for technical variation, age-and sex-related effects, height and weight, smoking, alcohol-related covariates, assessment centre, and genetic principal components before inverse-rank normalization 85.

Proteome-wide multiple-testing correction and over-representation analysis

To account for correlation between protein measurements, we estimated the effective number of independent protein tests. To do this, protein measurements were first adjusted for age, age², sex, age × sex, age² × sex, assessment centre, genotyping batch, Olink batch, and the first 20 genetic principal components. Pairwise Pearson correlations were then calculated across adjusted protein measurements, and eigenvalue decomposition of the resulting correlation matrix was used to estimate the effective number of independent tests. The effective number of tests was defined as the number of principal components required to explain 95% of the total variance 58 . This yielded 1,821 effective tests, corresponding to a significance threshold of P < 2.7×10 -5.

To assess whether proteins altered by lower genetically proxied NLRP3 activity were enriched for specific biological processes, we performed over-representation analysis using clusterProfiler (version 4.10.1) 86 . Proteins passing the multiple-testing threshold were tested for enrichment of Gene Ontology Biological Process terms obtained using msigdbr (version 25.1.1) 87, 88 , separately by direction of association. The background set comprised all 2,922 tested proteins. P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg method 89 , as Gene Ontology terms overlap heavily and Bonferroni correction would be overly conservative.

Selection of disease indications from clinical trial registries

To identify disease indications for which selective NLRP3 inhibitors have entered clinical evaluation, we searched ClinicalTrials.gov, the EU Clinical Trials Register (EudraCT), the EU Clinical Trials Information System (CTIS) and ISRCTN on 28 April 2026 using two complementary strategies.

Search 1 (target-level search): Each registry was searched for the term "NLRP3", restricted to interventional studies.

Search 2 (drug-name search): Each registry was searched for the following compound names: dapansutrile, OLT1177, DFV890, IFM-2427, selnoflast, RO7486967, RG6418, inzomelid, IZD174, somalix, IZD334, emlenoflast, NT-0796, ruvonoflast, NT-0249, VTX2735, VTX3232, VENT-02, ZYIL1, usnoflast, ACI-19764, AZD4144, HL-400, JTE-162, JT002, GDC-2394, and MCC950.

Results from both searches were merged and de-duplicated, with trials registered in more than one registry counted once. Each record was screened and a trial was included only if the intervention was a compound designed specifically to inhibit NLRP3. In addition, rheumatoid arthritis was included because a selective NLRP3 inhibitor (MCC950/CP-456773) was evaluated in a phase 2 trial but was not developed further after it raised serum liver enzyme levels 90 .
