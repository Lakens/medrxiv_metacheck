Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Repository Check works

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Human immune composition reflects genetic background and cumulative life-course exposures, yet how these influences shape immune architecture across populations and disease remains poorly understood. Here we integrate single-cell transcriptomics, matched clinical haematology and electronic health records from 5,079 individuals of European, British-Pakistani and British-Bangladeshi ancestries to quantify 26 circulating immune cell types and 62 transcriptional states. Introduction

The immune system is central to human health, defending against pathogens, surveilling for malignant transformation, and regulating inflammation while maintaining tolerance to self. Beyond inherited factors [1] [2] [3] , circulating immune-cell composition is shaped by cumulative life-course exposures and demographic factors such as age, sex and ancestry, and environmental influences such as adiposity, smoking and reproductive status [4] [5] [6] [7] . Most work dissecting this variation has isolated single factors, such as age, in controlled settings. Such studies cannot capture the joint effects of genetic background, life-course exposures and population context, so the combined contribution of these factors to immune variation remains poorly characterised, particularly across diverse populations.

Complete blood counts (CBCs) transformed clinical medicine by enabling quantitative assessment of blood composition, flagging deviations from reference ranges that signal infection, inflammation or haematological disease. Yet blood cell composition varies substantially among healthy individuals and populations for reasons that remain incompletely understood 4, 8, 9 , and conventional haematology collapses circulating immune cells into a handful of broad lineages, limiting resolution of finer scale phenotypes. Flow cytometry improves resolution, but predefined marker panels and differences in experimental protocols limit the breadth and comparability of immune phenotyping across cohorts 10, 11.

Single-cell transcriptomics overcomes these limitations, enabling unbiased, high-resolution characterisation of immune cell types and states across large sample sizes [12] [13] [14] [15] [16] and capturing cellular diversity invisible to CBCs and impractical to scale with FACS. Because single-cell genomics yields relative rather than absolute cell proportions, comparisons across individuals are inherently compositional: a change in one population necessarily shifts the relative abundance of others. Applied at population scale, however, single-cell technologies provide a detailed view of immune architecture and support rigorous modelling of how biological and environmental factors jointly shape immune composition across the life course.

Pairing single-cell genomics with large, population-representative cohorts and linked electronic health records (EHRs) offers a new way to model the relative contribution of demographic, epidemiological and disease-related determinants of immune variation across life 17, 18 . By enabling hypothesis-free investigation across multiple disease diagnoses, such resources can reveal how common diseases reshape immune-cell composition, uncover underappreciated immune mechanisms contributing to the pathogenesis of classically non-immune diseases, and identify immunologically distinct patient subgroups with potential relevance for disease stratification. They further provide opportunities to systematically evaluate emerging risk factors and identify early biomarkers of disease onset, particularly where multiple chronic conditions coexist.

Here, we leverage a single-cell molecular phenotyping resource of the immune system spanning thousands of participants of European, British-Pakistani and British-Bangladeshi ancestry (Giacopuzzi et al. 2026) to characterise population-level variation in peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) types at high resolution, moving beyond canonical CBC traits to finely resolved immune phenotypes. Results

High-resolution scCBCs enable population-scale quantification of immune-cell cross-sectional age profiles

We analysed CARDINAL, a single-cell genomics resource comprising ~6,000 individuals recruited through two UK population cohorts: 4,754 European-ancestry participants from UK Biobank (UKB) and 1,248 British-Pakistani and British-Bangladeshi participants from Genes & Health (G&H) (Giacopuzzi et al.). The two cohorts analysed here exhibit distinct age distributions (UKB: 53-85 years; G&H: 16-89 years; Supplementary Table 1), enabling complementary resolution of age-associated changes in immune architecture across the life course. The dataset comprises approximately 9.8 million peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) collected from fresh blood samples, together with matched Sysmex haematology measurements (Methods). Participants contributed a median of 1,471 (interquartile range 1,069 -1,923) PBMCs per donor in UKB and 1,738 (interquartile range 1,268 -2,367) in G&H.

To derive absolute immune-cell counts, we developed single-cell CBCs (scCBCs) by scaling single-cell subtype proportions to donor-matched Sysmex lymphocyte and monocyte counts (Methods; https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings. 1). This yielded estimated absolute counts for 26 immune cell types (cell type 2, CT2) and 62 cell states (CT3). After restricting analyses to samples with concordant lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratios across the two platforms (Methods; https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings. 2), the final dataset comprised 4,094 UKB and 985 G&H participants, corresponding to 86% and 79% of the initially profiled participants respectively (Supplementary Table 1, Fig. 1a). The concordance criterion was defined as exclusion of samples with a Sysmex-to-single-cell lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio lying outside [Q 1 -5*IQR, Q 3 + 5*IQR]; the two platforms showed proportional scaling (R=0.86 for UKB and R=0.84 for G&H; Methods; https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings. 2). By anchoring single-cell proportions to clinical blood counts, scCBCs enable quantitative comparisons of immune-cell abundance estimates across individuals and mitigate compositional bias inherent in single-cell data 19 . Consistent with this, proportion-based analyses showed the expected negative correlation bias, whereas this artefactual dependency was not observed in scCBCs (https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings. 3 4).

We next used scCBCs to quantify the associations between demographic and lifestyle factors on immune composition by modelling changes in scCBCs jointly as a function of age, sex, population background, body mass index (BMI) and smoking, hence estimating the mutually adjusted contribution of each variable (Methods). Due to personal data privacy restrictions (including UK-GDPR), the biomedical variables of the two cohorts are held in separate Trusted Research Environments, and therefore all analyses integrating single-cell data with clinical phenotypes were performed independently within each cohort. Nevertheless, this approach allowed us to quantify variable-associated changes in each cell state and compare their magnitude and direction across cohorts.

Associations with age recapitulated in both cohorts the profound remodelling of the immune compartment (Fig. 2a, https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings Tables 3 4), with over half of the 62 cell states (CT3 level) showing significant association with age (32/62 in UKB and 34/62 G&H; FDR < 0.05). These included progressive depletion of naive B cells 20, 21, 22 , naive CD4 + and naive CD8 + T cells 23 , mucosal-associated invariant T (MAIT) cells, γδ T cells and ILC3s 21 , alongside expansion of CD14 + 3 . and CD16 + monocytes, adaptive CD16 + NK cells, and increased CD4 + Treg effector populations and terminally differentiated EOMES + , NCAM1 + and ZNF683 + CD8 TEMRA states 23 . For each cell state an age-related effect was defined from standard deviations in scCBC change per 10-year increase in age. Despite the age difference for the two cohorts, the magnitude of age-associated changes was broadly comparable (median -0.06, range -0.48 to 0.20 in UKB and median -0.05, range -0.45 to 0.23 in G&H), with 18 of 62 cell types showing FDR-significant, directionally concordant associations. Restricting G&H to the shared age range (53+ years), 7 of its 10 significant associations were directionally concordant in UKB (Supplementary Table 5). Naïve early CD8 + T cells showing the greatest age-associated decline in both cohorts (β = -0.48 in UKB and β = -0.45 in G&H; 95% CI [-0.50, -0.46] and [-0.48, -0.42]; FDR-adjusted p = 5.75 x 10 -105 and 2.8 x 10 -44 , respectively). The broader age distribution of the G&H cohort (16-89 years) provided a finer assessment of stages of compositional variation during the pre-immunosenescence stage 24 , highlighting more dynamic patterns of variation in subgroups of cells (Fig. 2a, https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings. 5 6, https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings Tables 3 4). Naive lymphocytes, MAIT cells and several innate lymphoid populations showed monotonically declining counts throughout adulthood, while inflammatory monocytes and differentiated CD4 + and CD8 + T-cell states showed corresponding monotonically increasing variation. Further, non-linear population variation was observed for other subsets (Fig. 2b; https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings. 5 6), including for instance CD4 + central-memory T cells that increased until midlife and declined thereafter, with an estimated inflection point at approximately 52 years (FDR for non-linearity = 0.012) 21 . The extended age range also enabled robust quantification of rare populations, revealing age-associated depletion of ILC2s, innate lymphoid progenitors and plasmablasts, populations that conventional haematology cannot reliably resolve (Fig. 2a). Therefore, by averaging effects across individuals, these population-level patterns inferred from cross-sectional data inform understanding and quantitative assessments of age-associated longitudinal trajectories, complementing information from cell atlasing studies 20, 21.

We recapitulated the established immune dimorphism between sexes (Fig. 2a, https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings Tables 3 4) with greater abundance of adaptive immune populations in females -naive and memory B cells, naive CD4 + T cells and several CD8 + memory subsets 25, 26 -while males showed increased CD14 + , CD16 + and MHCII high monocytes together with cytotoxic CD16 + NK cells 27 . The resolution of our atlas further enabled identification of previously unreported associations, including increased abundance of early effector memory CD8 + T cells and depletion of IL7R + MAIT CD8 + cells in UKB females, suggesting selective sensitivity of these subsets to cohort structure or annotation depth. For cell states with FDR < 0.05, the magnitude of sex difference was greater in G&H (median = 0.26 s.d., range -0.43 to 0.37) than UKB (median = 0.17 s.d., range -0.40 to 0.44), possibly due to differences in age structure between the cohorts. CD14 + monocytes showed the greatest difference between sexes, with counts 0.37 s.d. and 0.43 s.d. higher in males than in females in UKB and G&H respectively (FDR-adjusted p = 2.31 x 10 -32 and p = 3.76 x 10 -5 ). These sex differences were highly variable across adulthood at the population level 28 . Across the 62 immune states, we observed persistent differences, progressive divergence and attenuation of sex effects when averaging between donors of similar age (https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings. 7 8). Innate populations displayed particularly pronounced age-associated patterns in G&H (Fig. 2g). CD16 + NK cells showed a significant overall age-dependent sex difference (FDR-adjusted p = 1.59x10 -3 ), with greater counts in males supported until approximately age 30. Early naive B cells also showed significantly different sex-specific age patterns (FDR-adjusted p = 0.025), with higher abundance in females supported from approximately age 57 onward. CD56 bright NK cells showed suggestive evidence of differing age patterns by sex (p = 0.010), with higher abundance in females supported from approximately age 60 onward (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Fig. 7 8). These age-associated patterns help explain the cohort differences: UKB's older age distribution predominantly sampled ranges where sex differences were already established, while G&H's broader age range captured their emergence across adulthood. Together, these analyses show that age-and sex-related associations are heterogeneous and cell-state specific, with sex differences that can strengthen, weaken, or reverse over the adult life course.

Extending this framework to fine-scale population variation, scCBCs uncovered previously uncharacterised structure within the G&H cohort. British-Pakistani and British-Bangladeshi donors https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings South Asian groups with distinct genetic and environmental backgrounds, including 4 . differences in autozygosity, population structure and consanguinity-associated founder effects 29 . After adjustment for the other covariates, CD14 + MHCII high monocytes were enriched in British-Pakistani donors, whereas IGHMlow memory B cells, adaptive CD16 + NK cells, CD4 + central-memory Th17 cells and naive-memory γδ T-cell subsets were enriched in British-Bangladeshi, indicating coordinated redistribution across adaptive and barrier-associated compartments (Fig. 2a, https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings Tables 3 4). These differences persisted variably across donor ages: while CD4 + central-memory Th17 and naive-memory γδ T cells showed significantly different age-related patterns between ancestry groups across all ages (FDR-adjusted p = 6.19x10 -3 for both), CD14 + MHCII high monocyte diversification was restricted only up to approximately age 41 (FDR-adjusted p = 0.022; Fig. 2d, https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings. 9). This pattern may reflect greater sensitivity of the myeloid compartment to early-life infectious exposures, with more persistent effects in adaptive and barrier-associated compartments 30, 31.

We finally investigated socioeconomic status (SES) as proxied by the index of multiple deprivation (IMD), a composite measure of area-level deprivation capturing income, education, employment, health, housing, living environment, crime and access to services. IMD showed no robust associations with scCBCs in either cohort (FDR < 0.05; https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings. 10a-b). Nominal associations (p < 0.05) nevertheless suggested subtle, cohort-specific effects on lymphoid and cytotoxic homeostasis. In UKB, IMD was positively associated with BMI and smoking (https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings. 10c-f), indicating that SES gradients may act largely through modifiable exposures such as adiposity and smoking. The recapitulation of established patterns of immune ageing and sexual dimorphism supports the reliability of scCBCs for quantifying immune-cell abundance at population scale, while providing substantially greater resolution than conventional haematology. By distinguishing 26 cell types and 62 functional cell states, scCBCs reveal how demographic factors shape immune composition across diverse population contexts.

Smoking and BMI remodel immune-cell composition across adulthood

Age and sex establish baseline immune composition through developmental and hormonal signalling, but lifestyle factors and exposures further shape immune homeostasis throughout life 32, 33 . Smoking and adiposity are major determinants of chronic inflammation and have both been implicated in accelerated immune ageing 7, [34] [35] [36] . We therefore asked how these two common exposures were associated with immune-cell composition in models accounting for age, sex and population background (Methods). To account for incomplete data at the time of PBMC collection, the most recent available BMI and smoking measures were taken as proxies, after confirming their temporal stability between baseline and recall measurements (Methods; https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings. 11).

Smoking was primarily associated with changes in the adaptive immune compartment (Fig. 2a, 3b, https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings Tables 3 4, https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings. 12 13). Current smokers showed elevated counts of memory B cells, central-memory CD4 + subsets, memory CD8 + cells, regulatory T cells 26, 37 , together with naïve B cells and plasma cells, associations that have not been previously reported to our knowledge. In UKB, smoking was further associated with naïve T cells, classical and S100 + CD14 + monocytes, early ILCPs and depletion of γδ effector T cells 38 , as well as several corresponding cell states. We also identified previously unreported expansion of transitional/late naïve B cells, plasma cells, consistent with tobacco-driven activation of the naïve B-cell pool and differentiation towards antigen-experienced states 32 . Immune cell composition in former smokers largely converged with that of never-smokers, with modest residual differences confined to regulatory and memory T-cell populations (Fig. 2a, 3b, https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings Tables 3 4), potentially indicative of recovery of immune cell composition following smoking cessation 37 . Our quantitative assessment in this population setting implicated 24 affected cell types in individuals classified as current versus never smokers in UKB, and 12 in G&H (FDR < 0.05), with median effects of 0.49 s.d. (range -0.22 to 0.99) in UKB and 0.44 s.d. (range 0.25 to 0.59) in G&H, compared with only up to three cell types differentiating former from never smokers in UKB, with small effects (range 0.11 to 0.13 s.d.). Adiposity (as proxied by BMI in this study) showed broader and more heterogeneous effects, implicating 25 of 62 cell states in UKB and 10 of 62 cell states in G&H (FDR < 0.05; Fig. 2a, https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings Tables 3 4). Median scCBCs changes were smaller in UKB (0.09 s.d., range -0.09 to 5 . 0.23 per one-standard-deviation increase in BMI) than in G&H (0.16 s.d., range -0.14 to 0.23). At finer resolution, we observed expansion of early and late naïve B cells, CD4 central memory subsets spanning Tfh, Th1, Th2, Th17 and Th22 lineages, and naïve Tregs (Fig. 2a, https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings Tables 3 4), extending prior work on obesity-linked broad B-cell and T-cell remodelling by resolving the specific cell states involved 34 . Further, NK CD16+ IFN+ and GNLY+/IL7R+ MAIT subsets all decreased with BMI in UKB, providing finer cell-state resolution relative to earlier reports of obesity-associated changes in NK and MAIT compartments 39 and suggesting a more selective erosion of innate-like immune surveillance in older individuals 40 . BMI-associated effects were strongly correlated with donor age: individuals classified as underweight, overweight or with obesity followed distinct distributions with age, with divergence from healthy-weight individuals becoming more pronounced at older age (Fig. 3a, https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings. 14 15). Sex-stratified analyses revealed broadly similar BMI-associated patterns in females and males (https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings. 16). CD14 + MHCII high monocyte counts were higher in healthy-weight compared to overweight and obese donors from approximately age 53 onward (Holm-adjusted p = 1.6x10 -3 and 1.2x10 -3 , respectively; BMI FDR-adjusted p = 0.0123). CD4 + central-memory Th1, Th17 and Th22 cells also showed significant BMI-dependent age patterns (FDR-adjusted p = 0.0123 for each), with higher abundance in obese individuals during early-to-mid adulthood relative to both healthy-weight and overweight groups. Early effector-memory CD8 + T cells showed a similar but weaker pattern (nominal p = 8.0x10 -3 ), with higher abundance in obese individuals supported between approximately age 33 and 46 (Fig. 2e). These population patterns support widespread age-dependent remodelling of immune compositional architecture with adiposity 34.

We next sought to unpick the observed correlation between immune cell populations, smoking, BMI and age. Of 32 (UKB) and 34 (G&H) age-associated cell states, 16 and 3 were also associated with BMI and 10 and 5 with current vs never smoking (FDR < 0.05). To quantify immune ageing, we derived an immune age-score in G&H (owing to its wider age distribution) using partial least squares (PLS) regression on scCBCs (Methods). The first latent component (PLS1) captured the major age-associated axis of compositional variation, explaining 41.5% of chronological age variance (https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings. 17b). Older immune profiles were characterised by depletion of naive lymphocyte and innate-like populations together with expansion of memory, terminal-effector and inflammatory myeloid states (https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings. 17c). After adjustment for chronological age and demographic variables, both current smoking (p = 3.70 x 10 -2 ) and higher BMI (p = 2.54 x 10 -4 ) were independently associated with increased age deviation, whereas former smoking showed no significant association (Fig. 3c-d).

Our population data therefore captured distinct modes of immune remodelling mediated by modifiable risk factors, confirming the deep perturbation of adaptive immune populations associated with smoking, and the broader and age-dependent involvement of adaptive and innate compartments with BMI. Despite these differences, both current smoking and higher BMI were associated with an older age-associated immune profile, highlighting immune composition as a potential quantitative marker of exposure-associated immune variation.

Shared immune programmes connect disease, population context and life-course immune remodelling

Immune composition integrates the cumulative effect of genetic background, modifiable exposures and ageing, providing a systems-level readout of host physiology. We therefore asked whether scCBCs could identify recurrent immune programmes associated with prevalent disease across two population cohorts with distinct demographic composition and disease burden. To characterise the disease landscape of the two cohorts, we first quantified phecode-defined conditions with ≥5% prevalence in either cohort, encompassing twelve major disease categories (Fig. 1b, https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings Table 2). UKB participants exhibited lower prevalence of metabolic disorders and smoking, whereas the G&H cohort showed greater burden of infectious, respiratory, dermatological and metabolic diseases (https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings Table 2). Conversely, cardiovascular and neoplastic diseases were more frequent in UKB. These differences recapitulated established epidemiological patterns 41 6 . and emphasised the need to account for population context when interpreting immune variation (Supplementary Fig. 18).

We next modelled immune cell abundance for 100 and 55 disease diagnoses in UKB and G&H (≥35 cases/disease; https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings Table 2) using generalised additive models adjusted for age, sex, ancestry, BMI and smoking (Methods). These signatures were summarised as a disease network, with edges capturing similarity between disease-cohort pairs based on their associated immune-cell states (≥2 shared states with concordant directions; Jaccard ≥0.2; Methods). Disease-associated immune signatures organised into coherent regions of the immune space, with related conditions clustered together, despite their clinical diversity (Fig. 4a). Rather than generating disease-specific immune architectures, many conditions converged on a limited number of recurrent cell-state programmes, indicating that diverse diseases remodel common components of the circulating immune system.

The most prominent programme linked several cardiometabolic diseases in UKB, and was dominated by inflammatory myeloid remodelling (Fig. 4b; https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings. 19 20 21 22, https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings Tables 6 7). Across coronary atherosclerosis, ischaemic heart disease, hypertension and related disorders, we observed coordinated expansion of CD14 + inflammatory monocytes, including S100 + and MHCII high states, together with innate lymphoid progenitors and terminally differentiated CD8 EMRA populations, with the most extreme association implicating ILCPs in angina pectoris (β = 0.59 s.d. higher ILCP counts in individuals with disease compared to unaffected individuals; FDR-adjusted p = 1.27x10 -3 ). These states are consistent with established roles in atherogenesis and vascular inflammation 42, 43 and with cytotoxic T-cell involvement in disease progression 44 . Metabolic diseases showed contrasting immune signatures across cohorts (Fig. 4b). In UKB, lipoprotein disorders shared lymphoid features of cardiovascular disease but lacked inflammatory CD14 + monocyte signals; pure hypercholesterolaemia additionally enriched innate-like CD8 + EMRA states. Obesity combined monocyte and cytotoxic lymphoid changes with pronounced CD4 + central memory and adaptive NK-cell expansion, without B-cell remodelling. Type 2 diabetes predominantly affected CD4 + and CD8 + populations, alongside increased S100 + CD14 + monocytes 45, 46 . In G&H, hypercholesterolaemia and type 2 diabetes shared prominent B-cell remodelling, including late naïve and IGHMlow memory states, together with MHCII high CD14 + monocytes and memory T-cell expansion, with diabetes showing broader T-cell changes 47 . Lipoprotein disorders primarily involved memory T-cell subsets. G&H obesity was distinguished by increased early ILCP, Th1-like CD4 + central memory and proliferating CD8 + cells.

A second programme captured shared features of immunosenescence across diverse conditions. In UKB, hypothyroidism, major depressive disorder and psoriasis were united by depletion of early and late naïve CD4 + and CD8 + T-cell subsets, with disease-specific remodelling of memory, humoral and myeloid states: MAIT CD8 + GNLY + cells were reduced in hypothyroidism; IGHMhigh memory B cells and CD14 + S100 + monocytes were expanded in major depressive disorder; and plasmablasts were increased in psoriasis (https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings. 19 20, https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings Tables 6 7). In G&H, musculoskeletal and pain syndromes (dorsalgia, fibroblastic disorders, limb or joint pain, Dupuytren's disease, rheumatism) showed a related signature, marked by loss of late naïve CD4 + and CD8 + T cells, reduced CD4 + central-memory Th17 and effector-memory populations, and depletion of early naïve B cells, alongside increased NK CD16 + adaptive and transitional subsets in limb pain and rheumatism (https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings. 21 22, https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings Tables 6 7). This axis points to contraction of naïve lymphocyte pools with compensatory shifts in memory and innate compartments, in line with established hallmarks of immunosenescence 24, 48.

Third, many infectious and barrier-associated phenotypes in G&H converged on a pervasive type I interferon (IFN) programme spanning myeloid, NK, T-and B-cell compartments (Fig. 4c, https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings. 21 22, https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings Tables 6 7). IFN-activated states were shared across respiratory, gastrointestinal, cutaneous and musculoskeletal conditions, suggesting that recent or persistent immune activation induces common interferon-responsive cell states irrespective of the affected tissue 49, 50 . These findings indicate that clinically distinct diseases frequently converge on a 7 . small number of conserved immune programmes reflecting inflammatory, adaptive-memory and antiviral responses.

Finally, we asked whether common diseases were associated with distinct patterns of immune-cell abundance across age groups at the population level. We modelled cross-sectional age profiles using B-splines, stratifying by disease status, sex, BMI and population background (Methods). Across multiple conditions, disease-associated differences varied with age and depended on sex, adiposity and ancestry, suggesting changes in the timing and magnitude of immune remodelling rather than uniform shifts in cell abundance.

Two representative examples illustrate these disease-associated differences in age profiles. In UKB, the elevation in CD14 + monocyte frequencies among males with ischaemic heart disease became more pronounced at older ages, whereas affected females showed age-associated patterns similar to unaffected controls, suggesting sex-specific remodelling of the innate compartment in heart disease 51, 52 (Fig. 4d). In G&H, hypercholesterolaemia was associated with higher CD4 + central memory T-cell abundance in early adulthood among British-Pakistani participants, with differences from unaffected individuals diminishing by approximately age 50. No comparable pattern was observed among British-Bangladeshi participants (Fig. 4d). Similar variation by disease status, age and host background was observed across multiple immune populations (https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings. 23), indicating that disease-associated immune differences vary across adulthood and demographic contexts.

Together, these analyses suggest that circulating immune composition reflects accumulated exposures and disease history through recurrent immune programmes shaped by age, sex and population context. The same diagnostic categories can therefore exhibit distinct immune profiles across cohorts with contrasting demographic and clinical characteristics, with implications for the generalisability of immune biomarkers and the design of immunomodulatory therapies.

Cardiovascular therapies are associated with disease-related immune programmes

Pharmacological exposure may contribute to the disease-associated immune signatures described above. We therefore assessed whether these signatures were associated with commonly prescribed cardiovascular therapies and sought to identify treatment-associated changes in immune composition independent of underlying disease. We focused on statins and antihypertensive therapies, two widely used drug classes with established immunomodulatory properties. Analyses included 1,744 individuals without cancer or autoimmune disease and accounted for underlying disease, sex, age, BMI, smoking and relevant clinical covariates: total, LDL and HDL cholesterol for statins; systolic and diastolic blood pressure for antihypertensives. We first modelled associations between each drug exposure and scCBC abundance across the cohort, and then jointly modelled disease status, drug use and their interaction to distinguish disease-from treatment-associated effects and to test whether treatment modified the immune alterations associated with disease (Methods).

At conventional cell-type resolution (CT2), statin therapy showed limited association with immune-cell abundance. By contrast, cell-state resolved analyses (CT3) revealed reproducible associations within myeloid and T-cell compartments (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Table 8). Statin use was associated with reduced CD16 + monocytes together with lower number of differentiated CD8 + T cell and T follicular helper states, consistent with selective attenuation of inflammatory myeloid activation and adaptive immune differentiation, rather than broad changes in immune cell composition 53, 54 . Antihypertensive therapy similarly yielded modest effects at the level of major immune lineages (e.g. classical monocyte and NK expansion with plasmablast depletion), but substantial heterogeneity across individual cell states (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Tables 8). Different antihypertensive classes were preferentially associated with distinct immune compartments, with significant associations for β-(ILC2 expansion) and α-blockers (naïve early B-cell expansion), and weaker associations for ACE (angiotensin-converting enzyme) inhibitors, ARBs (angiotensin II receptor blockers) and CCBs (calcium channel blockers; Fig 5a). These results indicate that pharmacological modulation is highly cell-state specific 55 and qualitatively distinct from compositional signatures of disease reported earlier.

To further test whether observational drug associations reflect the diseases for which treatments are prescribed rather than direct pharmacological effects, we next combined propensity-score matching (PSM) with generalised additive models (GAM) to distinguish treatment-associated immune remodeling from disease-associated immune signatures (Methods; https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings. 24). Across cardiometabolic diseases, statins use was associated with attenuation of disease-related immune alterations (Fig. 5b, https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings Tables 10). Among all 33 disease-cell-type pairs showing nominal evidence of a disease association (p < 0.05) across 6 cardiometabolic conditions in UKB, the disease effect was smaller in absolute magnitude among statin-treated individuals, with 18 associations attenuated and 15 reversed effect while remaining closer to zero. Expansion of innate lymphoid progenitors observed in angina, ischaemic heart disease and hypercholesterolaemia was reduced among statin-treated individuals (disease × statin interaction β = -1.37, -0.88, and -0.75, respectively; all p < 0.01; Fig. 5b). Likewise, statin use was associated with reduced expansion of innate-like cell states in angina, including NK CD56 bright cells, memory CD4 + T cells and IL7R + CD8 + MAIT cells (disease × statin interaction β = -1.46, -1.26, and -0.95, respectively; all p < 0.015). The largest effect was observed for NK CD16 + adaptive cells in myocardial infarction, where disease state was associated with a 1.22 s.d. higher counts in untreated donors, whereas the corresponding disease-associated effect among statin-treated donors was -0.58 s.d. (disease × statin interaction β = -1.80, p = 2.54 x 10 -4 ). Other examples were CD4 + MAIT cells in myocardial infarction (disease × statin interaction β = -1.44, p = 5.37 x 10 -3 ), NK CD56 bright and memory CD4 + T cells in ischaemic heart disease (disease × statin interaction β = -0.86 and -0.78, p = 8.41 x 10 -3 and 0.019, respectively). Together, these findings indicate that statin use is associated with attenuation of cardiometabolic disease-associated immune alterations towards profiles observed in unaffected individuals, consistent with partial normalisation of immune composition rather than a uniform treatment-associated shift 43, 54, 56.

Antihypertensive therapies likewise modified disease-associated immune states, although the affected populations differed across drug classes (Fig. 5c; https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings Tables 9 10 11 12). ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, calcium-channel blockers, α-blockers, β-blockers and diuretics were each associated with distinct combinations of T-cell, B-cell, NK-cell and monocyte states within hypertensive donors 55, 57 . Across all drug classes, 33 treatments-disease-cell-state associations met p < 0.05, involving 27 immune states. The strongest class-specific association was observed for α-blockers and CD4 + Treg effector cells (β = 1.23, p = 7.89 x 10 -4 ), with others including naive late CD4 + T cells and ARBs (β = -0.59, p = 9.92 x 10 -4 ), B naive early and α-blockers (β = 0.87, p = 4.65 x 10 -3 ), naive late CD8 + T cells and β-blockers (β = -0.43, p = 6.28 x 10 -3 ), and CD4 + central-memory IFN cells and ACE inhibitors (β = 0.41, p = 0.011). Overall, our results show that circulating immune composition reflects both disease status and treatment exposure, and that these signatures are distinct. These findings show that scCBCs can detect treatment-associated immune signatures in population cohorts and provide a basis for future longitudinal studies of pharmacological response.

Single-cell immune composition improves discrimination of common diseases

Given that immune composition reflects the cumulative effects of ageing, environmental exposures and disease, we next assessed its capacity to distinguish individuals with and without common diseases and to identify the immune-cell populations contributing to this discrimination. We trained penalised logistic regression models to distinguish individuals with and without approximately 80 common diseases using CT2 scCBCs alone, estimating discriminative performance by repeated five-fold cross-validation (Methods). We then added demographic and lifestyle covariates (age, sex, ancestry, BMI and smoking status) to assess whether immune composition provided additional discriminatory information.

Immune composition alone provided moderate discrimination for multiple chronic diseases across both cohorts (mean AUCs ~0.6-0.8), with the strongest performance observed for cardiometabolic diseases, demonstrating that disease-related information could be recovered from scCBCs without prior knowledge of demographic or lifestyle characteristics (Fig. 6b, https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings Table 13). Across all evaluated diseases, the mean AUC was 0.55 in UKB and 0.57 in G&H, and 13 of 82 and 12 of 40 conditions, respectively, achieved a mean AUC greater than 0.6. In UKB, coronary atherosclerosis, ischaemic heart disease, myocardial infarction, type 2 diabetes, obesity, hypercholesterolaemia and hypertension were consistently among the conditions with the highest discriminative performance. Conversely, acute infectious diseases showed little discriminatory signal, likely due to their transient nature (Supplementary Table 13). Similar patterns were observed in G&H, where type 2 diabetes, hypercholesterolaemia and hypertension achieved the highest discriminative performance, while musculoskeletal disorders, including chronic joint pain and Dupuytren disease, were also classified with AUCs above 0.6 (Fig. 6c, https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings Table 13). Of note, these signatures were not shared with obesity, potentially indicating that these are unlikely to capture obesity-induced joint pain or diabetes-associated Dupuytren disease.

We next examined the immune features driving discrimination, rather than focusing solely on overall discriminative performance. The contributing cell populations broadly recapitulated the disease-associated programmes identified above. Across both cohorts, cardiometabolic diseases were characterised by expansion of inflammatory monocyte populations and depletion of naive T cells and enrichment of differentiated memory and effector lymphocyte compartments, whereas G&H additionally exhibited stronger CD4 + central memory signatures and reduced NK abundance (https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings. 25, https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings Table 13). Together, these patterns suggest that disease discrimination was supported by coordinated changes across multiple immune populations rather than by any single cell type.

Finally, we assessed immune-derived discrimination alongside conventional demographic and lifestyle covariates. As expected, adding age, sex, ancestry, BMI and smoking status improved overall discriminative performance (https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings. 26, https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings Table 14). Joint models achieved AUCs approaching or exceeding 0.8 for obesity, breast cancer and coronary atherosclerosis in UKB, and for obesity, dupuytren/fibroblastic disease, type 2 diabetes and hypercholesterolaemia in G&H. Across diseases, mean AUCs reached 0.62 in UKB and 0.65 in G&H, with gains exceeding 0.1 for 20 and 14 conditions, respectively. Inflammatory CD4 + monocytes in vascular disease and memory T-cell populations in metabolic disorders retained disease associations in these models, suggesting that immune-derived discrimination was not fully explained by demographic and lifestyle differences between cases and controls.

These analyses therefore show that high-resolution immune composition captures disease-discriminative information beyond conventional demographic and lifestyle characteristics, particularly for cardiometabolic and musculoskeletal conditions. scCBCs provide interpretable quantitative profiles reflecting the combined influences of ageing, environmental exposures and disease across distinct population contexts.

Single-cell data immune composition resolves disease heterogeneity beyond clinical diagnosis

We next investigated whether high-resolution immune composition could resolve biologically distinct patient subgroups within diseases. For conditions with mean scCBC-based AUC > 0.7, we applied Gaussian mixture models to scCBCs to identify patient clusters, selecting the number of groups using the Bayesian information criterion. Clusters were subsequently characterised using donor variables (e.g. age, sex, ancestry, time to diagnosis), comorbidity profiles and quantitative disease biomarkers (Methods).

Across both cohorts, scCBC-based clustering identified clinically distinct subgroups within several common diseases (Fig. 6d-f). These clusters differed not only in immune-cell architecture but also in metabolic status, biochemical markers and comorbidity burden, indicating that immune composition captures clinically meaningful heterogeneity beyond disease diagnosis alone. Cardiovascular conditions in UKB shared recurrent inflammatory subgroups characterised by expansion of CD14 + monocytes and distinct lymphoid profiles (Fig. 6d-f, https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings. 27a). Patients in these inflammatory clusters had greater adiposity, more pronounced dyslipidaemia and longer disease duration, with clinical biomarkers further delineating distinct metabolic and vascular profiles 58 . Across angina, ischaemic heart disease and myocardial infarction, increased CD14 + monocyte abundance was a shared disease feature, whereas distinct B-and T-cell profiles differentiated patient 10 . subgroups, consistent with immune diversification over the course of disease 59 . Hypothyroidism likewise comprised immune-defined subgroups with distinct clinical profiles (Fig. 6e). One subgroup showed increased inflammatory monocyte abundance and depletion of naïve CD8 + T and MAIT cells, accompanied by adverse renal, hepatic and metabolic biomarker profiles. A second subgroup had relatively preserved naïve and central memory T-cell populations and less pronounced biochemical abnormalities. These findings reveal substantial immune and metabolic heterogeneity among individuals sharing the same diagnosis.

Comparable heterogeneity emerged in G&H. Among patients with iron-deficiency anaemia, a small subgroup (8/153; 5.2%) showed increased CD5 + B-cell abundance -a population associated with potential pre-leukaemic profiles in our dataset (Giacopuzzi et al.)-alongside lower vitamin B12 (mean 296.9 versus 486.4 and 542.1 in the other clusters; FDR-adjusted p=0.031 and 0.019, respectively) and higher urea (5.32 versus 4.04 and 3.59; FDR-adjusted p=0.037 for both comparisons; Fig. 6g, https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings. 27b). This subgroup links a distinct immune profile to concomitantly lower vitamin B12 levels, potentially reflecting combined effects of iron and vitamin B12 insufficiency on haematopoiesis 60 . In hypothyroidism, the smaller subgroup (17/75 patients; 22.7%) was older (49.5 versus 41.3 years; FDR-adjusted p = 0.020) and diagnosed later (43.9 versus 36.2 years; FDR-adjusted p = 0.024), with higher ALT (24.4 versus 21.3), fasting glucose (6.01 versus 5.32), HbA1c (48.3 versus 43.6) and total protein (75.6 versus 72.6; all FDR-adjusted p < 0.05; Fig. 6h, https://docs.google.com/document/d/14ihdihnSfdVMKbLjbcybodzjWJNjXs3Qq-xgVx3GIiY/edit#sfi_tuckerloadings. 27c). This subgroup exhibited a less favourable metabolic and hepatic biomarker profile despite the absence of diagnosed diabetes 61 , suggesting that age and metabolic context contribute to immune heterogeneity in thyroid dysfunction, although subgroup profiles differed between cohorts. Overall, these analyses show that single-cell immune profiling reveals disease heterogeneity beyond conventional diagnostic categories. scCBCs delineate immune-defined subgroups with distinct metabolic and clinical profiles, supporting their potential for patient stratification and providing a basis for prospective evaluation of progression, recurrence and organ complications.

Discussion

The immune system integrates genetic background with the cumulative effects of ageing, infection, metabolism and environmental exposures, yet these influences have traditionally been studied in isolation 4, 32 . Here we combine population-scale single-cell transcriptomics with matched haematology measurements and electronic health records to quantify immune-cell composition across 5,079 individuals from two population cohorts spanning white European, British-Pakistani and British-Bangladeshi ancestry. Previous population studies established that human immune variation is strongly influenced by non-heritable factors and that age, shared environment and exposures such as smoking contribute substantially to variation in circulating immune-cell populations 26, 32, 62 . We extend this framework to single-cell resolution, showing how age, sex, lifestyle, disease and treatment jointly shape transcriptionally defined immune populations, and that immune composition carries informative signals for disease discrimination and for resolving immunologically and clinically distinct subgroups within the same diagnosis. By deriving quantitative single-cell blood counts, we generalise the clinical complete blood count to high-resolution immune states and establish immune composition as an integrative phenotype linking life-course exposures, population context and disease heterogeneity.

Large population single-cell studies such as OneK1K demonstrated the power of single-cell genomics for resolving inter-individual immune variation and its genetic determinants 15 . A central advance of our work is the development of quantitative single-cell immune phenotypes that overcome an important limitation of conventional single-cell compositional analyses 63 . Relative cell proportions are intrinsically interdependent, making it impossible to distinguish true biological expansion of individual populations from secondary changes driven by fluctuations elsewhere in the immune compartment. Anchoring single-cell proportions to matched clinical haematology measurements enables estimation of absolute immune-cell abundance while preserving the transcriptional resolution afforded by single-cell genomics. More broadly, this framework illustrates how established clinical 11 . phenotypes and high-dimensional molecular measurements can be integrated to generate quantitative traits suitable for population-scale analyses.

Our analyses reveal marked restructuring of immune composition across the adult life course. Consistent with previous studies [20] [21] [22] [23] , ageing was characterised by contraction of naïve lymphocyte compartments together with expansion of differentiated cytotoxic and inflammatory myeloid populations, reflecting progressive immunosenescence. The broad age range of Genes & Health allowed us to resolve distinct, often non-linear, age-associated trajectories across immune populations that are difficult to capture in cohorts restricted to older adults. Sex effects were similarly dynamic, with several adaptive immune differences emerging only after midlife, indicating that immune sexual dimorphism develops progressively across adulthood 28 . Extending previous evidence that immune ageing can be non-linear 21, 62 , our findings show that age-associated immune variation is neither uniform across cell types nor shared identically across populations, but is shaped by both sex and population context.

Among modifiable exposures, smoking and BMI had pronounced effects on immune composition. Importantly, both exposures shifted the immune system towards an older state beyond that expected from chronological age, consistent with accelerated immune ageing 7, 34 . Smoking predominantly remodelled adaptive immune compartments, with effects that were largely reversible following smoking cessation, whereas adiposity produced broader alterations across innate and adaptive immunity that became increasingly pronounced with age. Notably, the attenuation of smoking-associated compositional changes after cessation contrasts with the persistent effects of prior smoking reported for adaptive immune responses and epigenetic state 7 , potentially suggesting a different rate of immune system recovery for cell composition compared to other functions of of the immune system, or other unmeasured effects not considered here. The data further enhances the fine-grained resolution of cellular compositional changes reported in previous studies. For instance, the observed expansion of early and late naïve B cells, CD4 + central memory subsets spanning Tfh, Th1, Th2, Th17 and Th22 lineages, and naïve Tregs, extends prior observations of obesity-linked broad B-cell and T-cell remodelling by resolving the specific cell states involved 34 . Similarly, UKB revealed BMI-associated decreases in NK CD16 + IFN + and GNLY + /IL7R + MAIT subsets, providing finer cell-state resolution relative to earlier reports of obesity-associated changes in NK and MAIT compartments 39 and suggesting a more selective erosion of innate-like immune surveillance 40.

A core finding of this work is that population context fundamentally shapes immune composition in health and disease. Even for shared diseases, immune programmes differed markedly between cohorts, with UK Biobank showing greater myeloid and cytotoxic remodelling and Genes & Health greater adaptive-memory, humoral and interferon responses. These differences likely reflect variation in age, multimorbidity, infectious exposure and disease prevalence, underscoring that immune signatures are not universal biomarkers but context-dependent products of pathology and population history 64 . At the same time, multiple clinically distinct diseases converged on a restricted set of conserved immune programmes. Rather than generating disease-specific immune signatures, cardiometabolic, inflammatory and infectious disorders repeatedly recruited shared modules involving inflammatory myeloid activation, adaptive-memory remodelling and type I interferon responses. This convergence suggests that diverse diseases exploit common components of immune organisation, with disease specificity arising primarily through differences in the relative contribution and timing of these programmes 65 . Other examples were the selective ILCP enrichment in digestive disorders (cholelithiasis, gastro-oesophageal reflux disease, other gastric-duodenal conditions) suggesting a shared precursor-like innate lymphoid programme across the upper gastrointestinal tract, potentially reflecting activation at mucosal barriers preceding differentiation into effector subsets for tissue repair and immune regulation 66 . Similarly, humoral remodelling was prominent in skin cancers and dermatitis (infective and seborrhoeic), with CD5 + B-cell enrichment. Overall, our model provides a framework for understanding the substantial overlap between genetic susceptibility, environmental exposures and chronic inflammatory diseases, and supports the concept that immune composition represents an intermediate phenotype through which multiple pathogenic processes are integrated [1] [2] [3] 10, 11 . The availability of electronic health records further enabled immune composition to be studied within its broader clinical context. Medication analyses demonstrated that cardiovascular therapies partially attenuated disease-associated immune states, highlighting the importance of accounting for treatment exposure when interpreting immune variation in observational studies. While our study size allowed explorations limited to the most-commonly used drugs, our data suggests treatment effects to be distinct from disease signatures. More resolution will come with larger, controlled experiments. For instance, the association of CD14 + monocytes and coronary atherosclerosis seems to increase in statin-treated patients, but only the disease-drug effect was significant, while the disease term was not. This may reflect limited power to detect the disease association among untreated individuals, or treatment status may capture differences in disease severity or timing, for example if individuals with more severe coronary atherosclerosis were more likely to receive treatment or initiated treatment after disease onset.

Beyond association mapping, high-resolution immune composition contributed information relevant to the discrimination of several common chronic diseases and identified immune-defined subgroups of patients sharing the same diagnosis but differing in metabolic characteristics and comorbidity burden. That this degree of biological and clinical structure can be recovered from a single cross-sectional measurement highlights the potential of prospective longitudinal immune profiling in population cohorts. Repeated sampling could move beyond between-individual associations to capture within-individual immune dynamics, establish how immune states evolve before and after disease onset or treatment, and identify dynamic biomarkers of progression and therapeutic response. Although the unprecedented resolution achieved in this work is beyond what can be implemented in the clinical setting, these results demonstrate that transcriptionally resolved immune composition captures clinically relevant biological information beyond conventional demographic and lifestyle variables. As population-scale single-cell datasets continue to expand and longitudinal sampling becomes increasingly feasible 67 , immune composition may become a useful quantitative biomarker for patient stratification, disease monitoring and therapeutic response.

The inherent limiting features of this study point to opportunities for future work. While scCBCs substantially improve the interpretability of single-cell compositional data by providing estimates of absolute immune-cell abundance, these remain inferred from matched haematological measurements rather than directly measured for each transcriptionally defined population. Furthermore, our analyses were restricted to peripheral blood mononuclear cells and therefore do not capture granulocyte populations or tissue-resident immune compartments that contribute to many diseases. The integration of electronic health records enabled immune composition to be studied across a broad range of diseases, exposures and treatments, although the observational design does not by itself establish causality and residual confounding from factors such as disease severity, treatment adherence or unmeasured exposures may remain. Finally, while the inclusion of two ancestrally and demographically distinct cohorts represents a major strength of this study, extending similar analyses to additional global populations will be essential for capturing the full spectrum of human immune diversity and local exposures.

In summary, quantitative single-cell blood counts reveal a structured landscape of human immune variation shaped by ageing, sex, modifiable exposures, disease and treatment. Common diseases repeatedly perturb a restricted set of shared immune programmes, but the expression of these programmes varies substantially across population contexts and captures clinically meaningful heterogeneity within individual diagnoses. As population-scale molecular cohorts become increasingly linked to longitudinal health records, this framework provides a foundation for dissecting how these influences combine to shape human immune variation and disease. 13.

Stability of non-genetic covariates over time

Smoking behaviour showed limited variability over follow-up. Between baseline assessment and PBMC collection, 245 individuals reported quitting smoking, whereas 39 reported initiating smoking.

BMI showed greater temporal variability, with 1,043 participants changing BMI category during follow-up. We therefore assessed the robustness of BMI-related associations using four alternative measures: BMI at baseline, BMI at recall, the polygenic risk score (PRS) for BMI, and clinical diagnoses of obesity. BMI at baseline refers to measurements collected between 2006 and 2010, while BMI at recall corresponds to the most recent measurement available from the imaging visits conducted from 2014 onwards. The PRS for BMI was computed by UK Biobank (Field 26216). Obesity diagnoses were extracted from electronic health records as described in methods; it is important to note that only the first recorded diagnosis was available, and thus we could not ascertain whether individuals subsequently reversed their obesity status.

To evaluate whether these covariates had consistent effects on immune cell-type abundance, we performed four separate linear models, one for each covariate: scCBCs ~ sex + age/10 + covariate.

Disease definition

We derived 330 disease definitions from the MVP-Finngen-UKB meta-analysis phecode set (https://mvp.finngen.fi/), manually supplementing with 20 additional autoimmune and mental health disorders to enhance coverage. Three were subsequently excluded due to duplication (essential hypertension) or challenges in extracting reliable diagnoses (glomerular disorders classified elsewhere and kidney transplant status).

Disease definitions corresponded to phecodes, which aggregate individual ICD codes into clinically meaningful categories, thereby reducing sparsity and enhancing statistical power in association analyses. Phecodes were mapped to ICD-10, Read2, and CTV3 codes using the UK Biobank Read-to-Phecode repository 71 . In UK Biobank, diagnoses were extracted from Hospital Episode Statistics (HES), cause-of-death records, cancer registries, and general practitioner (GP) clinical events using the ukbrapR package 72 . For Genes & Health, three-and four-digit ICD-10 codes were available, already curated by the cohort consortium, and also included SNOMED from primary care and other sources, where a SNOMED code could be mapped to a single ICD10. Diagnoses were extracted from their first recorded occurrence. Implausible dates (e.g. diagnosis pre-birth) were removed.

Associations between immune cell types and diseases

Associations between single-cell complete blood counts (scCBCs) and disease status were assessed using generalised additive models (GAMs), which allow non-linear associations between covariates and immune cell abundances. Analyses were limited to diseases represented by at least 35 cases. Separate models were fitted for each cell type, as specified below: UKB: scCBCs ~ sex + s(age, by = sex) + s(BMI) + smoking status + disease G&H: scCBCs ~ sex + s(age, by = sex) + ancestry + s(BMI) + smoking status + disease where s(⋅) denotes smooth functions estimated using B-spline basis expansions.

Covariates were processed in accordance with prior analyses: age and BMI were standardised to z-scores, and sex, smoking, ancestry, and disease status were encoded as categorical variables. To ensure comparability across models, scCBCs were variance-scaled within cell types while preserving mean biological differences. Accordingly, beta coefficients for disease status represent the expected difference in standardised cell abundance between cases and controls, after adjustment for covariates, with positive values indicating higher abundance in cases and negative values indicating lower abundance. In G&H, smoking data and BMI information were available for n=773 and 770 participants respectively. Control cohorts were stringently defined as individuals without diagnoses belonging to the same disease category (e.g., controls for type 2 diabetes excluded all metabolic disorders), mitigating potential misclassification and class imbalance. Participants with malignant neoplasms were excluded from both case and control groups to isolate disease-specific immune variation from oncological effects. P-values for disease effects were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate procedure. All models were fitted in R using the mgcv package 73 , with smoothness parameters estimated via restricted maximum likelihood (REML).

Disease network

A disease-cell type signature network was constructed from UKB and G&H cohort-specific disease-cell type (CT3) association results. For each disease and cohort, we defined a signed cell-type signature by retaining cell types with nominal association at p<0.05 and encoding the direction of the disease effect (β≥0 as up, β<0 as down). Disease-cohort nodes were retained only if they contained at least two such signed cell types. Nodes represent disease-cohort pairs; their size reflects the number of nominal hits. Edges were drawn between two nodes when they shared at least two signed cell types and their Jaccard similarity (intersection over union) was at least 0.2. Isolates were removed for display.

The network was visualised using a stress layout. Node colour indicated disease category and node shape indicated cohort (circle for UKB, triangle for G&H). Labels were shown only for the most connected or largest nodes to maintain visual clarity. All steps were implemented in R using igraph, tidygraph and ggraph, with random seed 123 for reproducibility.

Primary care prescription data pre-processing

Primary care prescription data were obtained from UK Biobank general practitioner (GP) records. Medications were identified using multiple coding systems, including Read v2, British National Formulary (BNF), and Dictionary of Medicines and Devices (dm+d) codes. The dataset also contained drug names, prescribed quantities, and issue dates. Records were restricted to participants with available linkage in the CARDINAL dataset. To improve the completeness of drug names, missing entries were imputed from Read v2 code descriptions using the lookup table provided by UK Biobank (Resource 592; https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/showcase/refer.cgi?id=592), which maps Read codes to canonical medication terms (67,614 code-term pairs). Drug names were harmonised by 18.

merging the dataset on Read v2 codes. Drug names were subsequently normalised to represent compound names only. All terms were converted to lowercase, and parenthetical expressions, dosage indicators, formulation descriptors (e.g., "tablets", "capsules", "cream", "patch"), and numeric values were removed. Cleaned names were then converted to title case and whitespace-normalised to produce concise drug identifiers. To consolidate synonymous entries, a collapsing rule was applied to replace longer or derivative names with their shortest unique prefix, yielding a single canonical representation for each distinct medication. The final dataset comprised 283,053 prescriptions from 2,260 CARDINAL participants, corresponding to 2,265 unique cleaned drug names.

Associations between immune cell types and drug prescriptions

We assessed the effect of commonly prescribed drugs, considered as comorbid exposures, on cell-type abundance, independently of the diseases for which they are typically prescribed. Specifically, we considered statins and antihypertensives, where statin exposure was defined as any prescription of atorvastatin, pravastatin, rosuvastatin, or simvastatin, while antihypertensive exposures comprised five classes: diuretics, calcium channel blockers (CCBs), ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), β-blockers, and α-blockers. Medication prescription data were available for 1,840 donors. After excluding donors with cancer or autoimmune disease diagnoses, analyses were performed on 1,744 individuals. Cell-type abundance (scCBC) was modelled for each immune cell type and each drug using generalised additive models (GAMs) of the form: scCBC ∼ sex + s(age) + s(BMI) + smoking status + s(risk factors) + prescribed drug, where smooth terms s(⋅) captured nonlinear effects of age, BMI and risk factors, and prescribed drug was a binary indicator for exposure to each specific medication (tested individually). Risk factors were selected individually for each drug to control for potential confounding due to differences in baseline levels of drug targets across donors. Specifically, HDL, LDL, and total cholesterol were included for statins, while diastolic and systolic blood pressure were included for antihypertensives. scCBCs were variance scaled, while all the numerical covariates were standardised.

Disentangling disease status and drug prescription effects on cell type abundance

To disentangle disease-associated immune alterations from medication-related effects, we extended our previous statistical models by jointly modelling disease status, prescribed drug use, and their interaction. Medication prescription data were available for 1,840 donors. After excluding donors with cancer or autoimmune disease diagnoses, analyses were performed on 1,744 donors. Controls were further filtered to exclude individuals with related conditions (e.g., for myocardial infarction analyses, controls without myocardial infarction but with ischaemia were removed).

We implemented a two-stage pipeline combining propensity score matching (PSM) with generalised additive models (GAMs) to estimate associations between cardiovascular drug prescriptions and standardised immune cell-type abundances (scCBC), stratified by disease status. Statin exposure was defined as any prescription of atorvastatin, pravastatin, rosuvastatin, or simvastatin. Antihypertensive exposures comprised six classes: diuretics, calcium channel blockers, ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, β-blockers, and α-blockers. Disease indicators included hypercholesterolaemia (E4_HYPERCHOL), myocardial infarction (I9_MI_STRICT), ischaemic heart disease (I9_IHD), hypertension (I9_HYPTENS), coronary artery disease (I9_CORATHER), and angina pectoris (I9_ANGINA).

For drug-disease pairs permitting matched cohorts (statins across diseases; antihypertensives across all classes), PSM was conducted separately for each disease using 1:1 nearest-neighbour matching without replacement (MatchIt package). Propensity scores were estimated via logistic regression on age, sex, BMI, smoking status, disease indicator, and risk factors (for statins: LDL, HDL, total cholesterol; for antihypertensives: diastolic and systolic blood pressure). When healthy (disease-free) drug-exposed donors were unavailable, matching was restricted to diseased individuals, conditioning on age, sex, BMI, smoking, and risk factors. Covariate balance was evaluated using Love plots of absolute standardised mean differences (threshold 0.1; cobalt).

Disease discrimination

Disease risk was modelled using penalised logistic regression with repeated cross-validation, followed by an unpenalised inference model. For each disease endpoint, we constructed a binary outcome (case vs control), and tested only with diseases at least 50 cases. Controls were stringently defined as individuals lacking any diagnosis within the same disease category (e.g., for type 2 diabetes, controls excluded all metabolic disorders), minimising misclassification and class imbalance. Participants with malignant cancers were excluded from both cases and controls to isolate disease-specific immune signatures from oncological confounds.

We fitted two complementary models for each endpoint: (i) a scCBC-only model, in which disease status was predicted using CT2 immune-cell abundance alone; and (ii) a combined model, in which the same scCBC features were augmented with established risk factors. For the combined model, continuous predictors were z-scaled. Only scCBCs at the CT2 level were used, to preserve biologically interpretable immune subsets while limiting overfitting.

To obtain robust disease discriminations, we performed stratified 5-fold cross-validation of an L1-penalised logistic regression (lasso) model. Within each training fold, we fitted a binomial lasso model using glmnet, optimising the penalty parameter via internal cross-validation with deviance as the loss function. To account for class imbalance, we incorporated inverse-frequency class weights to downweight the majority class. Out-of-fold predicted probabilities were generated for all individuals, and discrimination was quantified using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). To improve robustness and assess selection stability, we repeated the full 5-fold cross-validation procedure 5 times using independent random fold assignments. Predictors selected in at least 50% of runs were retained for downstream inference. For disease discrimination including risk factors, age, sex, smoking status and BMI were forced into the final model irrespective of penalised selection to ensure epidemiological interpretability. For interpretability, we refitted an unpenalised multivariable logistic regression model on the full dataset, including only the stable predictors plus forced covariates, and obtained regression coefficients, standard errors, Wald z-statistics, p-values and odds ratios. Overall model performance was summarised by the distribution of AUCs across all folds and repetitions (n = 25).

Gaussian mixture model endotyping

Disease endotypes were identified for diseases previously predicted with mean AUC > 0.7 (covariates + scCBC predictors), resulting in 18 diseases in UKB and 16 in Genes and Health. Only cases were considered; for each disease, cell-type predictors were pre-selected via lasso regression from prediction models, excluding intercept, age, sex, ancestry, smoking status, and BMI. Numeric cell-type abundance was z-score scaled. Gaussian mixture models (GMMs; mclust package) with 1-4 components were fitted to the scaled cell-type matrix using ellipsoidal covariance structures (EEE: equal volume/shape/orientation; VVV: variable volume/shape/orientation), with optimal model selection by Bayesian Information Criterion. Cases received hard classifications (1 to G) via maximum a posteriori probability. Endotype assignments were linked to donor metadata (age, sex, ancestry, smoking status, BMI, cell profiles) for downstream analyses.
