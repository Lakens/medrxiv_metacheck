How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Objective: We aimed to 1) evaluate whether AI algorithms could identify a very low-risk subpopulation Lung-RADS 1/2 cases suitable for a safe switch to biennial screening, and 2) externally validate Sybil, an imaging-based deep learning lung cancer risk prediction tool.

We gathered all lung cancer screenings (LCSs) from a tertiary safety-net hospital (n=3120, up to September 2024), manually extracted relevant clinical data, and analyzed Sybil's performance across various subgroups, with a specific focus on Lung-RADS 1 and 2 cases.

We simulated LCS interval extension using Sybil and the Brock model risk scores calibrated on a subset of the National Lung Screening Trial (NLST). Confidence intervals were obtained through bootstrapping.

Results: The median follow-up was 2.3 years (interquartile range 1.5-3.1), with a total 6-year cancer incidence rate of 2.3% (72/3120). Lung-RADS 1 and 2 cases comprised 90.5% of our scans. Sybil's overall area under the curve (AUC) ranged from 0.819 to 0.912; while its 2-year AUC for Lung-RADS 1 and 2 cases was 0.746. In the screening interval extension simulation, calibrating for a 30% shift to biennial screening translated into 33.8% of negative screens in our data being safely extended using Sybil, with no delayed cancers. The Brock model demonstrated an AUC of 0.513 on our data, with a sensitivity of 18.2% using the 1.5% threshold.

Conclusion: Sybil has the potential to safely extend LCS intervals for low-risk individuals, reducing radiology workload and opening spots for life-saving new screenings.

Introduction

The United States Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) has recommended lung cancer screening (LCS) since 2013 based on the results of the National Lung Screening Trial (NLST) and other randomized controlled screening trials (1). In recent years, with ever-rising imaging volumes, radiology practices have been facing an unprecedented workforce shortage (2). The rollout of lung cancer screening has also contributed to the workforce-volume mismatch, as screening contributes directly to increased imaging volume (3) as well as resultant downstream work of clinical consultations, clinical decision-making conferences, follow-up scans, and lung biopsies. These challenges are often exacerbated in lower-resource settings, such as safety-net hospitals. Previous studies have demonstrated the utility of artificial intelligence (AI) in reducing lung cancer screening workload by serving as the first reader (4, 5).

The USPSTF guidelines do not provide recommendations on how screenings should be followed; however, they mention Lung-RADS as an option. In practice, the Lung-RADS criteria are widely adopted as the management framework. According to the criteria, lowest-risk scans (i.e., Lung-RADS 1 and 2) are assigned a 1-year interval, while higher-risk individuals are assigned 6-month or 3-month intervals or a direct biopsy recommendation. Unlike the most recent version of Lung-RADS , the original version was not associated with a companion paper (6) (7) (8) (9), and studies continue to optimize the recommendations.

Some studies and guidelines have suggested that individuals at lower risk of developing lung cancer, who comprise the majority of LCS cases, may be suitable for extended screening intervals without delaying cancer diagnoses (10) (11) (12). However, there are currently no guideline-endorsed riskstratification tools for identifying this subgroup, and assigning individualized screening intervals is a topic of active investigation (13). Personalized breast cancer screening schedules using an AI risk prediction algorithm (Mirai) have previously been investigated (14, 15). Furthermore, while the 1-year performance of Lung-RADS is well documented (16), very few studies have reported the multi-year risk of malignancy across Lung-RADS categories, especially in the US (17), despite its crucial impact on follow-up schedules and policies.

In this study, we pursued 2 main goals: 1) investigating through simulation whether two lung cancer risk prediction tools (deep-learning and imaging-based Sybil (18) and the regression-based Brock model ( 19)) could identify Lung-RADS 1 and 2 cases at sufficiently low risk to extend these patients' screening interval to 2 years, and 2) externally validating Sybil on our data.

Methods

Lung cancer risk prediction

Sybil ( 18) is an image-based deep learning lung cancer risk prediction model that provides 1-to 6year risk scores (i.e., risk of developing cancer within that period of time after a screening instance) ranging from 0 to 1 for screening scans and is accessible via https://github.com/reginabarzilaygroup/Sybil. We chose the Brock model (19, 20), which predicts a nodule's risk score using demographics and radiological features, as a secondary risk-prediction tool and a comparison reference. The Brock model is being used in the International Lung Screening Trial (ILST) to designate screening candidates to biennial screening (21).

Screening interval extension simulation

We simulated the feasibility and safety of assigning very low risk individuals to biennial screening by further risk-stratifying Lung-RADS 1 and 2 cases, who already have low risk of cancer, using our AI tools. For this task, we used the 2-year Sybil risk score, as we intend to identify the risk of developing cancer within 2 years. We use the 1.5% threshold for the Brock model, which is the threshold being used in the ILST. The main outcome of interest was the trade-off between reduced workload (i.e., fewer scans and biopsies) and negative health outcomes (i.e., delayed cancers due to not screening at the 1-year mark). We defined at-risk-of-delay cancers as cancers that could be detected if the patient received the normal 1-year interval screening. The definition was not the temporal 2 nd -year cancer cases (i.e., between 365 and 730 days from the scan); they were determined through two criteria derived from the data to increase precision: 1) If the subject received an appropriate follow-up screen (9 -18 months after the initial screen to allow for variations) and the patient was diagnosed with cancer within 3 months of that screen or the scan demonstrated the cancerous lesion, or 2) if the subject did not have an appropriate follow-up screening but was diagnosed with cancer between 1 and 2 years after the initial scan (we assumed that these cancers would have been detectable).

Delayed cancers were defined as at-risk-of-delay cases assigned to biennial screening. Since Sybil provides continuous scores, we applied cutoffs based on the NLST data as our calibration dataset.

We did this within two frameworks, following a similar methodology in a previous study (22): the cutoff was set based on targeting 1) maximum allowed delayed cancer rate (i.e., 1 -sensitivity), or 2) minimum rate of reduction in follow-up scans of non-interval-cancer cases (i.e., specificity). We also investigated whether any negative interval biopsies (defined similarly to delayed cancers) could have been avoided as a means of reducing workload.

For delayed cancers and averted biopsies, due to their low denominators, we also report a modelbased probabilities based on the Sybil scores and the selected thresholds, in addition to the raw observed rates. Further description can be found in the statistical analysis section.

Data

Our institution

This is a HIPAA-compliant IRB-approved (IRB approval 17-22317) retrospective cohort study that follows the STROBE checklist. We retrieved all LCS CT studies from the Zuckerberg San Francisco Hospital's radiological database up to September 2024 (to allow at least 1 year of follow-up) (supplementary methods). The only exclusion criterion was restricted access to the patient's electronic health records. Reports with no explicit Lung-RADS assignment (7%) were assigned a score based on the report description and according to Lung-RADS v2022 by a medical doctor with 2 years of radiology research postdoctoral training, under the supervision of a fellowship-trained cardiothoracic radiologist. Age and sex were extracted as demographic data, and last available patient encounter with any provider, lung biopsy, and lung cancer diagnosis dates (if applicable) were manually extracted from patient charts to define clinical outcomes. Follow-up duration was defined as the interval between the date of the scan and the last observed encounter. Family history of lung cancer based on the electronic health records and emphysema and nodules details based on the radiology report were also extracted for a subset of Lung-RADS 2 cases (all positive second year cancer cases + random 100 cases with no cancer within 2 years given the difficulty in collecting the data needed for this model such as family history and radiological features from the reports) to calculate the Brock (19,20) risk scores. Lung-RADS 1 cases were excluded from the Brock analysis, as nodules with benign features were excluded from the Brock study and were automatically assigned to biennial screening in the ILST (19, 21).

NLST

We used a subset of Lung-RADS-assigned NLST cases, which were also used in testing Sybil (18), as our calibration dataset in the interval extension simulation, described below. These cases were Lung-RADS 1 and 2 cases that were not diagnosed with cancer in the first year, and had at least 2 years of follow-up or were diagnosed with cancer in the 2 nd year. This yielded 5542 screenings from 2139 subjects, forming our calibration dataset. More details on calibration can be found in the sections below.

Retrospective external validation of Sybil

We validated Sybil scores on our institution's data and in various subgroups. In all analyses, each score was used only with data for the years corresponding to that score; for instance, the 3-year score was only validated on cancer outcomes within the first 3 years after a scan, and only on cases with follow-ups up to that point.

Statistical analysis

Results

Overall descriptives

The final dataset comprised 3120 screenings (from 2125 patients) dating back to March 2015, 38 of which Sybil failed to provide a score for (Figure 1). The mean age of cases was 63.1 ± 6.9 years, of which 2625 (84.1%) were male. Total follow-up was 7785.3 years with a median of 2.3 years (interquartile range (IQR) = 1.5 -3.1 years). Within 6 years, 72 cases (2.3%) were diagnosed with lung cancer. Overall, Lung-RADS 0 to 4x cases comprised 1.4%, 22.0%, 68.5%, 3.8%, 2.5%, 1.7%, and 0.3% of the scans, respectively (Figure 2). The share of Lung-RADS 1, 2, 3, and 4a cases that received their next screening in a timely manner (within 2 years, 2 years, 1 year, and 6 months, respectively, to allow for variations) was 38.7%, 48.9%, 54.8%, and 40.8%, respectively. Visualization of intervals to the next screening is shown in Figure 3. Table 1 and Figure 4 show cancer statistics, classified by Lung-RADS, for all scans. One-year cancer incidence after only baseline scans was 1.8% (35/1927), and there were 14 and 11 cancer cases in the first 2 years after all and baseline-only Lung-RADS 1 and 2 scans (Table 1, supplementary table S1).

Screening interval simulation extension

We simulated transitioning the lowest-risk Lung-RADS 1 and 2 cases to a 2-year screening interval by determining cut-offs for Sybil scores based on the NLST dataset (i.e., the calibration dataset).

Maximum delayed cancer rates of 0%, 5%, and 10% for predicting 2nd-year cancer in the calibration dataset translated into 0/12 second-year cancers being delayed in testing, with modeled delayed cancer rates of 1.2% (95% CI 0.0 -5.6%), 2.3% (0 -12.2%), and 2.3% (0.1 -26.5%), respectively (Table 2). Targeting workload reductions of 10%, 20%, and 30% resulted in 0 delayed cancers in the test group (modeled 1.2% (0 -3.9%), 1.2% (0 -4.8%), and 4.9% (0.3 -14.2%), respectively), and yielded up to 33.8% (31.7 -36.0%) reduction of negative screenings and 1/9 (11.1%) averted negative biopsies.

The Brock model showed an AUC of 0.513 (0.333 -0.721) for predicting 2 nd -year lung cancers (Supplementary Figure S3). With a threshold of 1.5%, 9 out of 11 (81.8%) cases had a delayed lung cancer diagnosis.

We performed a sensitivity analysis by limiting only to baseline studies, as this is how the Brock model is being used in ILST. In the Sybil analysis, there were 0/9 delayed cancers up to 10% delayed cancer rate allowed, with modeled delays of 1.2% (0 -18.1%), 1.2 (0 -19.2%), and 1.2 (0 -24.1%) for 0%, 5%, and 10%, respectively. Workload reductions of up to 40% did not delay any cancers, with modeled delay rates of 0.6% (0 -3.1%), 0.6% (0 -3.1%), 1.3% (0 -10.0%), and 6.2% (0 -17.9%), for 10%, 20%, 30%, and 40% workload reductions, respectively. Brock model AUC for baseline cases was 0.521 (0.282 -0.783), with 6/8 cancers delayed (sensitivity = 25.0%).

Retrospective Sybil external validation

Sybil's AUC for predicting cancer ranged from 0.819 (95% CI 0.749 -0.879) (6-year score) to 0.912 (0.859 -0.954) (1-year score) (Figure 5). Nonetheless, when limited to Lung-RADS 1 and 2 cases, the performance of Sybil's 2-year score was modest, with an AUC of 0.746 (0.636 -0.850). The best performance was observed in the Lung-RADS 3 subcategory (AUC = 0.965 (0.885 -1.000)).

Discussion

In this study, we provided an overview of LCS cases at our tertiary safety-net hospital and evaluated whether AI tools could help identify suitable cases for switching to biennial lung cancer screening. At our institution, lung cancer screening volume increased rapidly in recent years, with more than a 40% increase from 2022 to 2023, and the number of screenings through September 2024 almost matching the 2023 total. This example of the rapidly growing burden on radiology departments emphasizes the need for novel methods, such as AI, to assist in the process.

Simulations showed promise for Sybil in assigning biennial screening intervals for the lowest-risk patients. In our data, up to 30% of screenings could be switched to a biennial schedule without any delayed cancers, with the caveat of a limited sample size of positive cases.

The 1.5% threshold for the Brock model, on the other hand, did not perform well on our data and delayed more than 80% of 2 nd -year cancer diagnoses. A previous analysis on this model showed that the 1.5% threshold missed 30% of the cancers developed within 2 years (23). Another analysis demonstrated that for 0% delayed cancers, only 2.7% of the cancer-free cases will be assigned to biennial screening (vs 5.7% for Sybil), and for 25.8% of the cancer-free cases to be assigned to biennial screening, 9.8% of the cancer cases will potentially be delayed (vs 0% for our data with 30% safe biennial assignment).

Sybil performed well overall at predicting cancer (AUCs >0.8 across all years and >0.85 for years 1-3), particularly given that our case was an external validation scenario. While its discriminatory performance for our main subgroup of interest (i.e., Lung-RADS 1 and 2 cases) was not satisfactory in terms of AUC, the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve showed a range of very high sensitivity with fair specificity, resulting in the favorable simulation results. This finding highlights the importance of evaluating beyond the raw AUC values and focus on performance at thresholds relevant to the clinical question.

The large number of benign lung cancer screenings makes identifying and extending the interval for even a small portion of them highly valuable for reducing workload and radiation. Hence, it is logical to consider a high-sensitivity, low-specificity threshold for risk-stratification scores. In our Sybil simulations, we reduced second-year screenings by 30% without delaying cancer diagnoses.

Assuming 1000 annual lung cancer screenings, the 30% reduction rate frees up 270 screening spots (0.3 * 1000 (total screenings) * 0.9 (share of Lung-RADS 1 and 2)) that could be used for new baseline screenings. The NELSON and MILD trial results indicated a number needed to scan of 130 and 167 to avert one lung cancer death, respectively (24, 25), meaning that approximately 2 lung cancer deaths would be averted by if 270 new patients fill the spots and adhere to their screenings.

We used Sybil in our study, which is an open-source model. A few previous studies have utilized proprietary deep learning models to extend lung cancer screening intervals. Landy et al. (22) recalibrated LCP-CNN (26) on the national lung screening trial (NLST) dataset to predict a 1-year cancer score, combined it with LCRAT (27), and observed that when the 66% lowest-score individuals were assigned to biennial screening, 18/195 cancers were delayed. When a maximum delay rate of 10% was assumed acceptable, the model safely extended the screening interval for 67.7% of the cases. In our study, the same threshold (90% sensitivity) resulted in a 16.6% rate of safe interval extension. In another recent study (28), researchers developed ScreenLungNet based on a commercial AI assistant, which, in a high negative predictive value (99.9%) setting, yielded a delayed cancer diagnosis rate of 2% and a specificity of 15%.

Many ethical and safety considerations arise when deploying externally validated AI algorithms in the clinical setting. Studies have reported various degrees of performance drop upon external validation of radiology AI algorithms (29, 30). Despite significant improvements in the generalizability of newer algorithms, clinical deployment requires some degree of validation in that specific setting. An important decision in this process is whether to validate retrospectively or prospectively, with the former being much more feasible and the latter being more accurate (31). Another often overlooked component of investigating AI solutions is aligning research and clinical metrics (31). For instance, in our study, while Sybil demonstrated high overall AUCs, its 2-year performance in Lung-RADS 1 and 2 cases, which related most to our use case, was only modest. On the other hand, analysis of the ROC curve indicated potential clinical benefit, as a high-sensitivity, low-specificity threshold could have been beneficial. Furthermore, the interpretability limitations of deep learning algorithms warrant caution in the extent of their involvement (32). Another aspect to consider when externally validating algorithms is the effect of scan acquisition parameters. Notably, Sybil has been shown to be robust across various acquisition settings (33).

Our study has several limitations. Firstly, the data were gathered retrospectively from a single center.

One consequence of this design is the use of different Lung-RADS versions, which has added heterogeneity to Lung-RADS categories. Another consequence is that not all screening scans were low-dose CTs. In addition, follow-up rates hovered around 50%, even for high-risk cases such as Lung-RADS 4a. This has most likely contributed to the small number of Lung-RADS 1 and 2 cases that developed cancer within 2 years, limiting the strength of our conclusions and analyses. While screening sessions would ideally occur annually at the same time of year, disruptions to the timeline were common. Moreover, occasionally, patients who were screened were not eligible under the USPSTF screening criteria. Finally, we could not perform a full analysis of the Brock model and had to rely on a subset of patients due to limitations in data collection.

To conclude, we demonstrated that Sybil could effectively identify very low-risk LCS subjects suitable for switching to a biennial screening schedule, effectively reducing the workload on radiology departments and reducing lung cancer mortality by freeing up space for new baseline screenings.

Further research, especially in a prospective setting, is required to validate this potential. Other candidate applications of Sybil in lung cancer screening include improving risk stratification of intermediate-risk subjects (i.e., Lung-RADS 3), enhancing patient communication through providing a numerical risk score, and assisting in assigning high-risk cases to biopsy vs short-term follow-up. All main analyses were conducted for screening instances, not persons (i.e., two screening sessions from the same person count as separate cases), as Sybil provides risk scores for scans, and screening interval decisions are also made based on individual scans. Confidence intervals for proportions were calculated using Wilson score interval with continuity correction. Performance measures for predicting binary outcomes included accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity. Discriminatory performances of Sybil's continuous scores were assessed using the area under the curve (AUC) metric. Confidence intervals for the AUCs were calculated via 1000-resample bootstrapping. To model the delayed cancers and averted biopsies, we assumed a normal distribution for the logit-transformed 2-year Sybil scores of all the at-risk-of-delay and biopsied cases and the modeled rates were the probability of a risk score < the threshold. The 95% CI was derived by 2000 resamples of the NLST calibration data for the cutoff and, for each cutoff, 20 resamples with replacement of our institution's screenings, with the mean and standard deviation of the logit-transformed scores re-estimated in each resample. The 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the resulting 40,000 estimates formed the interval. This accounts for uncertainty in both the cutoff and the score distribution. Analyses were performed in Python 3.12.10 using scikit-learn (v1.8.0), statsmodels (v0.14.6), and scipy (v1.17.0) libraries.
