Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Oropouche virus (OROV) causes recurrent outbreaks in South America, including Colombia. Although Culicoides paraensis is the principal recognized urban vector, the potential involvement of other Culicoides species remains incompletely understood. In Colombia, studies combining viral detection, host-feeding behavior, and ecological suitability are scarce.

We investigated Culicoides communities in urban and peri-urban environments of Puerto Carreño, Vichada, during two seasonal transitions. Species identification combined morphology and COI DNA barcoding, and blood-meal sources were identified using vertebrate molecular markers. OROV RNA was assessed by RT-qPCR targeting the L and S genomic segments, and maximum likelihood prevalence estimates with 95% confidence intervals were calculated accounting for unequal pool sizes, including a conservative estimate.

Introduction

Arboviruses pose a major public health threat as they can shift from zoonotic and sylvatic cycles to rural and urban transmission, driven by climate change, unplanned urbanization, human mobility, and land use change [1, 2]. In tropical regions, environmental change and socioeconomic conditions can alter interactions among wildlife, vectors, and humans, creating opportunities for viral emergence and re-emergence [3]. The cocirculation of endemic arboviruses such as dengue, Zika, and chikungunya complicates clinical diagnosis and surveillance, contributing to the neglect of other emerging pathogens that may present with overlapping syndromes [4]. Historically, OROV has been associated with sylvatic cycles involving sloths as vertebrate hosts, while mosquitoes have been proposed as potential vectors in these cycles [5, 6]. In urban settings in Brazil, the biting midge Culicoides paraensis is the principal vector [7]. However, in recent years, OROV has caused outbreaks across Brazil, Peru, Colombia, French Guiana, Panama, Ecuador, Venezuela, and Trinidad and Tobago, underscoring its regional expansion and growing clinical relevance [8]. While most infections manifest as self-limiting febrile syndromes, OROV has also been associated with meningitis, encephalitis, adverse pregnancy outcomes, and fatal cases, highlighting the need for an improved understanding of its transmission ecology and risk factors [1].

OROV, a member of the family Peribunyaviridae and genus Orthobunyavirus, possesses a tripartite negativesense RNA genome with segments classified as small (S), medium (M), and large (L). The L segment encodes the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, the M segment encodes glycoproteins involved in viral entry together with the non-structural protein NSm, and the S segment encodes the nucleoprotein and the interferon antagonist NSs [1]. This segmented genome allows genetic reassortment, an important mechanism of evolution in orthobunyaviruses [9]. Molecular assays targeting the L segment and the N gene within the S segment are used for OROV detection [10, 11], while sequencing of viral genomic fragments can support identification and provide information on their relationships with reference strains.

Although C. paraensis has long been considered the primary urban vector, expanding geographic reports of OROV transmission raise the possibility that additional vector species may participate in local transmission cycles in distinct ecological contexts [12, 13]. Establishing vector status requires complementary evidence from field and experimental studies, including accurate species identification, viral detection, host-feeding patterns, ecological associations, and experimental demonstration of vector competence [14, 15]. Integrating these lines of evidence is particularly valuable for identifying candidate species and prioritizing them for subsequent experimental evaluation [14, 16].

Although several mosquito species (family Culicidae) have been investigated as potential OROV vectors, Culicoides paraensis, a biting midge in the family Ceratopogonidae, remains the principal recognized urban vector. Experimental studies have also demonstrated susceptibility of Culicoides sonorensis to OROV under laboratory conditions [7, 13, 14]. Culicoides are hematophagous dipterans of the family Ceratopogonidae, whose minute size (<2 mm), cryptic species complexes, and wide distribution complicate their study.

Molecular markers, such as the mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COI) gene, aid species identification [17, 18]. These midges feed on mammals, birds, reptiles, and amphibians [19, 20] and act as potential bridges for zoonotic transmission. Their diverse breeding habitats, including muddy soils, marshes, riverbanks, manure, and decaying matter [21, 22], make surveillance and control difficult. Given these biological characteristics, distinguishing the Culicoides species that may contribute to OROV transmission requires an approach that integrates taxonomy, viral detection and sequence characterization, and ecological context. Such integrative strategies are particularly important in regions where entomological data are sparse, and surveillance systems may underestimate viral circulation.

In Colombia, OROV circulation has been documented in the Amazon and border areas with Venezuela, During 2024-2025, 26 human OROV cases were included in the national epidemiological records compiled for this study, while the co-circulation of two viral lineages was documented in the Colombian Amazon in 2024 [8][23], but the Culicoides species potentially involved in local transmission remain poorly characterized. National research efforts on Culicoides have largely focused on their nuisance role, leaving major gaps in knowledge regarding species distribution, host-use patterns, and vector potential for arboviruses [24, 25]. Vichada, with its mosaic of savannas, rainforests, and extensive hydrographic network, combined with socioeconomic dynamics linked to migration and land-use change [26, 27], constitutes a strategic setting for investigating the contribution of Culicoides to OROV transmission. In this study, we aimed to characterize Culicoides species in urban and peri-urban areas of Puerto Carreño using an integrated approach combining morphological and molecular taxonomy, host-feeding analysis, and OROV RNA detection. We also evaluated species-specific ecological suitability and spatial concordance with reported OROV cases. Together, these analyses provide complementary evidence to prioritize candidate species for experimental vector competence studies and inform entomovirological surveillance of OROV in Colombia.

Methods

Study area:

The study was conducted in the municipality of Puerto Carreño, the capital of the Vichada Department, Colombia, within the Orinoquía ecoregion at the confluence of the Meta and Orinoco rivers (Figure 1). This zone between the Orinoquía and Amazon Basin encompasses a complex mosaic of seasonally flooded savannas, morichales (palm-dominated wetlands), gallery forests, and temporary and permanent wetlands interconnected by an extensive hydrographic network [28]. Collections were conducted during two seasonal transition periods: rainy-to-dry (August-September 2024) and dry-to-rainy (May 2025). The sampling units corresponded to individual urban or rural households. At each site, two to five CDC ultraviolet light traps (model CDC 2506N, Curtis Dyna-Fog's Ltd., USA) were deployed according to property size. Traps were placed both indoors and within 10 m of the dwelling, with placement determined by local land use and nearby habitats likely to host Culicoides breeding or aggregation sites, such as stagnant water organic matter accumulation (e.g., lagoons and banana crops when present), animal enclosures (pigsties, stables, chicken coops), and human living spaces.

The traps were operated for two consecutive nights at each site (17:00-08:00), and collection containers were retrieved each morning. Each trap-night was considered an independent sampling unit; therefore, a trap operated for two consecutive nights contributed two trap-nights. After each sampling round, traps were relocated to new households meeting the same selection criteria. When unusually high midge abundance was recorded at a specific location, additional collections were undertaken during the same fieldwork session.

Capture abundance was standardized by the number of trap-nights to account descriptively for differences in trapping effort between sampling campaigns. Because sampling effort was unequal and collection sites were selected non-randomly, these standardized values were used to describe capture patterns rather than to infer seasonal changes in Culicoides abundance. Sampling completeness was assessed through rarefaction analysis performed in R, using site-level species counts standardized by trap per night. Rarefaction curves enabled the estimation of species richness and evaluation of capture effort across sites, minimizing bias associated with unequal efforts.

Field processing and specimen sorting

Specimens were transported to the Vichada Departmental Public Health Laboratory and chilled at -20 °C for approximately 2 h to induce temporary immobilization. Samples were sorted to retain Culicoides specimens, which were then separated into morphospecies using taxonomic keys [29] [30] [31] [32] [33] [34] [35]. Thus, individual morphological sorting preceded pooling, with representative specimens retained individually for taxonomic and molecular identification, blood-fed females separated for host-feeding analysis, and the remaining individuals pooled for viral RNA detection. Representative specimens of each morphospecies were preserved individually in RNAlater (Invitrogen) for nucleic acid stabilization. Blood-fed females were kept separately for blood meal source analysis, whereas the remaining individuals were grouped into pools of up to 60 specimens by species, collection date, and site for viral detection assays. All materials were shipped on dry ice to the Virology Group facilities at Universidad El Bosque (Bogotá, D.C., Colombia) and stored at -80 °C until downstream laboratory processing.

Integrative taxonomy of Culicoides spp.

Morphological determination

Representative specimens were processed using an integrative taxonomic approach to maintain morphological integrity and downstream suitability. Specimens were cleared in heated 85% lactic acid and dissected under glycerin. Blood-meal source analysis

The identification of vertebrate hosts was conducted on DNA extracted from blood-fed females using conventional PCR, following a previously published protocol [37] with modifications to the thermal cycling parameters to optimize the amplification efficiency. Gradient PCR assays were first calibrated with control blood samples from human, chicken, and duck hosts to define the optimal annealing conditions. Universal vertebrate primers and human-specific primers (Table 1) were used sequentially to detect single or mixed blood meal sources. Each reaction was performed in a final volume of 20 µL, containing 1× qScript PCR Kit (QuantaBio), 0.4 µM of forward and reverse primers, 1 µL of genomic DNA, and nuclease-free water. PCR products were resolved by electrophoresis on 2% agarose gels and documented using a gel imaging system.

Extracted DNA from uninfected C6/36 Aedes albopictus cell line and from a Culicoides male were used as negative controls, while nuclease-free water was used as a reaction control. Samples displaying amplicons of the expected size and sufficient intensity were purified and subjected to Sanger sequencing. The same samples were evaluated using both the universal vertebrate primers and the human-specific primers. The resulting chromatograms were edited in MEGA v12.0.11, and sequence identity was confirmed through BLASTn searches against the NCBI GenBank database. Host species were assigned based on a ≥ 98% sequence identity. This approach enabled the direct identification of vertebrate feeding sources and assessment of potential vector-host associations.

OROV RNA detection analysis

Viral RNA detection was assessed in pools of female Culicoides. Pool size varied according to species, collection site, and sampling date, ranging from 2 to 60 individuals. Specimens were preserved immediately after collection in RNAlater to maintain RNA integrity under remote field conditions. RNA extracted from the pools was initially screened for OROV using two independent one-step RT-qPCR assays targeting the L and S genomic segments (Table 1). RNA from the same pools was not always available for both assays, S2). OROV RNA was extracted from infected cell culture supernatants denominated LET-01 and LET-05, in separate laboratory areas and biosafety cabinets from those used for the field-collected Culicoides samples. During RT-qPCR setup, positive-control templates were added last, after the sample tubes had been placed in the thermocycler. RNA extracted from uninfected C6/36 Aedes albopictus cells served as a negative extraction control, and nuclease-free water was included as a no-template control in each run.

Pooled infection rate estimation

Because pool sizes varied among samples, OROV prevalence was estimated using a pooled-binomial maximum likelihood approach that explicitly accounts for individual pool sizes. Estimates and 95% confidence intervals were calculated using the pooledBin function in the R package binGroup (v2.2.3), with the maximum likelihood estimator and skew-score confidence intervals and are reported per 1,000 individuals.

Estimates were calculated separately for the L and S molecular targets and, using a more conservative criterion, for pools showing concordant positivity for both targets. This criterion reflects the concurrent detection of two genomic segments in Culicoides species not previously characterized in this context.

Nanopore sequencing and phylogenetic analysis of OROV

Samples were selected for amplicon sequencing using the Oxford Nanopore platform based on RNA availability and RT-qPCR results. Samples with Cq values below 36 for either target and samples positive for both the L and S targets were prioritized for sequencing. Multiplex amplification was performed using primer sets targeting L, M, and S (Table 1) genomic segments. Sequencing reads were filtered by length (250-800 bp) and mapped independently against OROV reference sequences using Minimap2 with settings optimized for Oxford Nanopore reads. Only primary alignments with a mapping quality (MAPQ) ≥20 were retained for downstream analyses. Read-derived consensus sequences were reconstructed independently for each sample and genomic region. Positions with a minimum read depth of 10× were retained, and the nucleotide supported by the 70% of reads at each position was incorporated into the consensus sequence; positions with equal support for alternative nucleotides were coded as ambiguous (N). Reference nucleotides were not used to fill uncovered or insufficiently supported positions, and discontinuous regions of coverage were retained as independent fragments. Negative controls were included during the Nanopore sequencing workflow to monitor potential contamination during library preparation and sequencing.

To assess sequence concordance and potential laboratory-source similarity, field-derived consensus sequences were compared with OROV control sequences generated independently by Sanger sequencing. Comparisons were restricted to homologous overlapping regions and included calculation of nucleotide identity and identification of nucleotide differences. For discrepant positions, nucleotide calls in the Nanopore consensus were evaluated against the underlying mapped-read data, including read depth, nucleotide counts, and majority-allele frequency. Pairwise genetic distances were calculated as uncorrected p-distances, representing the proportion of nucleotide differences across homologous positions, with gaps and ambiguous nucleotides excluded pairwise. Distances were calculated separately among field-derived sequences and between fieldderived sequences and laboratory controls for the L (173 nt), M (316 nt), and S (299 nt) segments. For the extended M-segment dataset (656 nt), distances between B76/B82 and publicly available reference sequences were calculated and summarized according to previously assigned phylogenetic groups.

For phylogenetic analyses, Nanopore-derived consensus sequences were aligned with publicly available OROV sequences representing the genetic diversity of the corresponding genomic segments from GISAID up to September 30, 2026. Alignments were constructed separately for the L, M, and S segments and restricted to homologous genomic regions; non-overlapping fragments were not concatenated. The 13 field-derived sequences were initially aligned separately for each genomic segment. For phylogenetic analyses, sequence subsets were selected according to fragment length and the availability of homologous regions in the reference sequences, aiming to retain the largest possible number of samples while maintaining a sufficiently long region for comparison. Because fragment lengths varied among samples, separate alignments were generated for some segments. For the M segment, two alignments covering the same genomic region but differing in sequence length and sample inclusion were analyzed. Most S-segment fragments were approximately 90 bp long and were excluded from phylogenetic analysis when their limited length did not allow meaningful comparison with reference sequences.

Nucleotide substitution models were evaluated using IQ-TREE 2, and Bayesian phylogenetic inference was performed in MrBayes using the selected segment-specific models. Markov chain Monte Carlo analyses were run for 5 × 10⁶ generations, with convergence evaluated using the average standard deviation of split frequencies, effective sample sizes, and potential scale reduction factors. Node support was expressed as posterior probabilities. Phylogenetic placement of field-derived sequences was evaluated in the context of publicly available OROV sequences and previously reported phylogenetic group assignments.

Ecological niche modeling

To infer the potential geographic distributions of the Culicoides species detected in this study, ecological niche models were generated using occurrence records from field collections supplemented with georeferenced data from the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF; https://www.gbif.org/) and previously published datasets [25, [39] [40] [41] [42] [43] [44] [45] [46]. Records were curated to remove duplicates, spatial outliers, and entries that lacked precise geographic coordinates. Only georeferenced records with adequate spatial resolution were retained.

Because the objective was to compare species-specific environmental structuring at a continental scale rather than to produce fine-resolution predictive maps, occurrence records were subjected to quality control before ecological niche modeling. Records with missing or invalid geographic coordinates, exact duplicate coordinate pairs, and locations outside the predefined geographic extent of the Americas (120°W-30°W; 60°S-35°N) were excluded. Occurrences with missing values for the environmental predictors retained in each species-specific model were also removed. No minimum-distance spatial thinning was applied; therefore, geographically proximate records with distinct coordinates could remain in the final occurrence datasets.

Environmental predictors representing climatic, topographic, vegetational, and edaphic attributes were obtained from publicly available global datasets. Bioclimatic variables (BIO1-BIO19), representing long-term summaries of temperature and precipitation, were downloaded from WorldClim v2.1 (1970-2000 normals; https://www.worldclim.org/) at 30 arc-second (~1 km) resolution. The mean annual wind speed at a height of 10 m was derived from the WorldClim monthly climate layers. Elevation data were obtained from NASADEM_HGT/001 (https://lpdaac.usgs.gov/), and soil conditions were represented by the soil organic carbon content in the upper 0-5 cm layer from SoilGrids (https://soilgrids.org/). All raster layers were resampled and harmonized to a spatial resolution of ~ 1 km in WGS84 (EPSG:4326) and clipped to a continental extent encompassing the documented biogeographic distribution of the studied taxa across the Americas (120°W to 30°W; 60°S to 35°N) prior to export from Google Earth Engine.

. Species distribution models were implemented in R (v4.5.1) using the maximum entropy algorithm (Maxnet package) and spatial raster tools from the terra package. Multicollinearity among environmental predictors was assessed independently for each species using the variance inflation factor (VIF). A threshold of VIF ≤ 10 was applied to identify and exclude highly collinear predictors, following established methodological criteria commonly used in ecological modeling and species distribution studies. For each species, a target of 2,000 background points was specified within the modeling extent. However, the effective number of background points used in model fitting varied among species, as determined by the species-specific modeling datasets and the availability of environmental information. The final number of background points used for each species is reported in Table 5. For each species, occurrence records were randomly partitioned into training (70%) and testing (30%) subsets, without spatial stratification. Habitat suitability surfaces were projected using a cloglog transformation. The modeling extent was defined to encompass the maximum documented geographic distributions reported for the studied taxa in previous systematic and faunistic studies, particularly for Culicoides insignis, thereby avoiding extrapolation beyond its known continental range.

Model performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and the True Skill Statistic (TSS), calculated on independent test data (pROC package). Binary suitability maps were derived using thresholds that maximized the sensitivity and specificity (Youden's index). Variable importance was quantified using a drop-AUC approach, in which each retained predictor was sequentially removed, and the resulting reduction in AUC in the test data was recorded. Predictors yielding positive drop-AUC values were interpreted as having independent explanatory contributions ("effective predictors").

To evaluate interspecific ecological similarity, pairwise Jaccard similarity indices were calculated based on the presence or absence of effective predictors for each species in the study. This metric quantifies shared, independently informative environmental drivers rather than spatial niche overlap. Final suitability maps were generated for Culicoides insignis, C. pusillus, C. ignacioi, and C. paraensis to compare the ecological structuring and potential geographic overlap among potential Oropouche virus epidemiological interest Culicoides species.

Species distribution and OROV case overlay

To assess the spatial concordance between predicted Culicoides species suitability and reported human OROV cases, species-specific suitability maps were overlaid with reported case distributions to visualize spatial cooccurrence patterns across the Americas. A fixed suitability threshold (≥ 0.4) was used to define highsuitability areas. This value approximated the range of species-specific optimal discrimination thresholds derived from model performance metrics and was consistently applied across taxa to enable standardized interspecific comparisons. The suitable areas were derived from Maxent models and projected as continuous cloglog outputs. Reported human OROV case data were compiled from the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO/WHO) and national surveillance reports from Brazil (11,888 cases), Panama (501), Peru (330), Colombia (26), Cuba (28), Venezuela (5), and Guyana (1) [8, 11]. For all countries except Venezuela (where data were available only at the national level), cases were assigned to first-order administrative units (e.g., departments or provinces).

To formally evaluate administrative-scale ecological concordance, suitability values were summarized within each first-order administrative unit by calculating the mean suitability. Differences between regions with and without reported OROV cases were assessed using Wilcoxon rank-sum test. In addition, suitability values were dichotomized using a fixed threshold (≥ 0.4) to generate binary high/low suitability categories, which were cross-tabulated against case presence to compute odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals using Fisher's exact test.

Discussion

This study integrates taxonomic, trophic, virological, and ecological approaches to characterize Culicoides communities and their potential relevance to OROV ecology in an understudied region of Colombia.

Morphological identification supported by COI barcoding documented local species diversity, while RT-qPCR and targeted sequencing provided molecular evidence of OROV RNA in C. insignis, C. pusillus, and C. ignacioi. Blood-meal analysis identified vertebrate hosts used by these species, and ecological niche models characterized their environmental associations across the Americas. Together, these findings provide speciesspecific evidence to guide entomovirological surveillance and prioritize further investigations into the potential involvement of Culicoides species in OROV transmission.

Applying an integrative taxonomic framework proved particularly informative for Culicoides, a genus characterized by subtle morphological differentiation and frequent cryptic species complexes. Reliance on a single line of evidence can lead to misidentifications and underestimation of diversity [17, 47]. This study generated the first COI sequence dataset for Culicoides species from Colombia. Phylogenetic analyses revealed well-resolved clades for C. insignis, C. ignacioi, C. foxi and C. pusillus, largely consistent with morphological identifications based on wing, head, and genital characters. However, several morphotypes did not cluster with available reference sequences or form monophyletic groups, highlighting the need to report barcode sequences for neotropical Culicoides species, as their morphological and genetic diversity remains incompletely characterized. Species richness was lower than that reported in sylvatic and peridomestic areas of the Brazilian Amazon [34], but slightly higher than that documented in rural areas with livestock in Brazil, Peru, and Argentina in studies with comparable sampling approaches [48, 49]. The community was strongly dominated by Culicoides insignis, accounting for over 70% of specimens and present at most sites. Similar dominance has been reported in Peru [49] and Argentina [50], supporting its characterization as synanthropic and ecologically tolerant. The marked intraspecific genetic variation observed in C. insignis is consistent with the diversity reported for widely distributed Culicoides species [51, 52]. The exclusive use of CDC light traps may have underrepresented C. paraensis, as human-attraction methods have shown higher collection efficiency for this species in other settings [53].

Molecular detection of OROV RNA in multiple Culicoides species provides evidence of the presence of the viral RNA in local midge populations. In C. insignis, prevalence estimates varied substantially according to the molecular criterion used, with the estimate based on concordant detection of both targets being substantially lower than those obtained from either molecular target individually. Using this more conservative criterion, the estimated prevalence was 3.61 per 1,000 individuals (95% CI: 0.94-9.60). Comparable entomological estimates for OROV remain scarce. During the 2024 outbreak in Cuba, OROV RNA was detected in one of two Ceratopogonidae pools analyzed, including 14 apparently non-engorged females in the positive pool, although specimens could not be identified to species [54]. In contrast, recent surveillance in outbreak areas of Minas Gerais, Brazil, detected no OROV-positive pools among 212 C. paraensis or 607 C. leopoldoi [53]. These contrasting findings emphasize the spatial and temporal heterogeneity of OROV detection in Culicoides populations and the limited availability of directly comparable entomological infection estimates. Interestingly, the prevalence estimated using concordant OROV detection in our C. insignis samples is of a similar order of magnitude to the 0.33% (approximately 3.3 per 1,000) BTV infection rate recently reported in unengorged C. insignis from Ecuador [55], although comparisons between different viruses, sampling designs, and analytical approaches should be interpreted cautiously. Furthermore, given the recognized role of C. insignis as a candidate vector of bluetongue virus in South America, the environmental conditions associated with its distribution in our models may also be relevant to surveillance of other Culicoides borne arboviruses, although their circulation including other Orthabunyavirus was not assessed in this study.

Although no OROV cases have been officially reported in Vichada, underdiagnosis or clinical misclassification with other febrile illnesses, such as dengue, cannot be excluded [4, 56]. Detection of both L and S genomic segments in C. insignis, including some unfed females, suggests that viral RNA detection is unlikely to be solely associated with recent blood ingestion, although this does not demonstrate disseminated infection or vector competence. The marked difference in positivity between the L and S molecular targets highlights the importance of assay-dependent interpretation of OROV RNA detection. Differences in analytical sensitivity, target-region characteristics, or RNA integrity may have contributed to this discordance.

Wise et al. [57] demonstrated that mismatches between diagnostic primers and an Ecuadorian OROV lineage compromised viral detection, with primer redesign improving analytical sensitivity by up to three orders of magnitude. This highlights how genetic variation in target regions can affect molecular detection and should be considered when interpreting differences between diagnostic targets.

Sequence-based characterization provides an additional level of molecular support for OROV detection in Culicoides and allows these detections to be interpreted within the genetic diversity currently recognized in South America. Recent genomic surveillance has revealed substantial OROV diversity in South America, including the co-circulation and changing relative frequency of genetically distinct viral populations in the Colombian Amazon [23, 58, 59]. In this context, the phylogenetic proximity of the midge-derived M-segment sequences to sequences associated with the recent outbreak is consistent with contemporary regional OROV diversity. Importantly, however, the field-derived sequences were not identical to previously reported sequences across the homologous regions analyzed; a pattern observed both for the longer M-segment region and for most of the shorter genomic fragments. This differentiation may reflect previously unsampled OROV viral diversity, particularly because OROV had not previously been documented or genetically characterized in Vichada, although the limited genomic coverage prevents determining whether these differences represent geographically structured diversity or broader variation among circulating OROV populations.

Recent genomic studies have demonstrated that the evolutionary relationships of OROV may differ among its three genomic segments, and formal assignment of contemporary lineages has relied on complete or nearcomplete genomic information [23, 58, 59]. Therefore, the partial sequences recovered here should not be interpreted as evidence for formal lineage assignment or reassortment. Instead, they support the genetic characterization of OROV detected in field-collected Culicoides and highlight the need to obtain complete or near-complete viral genomes directly from biting midges to determine how OROV diversity in vectors relates to that characterized from contemporary human infections in Colombia and elsewhere in South America. Blood meal analysis revealed opportunistic feeding by C. insignis on horses (Equus caballus), humans (Homo sapiens), and pigs (Sus scrofa), consistent with findings from Ecuador [55]. Similar feeding patterns in other hematophagous dipterans in Colombia suggest that shared vertebrate hosts in anthropogenically modified landscapes may facilitate viral spillover [60]. Although horses are not currently considered competent amplifying hosts for OROV, serological surveys in Brazil have reported neutralizing or hemagglutinationinhibiting antibodies against orthobunyaviruses in equines [61, 62], indicating exposure in peri-urban and rural settings.

A central contribution of this study is the comparative ecological characterization of potential epidemiological interest Culicoides species. Previous niche modeling of C. paraensis emphasized temperature-related variables and precipitation seasonality as dominant drivers of distribution [12]. Our models recover part of this signal, particularly temperature stability (BIO03) and precipitation metrics. Prior modeling of C. insignis identified temperature, precipitation during the warmest quarter, elevation, and vegetation indices as key predictors [63, 64]. Our results are broadly concordant, with elevation, precipitation variables (BIO18, BIO19), and wind speed emerging as independently informative predictors. Vegetation and soil variables exerted comparatively minor effects. Although precipitation did not uniformly dominate model performance, it creates temporary changes in surface moisture, and larval habitat availability [50]. Recent models have further identified humidity, soil moisture, and agricultural land use, particularly banana cultivation, as important correlates of C. paraensis suitability, consistent with the use of decaying banana stems and cocoa residues as larval habitats, while phylogeographic analyses have associated banana and cocoa cultivation, human population density, and C. paraensis suitability with OROV expansion [65, 66].

Environmental predictors identified among Culicoides species in our study, suggest that their ecological associations may be more heterogeneous than those commonly represented in mosquito-centered arbovirus models, highlighting the need to consider multiple vector species and their species-specific habitat requirements. Wind-driven movement is increasingly recognized as facilitating range expansion and connectivity in Culicoides [67], providing a plausible ecological explanation for the broad geographic distribution and genetic connectivity observed in C. insignis, although its role in shaping the distribution of this species in the study region remains untested. Furthermore, as occurrence records were not spatially thinned, geographic clustering and uneven sampling intensity in biodiversity databases may have influenced model predictions and performance metrics. Accordingly, the ecological suitability estimates should be interpreted as exploratory, particularly for species represented by relatively few occurrence records.

Spatial comparisons revealed heterogeneous associations between Culicoides predicted suitability and reported OROV cases. Previous projections for C. paraensis have shown discrepancies with reported Oropouche fever occurrence, interpreted as potential underdetection or involvement of additional vectors [12].

This heterogeneity likely reflects the coarse spatial resolution of available epidemiological data and withinunit environmental heterogeneity, where focal high-suitability areas may be diluted when averaged across large administrative regions. Stronger concordance for C. paraensis aligns with its recognized epidemiological role. Given the broad environmental suitability predicted for multiple potential epidemiological interest Culicoides species, including C. paraensis, the geographic extent of ecological permissiveness may exceed the currently documented distribution of human cases. OROV infection presents nonspecific febrile symptoms overlapping with dengue and other arboviral illnesses, increasing the likelihood of underdiagnosis and misclassification in passive surveillance systems [5]. Moreover, discrepancies were observed between ecological suitability and reported incidence [68]. Furthermore, reported case occurrence is influenced by surveillance intensity, diagnostic capacity, population density, human mobility, healthcare access, and reporting practices, none of which were explicitly considered in our spatial comparisons; therefore, ecological concordance should not be interpreted as direct evidence of epidemiological relevance or vector involvement.

Taken together, the taxonomic, trophic, virological, and ecological findings support further investigation of Culicoides species other than C. paraensis in the context of OROV circulation in Colombia. Among the species examined, C. insignis warrants particular attention because of its local abundance, repeated molecular detections of OROV, including in pools of females without detectable blood meals, and evidence of feeding on humans and domestic animals. OROV-derived sequences were also recovered from C. pusillus and C. ignacioi, although their limited sample sizes and less extensive host-feeding data restrict further interpretation of their epidemiological relevance. These findings do not establish additional OROV vectors or demonstrate a multi-vector transmission system. Rather, they identify species for which experimental studies of viral infection, dissemination, and transmission are needed to clarify their potential roles in OROV ecology.

In conclusion, integrating taxonomy, ecology, host-feeding patterns, and virological evidence provides a practical approach to identifying and prioritizing Culicoides species for further investigation in understudied regions. By generating convergent evidence within the same surveillance framework, this approach moves beyond isolated viral detections and single-species assumptions and enables candidate vectors to be progressively prioritized according to multiple complementary criteria. Although these findings do not establish additional OROV vectors, they illustrate how integrative entomovirological surveillance can simultaneously detect emerging viruses, characterize their ecological context, and generate the evidence needed to direct vector-competence studies and subsequent vector-incrimination efforts. 90 91 Figure S3.
