Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: Seasonal influenza is a common cause of severe illness, particularly in young children and older adults. In Australia, influenza vaccination programs using the inactivated influenza vaccine (IIV) largely focus on direct protection of older adults. Live-attenuated influenza vaccine (LAIV) is now available for children aged 2-17 years, offering an opportunity to complement existing IIV programs. Methods: We developed a mathematical model of seasonal influenza transmission and vaccination in Australia, calibrated to surveillance data. We evaluated the health impact of LAIV programs on influenza burden relative to a baseline IIV-only scenario that assumed current coverage levels. We compared the dose efficiency of LAIV programs targeting school-aged children with strategies that increased IIV uptake in adults.

Findings: An LAIV program targeting children aged 5-17 years was estimated to have the largest population-level impact, reducing influenza hospitalisations by 16% and deaths by 13%, assuming 55% coverage. An IIV program achieving 55% coverage in adults aged 50 years and older was predicted to have a similar population-level reduction in hospitalisations to a primary school-aged LAIV program (around 9.5%). Based only on doses administered, LAIV programs were more efficient at averting cases, hospitalisations, and deaths than strategies that expanded IIV uptake in adults. However, for outcomes in adults ≥65 years old, increasing IIV uptake in this age group was estimated to be the most efficient strategy, with a number needed to vaccinate of 1811 to avert one hospitalisation. Interpretation: Our results support the potential use of LAIV as an additional tool in the prevention of seasonal influenza in Australia.

Introduction

Seasonal influenza is a common acute respiratory infection that causes 3 to 5 million annual cases of severe illness globally [1]. In Australia, while influenza burden varies by year, seasonal influenza can result in an estimated 18,000 hospitalisations and 3,500 influenza-associated deaths annually [2], with annual observed deaths recorded as due to influenza ranging from approximately 400 to 1300 in recent years [3, 4]. Children aged under 5 years and adults over 65 years are among those at higher risk of severe influenza-associated illness [2].

Despite recommendations for influenza vaccination for people over 6 months of age [5], coverage in Australia with the inactivated influenza vaccine (IIV) remains suboptimal at around 30% in recent years [6], with the lowest level of age-specific coverage in children aged 6-17 years, at around 12% [7]. In 2026 an intranasally administered live-attenuated influenza vaccine (LAIV) became available in Australia for children aged 2-17 years [8]. This needle-free vaccine option serves as an alternative to the traditional injectable IIV and may provide a means to increasing influenza vaccine coverage in children.

Children are known to play a key role in influenza transmission, particularly whilst at school [9, 10, 11], meaning that increasing vaccination in this age group is expected to yield both direct and broader populationlevel benefits. While newly available in Australia, LAIV has been used in Northern Hemisphere countries for over two decades. In the United Kingdom (UK), for example, school-based LAIV programs have been introduced in a phased approach since 2013. In the 2014-15 influenza season (the second season in which school-based LAIV programs were piloted), general practitioner consultations, hospital admissions, and respiratory swab positivity were consistently lower in pilot areas compared to non-pilot areas [12]. Vaccine uptake exceeded 50% in eligible children in 2015/16 [13, 14].

In this study we use a mathematical model to estimate the potential age-specific and population-level impact of a school-aged LAIV program in Australian children, relative to the existing IIV-only program, and assuming rollout timing and uptake that could potentially be achieved via school delivery. We compare the estimated health impacts of LAIV programs with that of expanded IIV programs that target adults under a range of different realistic age-targeting and coverage assumptions.

This work was conducted as part of a multi-model exercise conducted by the Australia-Aotearoa Scenario Modelling Hub.

Methods

Influenza transmission model

We developed an age-structured, deterministic compartmental model of seasonal influenza transmission and vaccination (Figure 1A). In the model, the population is divided into nine age groups: 0-<0.5, 0.5-<1, 1-4, 5-11, 12-17, 18-49, 50-64, 65-79, and 80+ years. For each age group, individuals are stratified by infection status: susceptible, exposed but not yet infectious, infectious, and recovered. Infection status is then further subdivided by vaccination status: unvaccinated without prior immunity, unvaccinated with prior immunity, and vaccinated.

Seasonality in transmission as demonstrated by strong winter influenza epidemics is captured using a sinusoidal forcing function. We assumed no waning of infection-or vaccine-induced immunity over the modelled one-year time horizon, consistent with studies that found no evidence of waning of vaccine-induced protection over one season [15, 16]. We assumed that prior immunity conferred partial protection against infection and that this partial protection increased linearly over the first 18 years of life (from 0% at first to 21% at age 18 and uniform thereafter [17]).

Setting

Yearly population sizes for the model calibration were obtained from the Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS) [18]. We used an age distribution informed by Australian demographic data [18] and UK POLYMOD contact mixing patterns [19, 20].

To account for uncertainty in population-level prior immunity, we systematically varied the initial proportion of partially susceptible individuals due to prior immunity (0.05-0.75) and unvaccinated infectious individuals (10 -8 -10 -4 ), generating 40 distinct initial populations. The remaining population was susceptible without prior immunity, and we assumed that no individuals were vaccinated with the season's IIV or LAIV at the start of the simulation. For each of the 40 initial populations, free parameters were re-calibrated to influenza notification data as described below.

In each re-calibrated set of simulations, we explored the effects of vaccination scenarios in a fixed population of one million.

Influenza surveillance data and model calibration

We used Australian surveillance data to calibrate our model and produce a typical annual seasonal influenza epidemic. Briefly, we obtained laboratory-confirmed influenza cases from 2015 to 2025 for all states and territories from the National Notifiable Diseases Surveillance System (NNDSS) and grouped these data to the country level [21]. We combined these two data types (excluding years 2020 and 2021) to produce an agespecific influenza case rate. FluTracking is a participatory surveillance system that monitors self-reported symptoms and testing behaviour for respiratory infections, including for influenza. We used data on testing behaviour from these FluTracking surveys (Figure S1) to estimate the temporal trends in the probability that a person with symptoms would seek a test.

We calibrated modelled cases to weekly age-specific case notification data in Australia [21]. To do this, we calculated cases as infections scaled by the probability that that an infection would be symptomatic (81% [22]) and the age-specific probability that a person would seek a test (estimated from FluTracking [23]). We additionally introduced two scaling factors to health-seeking behaviour in children <18 years old and adults ≥65 years old to account for biases in the FluTracking sample. Noting that COVID test-seeking behaviour in the FluTracking data has been found to be higher than nationally representative cross-sectional survey [24], and to account for potential age-specific differences in test-seeking behaviour, we introduced two scaling factors to adjust health-seeking behaviour in children <18 years old and adults ≥65 years old. We fitted the model using a maximum likelihood approach, assuming weekly age-specific influenza cases were Poisson-distributed. We allowed key model parameters to vary, namely, the basic reproduction number R 0 , amplitude and phase shift of seasonal forcing, and scaling factors to health-seeking behaviour. We repeated this fitting procedure for all 40 sets of initial populations, resulting in 40 fitted parameter sets each with a distinct initial level of population prior immunity.

To estimate the risk of influenza hospitalisations, we obtained principal diagnosis data for 2018-19, 2022-23, and 2023-24 from the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare (AIHW) National Hospital Morbidity Database (Figure S2A) [25]. Age-specific influenza deaths were estimated using data on deaths due to acute respiratory infections in Australia in 2023, 2024, and 2025 from the ABS (Figure S2B) [4]. We computed the number of hospitalisations and deaths from each infection trajectory using these age-specific risks of influenza hospitalisation and death respectively.

A full description of the data, model calibration, and methods is provided in the Supplementary Material.

Vaccination strategies

We considered vaccination strategies that increased IIV coverage or introduced LAIV programs in different target age groups, compared to an IIV-only baseline in which coverage was assumed to be 55% for adults ≥65 years old, 20% for adults 50-<65 years old, and 10% for children 5-<18 years old (Table 1): • IIV 65: IIV coverage increased to 80% in adults ≥65 years old; • IIV 50: IIV coverage increased to 55% in adults 50-<65 years old; • LAIV 5-12: LAIV introduced to children 5-<12 years old with 55% coverage; • LAIV 5-18: LAIV introduced to children 5-<18 years old with 55% coverage.

For each scenario, non-target age groups received the standard IIV coverage rates at baseline. As a sensitivity analysis, we modelled IIV 50, LAIV 5-12, and LAIV 5-18 programs with coverage of the target ages reaching 30% or 80%. Note that in the IIV 50 high-coverage (80%) setting, adults ≥50 years old would reach 80%, equivalent to IIV 65 plus IIV 50, while in the IIV 50 low-coverage setting, adults aged 50-64 years would reach 30% and adults ≥65 years old would maintain the baseline coverage of 55%.

The baseline IIV coverage rates for different age groups were informed by the national influenza vaccination coverage data in Australia [7] (Table 1). For the IIV, we assumed 55% effectiveness against symptomatic disease in Australia [6]. We calculated 44% effectiveness against acquiring infection by assuming that the effectiveness against symptomatic disease in an infected person is 20% [26]. We set effectiveness against hospitalisation and death equal to that against symptomatic disease, as in Kelly et al. [27]. For the LAIV programs, we set an LAIV coverage of 55% in the target age group, consistent with data from the rollout of school-based programs in the UK [13, 14], and assumed a 65% vaccine effectiveness against symptomatic disease [28]. Using the same methods as for the IIV, we calculated a 56% effectiveness against acquiring infection and set a 65% effectiveness against hospitalisation for the LAIV. We used a 'leaky' vaccine that provided partial protection to all vaccinated individuals [29].

Rollout of both vaccines began in Week 10 of the year (start of March) (Figure 1B). The IIV was administered over 24 weeks, until Week 34 (end of August), based on data from the National Centre for Immunisation Research and Surveillance [7]. Assuming that the LAIV program is school-based and would be delivered before the peak influenza season, we simulated a 12-week rollout that lasted until Week 22 (end of May), within Terms 1 and 2 of the Australian school calendar [30, 31] (see Supplementary Material C).

Model outcomes

For each scenario (and set of initial conditions and parameter values), we calculated the age-specific cumulative infections, cases of disease, hospitalisations, and deaths due to influenza, over one year from January to December. We calculated the number needed to vaccinate (NNV), a metric that represents the number of individuals that must be vaccinated in order to prevent one outcome (case, hospitalisation, and death due to influenza); it was calculated as the ratio of 1) the total additional doses required by each strategy compared to baseline and 2) the number of averted outcomes in all ages and in adults ≥65 years old. We report the mean and range over 40 parameter sets.

Results

Model calibration

The fitted model broadly captured the seasonal and yearly variation in notified influenza cases: cases peaked during the middle of the year and increased annually from 2015 to 2025 (Figure S3). Baseline case trajectories were consistent with the confirmed influenza case data; calibration is illustrated in Figure 2A using case data for 2024, aggregated across ages groups and jurisdictions. We estimated a mean R 0 of 1.28 (range 1.13-1.45) across the 40 sets of initial parameters, corresponding to a population-level mean infection attack rate of 12.2% (range 11.7-13.5%) at baseline. We found a strong correlation between the fitted parameters R 0 , the initial proportion with prior immunity, and the initial infection prevalence, indicating a lack of parameter value identifiability (Figure S5). Modelled cumulative hospitalisations and deaths at baseline aligned with observed influenza-attributable hospitalisations and influenza deaths (Figure 2B and C). For the full model calibration of influenza cases by year and age group, see Figures S3 and S4.

Modelled baseline scenario

At baseline, we modelled a mean of approximately 122,000 (range 116,581-134,563) total infections per million population annually, resulting in approximately 15,000 (range 14,220-15,694) cases, 740 (range 737-742) hospitalisations, and 31.2 deaths (range 31.0-31.4) annually. These estimates correspond to approximately 14% of infections becoming cases, a 5% case-hospitalisation ratio, and a 0.2% case-fatality ratio (Table S2).

Estimated impact on influenza hospitalisations and deaths

Of the modelled scenarios, an LAIV program targeting children aged 5-<18 years (LAIV 5-18) was estimated to have the largest reductions in hospitalisations and deaths at the population level, averting 16% (range 14-17%) of hospitalisations and 13% (range 10-14%) of deaths (Figure 3B, C). In contrast, an IIV program targeting adults aged 50-64 years with 55% coverage (IIV 50) and an LAIV program targeting primary schoolaged children (LAIV 5-12) each reduced hospitalisations by approximately 10%. Increasing coverage in adults ≥65 years old (IIV 65) was estimated to achieve the smallest population-level reductions in hospitalisations at 4% (range 3.8-4.9%). For deaths at a population level, programs that target adults were predicted to lead to higher reductions than the LAIV 5-12 program; the IIV 50 program reduced deaths by 9.8% (range 7.1-12.8%), compared to 6.8% (range 5.2-7.6%) for LAIV 5-12 (Figure 3C).

When the outcomes were restricted to events averted in adults ≥65 years old, we found that programs targeting adults had a greater impact than LAIV 5-12: IIV 65 and IIV 50 each reduced hospitalisations by approximately 9%, compared to 6.6% for LAIV 5-12 (Figure 3B). An LAIV program targeting children aged 5-<18 years was still predicted to have the largest impact, reducing hospitalisations and deaths by 12.5% (range 10.5-13.4%) and 12.6% (range 9.9-13.8%) respectively (Figure 3B and C). Figure S3 shows the model fitted to the age-specific case data for 2015-2025 (excluding 2020 and 2021), and Figure S4 shows the model fit with all ages aggregated. More details on the surveillance data are provided in Figure S2 and the Supplementary Material. The health benefits in target and non-target age groups differed for each vaccination program, reflecting their direct and indirect impacts. Programs targeting older adults provided more direct protection, whereas those targeting children provided more indirect protection. For example, when coverage in adults ≥65 years old increased to 80% (IIV 65), 96% of averted deaths were in the target age group (Table S3). In contrast, only 3% of the deaths averted were in the target age group in an LAIV program targeting children aged 5-<18 years, showing that most deaths were averted through indirect protection. A similar pattern was observed for averted hospitalisations, with larger direct impact when targeting adults ≥65 years old and greater indirect impact from targeting children (Table S3).

Estimated impact on influenza cases

At the population level, we estimated that LAIV programs would have the largest impact on cases, with LAIV 5-18 averting cases by 20.7% (range 18.9-21.7%), while LAIV 5-12 averted 11.3% (range 10.2-11.6%) (Figure 3A). In comparison, we estimated that increasing IIV coverage from 55% to 80% in adults ≥65 years old (IIV 65) would have the smallest population-level impact on cases, reducing by 2.7% (range 2.3-3.1%).

Efficiency of the modelled influenza vaccine strategies

We calculated the number needed to vaccinate (NNV) to measure the efficiency of the modelled scenarios. Across all ages, the school-aged LAIV programs had lower NNVs than expanded IIV programs that targeted adults for preventing cases or hospitalisations, whereas a program targeting adults ≥65 years old (IIV 65) had the lowest NNV for preventing deaths (Table 2). For example, the NNV to prevent one hospitalisation in all ages was 527 for an LAIV program targeting primary school-aged children (LAIV 5-12), compared to 1358 for IIV 65 (Table 2). The NNVs were similar between the two LAIV programs across all outcomes, with LAIV 5-12 slightly more efficient than LAIV 5-18. For example, 527 vaccine doses were required to prevent one hospitalisation for LAIV 5-12, compared to 588 doses for LAIV 5-18, a difference of 61 doses (per million population).

When the NNV was restricted to adults ≥65 years old, increasing coverage in this age group (IIV 65) directly was the most efficient strategy, with NNVs of 1811 to prevent one hospitalisation and 18264 to prevent one death (Table 2). Both school-aged LAIV programs had lower NNVs (and hence were more efficient) than a program that increased coverage in adults aged 50-64 years.

Discussion

In this modelling study we estimated that an LAIV program targeting primary and secondary school students and achieving 55% coverage would have larger population-level benefits, reducing more influenza cases, hospitalisations, and deaths, compared to strategies that expanded IIV uptake in adults. School-aged LAIV programs were also estimated to achieve the greatest reductions in these outcomes among adults ≥65 years old, highlighting the substantial indirect protection provided by vaccinating school-aged children. When accounting for the number of vaccine doses administered, we estimated that at the population level, LAIV programs would generally be more efficient than strategies that increased IIV uptake in older adults. However, if we focused on measuring prevented outcomes in adults ≥65 years old, increasing IIV coverage to 80% in this age group was predicted to be the most efficient strategy.

While modelled health impact and efficiency are important considerations for influenza vaccination program policy, programmatic feasibility is also relevant. Australia has an established infrastructure that delivers vaccines through general practices, pharmacies, and residential aged care facilities, as well as school-based programs for diseases such as human papillomavirus [32]. However, uptake of influenza vaccination has declined across all age groups since the COVID-19 pandemic [33]. Hence, achieving 55% coverage (as assumed in our scenarios) would represent a substantial increase from the current uptake in Australian school-aged children. However, coverage exceeding 50% has been achieved through school-based programs that delivered intranasal LAIV in the UK and other European countries [13, 14], suggesting that this level of uptake may be feasible with school-based delivery. The barriers to increasing uptake are multiple, and include practical considerations (ease of access and cost), as well as behavioural and social factors such as perceived autonomy in vaccine decision-making, trust in health workers, and community leader influence [34]. Any modifications to existing influenza vaccine programs would need to consider acceptability and achievable uptake for differing population groups.

Compared to the IIV-only baseline scenario (with 24% coverage across the population), we found that an LAIV program achieving 55% coverage in children aged 5-17 years would reduce influenza cases by 21%, hospitalisations by 16%, and deaths by 13% across the population. Our findings are broadly consistent with those of de Boer et al. [35], who modelled LAIV vaccination in children aged 2-16 years in the Netherlands. Assuming 50% coverage, they estimated an average annual population-level reduction of 18% in cases, 7% in hospitalisations, and 10% in deaths over 20 years, relative to a baseline vaccination program with approximately 21% coverage across the population. However, our modelled impact was smaller than in Wenzel et al. [36], who estimated that vaccinating 5-16-year-olds at 55% coverage would reduce cases, hospitalisations, and deaths by a mean of approximately 50% annually relative to their baseline scenario in England and Wales over 19 influenza seasons. One factor in their higher estimated reductions may be differences in vaccine efficacy against infection, which ranged from 28% to 70% in their model and varied by the degree of matching between vaccine and circulating strain and by age and risk group.

We estimated that an LAIV program achieving 55% coverage in children aged 5-<18 years would avert around twice as many cases as an IIV program achieving the same coverage in adults aged 50-64 years. Baguelin et al. [37] found a similar difference relative to their baseline scenario without vaccination: targeting adults aged 50-64 years reduced cases by around 28%, approximately half the 55% reduction achieved by targeting children aged 5-16 years (where impact was reported as the average events for each outcome across a range of coverage scenarios from 15-70%). Similar patterns were observed for severe outcomes. In our study, targeting adults aged 50-64 years averted 44% fewer hospitalisations and 23% fewer deaths than targeting children aged 5-<18 years. Baguelin et al. reported comparable relative differences, where vaccinating adults aged 50-64 years averted 39% fewer hospitalisations and 26% fewer deaths than vaccinating children aged 5-16 years [37].

We estimated that an LAIV program targeting children aged 5-17 years would provide substantial indirect protection, with around 97% of averted deaths and 66% of averted hospitalisations occurring in the nontarget age groups. Other modelling studies reported similarly high indirect benefits from vaccinating children against influenza [38, 39, 35, 40, 41]. For example, de Boer et al. [35] estimated that, for a program targeting children aged 2-16 years, on average, around 99% of averted deaths and 52% of averted hospitalisations occurred within the non-target age group, indicating a large proportion of severe outcomes were prevented due to indirect protection from vaccinating children. In contrast, we found that programs targeting older adults primarily provided direct protection to the target age group, with relatively little benefit outside the target age group. Together, these findings highlight the potential for LAIV programs that target school-aged children, which could be implemented through school-based delivery, to reduce severe disease in older adults (those at higher risk of severe influenza) through indirect protection.

In our study we estimated that an LAIV program in children would be more efficient (measured as vaccine doses administered) in terms of averted cases, hospitalisations and deaths compared to programs that increase coverage in adults aged 50-64 years. This finding is consistent with a study that modelled influenza transmission over 14 seasons in England and Wales [39]. Baguelin et al. compared strategies that either increased coverage to 50% in children aged 5-16 years or in adults aged 50-64 years and estimated greater reductions in cases and deaths, and greater dose efficiency, when increased coverage was targeted to children.

Our study has several limitations. First, we assumed that LAIV was delivered over a 12-week period before the epidemic peak. While a slower modelled rollout may have reduced the estimated impact, our assumption may be conservative, as school-based influenza vaccination programs in the UK occurred over 6-8 weeks [42]. We also assumed that the relative speed of IIV uptake was the same for all age groups. In reality, adults ≥65 years old may be vaccinated earlier than other age groups [7]. Second, we assumed uniform vaccine efficacy across all age groups, and did not explicitly account for the use of different vaccines in adults ≥65 years old. However, given the use of adjuvanted and high-dose influenza vaccines to address lower efficacy from standard vaccines in that age group [43], our efficacy assumption is likely to be reasonable.

Third, there was a paucity of high-quality population-based surveillance data to inform our assumptions around underlying influenza burden, particularly for non-symptomatic and symptomatic infections. Using a combination of data sources we inferred a mean annual attack rate of 12.2%, but noted considerable heterogeneity in age-specific influenza attack rates in the literature [44]. In addition, while we use Australian data to estimate severe influenza morbidity and death burden, these data are still subject to both ascertainment and under-reporting biases. Consequently, the absolute number of events averted by each vaccination strategy should be interpreted in the context of this uncertainty. Fourth, our model used age-specific contact patterns from POLYMOD [19]. Social mixing patterns may have changed since this study was performed, particularly following the COVID-19 pandemic. The recent Reconnect survey, conducted in the UK between November 2024 and March 2025, reported 20% fewer contacts on average than POLYMOD, with higher contact rates in children under 5 years old but lower contact rates in those above 5 years old [45]. Changes in these mixing patterns could influence transmission and the magnitude of indirect protection provided by childhood vaccination.

With the recent introduction of LAIV products in Australia, there is a need for evidence to inform decisions regarding program design, target age groups, and implementation. However, few mathematical modelling studies have quantified the impact of LAIV programs, and most are focused on European settings [39, 35, 36]. In this study, we compared the health impacts and efficiency of school-aged LAIV programs with strategies that increased IIV coverage in adults in Australia. C Appendix Data

Influenza surveillance

Influenza is a notifiable disease in Australia. Notified influenza cases, disaggregated by age-group (a), notification week (w) and year (y), were obtained from the National Notifiable Disease Surveillance System (NNDSS) [21] from 2015 to 2025 (denoted D a,w,y ) for all states in Australia. The NNDSS data only includes cases with laboratory confirmation. Age group was stratified by under 1, 1-4, 5-11, 12-17, 18-64, 65-79, and 80 years or older. We excluded data from years 2020 and 2021 due to the implementation of nonpharmaceutical interventions during the COVID pandemic and limited influenza transmission.

Test-seeking behaviour

To estimate the conditional probability that people take a test given symptomatic infection (p d ) we used healthcare-seeking behaviour data from the FluTracking surveys; specifically, we calculated the proportion of reports of influenza symptoms where an influenza test was also sought, for each age group and for each year. We did not make any adjustment for the type of test (whether rapid antigen or polymerase chain reaction). FluTracking is a participatory influenza surveillance system in Australia and other participating regions, which includes regular surveys of volunteers with questions about symptoms and testing behaviour. FluTracking data for years 2015 to 2025 were analysed, and data from 2020 and 2021 were again excluded. The FluTracking surveys were available in age groups 0-4, 5-17, 18-64, and 65 years or older.

Vaccination

Influenza vaccination coverage data were obtained from the National Centre for Immunisation Research and Surveillance (NCIRS) from 2021 to 2024 [7]. Data on weekly cumulative vaccination coverage were available from the beginning of March to the end of August in each year, spanning notification weeks 10 to 34. Data were disaggregated into 5 age groups (6 months to 4 years; 5 to 14 years; 15 to 49 years; 50 to 64 years; and 65 years or older).

Influenza hospitalisations

We obtained principal diagnosis data for the years 2018-19, 2022-23, and 2023-24 from the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare National Hospital Morbidity Database [25]. The data contains separation counts from public and private Australian hospitals for all states and territories. Counts were disaggregated different proportions of susceptible and unvaccinated population. Results are presented over a one-year time horizon from January to December, where a single LAIV campaign is implemented before the winter season. In all simulations we ran the model over a single seasonal epidemic and calculated the weekly number of cases with the status quo vaccination schedule. We calibrated the model by allowing the (1) R 0 , (2) amplitude of seasonal forcing (A), (3) phase shift of seasonal forcing (ϕ), and (4) scaling factors (τ child and τ adult ) to adjust the proportion of infections that seek a test (p f t ) to vary. Infections were seeded at the beginning of the year. We assumed that cases are Poisson distributed, which assumes the variance is equal to the mean, and calculated the probability mass function thus, p(D a,w,y |µ) = e -µ µ Da,w,y D a,w,y !.

The log-likelihood of observing the NNDSS data was calculated thus, log(L(R 0 , A, ϕ|D)) = a w y log(p(D a,w,y |max(cases a,w,y , 10 -6 ))).

Note that we specified a weekly minimum number of infections of 10 -6 . The sensitivity of the model calibration to different numbers of people with prior immunity and infection prevalence at the beginning of the year was investigated by calibrating the model with the initial proportion of those with prior immunity (S P ) ranging from 5% to 75% distributed proportionally by age, which was determined by the approximate range of the proportion of people with Hemagglutination-Inhibition titre greater than 40 observed for H3N2 influenza in Australia [50]. People in the S P compartment had reduced susceptibility to infection (given by the r parameter; Table S1) relative to those without prior immunity. The initial infection prevalence ranged from 1E-8 to 1E-4.

Model outcomes of interest

To calculate the age-specific incidence, inc, we tracked the vaccination and age-specific total number of recovered infections (T ): dT X,a dt = γI X,a , where X ∈ U, V, P . We calculated the case incidence at each time step as, inc t,X,a = T X,a (t + dt) -T X,a (t).

The weekly incidence was calculated as, inc w,y,a = t=7(w+1) t=7w (inc t,U,a + inc t,V,a + inc t,P,a )N y , where N y is the population size of Australia for the given year for the model calibration or 1 million for the scenario modelling.

Model description

We developed an age-structured, deterministic compartmental model of seasonal influenza transmission and vaccination. In the model, the population is divided into 9 age groups: 0-<0.5, 0.5-<1, 1-4, 5-11, 12-17, 18-49, 50-64, 65-79, and 80+ years. The population is divided by infection status (susceptible, exposed, infectious, and recovered, represented by state variables S, E, I, R respectively) and vaccination status (unvaccinated without prior immunity, unvaccinated with prior immunity, and vaccinated, denoted by subscripts U, P, V respectively). Susceptible individuals become exposed but not yet infectious. After infection, they recover and become fully immune. Those who are vaccinated or have prior immunity gain a reduced susceptibility to acquiring infection by a proportional factor (1 -VE IN F ) and (1 -r) respectively. We use a gamma-distributed latent period, leading to a two-step transition from E to I [49]. Those who are vaccinated or unvaccinated with prior immunity have a reduced susceptibility to infection by a proportional factor (1 -V E IN F ) and (1 -r) respectively. Vaccine rollout

The rollout of both vaccines began in Week 10, with the IIV rollout taking place over 24 weeks and the LAIV rollout, over 12 weeks. We assumed a constant two-week delay from vaccine delivery to protection for both vaccine types. We vaccinated only susceptible or recovered individuals. We used a 'leaky' vaccine that provided partial protection against infection and hospitalisation to all vaccinated individuals [29]. We assumed that the vaccine had no effect on infectiousness in those who experienced breakthrough infection. The weekly rollout rate of each vaccine was determined by the overall vaccination coverage of the age group (Table 1) and the overall speed of delivery of that vaccine. The IIV delivery speed varied weekly and was determined using cumulative influenza vaccination coverage data in two steps. First, we aggregated the data by age group for each year. Second, we normalised the weekly rate by the cumulative total of that year and set the mean per week as the overall speed of IIV delivery. The speed of LAIV delivery was assumed to be uniform over the LAIV rollout.

Model calibration

We used FluTracking data [23] to inform the assumed age-specific fractions of infections that seek medical care. We modelled the proportion of people with symptoms who sought care prior to 2020 using a logistic regression model (p f t ) and the mean values for all years post 2021. The modelled increase in healthcareseeking behaviour from 2022 onwards captures differences in test-seeking and structural changes in question design following the pandemic. The age group (categorical) and year (continuous) were included as covariates in the logistic regression. A comparison of people seeking care for COVID-19 in the FluTracking data and a weekly, national behavioural survey established by the Australian government found test-seeking behaviour among those with symptoms was higher in the FluTracking participants (84.9%) compared to those in the national behavioural survey (58.8%) [24], therefore we scaled the estimated proportion of people seeking care in the FluTracking data by 58.8/84.9 (Figure S1). p d in children and adults ≥65 years old was informed during the model calibration by scaling the p f t,a values thus, otherwise.
