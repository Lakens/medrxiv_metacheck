About the personal-information flags. These are pattern matches, not confirmed disclosures, and they are tuned to over-report rather than miss something. Expect false positives:

  • Column names are matched against identifying words in English and other European languages (email, telefoonnummer, geboortedatum, …). A column called phone_number flags whether or not it holds one, and an empty or already-anonymised column flags on its name alone.
  • Values are matched against the shape of an identifier. An IP address pattern also matches a version string or a dotted numeric code; a card-number pattern requires both a real issuer prefix and a checksum, but a long numeric ID can still coincide.
  • Free text is flagged when a column holds long, mostly-distinct prose — the shape open-ended answers take. Most such columns contain no names at all.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Efficient and secure integration of epidemiological data with pathogen genome sequence data is essential for identifying genetic clusters, monitoring emerging mutations and targeting public health responses. However, this information is often collected across different organisations: epidemiological data is collected by public health units while genome sequence data is collected by diagnostic laboratories. Linking these sources often requires manual or semi-manual approaches, leading to unnecessary delays in identifying emerging outbreaks. To address this, we developed a proof-of-concept privacy-preserving distributed platform, SecureEpiLink, for automatic linkage of pathogen genome sequences and notification data across public health and diagnostic laboratories. SecureEpiLink uses cryptographic hashing to establish linkage, without exposing personal identifying information. This ensures that the resulting linked data can be used to identify the emergence of genetic clusters without identification of individuals comprising each cluster, with the original identifiable data remaining stored at source units. We benchmarked SecureEpiLink against manual linkage and another linkage service using HIV and HCV datasets from New South Wales, Australia.

SecureEpiLink performed similarly to manual linkage and outperformed previously used linkage algorithms, with all errors attributable to data entry errors in the underlying dataset. Lastly, we demonstrate how SecureEpiLink can be integrated with automated genomic epidemiology pipelines. SecureEpiLink is available from https://github.com/jolenefarrell/SecureEpiLink.

Introduction

Public health notification data provide critical information on the overall spread and demographic correlates of infectious disease [1]. Additionally, pathogen genome sequence data can be used to identify genetic clusters, their rate of growth, as well as the emergence of new variants of concern [2] [3] [4]. The linkage of genome sequence with public health notification data provides the most complete picture of the way in which a pathogen is spreading in the community, and which demographic and geographic groups are most at risk [5] [6] [7]. As such, centralised databases of pathogen sequence data and public health notification data such as AusTrakka [8] and GISAID [9] provide powerful resources for genomic epidemiology.

For example, numerous human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) studies have used molecular epidemiology to identify key factors that link a genetic cluster, which can be further utilised to design effective public health interventions [3]. Notably, the US and Canada have implemented molecular epidemiology as part of their disease control programmes [10, 11]. However, linking notification and genome sequence data is particularly complex for stigma-associated pathogens or where transmission is criminalised, such as with HIV [12], hepatitis-C virus (HCV) [13] and Mpox [14]. Data linkage platforms must therefore preserve the security of personal identifying data for linkage to be both successful, viable and acceptable to the at-risk community.

Recent advances in distributed computing, cryptographic protocols and privacypreserving record linkage provide a promising alternative to traditional centralised systems, which often require the transfer of sensitive and identifiable data into a single repository. Despite this, practical implementations of distributed or decentralised platforms are limited. This study provides a proof-of-concept for distributed and deidentified data linkage and sharing platform, SecureEpiLink, that enables linkage of public health notification data with pathogen genome sequence data. SecureEpiLink allows organisations to store and manage their own data while linkage is performed across the data network using cryptographic hashing instead of exposing patients' personal identifying information.

. We demonstrate use of SecureEpiLink using empirical HIV and HCV datasets from New South Wales (NSW), Australia. Pathogen sequence data for notifiable pathogens is generated by multiple public and private diagnostic labs across Australia [15].

Notification data, which contains information on the demography of the patient, including age, their postcode and ethnicity, is collected by public health units, and typically stored within the Department of Health in each state jurisdiction. There are linkage services such as Centre for Health Record Linkage (CHeReL) [16] in NSW, which link pathogen genome sequence with notification data using an established deterministic algorithm. However, linkage takes place at arbitrary intervals due to request processes and financial investment requirements, limiting the ability for realtime analysis. Here, we show that SecureEpiLink achieves comparable-or-higher linkage success to deterministic linkage run at CHeReL for empirical HIV and HCV datasets. We further demonstrate use of SecureEpiLink with a simulated dataset, showing that all linkage failures are attributable to errors and inconsistencies in underlying datasets. We also demonstrate how SecureEpiLink can be integrated with automated bioinformatics pipelines. Lastly, we discuss considerations for how distributed platforms such as SecureEpiLink can be further developed and deployed at scale.

Methods

Overview

Below, we describe SecureEpiLink's linkage procedure, as well as how we compared its performance to CHeReL and manual linkage using empirical and simulated datasets. We then demonstrate how SecureEpiLink can integrate with bioinformatic and datavisualisation pipelines. We evaluate linkage success for HIV by using records from the NSW HIV resistance database from 2004-2023 and NSW Notifiable Conditions Information Management System (NCIMS) database, and for HCV by using data from the SToP-C [17] and HITS-p [18] studies of HCV among prisoners (ethics approval 2024/ETH00857). We then use another previously published NSW HIV genomic dataset [19] with randomised metadata to demonstrate how SecureEpiLink can integrate with reporting pipelines.

Description of linkage protocol

We used a one-way cryptographic hash function to de-identify and link notification and genomic datasets. A linkage identifier was generated for each record by hashing concatenated identifiable fields and appending a randomly generated string, also known as a salt. Notification and genomic records were then matched on the generated linkage identifiers. We assumed that there was a single genomic-notification record pair, where successful linkage was defined as one genomic record matching one notification record uniquely. Failed linkage was defined as either undetermined, where a genomic or notification record matched multiple records in the corresponding dataset due to those records sharing the same linkage identifier (also referred to as a linkage collision), or unsuccessful, where one genomic or notification record matched no records in the corresponding dataset.

Distributed data linkage platform

We developed a proof-of-concept platform, SecureEpiLink, to showcase the utility of the linkage protocol. SecureEpiLink was implemented using Node.js (v24) and the Express web application framework, with development carried out in an isolated test environment, separate from any production systems, allowing for modular development, experimentation and quick prototyping. Isolated environments allow for modular development and prototyping as all the components can be built and tested without affecting real data or systems. We designed API endpoints to facilitate data linkage using standard HTTP methods, including GET and POST, and simple authentication was handled through shared API keys included in all request headers. SecureEpiLink was containerised with Docker, a process that allows all code and external dependencies to be packaged together in an isolated lightweight environment. All relevant data was stored in a MySQL database, and SecureEpiLink was configured to produce structured JSON logs. We used 347 previously published HIV genomes [19] (GenBank accession numbers MW167298 -MW167644) for which we simulated notification times and key metadata categories, including personal-identifiers, to test the platform. Distributed data linkage platform

SecureEpiLink was developed to support two types of data collecting organisations, or data nodes: pathology laboratories and public health departments/units. Laboratory data nodes are designed to represent pathology laboratories responsible for pathogen sequencing, and public health unit nodes to represent data collecting organisations storing public health notification metadata. SecureEpiLink was based on a distributed architecture model in which each data collecting organisation, either laboratory or public health notification unit, would manage its own data node. Within-jurisdiction data flow between these two node types would be bi-directional and cooperative, with data linkage completed at the laboratory node, and sequence metadata and files (fasta) being shared from the laboratory node to the public health unit node upon successful linkage.

The linkage, data and analysis flow between public health and laboratory nodes was designed in three key stages: data node initialisation, data linkage and sharing, and bioinformatic analysis and report generation (Figure 2). Peer-to-peer communication between data nodes to facilitate the data linkage and sharing component was managed via RESTful APIs, an industry standard for building public facing APIs and web services, where each node operates as an independent service. These APIs were secured using HTTPS, behind a reverse proxy. Between-node requests were authenticated using a unique key assigned to each registered node, intending only to demonstrate the approach in a prototype setting. Production deployments would require certificates from a trusted certificate authority, stronger authentication mechanisms, and secure key management. Data linkage and sharing via these APIs was designed as a data linkage transaction between a Laboratory node and a Public Health Unit node (Figure 3). A data linkage transaction is initiated from the laboratory node, where a oneway cryptographic hash function is used to de-identify the participant data and generate linkage identifiers at both node types. These linkage identifiers are generated from the combination of a record's sensitive fields, name code and date of birth, and a salt, which are then hashed or transformed into non-decodable form. During linkage only the hashed, anonymised identifiers are shared between nodes, and once linkage has occurred, only the non-sensitive fields are shared between nodes. After the completion of a linkage transaction, linked records and genomic sequence files are stored at the public health unit node. The salt can then be destroyed, ensuring privacy and adding a layer of security by preventing further linkage or re-identification. The entire linkage and data sharing process is managed within one transaction which is identifiable through a correlation identifier. The database schema was designed to support both node types, laboratory and public health unit, with each node type maintaining its own consistent data model (Supplementary Figure 2). Several common entities were shared between node types to ensure interoperability. Server-side checks were included to ensure data integrity, supported by the development of mock upload templates and data dictionaries for HIV genomic and notification records. Mock upload templates and data dictionaries allow users to prepare and format their data in the expected structure, reducing the probability of data-integrity errors reaching the database. Uploaded datasets were stored across the Records and Metadata tables, with flexible configuration of metadata, linkage and sharing fields. The Public Health Unit node data model contained two additional tables for storing the linked, anonymised records and associated metadata. Linked records in these tables were identified with new, newly generated External Identifiers, unique to SecureEpiLink.

The HIV simulation datasets, including genomic sequence files, were successfully uploaded across two mock laboratory type data nodes and one mock public health unit data node. A linkage transaction was then initiated between each laboratory data node and the public health unit node to link genomic and notification records, where 330 out of a total of 347 records (95.1%) (Table 7) across all three data nodes were successfully linked. All successfully linked records and genomic sequence files were stored at the mock public health unit node.

Report generation pipeline

SecureEpiLink was enhanced to support the execution of an analysis pipeline, generating a pre-designed HTML report summarizing linked records and metadata stored at the public health unit node. This process is initiated by a POST API request that triggers a SnakeMake pipeline, followed by a subsequent POST API request that generates the final HTML report. Separating the pipeline and report generation steps allows for greater future implementation flexibility, particularly in scenarios where reports need to be regenerated without repeating expensive bioinformatic computations, or varying report versions need to be generated for different stakeholders based on the same bioinformatic outputs. Example visualisations generated from the pipeline and reporting component included a hypothetical molecular cluster network, network statistic summaries and proportional distribution plots of metadata variables over 6-month time intervals (Figure 4). Although the linkage, and the analysis and reporting components of the system were both logically integrated, sharing the same node-specific MySQL database, both components were segregated by running of each part of the system in self-contained environments (Supplementary Figure 3). This supports inter-component data consistency, while maintaining modularity and flexibility for future development and scaling.

The proof-of-concept linkage platform, with analysis and reporting components, is provided in a minimal, open-source format to allow for replication of the linkage, pipeline and reporting outputs. Discussion

The showcased privacy-preserving linkage protocol, which enables record matching without the need for sharing of sensitive and identifiable fields, was successfully implemented within a proof-of-concept distributed data linkage and sharing platform.

We demonstrated feasibility of data linkage and sharing across independently maintained datasets, in simulated conditions. This emulates the scenario where each data collection organisation manages its own data autonomously, while SecureEpiLink directly integrates linkage functionality to support timelier, near real-time data integration. Such an approach could enable faster detection of genetic clusters and more responsive public health action compared to current retrospective and centralised linkage workflows.

While distributed platforms allow for effective data storage, linkage and sharing capabilities, there is significant added complexity in development, implementation and maintenance of an operationalised distributed platform. Mechanisms need to be introduced, managed and maintained to standardise storage, queries and results across possibly heterogeneous data sources with varying policy and governance requirements.

Similarly, mechanisms for code versioning, deployment of new features and critical updates, and conflict resolution are required to ensure synchronisation and consistency across all nodes within the distributed network. Secure communication protocols also need to be maintained not only within data nodes, like in a centralised system, but also between distributed nodes. The key considerations, impacts and approaches are summarised in Table 8. Despite these operational and technical challenges, our proofof-concept implementation demonstrates that distributed linkage using hashed anonymised identifiers, specifically name code and date of birth, can achieve success rates comparable to established protocols.

Platform operationalisation and maintenance

Operating and maintaining a distributed system requires consistent deployments, updates and monitoring across many nodes without the use of a central authority.

Build dependable automation pipelines, strong version control protocols, and ensure flexibility to scale is at the forefront of design.

Security and authentication

As a distributed network grows, so does the importance of security, as each additional node increases the number of potential points of compromise.

Use techniques such as certificated-based authentication methods, central node registries, rolebased access control, encrypted communication, and detailed system logging to strengthen the platform.

The ability to perform de-identified linkage is particularly relevant for pathogens such as 448 HIV, which present significant ethical and legal considerations associated with stigma 449 and criminalisation [13, 14]. These factors require strict separation of identifiable 450 information from analytical datasets and highlight the importance of privacy-preserving methods such as the one demonstrated here.

While this approach offers clear advantages for data-security, the effectiveness of the method was found to be highly dependent on the uniqueness of the linkage field combinations, reflecting the inherently deterministic nature of hashing. In practice, our evaluation showed higher success rates on small datasets, and highlighted significant constraints with larger datasets. With the number of historical cases of newly acquired HIV reported over the last 10 years in NSW being less than 400 per year [20], the occurrence of duplicate linkage keys, and therefore failed linkages, would be negligible.

As the testing datasets scaled to 12 million records, approximately 8% of records had duplicate identifiers (Table 3), highlighting possible limitations in scalability to national or international implementations. This observation is consistent with the birthday problem, which describes the quadratic growth of the absolute count of accidental collisions, in this case different records sharing the same name code and date of birth, as the dataset grows by a factor of 10. This limitation in using simply name code and date of birth for linking records reliably in large datasets could be mitigated by the inclusion of more linkage fields, or more complete information in the fields.

A further constraint of hash-based deterministic linkage is its susceptibility to minor data entry errors and character-level differences, such as accents, apostrophes and hyphens within name fields. Fields also vary in how prone they are to mismatch. Postcodes are particularly prone to mismatch for two reasons: they are time-varying variables that may change between the date of testing and the date of notification, or they can often be unreliable with postcodes of medical clinics being recorded rather than an individual's residential postcodes. Prison, or incarceration facility, names are also prone to mismatch. They are often recorded as free text fields, with significant variation in spelling and formatting, and are at risk of changing over time due to nomenclature shifts, facility mergers and administrative rebranding. Identifiers used in linkage should therefore be uniform, with rigorous data collection and standardisation practices and data checking implemented at the point of data entry.

A key limitation of this study is that the linkage of HIV notification records was done without our direct access to the NCIMS database, and CHeReL's linkage process is closed source, resulting in an inability to access the missing value counts or note the characteristics of unlinked records. Therefore, the extent of the effect of missing values on HIV linkage success, and understanding the factors driving failed linkages, is unclear.

In the linkage of HCV data, missing and inconsistent linkage fields in records were a common cause of unlinked records, and while this is likely a cause of unlinked records for the HIV data, it cannot be determined definitively.

While optimising linkage outcomes was not the primary aim of this study, linkage success remains a critical factor of the comprehensiveness and quality of the final linked datasets, which in turn directly influences downstream genomic and epidemiological analyses to guide public health interventions. The adoption of data guidelines will be essential to enable a robust and reproducible implementation of this linkage protocol and distributed data linkage and sharing platform. This includes community-agreed metadata standards, controlled vocabularies, and shared ontologies which support interoperability and reusability while still allowing for sensitive data to be tightly accesscontrolled.

Addressing the key constraints in relation to both the linkage protocol and platform operationalisation will be essential to enabling successful multi-jurisdictional implementation of a distributed linkage, analysis and reporting platform such as SecureEpiLink. Future work should focus on several key areas including the exploration of alternate linkage approaches that address scalability limitations, aligning with policy and public health specialists, and operationalising SecureEpiLink with robust orchestration tools.

Furthermore, SecureEpiLink should be extended to incorporate critical features and functionality that ensure usability, such as administrative interfaces for managing key aspects such as pathogen record data dictionaries and data upload templates.

Capturing these requirements ensures that the system can be reliably used, maintained, and governed in real-world settings, while supporting reproducibility and accountability across analyses.

Conclusion

Distributed, privacy-preserving data sharing and linkage showcases a promising alternative for traditional centralised systems, allowing for data collecting organisations to manage their own data, while also providing a strong foundation to genomic and epidemiological analysis and reporting on linked datasets. Scaling SecureEpiLink across jurisdictions will require the integration of SecureEpiLink with existing health data systems, development of secure methods for verifying user identity and protecting data, and structured processes for system roll out, maintenance, and updates.
