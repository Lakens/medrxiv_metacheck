Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Abstract

Background: Decisions about deploying large language model (LLM) agents in health care require the cost of completed work in production. Published evidence reports benchmark accuracy, benchmark-level cost, and time or financial outcomes for ambient documentation, and list prices alone do not determine the cost of completed work. To our knowledge, no deployed clinical agent fleet has published a measurement method that ties compute cost, latency, failures and retries to completed work.

Objective: This study aims to estimate the compute cost of completed work by the orchestrated clinical agents of a deployed LLM agent fleet over a 12-week window of production telemetry, using estimators, data-quality gates and an analysis plan fixed before any window data are extracted for the study.

Methods: The setting is a private outpatient clinic in London, United Kingdom, where wai, a multi-agent system supporting clinic operations, is deployed. The population is every run of the 20 orchestrated clinical documentation and decision-support agents starting between September 21 and December 13, 2026. The run store writes 2 records for every run, a non-terminal record before the model call and a terminal record after it, and has no attempt counter or parent-run link. Estimators defined on that structure cover cost per resolved execution and per completed intention, retry and failed-intention shares of compute, error and failure rates, latency, cost per draft offered for clinician review and a latency-cost frontier, by agent, task class and acceptance stratum. Cost is recorded in GBP pence, converted from provider-returned US dollar cost at a fixed rate and rounded per model call. The plan fixes weekly aggregation, small-cell suppression, bootstrap intervals over weeks, 2 prespecified comparisons, 5 sensitivity analyses and 4 data-quality gates whose failure converts an estimate into a bound or an unmeasured stratum. Two staff-level measures proposed during planning were dropped. Revenue, pricing, margin and staffing inference are excluded.

Results: The measurement window opened on September 21, 2026, and closes on December 13, 2026, and telemetry is being recorded in routine operation. No window data had been extracted for this study at submission. Aggregate whole-table counts that include window records were extracted on November 19, 2026, for companion manuscripts and are disclosed. Extraction will begin no earlier than December 15, 2026, and the results are expected to be submitted for publication in 2027.

Conclusions: The compute cost of completed clinical agent work can be specified as estimators over the run-level telemetry this deployment records, provided paired recording rows are separated from retries, missing terminal states are bounded and rounding of recorded cost is reported. This protocol fixes those estimators, and the reporting consequence of each known instrumentation defect, before extraction.

Trial Registration: None. The analysis plan will be registered on Open Science Framework Registries before any window data are extracted for this study.

Introduction Background

Health systems are moving large language model (LLM) tools from drafting text toward agents that take actions in clinical operations. Ambient documentation is, to our knowledge, the clinical LLM application with the most mature evidence, including randomized trials. Two pragmatic trials published in December 2025 found small or inconsistent reductions in documentation time, with a significant reduction in work exhaustion in one trial and a direction of benefit that requires confirmation in the other [1, 2]. The accompanying editorial anticipated that next-generation products would extend into orders, prior authorization and billing [3]. A 5-site difference-in-differences study of 8581 clinicians estimated 16.0 fewer documentation minutes per 8 scheduled hours among adopters [4]. Agent research has advanced in parallel. Agents have been benchmarked in a virtual electronic health record (EHR) environment [5] and evaluated in a sandboxed EHR able to order tests and prescribe [6], and a prospective deployment of an agentic workflow for hospital course summaries reported its safety outcomes [7]. An evidence map found early agent deployments concentrated in administrative workflows [8]. A systematic review of 519 LLM evaluations found that only 5% used real patient care data [9].

The economic evidence for these systems is thinner than the evidence on accuracy and time. For ambient scribes, a single-system analysis of more than 1.2 million ambulatory encounters reported changes in physician financial productivity [10], and an invited commentary set these gains against per-clinician subscription fees [11]. A national evaluation of ambient voice technology in the National Health Service (NHS) in England that includes cost-consequence and budget-impact modeling is at the protocol stage [12]. These analyses price a tool by subscription and measure outcomes at the level of the clinician or the encounter. They do not expose the compute cost of a unit of work. Benchmark studies have begun to report computational cost alongside accuracy [13], to show that concatenating clinical tasks reduces cost under controlled load [14], and to relate accuracy to token budgets across model sizes [15]. Outside medicine, an analysis of agent benchmarks argued that agents should be evaluated jointly on cost and accuracy [16]. Two 2026 preprints report figures close to production. The governance framework for an EHR-embedded documentation agent tracks cost and latency and reports completion after retries absorbed transient model errors [17], and a simulated scheduling agent reports median latency and an average operational cost per appointment [18]. A study of 983,154 patient message responses by 1542 clinicians found that adoption of a drafting tool was associated with longer message turnaround, although use of a draft was associated with shorter turnaround, which indicates that latency is an operational outcome as well as an engineering metric [19].

Trends in inference prices complicate the measurement problem. The price of reaching a fixed level of benchmark performance fell by between about 9-fold and 900-fold per year depending on the performance milestone [20]. A subsequent analysis of the largest price dataset assembled to date estimated that the price of a given level of performance fell about 5-fold to 10-fold per year for frontier models, while the price of running frontier models rose between 3-fold and 18-fold per year because of larger models and greater reasoning demands [21]. The cost of completed work therefore depends on which model is called, how many tokens each task consumes, how calls are orchestrated, and how often they fail or are retried, and the list price sets none of these.

The economically relevant unit is a completed piece of work rather than a single model generation, because runs fail, are retried and fan out across agents. Cost per generation omits the compute spent on failed and retried attempts. Computing the cost of completed work requires that every run is recorded, that a retry can be distinguished from a second record of the same run, and that missing terminal states can be detected. Regulators have asked for related logging for other purposes. NHS England guidance for ambient scribing expects deployer monitoring independent of the manufacturer [22], the United Kingdom National Commission into the Regulation of AI in Healthcare recommended logging to detect performance drift [23], and the European Union Artificial Intelligence Act requires automatic event logging and log retention by providers and deployers of high-risk systems [24]. The Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) AI Airlock report found that human oversight may not function as a fixed safety control over a device's lifecycle [25]. Reporting standards for health economic evaluation, including the extension for interventions that use artificial intelligence, require transparent reporting of resource use [26, 27], and reporting guidance exists for studies that use LLMs [28] and for analyses of EHR-use metadata [29]. To our knowledge, no deployed clinical agent fleet has published a measurement framework that links compute cost, latency, failures and retries to completed work.

Objective

This study aims to estimate the compute cost of completed work by the orchestrated clinical agents of an LLM agent fleet over a prespecified 12-week window of production telemetry. This protocol sets out the units of analysis, the estimators, the rules for separating recording rows from retries, the analysis plan with its denominators and failure conditions, and the state of the deployment's instrumentation before the window, so that each is fixed and publicly registered before any window data are extracted for the study.

Methods

Study Design

This is a single-site prospective observational study of routinely recorded production telemetry over a prespecified 12-week window, with an analysis plan registered before extraction. The study is not an economic evaluation. It measures compute resource use per unit of work, which could serve as an input to an evaluation reported under the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards 2022 (CHEERS 2022) and its extension for artificial intelligence (CHEERS-AI) [26, 27]. Reporting follows the applicable items of TRIPOD-LLM [28], with items that do not apply to a protocol marked as such (Multimedia Appendix 2).

Setting and System

The setting is a private outpatient clinic in London, United Kingdom. The system is wai, a multi-agent system supporting clinic operations, developed and operated by Wai Health Inc, which supports patient messaging, voice contact, scheduling, documentation drafting, results handling and proactive monitoring. Its architecture separates proposal from commitment: agents propose actions, and for actions that cross the approval boundary a person accepts the proposal before the effect is committed [30]. Version 0.2 of the code-derived action inventory (September 8, 2026) records 153 agent actions in 23 functional domains [30, 31]. The deployment is described in a companion manuscript [32].

This study covers the orchestrated clinical pipeline, the part of the fleet whose every run is written to a single run store. The pipeline comprises 20 agents that draft clinical summaries, patient summaries, letters, sick notes and clinical notes, extract action points, and return decision support to clinicians (risk screening, differential diagnosis, safetynetting,drug interaction checking, coding, guideline lookup, investigation suggestion, risk scores, mental health risk triage, medication review, fit-to-fly assessment and note critique). They correspond to rows AA-01 to AA-20 of the inventory. Other agents in the fleet, including the messaging, voice and operations agents, do not write to the run store, and the call-level model log that some of them write carries no run identifier, so their compute cannot be attributed to units of work and is outside this study. As an indication of scale, the run store held 13,250 records written by 19 agents between May 24 and September 20, 2026 (6643 non-terminal, 5880 done and 727 error), counted under a version-locked extraction of pre-window records only.

Models

The orchestrator assigns each agent a model tier, and each tier resolves through a configured chain of models accessed through OpenRouter. In the production configuration read on November 19, 2026, the standard, low-cost and critic tiers used by the 20 agents resolved to deepseek/deepseek-v4-pro (DeepSeek), with deepseek/deepseek-v4-pro and then z-ai/glm-5.2 (Z.ai) as fallbacks, and 2 guidance skills named deepseek/deepseek-v4-flash directly. The date on which each live configuration value was set is not recorded, and the code changes affecting model selection that were committed during the window are listed in Multimedia Appendix 1. The model mix during the window is therefore measured from the data rather than taken from configuration. The terminal record of each run stores the model that answered, whereas the non-terminal record stores the model requested, so the model mix is computed from terminal records only. Training cutoff dates of the provider models are not available to the authors. Prompt versions are recorded per run.

Data Sources

The primary data source is the run store, which records each orchestrated run with the agent identifier, model identifier, input hash, output hash, input and output token counts, latency, cost, prompt version, status, start time and finish time. Status takes one of 6 values: queued, running, done, error, skipped or cost-capped. The run store holds no direct patient identifier. Each record carries an encounter identifier and a free-text error field, neither of which is extracted. The secondary sources are the action inventory version 0.2 [30, 31], which maps each agent to its acceptance class, and the orchestrator code and configuration at commit 687f45e295, which define how records, cost and retries are written. The call-level model log, the communications approval record and the audit trail are not used.

How a Run Is Recorded

Every orchestrated execution writes 2 records. The orchestrator inserts a non-terminal record with status running before the model call and then inserts a second, separate record with the terminal status, the token counts, cost, latency and output hash after it. The first record is never updated or removed. On the normal path, usage, cost and terminal status are written together in the second insert, so if that insert is lost the evidence that the run consumed tokens is lost with it. When the model call or its retry raises an exception, the error record is written with no token counts, cost or latency, even if an earlier attempt had consumed tokens.

Retries occur at 3 levels, and none is linked in the run store. Within a call, a failed model candidate fails over to the next model in the tier's chain, and the failed attempt's usage is not recorded. Within a run, an output that fails parsing, schema validation or the output-language check is retried once, preferring a different model, and the tokens, cost and latency of both attempts are summed into the one terminal record. Across runs, a failed job is requeued with exponential backoff for up to 3 attempts, and a job whose worker died is requeued after a stale period. The run store has no attempt counter, parent-run identifier or job identifier. A requeued job produces the same input hash only if its payload and idempotency key are unchanged, and one document path includes the current time in its key, so its retries always appear as new intentions.

Recorded cost is in GBP pence, although the field name refers to cents. For each model call, the orchestrator takes the cost returned by OpenRouter when the response carries one and converts it at a fixed 79 pence per US dollar, rounding to a whole penny per call. Only when no cost is returned does it multiply token usage by a rate table held in code, last changed on July 14, 2026, which assigns zero cost to any model it does not list. Per-call values are summed over tool turns and the schema retry into the run's cost. Because each call is rounded to a whole penny, many low-cost runs record 0: 31.3% of done records before the window recorded a cost of 0. Cache tokens are priced but not stored, so recorded token counts cannot reproduce recorded cost.

Units of Analysis

The units of analysis are defined in Table 1, and the measurement chain is shown in Figure 1. The central distinction is between a record, an execution and an intention. A record is a row in the run store. An execution is 1 orchestrated attempt, represented by its terminal record, or by an unpaired non-terminal record when the terminal record is missing. An intention is the unit of work an agent was asked to perform, which may require more than 1 execution if earlier attempts fail. Executions are grouped into intentions by the intention key, the agent identifier together with the input hash, so that 2 agents given the same input are never merged. Grouping by key identifies only retries across runs that reuse the same input. Failover within a call and the schema retry within a run are inside 1 execution, and retries that change their input appear as new intentions, which biases the retry share downward and intention failure upward.

Three features of these definitions follow from the measurement problem. First, E2 places the cost of failed and retried executions in the numerator and only completed intentions in the denominator. Recording, Duplicate and Retry Rules

The rules are defined on the 2-record structure described above. In a version-locked extraction of pre-window records (May 24 to September 20, 2026), 6605 of 6643 non-terminal records shared agent identifier, input hash and exactly the same start time with a terminal record, and none carried token usage or cost. Of 6604 intention-key groups, 6537 (99.0%) were exactly 1 non-terminal and 1 terminal record, 31 were a single non-terminal record with no terminal record, and 36 were larger groups. No key had 2 done records. The framework applies 4 prespecified rules.

• Rule D1. A non-terminal record with no token usage or cost that shares agent identifier, input hash and start time with a terminal record is a recording row and is excluded. Pairing is 1 to 1 in start-time order, so that a group of 2 non-terminal and 2 terminal records yields 2 executions.

• Rule D2. Terminal records sharing an intention key are separate executions of 1 intention, and all of their costs are counted. A non-terminal record left unpaired by rule D1 is an execution whose terminal state is missing and is handled under gate G1.

• Rule D3. Groups that rule D1 cannot pair 1 to 1, for example 2 non-terminal records with the same start time and 1 terminal record, are unclassifiable. For these groups, intentionlevelestimators are computed under a lower bound, in which every unpaired non-terminal record is treated as a recording row, and an upper bound, in which every unpaired non-terminal record is treated as a failed execution.

• Rule D4. Executions sharing an intention key form 1 intention only if they started within 24 hours of the first execution in the group. Later executions with the same key start a new intention, which accommodates scheduled re-evaluation of unchanged inputs. Grouping windows of 1 hour and 7 days are sensitivity analyses. A unique index on encounter, agent and input hash applies to done records only. It prevents 2 completed records of the same work but does not constrain non-terminal or error records. Neither a fix for the 2-record write path nor a process that resolves non-terminal records in the run store existed at commit 687f45e295 (November 19, 2026). If either is deployed during the window, its first effect visible in the run store is treated as a change point and estimators are reported for the periods before and after it.

Prespecified Analysis Plan

The measurement window is 12 consecutive ISO weeks, weeks 39 to 50 of 2026, from Monday, September 21, 2026, 00:00 to Sunday, December 13, 2026, 23:59 London time. All executions that started within the window are eligible, and telemetry is aggregated by ISO week in London time. Runs that started within the window are followed for 24 hours after the window closes to allow terminal records to be written. The run store has no field that marks a test or sandbox run, so runs generated by staff testing in the production environment cannot be separated and are included.

Estimates are reported for the whole pipeline, for each task class, for each acceptance stratum, for each agent, and by configuration for the latency-cost frontier. Weekly cells with fewer than 5 events are suppressed in any published table. An estimator whose denominator is below 50 in a stratum over the window (resolved executions for E1 to E5, completed intentions for E6a and E6d) is reported as unmeasured for that stratum and is not pooled with neighboring strata.

Cost estimators are ratios of sums. Proportions are reported with their numerators and denominators. Intervals are 95% percentile intervals from a nonparametric bootstrap that resamples ISO weeks with replacement (2000 replicates), which preserves within-week correlation between runs. Because 12 weeks give limited information about between-week variance, and percentile intervals from few clusters tend to undercover, these intervals will be interpreted as approximate. The weekly series is the primary display, and the number of weeks contributing to each interval is reported. Latency quantiles are computed empirically with intervals from the same bootstrap. No hypothesis tests of trend are performed.

Model changes, prompt version changes, fix deployments and documented incidents are marked on the weekly series. Model and prompt changes are dated from the run store itself, as the first window day on which a new model identifier or prompt version appears on terminal records. Code changes committed during the window that affect model selection are marked at their commit date and labeled as commit dates, because deployment dates are not recorded (Multimedia Appendix 1). Where at least 2 weeks of data lie on each side of a change, estimates before and after the change are reported with their intervals.

Two prespecified comparisons, each with an expected direction, are examined by estimation rather than testing. First, retry share (E3a) is expected to be higher in the deep reasoning class than in the fast triage class, reported as a difference with its 95% CI. Second, the number of resolved executions per completed intention (E2 divided by E1) is expected to be higher in the deep reasoning class, reported as a difference with its 95% CI. The expected directions were carried over from the initial plan, which drew on pre-protocol inspection of run-store data from before the window. Those data are not reported here and do not enter any analysis.

Five sensitivity analyses are prespecified: (1) the lower and upper bounds of rule D3; (2) intention grouping windows of 1 hour and 7 days (rule D4); (3) replacement of recorded zero costs on done records by an approximation computed from recorded token counts and the code rate table, which excludes cache tokens and is reported as an approximation; (4) imputation of cost for error records written without usage, using the median cost of error records with usage for the same agent and week; and (5) exclusion of weeks with documented incidents affecting the run store.

Extraction produces aggregates only. The queries are version-controlled, fixed to the window dates rather than to the time of execution, and run by SY at a recorded commit, and each extract file is hashed at creation. Gate outcomes are computed by the queries against the registered thresholds, which leave no discretion. No record-level rows, free text, encounter identifiers or input hashes leave the production environment.

Scope and Exclusions

The framework measures compute resource use only. It contains no revenue, pricing, margin or staffing inference, reports unit costs and proportions rather than total expenditure, and supports no comparison of agent work with human work, which would require time-and-motion measurement of the human alternative. Engineering effort, infrastructure, oversight time and review time are real costs that the framework does not measure, so its estimates are not estimates of total cost of ownership. Agents outside the orchestrated pipeline are excluded because their compute is not recorded per unit of work.

Discussion

Expected Findings

This protocol specifies estimators that link compute cost, latency, errors and retries to completed intentions in the orchestrated clinical pipeline of a multi-agent LLM system deployed in a single private outpatient clinic. Because the properties on which their validity depends are not all in place, with every run written as 2 records, terminal records sometimes missing and recorded cost rounded per call, the plan specifies in advance whether each estimate is reported as a point estimate, as a bound or as unmeasured.

Three kinds of result are expected. The first is the pair of prespecified comparisons, with a higher retry share and more resolved executions per completed intention in the deep reasoning class than in the fast triage class. The second is the gate outcomes, which are findings in their own right. Because no process resolves missing terminal records, gate G1 may fail for some strata, and estimates for those strata would then be reported as bounds. The third concerns the recorded cost itself. If the share of zero-cost done records seen before the window persists, cost estimators for the lowest-cost agents will understate their compute, and the token-based approximation will show by how much. Each of these outcomes will be reported.

Checking the plan against the code led us to revise a premise of the earlier plan. That plan treated a group of records sharing an input hash as the attempts behind 1 retried intention, and later as a mixture of retries and occasional duplicate writes. The write path shows that the second record is the rule rather than a defect: every execution is written twice. Retry detection from input hashes therefore requires a pairing step before any grouping, and the same consideration applies to any deployment that writes a pending record and a separate completion record for each run. We also found that the cost field, named in cents, holds pence. A unit cost read from such a field without checking the code would be wrong by the exchange rate.

Comparison With Prior Work

Economic evidence on deployed clinical LLM tools concerns ambient documentation and is reported at the level of the clinician, the encounter or the subscription [10, 11]. Randomized and multisite studies measured time and well-being rather than cost [1, 2, 4], and the editorial accompanying the trials concluded that the productivity case was not yet established [3]. The planned NHS evaluation will model cost consequences at trust level [12]. The present study measures a different quantity, the compute consumed per unit of completed work, which a subscription price does not reveal. It is complementary to these evaluations and could supply some of the resource-use measurements that a CHEERS-AI-conformant evaluation requires [27].

Benchmark studies report cost per query or per task under controlled conditions [13] [14] [15], and analyses of agent benchmarks have argued for joint reporting of cost and accuracy [16]. These designs do not include retries in production, failed intentions or fan-out across agents. Cost-of-pass, defined as the expected cost of producing 1 correct output, divides cost per attempt by the success rate [33]. Estimator E2 has the same structure. It applies it to production intentions rather than to benchmark items, and it adds the pairing of recording rows, the bounds for unclassifiable groups, the separation of agents without enforced acceptance and the data-quality gates that production telemetry requires. Estimator E7 places cost and latency on the same axes for the configurations of each agent, in the spirit of the joint cost-accuracy evaluation proposed for agents [16].

Of the 2 preprints closest to production, one reports completion after retries absorbed transient model errors [17], a rate that does not show the compute spent on the failed attempts, which estimators E3a and E3b report. The other reports a cost per appointment relative to manual scheduling [18], a comparison the present study does not make because it does not measure the human alternative. Production evidence that drafting tools can lengthen message turnaround [19] supports treating intention latency (E5b) as an outcome in its own right.

Prices for a fixed level of capability have fallen quickly and unevenly across tasks [20], while the price of running frontier models has risen with model size and reasoning demands [21]. A unit cost measured in one window is therefore bound to its model mix, prompt versions, price schedule and exchange-rate convention, and the frontier estimator is designed to be recomputed whenever a configuration changes rather than extrapolated. The same telemetry could serve the deployer monitoring and logging described in the Introduction [22] [23] [24]. The high-risk obligations of the European Union Artificial Intelligence Act for artificial intelligence in medical devices now apply from August 2, 2028 [34]. The cost of a draft offered for review (E6a) says nothing about whether the draft was used. The AI Airlock finding that oversight may weaken as users come to trust accurate outputs [25] is a reason to read any fall in that cost together with a measure of review fidelity, such as the one proposed in a companion manuscript [35], before it is interpreted as an efficiency gain.

Limitations

This study has limitations. First, the usefulness of the estimators cannot be judged until the window closes. If gate G1 or gate G3 fails at the level of the whole pipeline, the results paper will report gate outcomes rather than cost estimates. Second, the setting is a single private outpatient clinic, and the study covers only the 20 orchestrated agents of a larger fleet; the messaging, voice and operations agents are excluded because their compute is not recorded per unit of work. The definitions are intended to be site-independent, but they have been checked against 1 schema only, and the estimates will not generalize to larger or differently organized services. Third, compute cost is not total cost of ownership. Engineering, infrastructure, oversight and review time are excluded, and compute unit costs read as total costs would favor automation. The estimates are intended to inform model routing, retry policy, prompt changes and monitoring, not adoption decisions. Fourth, the recorded cost is an imperfect measure of compute. It is converted at a fixed exchange rate, rounded to a whole penny per call so that many low-cost runs record 0, omits the usage of failover attempts within a call and of error records written on the exception path, and cannot be reproduced from stored token counts because cache tokens are not stored. The sensitivity analyses bound some of these effects but cannot remove them. Fifth, retries are linked only when they reuse the same input, because the run store has no attempt counter or parent run, which biases retry share downward and intention failure upward. Sixth, the run store has no field that marks a test run, so staff testing in production is included. Seventh, the plan was elaborated after the window opened, the expected directions of the 2 comparisons came from pre-protocol data, and aggregate whole-table counts that include window records were extracted for companion manuscripts before the final revision. This creates a risk of analytic flexibility, which is mitigated by listing every elaboration with its reason, disclosing the exception in full and registering the plan with a public time stamp before extraction. Eighth, this is a description and evaluation plan written by the people who built and operate the system: OE and SY hold equity in its developer, and SY will run the extraction. The gates and thresholds were fixed before extraction, and aggregate extracts with their generating queries will be available to editors and reviewers. Ninth, estimates depend on provider prices and the model mix during the window, both of which change quickly [20, 21], so they will describe a configuration at a time rather than a stable property of the system.

Conclusions

The compute cost of completed clinical agent work can be specified as a set of estimators over run-level telemetry of the kind this deployment records, provided paired recording rows are separated from retries, missing terminal states are bounded and the unit and rounding of recorded cost are known. In this deployment, those conditions were not all met before the window opened, and the plan treats them as rules and gates rather than assuming them. Whether the estimators yield informative unit costs will be determined by the prespecified window, and replication in other deployments would be needed before the measures could be proposed as a reporting standard for clinical artificial intelligence.
