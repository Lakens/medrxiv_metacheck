Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Abstract

Objectives: Toxoplasmosis represents a significant global health burden, particularly among immunocompromised patients and newborns of mothers who contracted a primary infection during pregnancy.

In the newborn, maternally transferred Toxoplasma gondii-specific IgG persist for up to six months and mask the IgG newly synthesized by the infant, delaying diagnosis. Individual polymorphisms of the IgG heavy chain constant region (IGHG) can distinguish the two origins, but have so far been characterized only in total circulating IgG. We assessed whether this discrimination can be achieved on the IgG fraction directed against the parasite itself.

Methods: Recombinant T. gondii antigens (TgSAG1, TgGRA7, TgAMA1) were produced and evaluated by ELISA on 85 seropositive and 95 seronegative plasma samples. Parasite-specific IgG were then immunopurified from a mixture of plasma from two donors, one seropositive and one seronegative, carrying distinct IGHG1 and IGHG3 alleles known from DNA sequencing, and their Fc/2 fragments were analyzed by middle-down and bottom-up LC-MS/MS.

Results: TgSAG1 proved the most effective antigen; combined with TgAMA1 and/or TgGRA7 it reached 100% sensitivity and 97% specificity. Middle-down analysis offered high specificity but insufficient sensitivity for the microliter plasma volumes required in newborns. Bottom-up analysis identified the peptide WQQGNVFSCSVMHEGLHNHYTQK, characteristic of IGHG1*07, exclusively in the seropositive donor, in agreement with DNA sequencing data.

Conclusion: Combining antigen-affinity immunopurification with IGHG proteotyping allows an individual IGHG allele to be assigned within a pathogen-specific polyclonal IgG fraction representing less than 0.5% of total plasma IgG. This first demonstration, obtained in a two-donor context, opens the way to distinguishing maternal from fetal specific IgG for the early diagnosis of congenital toxoplasmosis.

1. INTRODUCTION

Toxoplasmosis is a cosmopolitan parasitosis that affects one-third of the world's population [1]. This infection, caused by Toxoplasma gondii, is mostly asymptomatic, except in immunocompromised individuals and in fetuses and infants infected in utero. Congenital toxoplasmosis (CT) results from the transplacental passage of the tachyzoite blood stage of the parasite, which occurs in 30% of cases of primary infection during pregnancy [2]. The earlier the contamination of the fetus, the more serious the clinical consequences (death in utero, premature delivery, or term childbirth with perivisceral involvement), resulting mainly in neuro-ocular damage in case of later contamination [3, 4]. Appropriate prenatal screening and treatment can significantly reduce the risks and severity of CT, which still presents a significant clinical challenge.

In countries that have chosen to implement prenatal toxoplasmosis monitoring, guidelines such as those established by the French National Authority for Health (HAS, Haute Autorité de Santé) [5] recommend monthly serological monitoring for T. gondii seronegative pregnant women to detect seroconversion by measuring immunoglobulin IgG and IgM levels [6]. However, in countries without automated screening and monitoring during pregnancy, the difficulty in diagnosing CT is exacerbated. This is the case on the African continent, where the toxoplasmosis prevalence rates fluctuate between 21%-74% depending on the region, with the highest rates in central African countries, and where no systematic recommendations are applied for pregnancy follow-up to prevent CT [7]. In France, the seroprevalence of toxoplasmosis in pregnant women has decreased over time, from 54.3% in 1995 to 31.3% in 2016, thanks to the national program established by the HAS for the prevention of congenital toxoplasmosis [6].

In addition to prenatal evaluation, neonatal biological diagnosis of toxoplasmosis is essential in the case of (i) suggestive clinical signs in the newborn with no information on the serological status of the mother, (ii) seroconversion diagnosed during pregnancy, (iii) not or poorly followed pregnancy, and (iv) for enhanced effectiveness of treatments administered as soon as possible to the newborn. This diagnosis is based mainly on the search for parasitic DNA in the placenta, amniotic fluid, cord blood, or newborn blood collected at delivery, in case of confirmed maternal seroconversion [6]. Serological tests are also performed on newborns. Indeed, as maternal IgA and IgM do not cross the placental barrier, they represent good markers of congenital infection in the newborn [8]. Nevertheless, some IgM could persist for years after the initial infection [9]. It has been demonstrated that IgM serology alone should no longer be considered an acute infection marker because of the heterogeneity of its kinetic response. [10] Detection of IgG synthesized by the child has diagnostic value only after 6 months of life, once maternally transmitted IgG has been eliminated. The immunoblotting techniques comparing the mother and child IgG profiles directed against several toxoplasmic antigens (Ags) remain difficult to interpret [8]. Nevertheless, the combination of several complementary tests constitutes a decision tree for a neonatal biological diagnosis of CT [6]. Early characterization of specific IgG neosynthesized by the newborn would be highly useful for diagnosing congenital T. gondii infection in the neonatal period.

To this end, a diagnostic test that differentiates maternal-specific IgG from newborn-synthesized IgG would be appropriate. The heavy chain (HC) of each IgG consists of a variable domain (VH) followed by three constant domains (CH1, CH2, and CH3-CHS), with a hinge region separating CH1 and CH2.

The CH2 and CH3-CHS domains constitute the fragment crystallizable region or Fc region [11]. For each of the four IgG subclasses, IgG1, IgG2, IgG3, and IgG4, amino acid polymorphisms are described within the CH domains, mainly in the Fc fragments [12]. In the April 2025 edition, 73 alleles from the ImmunoGlobulin Heavy Gamma chain (IGHG) genes were referenced in the international database ImMunoGeneTics information system® (IMGT® [13], see S1 Table). They consist of 17 IGHG1, 19 IGHG2, 29 IGHG3, and 8 IGHG4 alleles, respectively, for 6, 5, 18, and 5 distinct protein variants. To our knowledge, the exploration of their potential as a diagnostic tool in neonatal blood to distinguish pathogen-specific IgG transferred by the mother from that synthesized by the newborn in response to an in utero infection has not been documented. Our working group has progressively established the feasibility of this approach [14, 15]. Dechavanne et al. first defined proteotypic peptides discriminating the G3m allotypes and the IGHG3 alleles, and showed that these could be detected and quantified by mass spectrometry in total IgG3 purified from plasma, including in a mother and her infant followed over the first months of life. Dambrun et al. subsequently extended the analysis to the four IGHG subclasses and validated it at the molecular level, alleles assigned by proteomics on total IgG matching those deduced from DNA sequencing of the CH2 and CH3-CHS exons in 95% of cases. A middle-down strategy applied to intact Fc/2 fragments of total polyclonal IgG further improved the specificity of allele discrimination [16]. All of this work was nevertheless carried out on the whole circulating IgG pool.

Transposing it to a diagnostic setting requires isolating the IgG directed against the pathogen itself, a fraction amounting to less than 0.5% of total plasma IgG, and resolving the alleles of two individuals within a single sample. Unlike the characterization of monoclonal therapeutic antibodies (Abs), where a single IGHG variant is present at high concentration, the present work addresses a substantially more challenging analytical context. The simultaneous detection of up to eight co-occurring IGHG allelic variants in a polyclonal, Ag-specific IgG fraction representing less than 0.5% of total plasma IgG introduces near-isobaric signal overlap, severe sensitivity constraints, and allele-assignment ambiguities that require dedicated chromatographic and data-analysis strategies.

The isolation of plasma IgG specific to the T. gondii tachyzoite stage requires the use of the most adapted recombinant Ags for a diagnostic purpose in a CT context [17, 18]. The TgSAG1, TgGRA7 and TgAMA1 Ags have proven to be effective indicators of a T. gondii infection. Notably, dense granule Ags like GRA7 serve as specific markers for the acute phase of toxoplasmosis [19], while the major surface Ag SAG1 has shown high sensitivity in serology tests for both acute and chronic phases of the infection [20]. The TgAMA1 apical membrane Ag is also investigated for its diagnostic potential, especially in the early infection stage [21].

In that context, the ultimate goal of our project is to provide an innovative approach to support an early diagnosis of congenital toxoplasmosis, based on the exclusive presence of T. gondii-specific IgG newly synthesized by the fetus, regardless of residual maternal IgG. This approach is based on a dual method that combines immunological isolation of mother-and child-T. gondii-specific plasma IgG, followed by mass spectrometry detection of the individual IGHG protein alleles. In the present study, we first evaluated the quality (specificity and sensitivity) of IgG directed against the selected T. gondii recombinant Ags (TgSAG1, TgGRA7 and TgAMA1) using 180 plasma samples from a biobank of toxoplasmosis-seronegative and seropositive pregnant women. Second, we presented an intermediate proof-of-concept step of our alternative diagnostic approach, which consisted of using T. gondii-specific IgG isolated from 2 plasma donors, respectively seronegative and seropositive for toxoplasmosis, whose IGHG alleles had been determined using the DNA sequencing method described in [15]. A middle-down tandem MS protocol was designed exclusively for the proteomic assessment of these alleles, with a focus on the CH2 and CH3-CHS domains of the Fc fragment. We specifically further investigated the IgG1 and IgG3 subclasses by bottom-up proteomics for their prominent role in T. gondii congenital infection [22]. These two subclasses are all the more interesting because of the abundance of IgG1 (65 to 70% of total IgG) [23] on the one hand and the high amino acid polymorphism of IgG3 (18 protein variants) on the other hand.

2.2 Measurement of IgG specific to T. gondii recombinant antigens

2.4.4 Fc/2 capture

Finally, the different fractions were incubated on Capture Select FcXP (Affinity Matrix, ThermoFisher Scientific), washed, eluted, and neutralized using the protocol described above to isolate the Fc domains. 2.5.1 Middle-down analysis

IGHG alleles were detected on a Q Exactive HF Orbitrap coupled with a nanoRSLC system. Samples were separated on a C4 column and acquired in DDA mode (240,000 resolution; top 10 HCD fragmentation). Spectra were deconvoluted using Freestyle 1.8 and searched with Byonic against a custom IMGT-derived IGHG library (10 ppm precursor tolerance; filters: score >250, False Discovery Rate <1%). Results were manually validated.

2.5.2 IGHG sequence library

A reference database was built from IMGT FASTA sequences covering CH2-CH3-CHS regions of IGHG1-4 completed with the amino-acid sequences of Khatri et al. [27]. Full CH1-to-M2 coding sequences were included to ensure complete coverage of all proteolytic peptides. One previously identified unreferenced IGHG1 allele was also added.

2.5.3 Bottom-up sample preparation

Fc/2-containing fractions were reduced with 5 mM DTT for 30 min at 56°C, alkylated with 25 mM iodoacetamide at room temperature in the dark, and digested overnight at 37°C with trypsin, LysC, or both. Peptides were desalted with ZipTip C18 (Millipore, Merck).

2.5.4 Fc/2 chromatographic separation

After evaluating C4, MabPac RP, and Poroshell 300SB-C3 stationary phases, the latter was selected and connected on a UHPLC Vanquish system (80 μL/min, 60°C) coupled to an Orbitrap Tribrid Eclipse as the operational middle-down platform.

2.5.5 DDA and DIA bottom-up proteomics

Peptides were injected onto a Neo Vanquish nanoLC coupled to an Orbitrap Astral. Separation was performed on a 25 cm C18 Aurora column using a 40-min gradient. DDA was run in Top100 mode; DIA used 2 m/z windows over 450-1500 m/z. Identification was performed using Sequest-HT (DDA) and DIA-NN (DIA), with carbamidomethylation fixed and oxidation/deamidation variable. Candidate allelespecific peptides were verified at the MS1 and MS2 levels with 30 ppm tolerance.

The performance of the three recombinant Ags in terms of sensitivity (SS) and specificity (SP) of IgG responses directed against them was determined from serological results for the three investigated population groups.

3.4 The middle-down strategy provides high specificity, enabling IGHG allele discrimination

Because of the high similarity of the protein sequences encoded by the IGHG alleles, their discrimination requires higher coverage to improve the analytical specificity. In a preliminary step, we implemented a middle-down strategy [16], and demonstrated IGHG allele molecular characterization of intact Fc/2 fragments (~211 aa) by nano-LC-MS/MS of polyclonal total IgG. For this purpose, the total plasma IgG of the HVDtot donor were enriched on protein G, deglycosylated (PNGase F), cleaved with IdeS, and reduced with TCEP before nano-LC-MS/MS on a Q Exactive with HCD fragmentation ( S2 Fig). Up to 50% sequence coverage per allele was achieved, enabling discrimination of variants with one-to-three-point mutations in CH2-CH3-CHS. Two challenges remained: applying the approach to low-abundance Ag-specific IgG, and resolving complexity from co-eluted IgG fragments from two individuals. Co-elution of near-isobaric Fc/2 variants on the PepMap300 C4 nano-LC column was a major bottleneck. The Poroshell 300SB-C3 (300 Å) improved the Fc/2 spreading along the retention time axis with reproducible elution profiles. However, the transition to UHPLC scale (80 µL/min vs. 180 nL/min) resulted in a ~500-fold increase in flow rate, accompanied by a corresponding loss of Electrospray Ionization sensitivity, which was incompatible with low-abundance T. gondii-specific fractions.

4. DISCUSSION

>DISCUSSION

4.1 Isolation of highly specific plasma IgG from T. gondii: choice of recombinant antigens, diagnostic value, limitations

Among the three recombinant T. gondii Ags produced in this study, TgSAG1 proved to be the most reliable for the diagnosis of toxoplasmosis, as previously demonstrated [31]. Given that TgAMA1 also exhibited good sensitivity and specificity, it could effectively complement TgSAG1. The recombinant TgAMA1 Ag demonstrated promising potential for the early diagnosis of T. gondii, particularly in its the full-length protein form (as used in our study), which shows stronger interaction with IgG (99.4%) and IgM (80.0%) than isolated N-terminal or C-terminal fragments [21]. TgGRA7 proved to be the least sensitive and least specific of the Ags studied. It is likely less reliable when used alone but useful when combined with other Ags to improve the accuracy of serological tests for diagnosing acute and chronic toxoplasmosis, as suggested by Mamaghani et al. (2020) [32]. Furthermore, the development of rapid diagnostic tests, such as DOT-ELISA, has shown that TgGRA7 exhibits 100% sensitivity and 97.2% specificity for IgM detection when combined with TgSAG1. For IgG detection, the combination of TgSAG1 and TgGRA7 achieved 100% sensitivity, confirming the utility of TgGRA7 in the rapid serological diagnosis of acute and chronic toxoplasmosis, whether in the laboratory or in the field [33].

In general, TgSAG1 appears to be the best choice if a single Ag is used, whereas combining it with other Ags would likely yield a more comprehensive diagnostic strategy by reducing the proportion of false positives [34]. Among the combinations identified above, the individual IgG response against TgSAG1, combined with responses against TgGRA7, TgAMA1, or both, demonstrated the highest values for sensitivity, specificity, PPV and NPV. Hence, it was deemed the optimal combination for accurate serological diagnosis. With our aim of developing a tool for the early detection of toxoplasmosis, combining TgGRA7 with the key antigen TgSAG1 could enable to capture the IgG kinetics across the various phases of human T. gondii infection and optimize the detection of parasitespecific IgGs. Furthermore, the addition of TgAMA1 proves highly useful in compensating for the inconsistency of TgGRA7.

We therefore purified specific IgG directed against the 3 recombinant Ags. We initially chose to purify T. gondii-specific IgGs separately, using columns coated with a single Ag at a time to avoid any antigenic competition or steric hindrance that might occur with columns coated with multiple Ags. Indeed, antigenic competition can arise when multiple Ags are in contact, leading to the preferential selection of IgGs directed against specific Ags. For example, the study of Shnawa et al. (2023) [35] demonstrated that the simultaneous presence of E. coli and P. aeruginosa Ags in an isolation column resulted in lower retention of specific IgGs compared to the retention observed when the Ags specific to each pathogen were presented separately. The IgG fraction purified from our toxoplasmosis seropositive (HVD+) plasma contained a higher proportion of anti-TgSAG1 IgGs compared to IgGs directed against the other two Ags, which were present only at residual levels, particularly in the case of TgGRA7. This result is consistent with the fact that a former T. gondii-seropositive individual maintains a robust and long-lasting Ab response against TgSAG1 [31]. However, a method combining all three Ags would be more advantageous in terms of processing time and purification yield, especially if the available plasma volume requires an iterative method necessitating the reuse of the same plasma for each Ag column. We plan to reach this final stage by combining our three recombinant Ags into a single chimeric Ag to optimize the purification protocol. Thus, the use of a single chimeric Ag could improve diagnostic performance for the early detection of T. gondii infection [36]. Sensitivity and specificity are generally determined against a single gold-standard test; however, in our study, serological results for toxoplasmosis were obtained using various commercial diagnostic tests (Platelia™ Toxo IgG; recomWell Toxoplasma IgG; VIDAS® TOXO IgG II) depending on the population groups included. The "negative" and "positive" serological results from these tests were used to evaluate the diagnostic performance of our three recombinant T. gondii Ags. The Ags underlying these tests, as well as the technical conditions of their realization, were not considered. That said, potential heterogeneity in IgG levels among the commercial tests used may have contributed to enhancing the SS, SP, NPV, and PPV values obtained from these pooled results, particularly for TgSAG1.

4.2 Characterizing T. gondii-specific IGHG alleles in low-volume samples paves the way for clinical proteomic applications

The unique challenge of this study -which made it far more complex than the analysis of therapeutic monoclonal Abs typically studied-laid in characterizing the allelic composition of a minor fraction of T. gondii-specific IgGs within a mixture of polyclonal Abs from two individuals, all while using a sample volume limited to a few microliters of plasma to ensure compatibility with clinical monitoring of newborns.

From a proteomics perspective, we pursued two objectives: (1) to validate the bottom-up proteomics approach for characterizing IGHG alleles in low-abundance, Ag-specific IgG fractions, and (2) to assess whether DNA-based molecular allele assignments could be recapitulated at the protein level in a mixeddonor context. This work highlights the critical role of yield constraints when combining middle-down and bottom-up strategies.

Our foundational work [16] established the first proof-of-concept for discriminating IGHG alleles at the intact Fc/2 fragment level using LC-MS/MS on total polyclonal IgGs. Using HCD fragmentation of deglycosylated Fc/2 fragments (~211 aa, ~24 kDa) on a Q Exactive mass spectrometer, we achieved sequence coverage of up to 50% per allele, which proved sufficient to distinguish single amino acid substitutions. IGHG1 alleles were characterized most consistently, reflecting the predominance of IgG1 in serum (~65-70%). However, two prerequisites for clinical application have emerged: working with ultra-low-abundance Ag-specific IgGs and resolving the complexity of samples containing IGHG alleles from two individuals (with up to 16 alleles theoretically coexisting for two unrelated individuals, or 12 alleles in the case of a mother and her newborn).

This study directly addressed both challenges. T. gondii-specific IgG account for only 0.05-0.5% of total plasma IgG (mean total serum IgG level: ~10 mg/mL; anti-TgSAG1 IgG level in seropositive individuals: 5-50 μg/mL, mean: 16 μg/mL; comparable responses for anti-TgAMA1 and anti-TgGRA7 Abs). This extremely low proportion, several orders of magnitude below the 200-ng detection threshold of our proof-of-concept study, posed a major limitation for LC-MS. Despite multiple attempts under various conditions, middle-down proteomics failed to yield allele-specific spectra. Although current highresolution instruments are 1-2 orders of magnitude more sensitive than the original Q Exactive model, and affinity enrichment could be optimized, neither factor would fully compensate for the sensitivity loss resulting from the switch from nano-to micro-LC. Miniaturizing the LC separation could ultimately solve this problem, but it requires robust, reproducible nano-LC columns with dedicated large-pore stationary phases, which are not yet commercially available.

Our results established the feasibility of detecting disease-specific IGHG alleles in purified IgGs isolated from a pooled plasma sample -comprising material from one seronegative and one seropositive donor for toxoplasmosis -whose IGHG protein alleles had been previously identified via DNA molecular analysis. This work enabled the detection of amino acid polymorphisms in the Fc/2 fragment of the T. gondii-specific IgG and the identification of the IGHG1*07 allele exclusively in the seropositive sample, consistent with DNA sequencing data. However, further optimization is required before considering clinical application of this approach. Middle-down proteomics offers, in principle, analytical superiority: it remains the most specific strategy for distinguishing IGHG alleles as it targets intact Fc/2 fragments, thus avoiding ambiguities associated with shorter tryptic peptide sequences common to multiple alleles and subclasses. Chromatographic separation of quasi-isobaric Fc/2 variants poses the primary analytical challenge. Although some stationary phases offer the required selectivity, they are not yet available in the nanoscale format (internal diameter ≤ 320 μm) required to achieve the sensitivity characteristic of nano-LC. Consequently, the in-house fabrication of packed capillary columns with wide-pore C3 or Polystyrene-divinylbenzene stationary phases, in a format robust and reproducible enough for routine nano-LC use, represents a key technical objective for advancing the middle-down strategy in this study. Integrating this approach into the diagnostic workflow should be the next logical step, leveraging the sensitivity gains offered by the current generation of instruments.

Sensitivity remains the primary bottleneck: T. gondii-specific IgG are highly diluted relative to total IgG, and neonatal samples impose even stricter volume constraints. The use of multi-Ag affinity columns combining TgSAG1, TgAMA1 and TgGRA7 in a single step would increase the yield of specific IgGs and reduce processing time, a design already considered in the purification strategy. Furthermore, DDA and DIA approaches are complementary: DDA provides robust spectral peptide matches, whereas DIA offers broader and more reproducible peptide coverage. This highlights the need to integrate targeted DDA-based confirmation into future developments. Finally, it will be necessary to extend the search for allele-specific peptides to the IGHG2 and IGHG4 subclasses, as well as to other donors with fully characterized genetic profiles (DNA sequences), in order to evaluate the generalizability of this proteotypic strategy across the full diversity of human IGHG alleles. The resulting development priorities, and the stage they define in the maturation of the approach, are summarized in the Conclusion.

5. CONCLUSION

The present results demonstrate, as a proof of concept, that IGHG alleles can be detected within disease-specific IgG purified from a mixture of plasma from two distinct donors. The objective is a postnatal test able to attribute T. gondii-specific IgG to the newborn rather than to its mother, from a few microliters of blood and within the first weeks of life, at a stage where conventional serology cannot discriminate between the two origins. The step established here is the central link of that chain: parasitespecific IgG can be isolated with sufficient purity on recombinant antigens, and the resulting polyclonal fraction, although it represents less than 0.5% of total plasma IgG, still carries an allele-specific signature that bottom-up proteomics assigns unambiguously, in agreement with molecular data.

Middle-down proteomics nevertheless remains the ideal analytical strategy, as it reads the polymorphic positions within a single intact Fc/2 polypeptide and thereby avoids the allele and subclass ambiguities inherent in short tryptic peptides. As detailed in the Discussion, its deployment is held back by two coupled constraints, namely the chromatographic resolution of quasi-isobaric Fc/2 variants and the sensitivity loss imposed by the chromatographic scale at which that resolution is currently achievable; lifting them jointly, through dedicated nano-scale wide-pore columns, is the principal development ahead. The complementary steps identified above, from the yield of the immunopurification to the coverage of the IGHG2 and IGHG4 subclasses and the extension to further sequenced donors, and then to true mother-newborn pairs, define the remaining path to clinical evaluation.

In summary, this study complements current research focused on serum IgGs for therapeutic purposes [37, 38].
