Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Abstract

Background: In women with uterine fibroids counseled for myomectomy, consent typically involves discussing the preoperative probability that the surgery will end in hysterectomy. To our knowledge, no published tool estimates this probability, including in sub-Saharan Africa. This study aimed to temporally validate the Estimating Fibroid Factors Associated with Hysterectomy (EFFAH) score (an eight-item bedside score) in a later cohort at a district hospital in Ghana, to define risk groups for counseling and triage.

In this retrospective cohort study with temporal external validation, the development cohort comprised 392 records of women counseled for myomectomy with possible hysterectomy who underwent surgery at Catholic Hospital, Battor, in 2013 to 2014. A pilot analysis was conducted in July 2025 to arrive at the odds ratio used to calculate a validation sample size of 416. The validation cohort comprised 800 women counseled for myomectomy with possible hysterectomy in 2017 to 2019. The score assigns points to eight items from the history, abdominal examination, hemoglobin and renal ultrasound. We assessed discrimination, calibration and net benefit of the development equation with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), updated it by closed testing and compared it with AutoScore, a random forest-based points score.

Results: Hysterectomy was performed in 31.6% of women in the development cohort and 21.1% in the validation cohort. The C-statistic was 0.76 (95% CI: 0.70, 0.80) at development and 0.73 (95% CI: 0.69, 0.77) at validation. The development equation overestimated risk, with an observed-to-expected ratio of 0.69 (95% CI: 0.60, 0.78) and a calibration slope of 0.83 (95% CI: 0.63, 1.03), and an intercept-only update was selected.

The observed proportion with hysterectomy was 6.2% for scores of 0 to 2, 23.5% for 3 to 4 and 42.2% for ≥5; a score below 3 yielded a negative predictive value of 93.8% (95% CI: 90.1, 96.2). AutoScore yielded a Cstatistic of 0.74 (95% CI: 0.69, 0.78) and did not outperform the score (difference 0.01; 95% CI: -0.02, 0.04).

The EFFAH score ranked women by their risk of hysterectomy in a later cohort at the same hospital, and an intercept update aligned its average predicted risk with the observed proportion in that cohort.

Clinically, about 6 in 100 women scoring 0 to 2 and about 4 in 10 women scoring ≥5 underwent hysterectomy; for the latter, explicit consent for hysterectomy, a senior surgeon and readily available crossmatched blood are proposed. Because it uses only routine preoperative findings, the score could support consent and resource planning in resource-limited and resource-rich settings alike, after local recalibration and prospective multicenter evaluation.

Introduction

Leiomyomas are the most common benign tumors of the female reproductive system; their global incidence increased from 6.0 million in 1990 to 10.1 million in 2021, with the burden shifting toward countries with a low sociodemographic index 1 . Among Black women, cumulative prevalence by 50 years exceeds 80% 2 , two to three times the risk in white women 3 . In sub-Saharan Africa, women often present late with a large uterus; 88.7% of 300 women in Lagos had a uterus of ≥20 weeks at presentation 4 , and transfusion at abdominal myomectomy ranges from 22.9% to 58.2% in Nigerian series [5] [6] [7] . Hysterectomy is often culturally unacceptable because of the value placed on childbearing and the uterus [8] [9] [10] , and myomectomy is the fertilitysparing option 11 . For a woman who wishes to preserve her uterus, counseling and consent address myomectomy with possible hysterectomy. The preoperative probability of hysterectomy is therefore central to informed consent. Women who desired fertility but underwent hysterectomy have reported worse quality of life afterwards 12 , and women with fibroids report wanting complete information before surgery 13.

To the best of our knowledge, no published tool estimates this probability before surgery. In Nigeria, surgeons have recommended consent for hysterectomy before myomectomy 14 and have obtained consent for open myomectomy with possible hysterectomy when fibroids are very large 15 . Guidance from the Society of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists of Canada similarly states that women should be counseled about the risk of requiring a hysterectomy at a planned myomectomy but provides no estimate 16 . The practice bulletin of the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists on fibroids similarly recommends shared decisionmaking and myomectomy for women who want uterine preservation without addressing consent for hysterectomy 17 . Reported frequencies further seem to vary with setting and case mix; 3.0% of 13,213 myomectomies in a United States registry underwent a hysterectomy under the same anesthetic 18 , 1.0% of 35,151 abdominal myomectomies in California were converted to hysterectomy 19 , and 6% of repeat abdominal myomectomies for large uteri in the U.K. ended in hysterectomy for bleeding 20 . The registry analysis reported associations with age and operative time instead of a prediction model, and its authors could not determine whether the hysterectomies were planned 18, 21 . In Nigeria, hysterectomy followed one of 276 abdominal myomectomies in Ibadan 22 , two of 224, both at re-operation for bleeding 23 , and two planned myomectomies in a Kano series of 105 women 14 . Existing prediction tools in fibroid surgery address transfusion at myomectomy 24, 25 , conversion of laparoscopic to open myomectomy 26 or conversion and complications at hysterectomy [27] [28] [29] . All were developed in high-resource settings, several include surgical route or operative time as predictors, and none was developed for a district hospital or limited-resource setting where myomectomy may be performed by open surgery and the uterus is often at or above the umbilicus at presentation.

The Estimating Fibroid Factors Associated with Hysterectomy (hereafter EFFAH score) was developed at Catholic Hospital, Battor, a district hospital in the Volta Region of Ghana. It sums points for eight items recorded at the preoperative assessment: age, parity, uterine size on abdominal examination, previous myomectomy, previous laparotomy, hydronephrosis on the renal scan performed with the routine pelvic ultrasound, hemoglobin concentration and past transfusion. Uterine size, low hemoglobin concentration, previous myomectomy and previous abdominal surgery are established markers of bleeding, transfusion or hysterectomy in fibroid surgery 7, 22, [30] [31] [32] [33] . Hydronephrosis becomes more frequent as the uterus enlarges 34 , and older age was found to be the strongest preoperative factor associated with hysterectomy at myomectomy in a United States registry 18 . Evaluation of a prediction score in patients outside the development cohort requires assessment of calibration and net benefit as well as discrimination [35] [36] [37] . Whether a machine learning model would outperform a simple points score is also an empirical question; a systematic review found no such advantage in most clinical settings 38.

This study aimed to validate the EFFAH score for predicting hysterectomy among women counseled for myomectomy with possible hysterectomy in a later cohort at the same hospital and to update its equation for that period. The secondary aim was to compare the a priori score (whose points were allocated based on expert opinion) with AutoScore, a random forest-based points score built from the same items.

Methods

Study design and reporting

This was a retrospective cohort study with temporal external validation at a single center. The score was first analyzed as a pilot in the development cohort, which informed the sample size, and was then evaluated without changing its items or points in a later validation cohort whose records were extracted from patient charts and theater registers. We report the study in accordance with the TRIPOD+AI statement, the update of the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) guideline for regression and machine learning models 35.

Setting and participants

Both cohorts were drawn from the Department of Obstetrics and Gynaecology, Catholic Hospital, Battor, North Tongu District, Volta Region, Ghana, a district hospital that provides gynecologic surgery and serves as a major referral and training center [39] [40] [41] [42] . In both cohorts, women were eligible if they had been counseled preoperatively for myomectomy with possible hysterectomy for uterine fibroids. The development cohort comprised all 392 eligible surgical records from 2013 and 2014. For the validation cohort, the study team screened 1,132 records of women who underwent surgery between 2017 and 2019. Records were excluded if the woman had been counseled for hysterectomy only, if the operation was also performed for another indication, such as a pelvic abscess, or if the record duplicated another; records without an eligibility status were also excluded (Figure 1). The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Catholic Hospital, Battor (CHB-ERC-002/01/24), which also waived the need for individual consent on the basis that this was a retrospective analysis of anonymized records.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was hysterectomy (total or subtotal abdominal hysterectomy) at the index surgery, as coded by the study team from the operation record. The outcome therefore reflects operative findings (the surgeon and the woman's consent) as well as lesion severity (indirectly). Secondary outcomes were estimated blood loss, intraoperative blood transfusion and relaparotomy, taken from the complications section of the theater register.

The EFFAH score

The eight items and their points are shown in Figure 2A; the points were fixed a priori by expert opinion and were not re-estimated in either cohort. Two items were scored differently from the original scoring form because of how they were recorded in the data. First, the form assigns 2 points for one previous myomectomy and 3 points for two previous myomectomies, but the records stated only whether a previous myomectomy had taken place; any previous myomectomy was therefore assigned 2 points, and the maximum total was 15 instead of 16. Second, the form specifies hemoglobin at the first encounter with the patient, whereas both cohorts were scored on the last preoperative hemoglobin, the value used in the pilot analysis; hemoglobin at first encounter was examined in a sensitivity analysis.

Risk groups and proposed clinical actions

Three risk groups were defined from the development cohort at the score values where the observed proportion with hysterectomy increased most steeply: low (0 to 2 points), intermediate (3 to 4) and high (≥5).

We linked each group to a proposed clinical action (Table 1), i.e., counseling with the frequency of hysterectomy for the low-risk group; counseling and consent for possible hysterectomy for the intermediaterisk group; and a senior surgeon, readily available cross-matched blood and consideration of referral for the high-risk group. The groups were chosen after the observed proportions in both cohorts had been inspected, and the actions are proposed uses of the score that were not evaluated in this study.

Sample size

The sample size for the validation cohort was calculated in July 2025 from the pilot results with G*Power 3.1.9.7 (Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany) 43 . Using the two-tailed Wald z test for a logistic regression coefficient with an odds ratio (OR) of 1.89 per score point, a probability of hysterectomy of 0.2 under the null hypothesis, a two-sided alpha of 0.05, a power of 0.8 and a binomial predictor distribution, the required sample size was determined to be n = 416. Because this calculation treats the score effect as a single contrast, we also report the precision achieved for the C-statistic, the observed-to-expected (O/E) ratio and the calibration slope using the formulas put forward by Riley et al. for external validation 44, 45 . No sample size was calculated for the development cohort, which comprised all eligible records from 2013 and 2014.

Missing data

Missing score items were handled via multiple imputation by chained equations 46, 47 . For the development cohort, we used the 20 imputed datasets created for the pilot (mice default methods, 10 iterations). For the validation cohort, we imputed missing items with the same approach (20 datasets, 10 iterations; predictive mean matching for parity and hemoglobin, logistic regression for binary items and polytomous regression for abdominal examination and renal ultrasound), with the outcome and the number of living children in the imputation model. Items were imputed as raw values, and the score was calculated after imputation. Estimates were combined with Rubin's rules; C-statistics and proportions were pooled on the logit scale 46 . The imputation diagnostics are reported in Table S1 and Figures S1 and S2.

Statistical methods

Development cohort:

In each imputed dataset, we fitted a logistic regression of hysterectomy on the total score and pooled the coefficients with Rubin's rules. The pooled intercept and slope form the development equation, which converts a total score into a predicted probability. A logistic regression with the eight items as separate predictors was fitted in the same way to show the contribution of each item (Table S2). Linearity of the log odds in the total score was tested by comparing a restricted cubic spline with three knots with the linear term, using a likelihood ratio test pooled across imputations (Table S3). Collinearity among the eight items was assessed with generalized variance inflation factors adjusted for the degrees of freedom of each item. Influential records were identified with Cook's distance and with the standardized change in the score coefficient when each record was omitted (dfbetas).

Validation and updating:

We applied the development equation unchanged to the validation cohort.

Discrimination was assessed with the C-statistic. Calibration was assessed with the calibration intercept (calibration in the large), the calibration slope, the O/E ratio, the Brier score and a flexible calibration curve 36, 48 . Because the proportion with hysterectomy differed between the two periods, the equation was updated to the validation cohort using the closed testing procedure described by Vergouwe et al. 49 . This procedure compares three nested models: the unchanged development equation; recalibration in the large, which re-estimates the intercept while the coefficient of the linear predictor is fixed at 1; and logistic recalibration, which re-estimates both the intercept and the coefficient of the linear predictor. In each imputed dataset, we fitted the recalibration model 𝑙𝑜𝑔𝑖𝑡(𝑝) = 𝑎 + 𝑏 × 𝐿𝑃, where 𝐿𝑃 is the linear predictor of the development equation, and pooled a and b with Rubin's rules. The joint hypothesis 𝑎 = 0 and 𝑏 = 1 was tested with a pooled Wald test (D1 statistic); if it was rejected, the hypothesis b = 1 was tested, and recalibration in the large was retained unless this second test was also rejected, with alpha 0.05 for both tests (Table S3). Performance estimates of the updated equation in the validation cohort are considered apparent; optimism was estimated with 1,200 bootstrap samples that repeated the entire testing and updating procedure.

The updated equation gives the adjusted risks in Table 1 and Figure 2. Two sensitivity analyses scored hemoglobin at first encounter, as specified on the form, and restricted the analysis to women with all eight items recorded (complete cases) (Table S4).

Risk groups and clinical utility:

For each risk group, we report the proportion with hysterectomy in both cohorts and, for the cut-offs at 3 and 5 points, the sensitivity, specificity and positive and negative predictive values (Table S5). Decision curve analysis estimated the net benefit of using the development equation to identify women at high risk of hysterectomy, across threshold probabilities of 10% to 40%, compared with treating all or no women as high risk 37, 50, 51 . Estimated blood loss (median and interquartile range [IQR]), intraoperative transfusion and relaparotomy were summarized by risk group in each cohort (Table S6).

AutoScore comparison:

As a secondary aim, the score was compared with a points score built with AutoScore 52 , a framework that generates integer risk scores from routinely collected data. AutoScore was developed in the first imputed development dataset, which was split by folder number into training (60%), validation (20%) and test (20%) sets. The eight items were ranked by random forest variable importance (100 trees), and all eight were retained. Age, parity and hemoglobin were categorized at their 5th, 20th, 80th and 95th percentiles, after which the parity cut-off was set at 1 birth and the hemoglobin cut-off at 9 g/dL on clinical grounds. A logistic regression of hysterectomy on the categorized items in the training set gave coefficients that were converted to integer points, scaled so that the maximum total was 50. The total points were converted to a probability by logistic regression in the development dataset. Results

Participants and development cohort

The development cohort comprised 392 records, of which 124 (31.6%) ended in hysterectomy, and the validation cohort comprised 800 records, of which 169 (21.1%) ended in hysterectomy (Figure 1). Case mix was similar in the two periods (Table 2). The median age was 37 years in both cohorts (IQR, 33-40 and 33-41), most women were nulliparous, and the uterus was palpable at or above the umbilicus in 58.8% of development and 60.2% of validation records. Previous laparotomy (6.4% and 9.1%), past transfusion (13.7% and 18.0%) and preoperative hemoglobin below 10 g/dL (10.6% and 13.3%) were slightly more frequent in the validation cohort. Renal ultrasound was missing for 177 (45.2%) development and 142 (17.8%) validation records, and each other item was missing for less than 7% of records (Table S1).

In the development cohort, the OR for hysterectomy per additional score point was 1.90 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.60, 2.25), giving the development equation with a C-statistic of 0.76 (95% CI: 0.70, 0.80).

There was no evidence of nonlinearity in the score (p = 0.247).

In the eight-item model, older age (adjusted OR [aOR] = 1.20 per year; 95% CI: 1.13, 1.27), a uterus palpable at or above the umbilicus (aOR = 9.62; 95% CI: 1.94, 47.79), previous myomectomy (aOR = 5.84; 95% CI: 1.65, 20.68) and previous laparotomy (aOR = 3.25; 95% CI: 1.20, 8.82) showed the strongest associations with hysterectomy (Table S2).

Validation and updating

In the validation cohort, the development equation retained its discrimination, with a C-statistic of 0.73 (95% CI: 0.69, 0.77), but overestimated risk; the O/E ratio was 0.69 (95% CI: 0.60, 0.78), the calibration intercept was -0.60 (95% CI: -0.79, -0.42) and the calibration slope was 0.83 (95% CI: 0.63, 1.03) (Table 3). The observed hysterectomy proportion was lower than the predicted risk in every score group, from 2.7% observed against 8.4% predicted at scores of 0 to 1 to 46.4% against 84.0% at scores of ≥7 (Figures 3 and 4).

The joint test of the calibration intercept and slope rejected the development equation (p < 0.001), whereas the test of the slope alone did not (p = 0.100), so only the intercept was re-estimated, giving the updated equation logit(𝑝) = -3.619 + 0.641 × score.

Risk groups and clinical utility

The proportion with hysterectomy increased across the three risk groups in both cohorts (Table 1, Figure 3).

In the development cohort, they were 9.1% (95% CI: 5.0, 15.9) in the low-risk group, 33.4% (95% CI: 26.4, 41.2) in the intermediate-risk group and 63.8% (95% CI: 52.3, 73.9) in the high-risk group. In the validation cohort, they were 6.2% (95% CI: 3.8, 9.9), 23.5% (95% CI: 19.5, 28.1) and 42.2% (95% CI: 34.2, 50.6), and the corresponding adjusted risks from the updated equation were 7.7%, 19.8% and 49.3% (Figure 2). About one third of validation records fell in the low-risk group. A score of ≥3 identified 90.3% (95% CI: 84.7, 94.0) of validation hysterectomies, and a score below 3 yielded a negative predictive value of 93.8% (95% CI: 90.1,

A score of ≥5 yielded a specificity of 86.7% (95% CI: 83.8, 89.2) and a positive predictive value of 42.2% (95% CI: 34.2, 50.6) (Table S5).

Using the development equation to identify women at high risk of hysterectomy gave a higher net benefit than treating all or no women as high risk at all threshold probabilities from 10% to 36% (Figure 5); at a threshold of 20%, the net benefit was 0.072, compared with 0.014 for treating all women as high risk. In the validation cohort, median estimated blood loss was 300 mL in the low-risk group, 389 mL in the intermediaterisk group and 329 mL in the high-risk group, and intraoperative transfusion was recorded for 1.8%, 3.7% and 5.6% of women in these groups; relaparotomy was infrequent in all groups (Table S6).

AutoScore comparison and sensitivity analyses

AutoScore yielded a C-statistic of 0.74 (95% CI: 0.69, 0.78) in the validation cohort, which did not differ significantly from that of the EFFAH score (difference 0.01; 95% CI: -0.02, 0.04; p = 0.542). Its calibration was poorer, with a calibration slope of 0.62 (95% CI: 0.47, 0.78) and an O/E ratio of 0.66 (95% CI: 0.58, 0.75) (Table S7, Figure 6).

Scoring hemoglobin at first encounter yielded a C-statistic of 0.73 (95% CI: 0.69, 0.77) and a lower O/E ratio of 0.60 (95% CI: 0.53, 0.69). The complete-case analysis of 602 records gave a C-statistic of 0.72 (95% CI: 0.67, 0.76) and an O/E ratio of 0.67 (95% CI: 0.57, 0.78) (Table S4). With 800 records and 169 hysterectomies, the approximate 95% CI widths were 0.08 for the C-statistic, 0.27 for the O/E ratio and 0.39 for the calibration slope.

Discussion

In a later cohort at the same district hospital, the EFFAH score ranked women counseled for myomectomy by their risk that the operation would end as hysterectomy almost as well as in the cohort in which its equation was fitted, and its three risk groups separated women at low, intermediate and high risk in both periods. What did not transfer in the validation was the absolute level of risk; fewer operations ended in hysterectomy in 2017 to 2019, so the development equation overstated the risk of women at every score, and re-estimating the intercept corrected this on average. A data-driven points score built from the same items with the AutoScore random forest approach discriminated no better and was less well calibrated.

The decreased proportion of women with hysterectomy between the two periods occurred although the validation cohort had, if anything, a slightly less favorable case mix. The shift therefore likely reflects a change in practice more than a change in the women operated on, for example in surgical experience with large fibroid uteri, operative technique or the threshold at which surgeons proceed to hysterectomy, none of which the score captures. Preserved discrimination with shifted calibration in the large is the expected pattern when a model is applied where the outcome frequency differs, and an intercept update is the recommended first correction 36, 53 . In empirical comparisons, it performed as well as more extensive revision with validation samples of this size 54, 55.

Hysterectomy was far more frequent in both cohorts than in the United States series cited in the Introduction 18, 19 . Two features of this study may explain the difference. The outcome in this study was hysterectomy among women counseled for myomectomy with possible hysterectomy, which includes hysterectomies decided during surgery based on operative findings and prior consent, not only hysterectomies necessitated by hemorrhage. The women also presented with large uteri, as in other sub-Saharan series 4,5 , and high hysterectomy frequencies have been reported elsewhere with unfavorable case mix: 6% of repeat abdominal myomectomies for large uteri in the UK 20 and 8.4% of laparoscopic myomectomies in a Korean series 56 . The near-absence of conversion in the Nigerian series cited earlier may reflect their narrower outcome, unplanned conversion during myomectomy, not necessarily a lower underlying risk alone 7, 14, 22.

Published tools most similar in format include points scores for transfusion at myomectomy from a United States registry: one with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.792 and bootstrap internal validation only 24 , and one with a preoperative AUC of 0.69 that its authors judged too low for clinical use 25 . An integer score for conversion of laparoscopic to open myomectomy from a single French center had a C-statistic of 0.87 without validation 26 . Externally validated models for conversion of laparoscopic to open hysterectomy in the UK had AUCs of 0.65 to 0.68 with calibration slopes of 0.97 to 0.98 27 . Recent machine learning classifiers of surgery type in fibroid disease reported hormone-based accuracy of 1.00 in a cohort not restricted to women seeking uterine preservation, without calibration or independent validation 57 . Another bedside score developed in 168 hospitals across Africa for severe postoperative complications, the African Surgical Outcomes Study surgical risk calculator 58 , had an AUC of 0.73 at external validation in Nigeria 59 . The discrimination of the EFFAH score lies within the range of these validated tools, and this study adds the calibration, updating and net benefit assessment that TRIPOD+AI requires 35 . Within this study, AutoScore offered no discriminative advantage, consistent with a systematic review that found no AUC benefit of machine learning over logistic regression in comparisons at low risk of bias 38 , and its poorer calibration in the new period probably reflects point weights estimated from a small development sample.

For counseling, the score turns routinely collected findings into a frequency that a woman can weigh before she consents. Guidance from Canada recommends counseling about the risk of hysterectomy at a planned myomectomy without providing an estimate 16 , guidance from the United States does not address it 17 , and consent guidance recommends presenting surgical risks as frequencies with verbal descriptors 60 . The adjusted risks of the three groups provide such frequencies. A low score supports reassurance, and a high score supports preparation for hysterectomy; because overestimation persisted at the highest scores, where few women contributed, risks at scores of ≥6 are better communicated as high than as precise percentages.

Because calculation requires only addition, the items contributing to a woman's risk are visible to her and to the surgeon.

The same score could serve different needs in different settings. Where resources are limited, women often present with large uteri 4 , myomectomy is usually performed by open surgery, and transfusion is more frequent [5] [6] [7] . In such cases, the score could direct resources before surgery, i.e., readily available cross-matched blood, a senior surgeon or referral could be reserved for women at high risk, while women at low risk, about one third of this cohort, could be operated on with standard preparation. Because it needs no magnetic resonance imaging, hormone assay or additional laboratory test, it can be applied wherever a history, an abdominal examination, a hemoglobin measurement and an abdominopelvic ultrasound are available. In highresource settings, hysterectomy at a planned myomectomy is less frequent but has been described as an underestimated risk 18 . In these settings, the score could give consent a number and inform planning, such as the choice of surgeon or route, although imaging may add information that the score does not capture, and the lower baseline frequency means the intercept would need re-estimation.

At Catholic Hospital, Battor, the updated equation can inform counseling now. Elsewhere, in district hospitals and tertiary centers alike, the intercept should be re-estimated wherever the proportion with hysterectomy differs, and discrimination should be confirmed, because surgeons, case mix and the threshold for hysterectomy vary 61, 62 . The next step is geographic validation in Ghana, the region and high-resource settings, ideally prospective, with the score recorded at the preoperative visit and the intended operation documented. Whether triage guided by the score changes transfusion, referral or the frequency of hysterectomy needs evaluation before it is adopted clinically. Future studies may examine outcomes beyond perioperative complications in women with high EFFAH scores whose operations end as myomectomy instead of hysterectomy.

Strengths and limitations

In terms of strengths, the EFFAH score points were taken from the scoring form and were not re-estimated, and the validation sample size was fixed from the pilot before the validation records were extracted, so the achieved precision can be judged against the criteria proposed for external validation 44, 45 . Calibration was also assessed across the calibration hierarchy 48 and the updating method was selected by closed testing (instead of by inspection) 49.

The study, however, has several limitations. First, this is a single-center temporal validation, and discrimination and calibration in other hospitals, where surgeons, case mix and the threshold for hysterectomy differ, are unknown 61, 62 . Surgeons, operative protocols and blood availability were not recorded for the development cohort, so changes in the setting between the two periods could not be described. Why a woman wished to keep her uterus (whether for future childbearing or for religious or other personal reasons) was not recorded; age and parity reflect it only indirectly, and it may change how readily the surgeon proceeds to hysterectomy. Second, the risk-group cut-offs were informed by the observed proportions, so the proportions within each group are optimistic and the cut-offs at 3 and 5 points require confirmation in new data. Third, the updated equation was evaluated in the cohort used to update it, so its performance estimates are apparent, although bootstrap optimism was small. Fourth, renal ultrasound was missing for almost half of development records and was multiply imputed, even though the complete-case analysis gave similar discrimination. Fifth, AutoScore was trained on about 235 records (60% of one imputed development dataset), and an advantage for machine learning models trained on larger datasets or additional variables cannot be excluded.

Conclusion

Among women with leiomyomas counseled for myomectomy with possible hysterectomy at a district hospital in Ghana, the EFFAH score (an eight-item bedside score built from routine preoperative findings) ranked women by their risk of hysterectomy in a later cohort, and an intercept update aligned its predicted risks with the observed proportion. A data-driven alternative built with AutoScore offered no improvement. The score gives women and surgeons an individual risk estimate to discuss at consent and could guide the use of blood, senior surgeons and referral where these are scarce. Because it requires no imaging beyond ultrasound and no laboratory test beyond hemoglobin, it could become a common basis for counseling in resource-limited and resource-rich settings alike, once its intercept has been re-estimated locally and its performance confirmed in external validation.
