Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

P-Value Text
p > 0.05 Mean parent-minus-child differences were 0.20 for sensory sensitivity, 0.33 for lack of interest, and 0.09 for concern (all p > 0.05).

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 3 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

p = .133 Thus, overall diagnostic agreement was 75.0%, with fair but non-significant agreement beyond chance (κ = 0.31, p = .133; Table 4).
p = .371 This directional imbalance was not statistically significant (McNemar’s χ² = 0.80, p = .371).

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Involvement of persons with lived experiences

Background

Avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID) is a feeding disorder characterized by persistent avoidance or restriction of the amount and/or variety of food intake without weight-or shaperelated psychopathology that defines several other eating disorders (1). The eating disturbance is typically driven by one or more partially overlapping dimensions: sensory sensitivity to food characteristics (e.g., smell, taste, and texture), lack of interest in food or eating, and concern about aversive consequences such as choking, vomiting, or abdominal pain (1, 2). Population-based studies estimate the prevalence of ARFID at approximately 1-2%, with broadly similar rates across sexes (3) (4) (5). Although onset can occur across the lifespan, ARFID commonly begins in childhood (6, 7). Therefore, assessments likely occur in a pediatric context, where multi-informant assessment is the norm. The Pica, ARFID, and Rumination Disorder Interview (PARDI) is a semi-structured clinical interview designed to assess the presence and severity of ARFID and includes separate child and parent versions to support cross-informant assessments (8). Understanding the extent to which child and parent informants agree is particularly important when only one perspective is available, as differences between informants may affect diagnostic classification and estimates of symptom severity. The present study addresses this need by evaluating PARDI cross-informant agreement for ARFID diagnosis, individual diagnostic criteria, ARFID dimensions, and overall severity.

To meet diagnostic criteria for ARFID, restricted food intake must result in at least one clinically significant consequence: weight loss or faltering growth (Criterion A1), nutritional deficiency (A2), dependence on enteral feeding or oral nutritional supplements (A3), or marked interference with psychosocial functioning (A4). These consequences differ in how directly they can be observed, making informant perspective particularly relevant in pediatric assessment. Whereas children have privileged access to internal experiences such as disgust, anxiety, appetite, and interoceptive cues, parents are often better positioned to observe intake, growth, supplement use, and impairment across daily routines. Criterion A4 (psychosocial impairment) may be particularly susceptible to parent-child disagreement, as different aspects of impairment are likely to vary in salience between informants. Children may be more attuned to the immediate subjective impact of their eating difficulties, such as embarrassment and anxiety when eating with peers-experiences that may not be readily apparent to parents. Parents, in turn, may be better positioned to recognize broader patterns of functional impairment, such as reduced participation in everyday activities, disruption of routines, or the need for ongoing accommodation around eating. ARFID cross-informant validation studies are still rare, but the few available studies suggest that parent-child reports are related but not interchangeable (9, 10). In a caregiver-youth study using the Nine Item ARFID Screen (NIAS; 11), caregivers reported higher scores than children across all three subscales, especially for low appetite, and caregiver ratings more often crossed screening thresholds (9). Another study comparing parental and adolescent ratings found no significant differences on any of the NIAS subscales, although some differences emerged at the item level (12).

Using the Eating Disorder Examination (EDE)-ARFID module, Schmidt et al. (10) found high parent-child agreement for diagnosis while agreement varied across individual diagnostic criteria.

Parent-child discordance is not specific to ARFID, with previous studies demonstrating informant differences in child and adolescent eating disorder assessments. Swanson et al. (13), for example, found low concordance between parental and adolescent reports of eating disorder symptoms at ages 14 and 16, with patterns of discordance varying across symptoms. Mariano et al. (14) found that children younger than 13 years reported less severe cognitive eating-disorder symptoms than their parents, whereas adolescents aged 13-18 years generally reported greater symptom severity than their parents. Together, these findings suggest that parent-child agreement can vary depending on the symptoms being assessed, what each informant is able to observe or report, and the child's developmental stage. Patterns of agreement and disagreement can therefore reveal how eating difficulties are captured from child and parent perspectives. The clinical relevance of incorporating multiple perspectives is supported by House et al. (15), who found that a cross-informant assessment showed closer agreement with multidisciplinary clinical ED diagnosis than an individual interview with the young person alone. ARFID assessment instruments, including the PARDI, continue to undergo validation (16). Despite the PARDI's explicit cross-informant design, parent-child agreement for its diagnostic and dimensional ratings has not previously been reported. The present study addresses this gap by comparing parent-child dyad PARDI interviews in 11-14-year-old children screening positive for ARFID in the ARFID InitiativE Sweden (ARIES) cohort. We examined agreement for ARFID diagnosis, diagnostic criteria A1-A4, the three ARFID dimensions, overall severity, and individual PARDI items. Mapping both convergence and disagreement between parent and child ratings may help determine which aspects of ARFID are consistently captured across informants, when combining perspectives provides clinically useful additional information, and may help inform effective treatment planning.

Method

Study design and participants

Participants were recruited from ARFID InitiativE Sweden (ARIES), a Swedish cohort study investigating genetic and environmental factors associated with ARFID in children aged 6-14 years (17). Participants were recruited via social media, healthcare services, school health services, and advocacy organizations. Enrollment occurred between May 2024 and February 2026. Parents completed online screening questionnaires and additional measures concerning eating behavior, health, neurodevelopmental and psychiatric conditions, quality of life, and family characteristics. ARIES screening cases were required to (1) screen positive for ARFID on either the parent-report version of PARDI-ARFID Questionnaire (PARDI-AR-Q; 18) or the parent-report version of NIAS, (2) screen negative for clinically significant non-ARFID eating disorder symptoms on the Parent Eating Disorder Examination Questionnaire (PEDE-Q; 19), and have no lifetime diagnosis of anorexia nervosa or bulimia nervosa (Table 1). This approach has demonstrated good diagnostic performance in accurately identifying probable ARFID and excluding non-cases when evaluated against the PARDI (20).

For the present study, children aged 11-14 years who had been classified as ARIES screening cases were eligible. At the time of sampling, 451 children in this age range met the screening case requirement. To ensure that screening and interview assessments reflected a similar time period, invitations were sent no later than seven weeks after parents had consented to participation in ARIES.

Sixty-six parent-child pairs were invited, with invitations stratified by sex to obtain a broadly balanced distribution (37 boys and 29 girls). Twenty-nine pairs (44%) initially agreed to participate. Four children withdrew after their parent had been interviewed, two pairs were excluded because the interviews could not be completed as required, two pairs cancelled before interview, and one pair did not respond to scheduling attempts. Twenty pairs (69%) completed both interviews and constituted the analytic sample (Figure 1).

Measures

The Pica, ARFID, and Rumination Disorder Interview (PARDI) is a semi-structured, cross-informant interview developed to assess pica, ARFID, and rumination disorder (8, 21). Age-appropriate child and parent versions include screening for competing eating disorders and relevant medical or psychiatric conditions, followed by diagnostic and dimensional assessments. Items are answered dichotomously or rated on a 0-6 scale. The ARFID section yields dimensional mean scores for sensory sensitivity, lack of interest in food or eating, and concern about aversive consequences, together with an overall severity score, where higher scores indicate greater symptom severity.

Based on DSM-5 criteria, an ARFID diagnosis requires (1) presence of avoidant or restrictive eating, (2) presence of at least one consequence under Criteria A1-A4, (3) duration exceeding one month, and (4) fulfillment of exclusion requirements: the eating disturbance must not be better explained by (1) lack of available food or a culturally sanctioned practice (Criterion B) or (2) anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, or related weight-or shape psychopathology (Criterion C), and, when a medical, neurodevelopmental, or other mental condition is present, the eating disturbance exceeds what would ordinarily be expected from that condition and warrants additional clinical attention (Criterion D).

Procedure

Child and parent interviews were conducted separately by video conference between April and November 2025 and were recorded for quality assurance. The interviewer group comprised one doctoral student, two medical students, and two undergraduate psychology students. All interviewers completed six hours of training that included an introduction to ARFID and the PARDI, independent rating of a recorded interview, and group discussion of the training interview ratings. After the training, all interviewers completed a supervised interview that was subsequently reviewed with the study team. Difficult ratings were reviewed in bi-weekly consensus meetings throughout data collection. Different interviewers assessed the child and parent in each pair to reduce direct carryover of information. Parent interviewers were blinded to ARIES screening status and did not have access to questionnaire data during interview administration or scoring. As only children who screened positive for ARFID were interviewed, child interviewers were aware of their screening status. Current height and weight of the child were reported by both the parent and the child, and parent-child reported values were highly correlated (r = .99) for both height and weight. Sex-and age-adjusted standard deviation scores (SDS) were calculated for parent-reported height, weight, and body mass index (BMI) using Swedish age-and sex-specific reference norms (22, 23). Pairs where only one member could provide current anthropometric information (i.e., either the child or the parent; n=6) were excluded from analyses specific to Criterion A1.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed in R version 4.5.0, with statistical significance set at p < .05. Crossinformant agreement for ARFID diagnosis and Criteria A1-A4 (categorical ratings) was quantified using percentage agreement and Cohen's kappa (κ). κ values were interpreted as slight (0.00-0.20), fair (0.21-0.40), moderate (0.41-0.60), substantial (0.61-0.80), or almost perfect (0.81-1.00) (24).

McNemar's tests assessed whether parent-child disagreements showed a systematic directional pattern, such as parents endorsing a criterion more often than children when the two informants disagreed (25). Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) with 95% confidence intervals were used for sensory sensitivity, lack of interest, concern, and overall severity (continuous ratings). ICCs below 0.50 were interpreted as poor, 0.50-0.75 as moderate, 0.75-0.90 as good, and above 0.90 as excellent (26). Paired t-tests examined mean differences between parent and child scores. At the item level, mean parent-minus-child differences assessed the direction and magnitude of rating discrepancies, with positive values indicating higher parental ratings and negative values higher child ratings.

Spearman's rho assessed rank-order correspondence, that is, the degree to which higher parental ratings were associated with higher child ratings across dyads (27). Interrater reliability was evaluated with Cohen's kappa for categorical ratings and ICCs for continuous scores. There were no missing data, as all PARDI diagnostic ratings were completed during the interview.

Interrater reliability of interviewers

During the initial phase of data collection, 23 interviews (17 parent and six child interviews) were independently double rated to evaluate interviewer reliability. Interrater reliability estimates were calculated using interviewers' original independent ratings prior to consensus discussions. Diagnostic classifications used in subsequent analyses were based on consensus ratings after discrepant cases had been reviewed and resolved. Reliability between interviewers was moderate to excellent. Agreement for ARFID diagnosis was 91.3% (κ = 0.80, p < .0001). Agreement was 82.6% for Criterion A1 (κ=0.55, p=0.0085), 100% for Criterion A2 (κ=1.00, p<0.0001), 95.7% for Criterion A3 (κ=0.83, p<0.0001), and 82.6% for Criterion A4 (κ=0.62, p=0.0031). Agreement for the exclusion Criteria B-D was 100%. ICCs indicated excellent reliability for Sensory Sensitivity (ICC=0.89, 95% CI [0.77, 0.95]), Lack of Interest (ICC=0.94, 95% CI [0.87, 0.98]), and Severity (ICC=0.96, 95% CI [0.92, 0.98]), and good reliability for Concern of aversive consequences (ICC=0.77, 95% CI [0.54, 0.90]).

Parents received written study information and provided written consent in the ARIES study and oral consent in the sub-study including PARDI interviews. Children received ageadapted information emphasizing that participation was voluntary and provided oral assent before the interview. Recordings were stored on a password-protected SharePoint site accessible only to the research team and are scheduled for deletion six months after study completion.

Results

Participant characteristics

The final sample comprised 20 parent-child pairs (13 girls and 7 boys). Given the small sample, no sex-stratified analyses were undertaken. Mean age at the parent interview was 13.4 years (range 11.6-14.9). On average, the parent interview occurred seven days after the child interview. Parent-reported mean SDS for weight, height, and BMI were -0.6, 0.1, and -0.9, respectively (Table 2). For 18 of the 20 children, the same parent completed the ARIES screening and the PARDI interview. For one child, a different parent participated in the PARDI interview, and for another, both parents had completed the ARIES screening together, although only one was interviewed. Nineteen parent interviews were completed by the mother and one by the father. Parent-reported co-occurring conditions included attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (n = 7), constipation (n = 5), autism (n = 3), asthma (n = 3), and anxiety disorders (n = 3).

Agreement for ARFID dimensions and overall severity

Parents' mean scores for all three ARFID dimensions and overall severity were higher than children's, however only the difference in overall severity was statistically significant (DM = 0.42, p = .003; Table 3). Mean parent-minus-child differences were 0.20 for sensory sensitivity, 0.33 for lack of interest, and 0.09 for concern (all p > 0.05). Cross-informant agreement differed markedly by domain.

Agreement was poor for sensory sensitivity (ICC = 0.42, 95% CI [-0.02, 0.72]), moderate for lack of interest (ICC = 0.63, 95% CI [0.29, 0.83]), excellent for concern (ICC = 0.95, 95% CI [0.87, 0.98]), and moderate for overall severity (ICC = 0.71, 95% CI [0.25, 0.89]; Table 3 & Figure 2). The wide confidence intervals for sensory sensitivity, lack of interest, and overall severity indicated substantial uncertainty around the point estimates.

Agreement for ARFID diagnosis and individual diagnostic criteria

ARFID diagnostic criteria were met in 17 parent interviews (85.0%) and 14 child interviews (70.0%).

Informants agreed on an ARFID diagnosis in 13 pairs (65.0%) and on no diagnosis in two pairs (10.0%); five pairs were discordant (25.0%; Figure 3). Thus, overall diagnostic agreement was 75.0%, with fair but non-significant agreement beyond chance (κ = 0.31, p = .133; Table 4). Among the five discordant pairs, four were classified as ARFID based on the parent interview but not the child interview, whereas one pair showed the opposite pattern. All five diagnostic discrepancies corresponded to disagreement on Criterion A4 (psychosocial impairment): A4 was met in the parent but not the child interview in the four parent-positive/child-negative pairs, and in the child but not the parent interview in the remaining pair. This directional imbalance was not statistically significant (McNemar's χ² = 0.80, p = .371). Age distributions were broadly similar between concordant and discordant pairs. Agreement was higher for the more readily observable consequences. Criterion A1 was met in four parent and four child interviews, with perfect classification agreement (100%; κ = 1.00). Criterion A2 was met in one parent and two child interviews, yielding 95% agreement (κ = 0.64). Criterion A3 was met in five parent and four child interviews, with 95% agreement (κ = 0.86). In contrast, Criterion A4 was met in 17 parent interviews and 12 child interviews, resulting in 65% agreement (κ = 0.19)-the lowest of the four consequences. McNemar's tests were not statistically significant for Criteria A2, A3, or A4.

Item-level agreement

Across the 54 PARDI items, parents gave higher mean ratings on 35 items (64.8%), children gave higher ratings on 15 (27.8%), and ratings were equal on four (7.4%; Figure 4). The overall mean parent-minus-child difference on item ratings (scale 0-6) was 0.24 (SD = 0.49, range: -0.89-1.45) and differed significantly from zero (t(53) = 3.57, p < .001), indicating an overall tendency toward higher parental ratings across items. Although parental ratings were generally higher, the direction of informant differences varied across individual PARDI items. The largest differences in the direction of higher parental ratings were observed for difficulty meeting calorie needs (item 33), impact of eating difficulties on family relationships and everyday family life (item 40), difficulties at home involving arguments or conflict with family members (item 41), stressful mealtimes (item 42), and needing prompting or reminding to eat (item 64). The largest differences in the direction of higher child ratings were observed for restricted intake of vegetables, dairy products, and carbohydrates (items 29b, 29d, and 29e), recent loss of interest in food (item 31), reliance on distraction during meals (item 44), discomfort with messy or sticky foods (item 50), difficulty recognizing hunger cues (item 60), and restriction due to fear of abdominal pain (item 74b).

Rank-order correspondence between parent and child ratings varied substantially, with Spearman's ρ ranging from -0.25 for strong temperature preferences (item 54) to 1.00 for avoidance related to feared consequences such as vomiting, choking, allergic reactions, or pain (item 79), with a median of 0.47. High correlations were observed for recent weight stagnation (item 34; ρ = .89) and reliance on oral nutritional or high-energy drinks (item 38; ρ = .92), as well as fears of vomiting (item 75; ρ = .99), choking (item 76; ρ = .88), and allergic reactions (item 77; ρ = .91). In contrast, correspondence was weaker for trying new foods (item 32; ρ = .05), distraction during meals (item 44; ρ = .04), encouragement to eat (item 46; ρ = .01), food-color-based avoidance (item 58; ρ = -.18), and eating less during positive emotional states (item 66; ρ = .15). Taken together, findings indicate that the degree of parent-child correspondence depends strongly on the specific symptom being assessed.

Discussion

This study evaluated parent-child agreement on the PARDI in children aged 11-14 years from the ARIES cohort. Diagnostic classifications were often concordant, while agreement across individual criteria ranged from very high for several nutritional and physical consequences to substantially lower for psychosocial impairment. Among the ARFID dimensions, agreement was strongest for concern about aversive consequences and weakest for sensory sensitivity; parents also rated overall severity significantly higher than children. At the item level, parents generally gave higher ratings, although both the direction of rating differences and rank-order correspondence varied across individual items.

Together, these findings indicate that parent and child PARDI interviews capture overlapping but non-equivalent aspects of ARFID.

Despite the overall diagnostic concordance, five of the 20 (25.0%) parent-child pairs were not concordant. Four were classified as ARFID based on the parent interview but not the child interview, while the reverse occurred in one pair. These discordant cases indicate that differences in crossinformant perspectives can affect diagnostic outcome.

Looking beyond the overall diagnosis, agreement differed markedly across the individual diagnostic criteria. Parent-child agreement was high for weight or growth, nutritional deficiency, and dependence on nutritional supplementation (Criteria A1-A3), but substantially lower for psychosocial impairment (Criterion A4). This pattern may reflect differences in how readily these consequences can be observed or documented. Information on growth, nutritional deficiency, and nutritional support may be available from measurements, healthcare contacts, or established treatment routines, whereas psychosocial impairment is more dependent on context and perspective. Parents may be more aware of how the child's eating difficulties affect shared meals, eating outside the home, or family routines, whereas children may be more aware of anxiety or embarrassment that parents do not experience firsthand. Children may also minimize the psychosocial impact/consequences of their eating difficulties, including difficulties in interpersonal or social functioning, describing the consequences as less severe or disruptive, which could contribute to disagreement when parents report greater impairment. The lower agreement for A4 may therefore reflect differences in how psychosocial impairment is experienced, observed, or reported across family, mealtime, and social contexts.

Agreement also varied across the three ARFID dimensions. Agreement for concern about aversive consequences was excellent, contrasting with poorer agreement for sensory sensitivity and lack of interest. The excellent agreement for concern should, however, be interpreted with care. Concern scores were low for most participants, and clustering near zero can produce apparent concordance when both informants consistently report absence of the construct. When concern was present, however, items describing specific feared outcomes also showed very high rank-order correspondence. Fear-related episodes may be salient, discrete, and readily communicated within families, whereas sensory dislike and diminished appetite may be persistent but less easily observed or verbalized.

One clear difference between informants emerged for overall ARFID severity, which parents rated significantly higher than children. Although parent means were also numerically higher across ARFID dimensions, none of these differences was statistically significant. The higher parent-rated severity may partly reflect aspects of the eating difficulties that are particularly apparent to parents in everyday life. Several of the more pronounced parent-higher item differences concerned difficulty meeting nutritional needs, stressful mealtimes, and the impact of eating difficulties on home and family life. Item-level analyses provided a more detailed picture of these informant differences. Although parents reported higher mean ratings on most items, children reported higher ratings on some items, including restriction of particular food groups, dropping previously liked foods, reliance on distraction during meals, discomfort with messy or sticky foods, difficulty recognizing hunger cues, and restriction because of fear of abdominal pain. For some of these items, such discrepancies may become even more likely as children move into adolescence and gain greater independence around eating. More meals may then occur outside the family home, reducing opportunities for direct parental observation.

Rank-order correspondence also varied substantially across items. Parent and child ratings corresponded closely for recent lack of expected weight gain, reliance on oral nutritional or highenergy drinks, and specific fears of vomiting, choking, and allergic reactions, whereas correspondence was weak for willingness to try new foods, distraction during meals, encouragement to eat, food-color-based avoidance, and eating less during positive emotional states. Together, these findings indicate that both the direction of informant differences and the degree of parent-child correspondence vary considerably across individual PARDI items.

The present findings are broadly consistent with previous cross-informant research in ARFID. Using the NIAS, Carmody et al. (9) found that caregivers generally reported greater ARFID symptom severity than their children, resembling the overall parent-higher pattern observed in the present study. Barnhart et al. (12) found a similar but non-significant pattern across the NIAS subscales.

Validation of the EDE-ARFID module similarly showed that parent-child agreement varied across individual diagnostic criteria, although overall diagnostic agreement was substantially higher than in the present study (75%): 94% for the child-parent comparison and 96% for the adolescent-parent comparison (10). The reasons for this difference are not entirely clear. One possible explanation lies in sample composition. Their cohort included non-treatment-seeking controls, none of whom met criteria for current ARFID, whereas our sample consisted entirely of screen-positive participants. This may have increased the likelihood of concordant negative classifications in their sample, while different parent and child accounts in our sample had greater potential to result in different diagnostic classifications. Taken together, these findings reinforce the central point that even though child and parent assessments of ARFID overlap, considering both perspectives can uncover meaningful differences that either assessment alone may miss.

Strengths and limitations

The current study has several strengths. It addresses the previously unexamined cross-informant agreement across diagnostic, dimensional, and item-level outcomes of the PARDI. Parent and child interviews were conducted separately by different interviewers, reducing direct carryover of information between assessments. Recurring consensus meetings supported standardized scoring, and double ratings showed moderate to excellent interviewer reliability across outcomes.

The principal limitation is the small sample size, which reduced statistical power and produced wide confidence intervals around several agreement estimates. In particular, the study had limited power to detect systematic directional differences in discordant diagnostic classifications. The relatively small sample size also prevented us from investigating potential moderators of parent-child agreement such as child sex, autistic traits, or anxiety traits. Furthermore, the sample was restricted to children aged 11-14 years who had screened positive for ARFID in a large-scale epidemiological study, limiting generalizability to other age groups and populations, such as treatment-seeking or clinically diagnosed individuals. Importantly, screening status in the ARIES study was determined using parent-report questionnaires, which may have preferentially selected for parent-visible forms of ARFID, potentially influencing the parent-child agreement patterns observed in this study. Anthropometric information was reported rather than directly measured, which may have reduced the precision of the information used to assess Criterion A1. Further, the observed agreement patterns may differ in larger and more diverse samples, for example, among younger children (<11 years) or adolescents >14 years, clinically diagnosed or treatment-seeking individuals, screen-negative participants, or samples with a more balanced representation of mothers and fathers as parent informants. These findings should therefore be regarded as preliminary estimates rather than fixed properties of the PARDI.

Clinical and research implications

The findings support a cross-informant approach when the PARDI is used with children. When child and parent accounts differ, the PARDI provides an opportunity to clarify how the child's functioning is affected across different contexts and what each informant has observed. If only one interview is possible, clinicians should be aware that ratings may vary depending on the informant, particularly for severity and psychosocial impairment.

Importantly, the present study compared child and parent PARDI interviews rather than treating either reporter as the reference against which the other was judged. For some aspects of ARFID, one informant may have access to more accurate or readily verifiable information, whereas for others, differences may reflect genuinely different experiences or observations. Further, there was no separate clinical assessment integrating both accounts with anthropometric, medical, and functional information. Higher parental ratings should therefore not be interpreted as parent over-reporting, nor lower child ratings as child under-reporting. Instead, these discrepancies may identify areas that require further clarification during assessment. This may be particularly relevant for psychosocial impairment, where parent-child agreement was lowest and where considering both perspectives may therefore be especially informative during assessment.

Larger studies should estimate agreement across age groups, ARFID dimensions, sexes, cultural contexts, and clinical settings. They should include multiple caregivers where possible, document which caregiver is most involved in meals, and incorporate direct anthropometric measurement, relevant medical records, and clinician consensus. For epidemiological and treatment research, the choice of informant may also affect case definition, baseline severity, and apparent change over time.

Studies should prespecify whether diagnosis is based on parent report, child report, or clinical integration, and should use the same approach at follow-up. Reporting both informant-specific and integrated results would make findings more comparable and help determine whether discrepancies themselves carry prognostic information.

Conclusion

Parent and child PARDI interviews showed meaningful but incomplete agreement in children screening positive for ARFID on parent-reported questionnaires. Concordance was strongest for more readily observable consequences and concern about aversive outcomes, whereas sensory sensitivity, lack of interest in food or eating, overall diagnosis, and especially psychosocial impairment were more informant-dependent. Parents generally reported greater overall severity, whereas children endorsed some specific eating experiences more strongly, such as difficulty recognizing hunger cues, discomfort with messy or sticky foods, and fear of abdominal pain. These findings support integrating multiple perspectives in pediatric ARFID assessment and provide a basis for larger studies of the PARDI's cross-informant validity.
