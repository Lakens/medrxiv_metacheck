Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Medical large language models have shown promise in medical knowledge retrieval and clinical reasoning but remain limited in quantitative reasoning over longitudinal health data, partly due to the lack of effective data construction and model training strategies tailored to large-scale prospective cohorts. Here, we present HealthFound, a health world model trained on 12.4 million constructed examples, including 14-year longitudinal biomedical records from 501,936 participants and datasets for medical knowledge and general reasoning. We propose a three-stage training framework comprising progressive curriculum supervised fine-tuning, rejectionsampled retention fine-tuning, and task-verifiable policy optimization. This enables the model to effectively parse heterogeneous quantitative data and perform robust quantitative reasoning about incident diseases and health trajectories. HealthFound achieves the highest mean accuracy across eight public medical benchmarks. On the task of predicting 1,074 future disease onsets, it outperforms the closest large language model by approximately 10 percentage points on average. In two independent external validations (MIMIC-IV and NHANES), it demonstrates superior zero-shot generalization, achieving improvements of over 5.2% across 417 disease endpoints. The model also achieves top results on tasks involving literature-derived question answering and quantitative indicator imputation. Furthermore, HealthFound generates interpretable reasoning rationales that are consistent with established biomedical knowledge. Collectively, our results demonstrate that HealthFound enables medical LLMs to evolve from static knowledge repositories into dynamic quantitative reasoning engines over longitudinal health data.

Introduction

Medical large language models (LLMs) have made promising progress on medical examinations, clinical question answering and other forms of clinical reasoning 1,2 . This progress is seen in both general-purpose LLMs and models adapted to medical specialties 3,4 . Much of their competence stems from pretraining or fine-tuning on biomedical literature 5 , clinical notes 6 and medical instruction data 7, 8 . These data are well suited for learning medical facts, interpreting clinical vignettes 9 and producing plausible explanations 3,4,10 . However, several critical gaps remain. A model may know how diabetes or heart failure is described, but this knowledge does not establish whether it can interpret an individual's complex routine measurements-such as vital signs, blood tests or imaging results-and reliably infer later disease outcomes 11 . It also remains unclear how reliably such models can infer an unobserved measurement from the rest of a health profile. Beyond individual disease predictions, another challenge is whether these models can anticipate how a person's health may evolve, including which clinical events might occur and when.

These limitations reflect the nature of the training signal. Biomedical literature, clinical notes and examination questions are predominantly qualitative and descriptive. They encode concepts and clinical decisions, but provide relatively little supervision over how measurements covary across individuals, how they change over time, or how pre-disease profiles relate to later outcomes. We posit that repeated quantitative observations linked to subsequent outcomes 12 could supply this missing signal, allowing a language model to learn the statistical structure underlying evolving human health states 13, 14 . This provides a foundation for a health world model that learns relationships between past observations, current health states and future outcomes. Such a model could use these relationships to infer missing measurements, predict disease risk and generate possible future health-event trajectories, including their estimated timing.

Large-scale prospective population cohorts offer such an opportunity 14, 15 , yet enabling models to naturally learn from cohort data has remained a formidable challenge. A typical large cohort combines demographics, lifestyle exposures, physiological measurements, laboratory biomarkers, molecular profiles, imaging-derived phenotypes and linked longitudinal outcomes 14, 16 for tens to hundreds of thousands of participants 14, 15 . These data are predominantly numerical, longitudinal and participant-linked-not in the textual form that language models are designed to process. Machine-learning methods have also been applied to large electronic health record datasets 11, 17 . Shared EHR foundation models have also been adapted across hospitals 18 . Crucially, however, LLMs already encode rich biomedical knowledge from their pretraining. We therefore hypothesize that a continuedlearning strategy, which enables LLMs to integrate textual biomedical knowledge with prospective cohort data, can substantially enhance model performance across multiple dimensions.

Two recent lines of work show that longitudinal health records can be modelled directly 19 . A generative transformer trained de novo on sequences of coded health events reproduces the rates of more than 1,000 diseases in UK Biobank and transfers to the Danish national registries 20 , and semantic embeddings of medical concepts improve the portability of dedicated risk models across coding systems and healthcare systems 21 . GRASP uses fixed embeddings of medical concepts in a downstream risk model 21 , whereas other work encodes whole patient records and also evaluates model fine-tuning 22 . These studies focus on clinical prediction and do not assess the combination of cohort-based quantitative reasoning and broad medical question answering. We ask whether a medical language model that already encodes biomedical knowledge from text pretraining can additionally absorb quantitative supervision from a prospective cohort without losing the competence with which it began.

Nevertheless, learning from raw cohort data presents several practical challenges. First, tens of thousands of coded clinical fields must be converted into readable sequences 22 without loss of numerical values, units or the temporal ordering of repeated observations 23 , and the model must learn to interpret these elements. Second, prospective cohort data are participant-centric, requiring the model to understand what each piece of information represents and when it was recorded in order to support prospective reasoning 11,17 . Third, extensive training on cohort data risks eroding the biomedical knowledge already acquired during pretraining, potentially diminishing previously established capabilities 24.

To address these challenges, we designed a framework for prospective-cohort learning in biomedical LLMs and developed HealthFound as a health world model capable of reasoning over observed health states, inferring missing information and predicting future health events within a unified framework. This framework is specifically designed to enable biomedical LLMs to learn from complex quantitative longitudinal data. Our contributions span data construction, training strategy and evaluation. For data, we converted records from 501,936 UK Biobank participants 14 into readable longitudinal health profiles through semantic mapping and temporal alignment, supplemented by an additional 7.4 million medical and general reasoning examples to provide broader knowledge and instruction-following supervision. To integrate longitudinal cohort data without eroding existing medical knowledge, we designed a three-stage training framework. A curriculum stage progressively introduces cohort tasks while interleaving medical reasoning data. A rejection-sampled retention stage identifies and rehearses weakened abilities, effectively repairing forgotten knowledge. Finally, a policy optimisation stage uses verifiable rewards to strengthen quantitative reasoning, anchored to the retained model to prevent capability drift 25 . We used multiple benchmarks to evaluate model performance. These include eight public medical benchmarks for assessing general medical competence, as well as tasks for literature-derived question answering, quantitative indicator imputation and future disease prediction across over 1,000 endpoints. We further tested the zero-shot generalizability of HealthFound on two independent datasets-MIMIC-IV and NHANES 26,27 -which represent hospital records and population-survey measurements, respectively. This provides a rigorous assessment of transferability across diverse real-world settings.

Results

Study design and evaluation framework

Figure 1 summarizes the study design, from data construction to evaluation. The cohort data comprised records from 501,936 UK Biobank participants, converted into readable longitudinal health profiles through semantic mapping and temporal alignment. These included approximately 3.8 million disease-prediction examples across 1,074 endpoints, 944,000 natural-language health records, 186,000 quantitative indicator-inference examples and 72,000 scientific question-answer examples. We additionally constructed 7.4 million medical and general reasoning examples. The training mixture totalled approximately 12.4 million examples (Fig. 1a).

HealthFound was trained through three stages (Fig. 1b). First, curriculum supervised fine-tuning (SFT) gradually increased the proportion of cohort-based training examples while retaining medical and general reasoning data, enabling the model to learn from longitudinal health records. Second, rejection-sampled retention fine-tuning (RFT) generated candidate responses to disease prediction task, selected those passing correctness and formatting checks, and used them alongside the original answers to preserve the model's existing reasoning abilities. Finally, HealthFound Policy Optimization (HFPO) improved quantitative reasoning ability using task-specific rewards for answer accuracy and consistency, while limiting divergence from the RFT model and continuing to reinforce general reasoning abilities.

We evaluated HealthFound across several downstream tasks (Fig. 1c-f). Using UK Biobank data, we assessed disease prediction across 1,074 endpoints using only pre-onset health records, generation of future health-event trajectories, and inference of missing biomedical measurements. We further evaluated medical knowledge and reasoning using eight public medical benchmarks and literature-derived question answering. To explore what the model had learned, we compared disease representations with those of the unadapted backbone and examined how masking clinical features affected prediction accuracy. Finally, we assessed zero-shot generalization on two external datasets: MIMIC-IV (408 incident disease endpoints) and NHANES (nine prevalent-disease tasks).

Predicting future disease onset across 1,074 endpoints

We evaluated disease classification across 1,074 UK Biobank endpoints 14, 15 using health records truncated before the first recorded diagnosis (Fig. 2). The interval from the end of the input record to diagnosis had a median of 6.5 years and a maximum of 16.9 years. All models received the same health profiles and returned high-risk or low-risk predictions. Accuracy, positive-class F1, sensitivity and specificity were evaluated for each endpoint.

Compared with general-purpose LLMs, HealthFound achieved the highest mean accuracy in every clinical chapter (Fig. 2a). Its overall mean accuracy was approximately 68.9%. Relative to the strongest comparator within most chapters, the mean advantage was 10.0 percentage points and ranged from 7.9 to 11.5 percentage points. This advantage was observed across endocrine/metabolic, cardiovascular, genitourinary, respiratory, musculoskeletal, mental, neurological, digestive, ophthalmic and neoplastic disease groups.

A total of 412 of the 1,074 endpoints reached an accuracy of at least 70% with HealthFound, compared with 347 for XGBoost, 193 for Delphi-2M, 40 for Gemini-3-Flash, 34 for DeepSeek-V3.2, 35 for GPT-5.4, 24 for Qwen3, 14 for MedGemma-27B, and none for MiniMax-M2.7. In representative comparisons with GPT-5.4, accuracy was 78.01% versus 72.50% for heart failure and 84.46% versus 79.00% for diabetic neuropathy (Fig. 2b). Differences were also observed among respiratory, genitourinary, neurological and ophthalmic endpoints, consistent with the broad advantage observed across clinical chapters.

Comparisons with publicly available medical LLMs tested whether this advantage extended to domain-specialized models. HealthFound achieved the highest overall disease-prediction accuracy, exceeding the next-best medical LLM, MedGemma-27B, by 11.8 percentage points (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Table 1). Comparisons with Delphi-2M, XGBoost and logistic regression also favoured HealthFound, with an advantage of 5.9, 2.3 and 7.3 percentage points (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Table 2). We further evaluated 23 low-prevalence diseases at observed case proportions of 0.05%-5%. HealthFound retained the highest overall performance in this comparison. (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 2)

Chapter-level positive-case detection rates were generally lower among cases with longer intervals between the input cut-off and the first recorded diagnosis (Fig. 2c). Across clinical chapters, rates were approximately 0.50-0.74 in the 0-2-year interval, compared with 0.20-0.40 in the 12-14-year interval. Fig. 2d summarizes trajectory recall across models. HealthFound used a mean of 138.6 reasoning tokens per prediction, fewer than DeepSeek-V3.2 (985.6), Qwen3 (806.7) and MiniMax-M2.7 (775.9), but more than GPT-5.4 and Gemini-3-Flash (Fig. 2e). The performance advantage was therefore not associated with longer generated reasoning traces.

Predicting longitudinal health-event trajectories

To evaluate whether HealthFound could predict how an individual's health might evolve over time, we compared it with Delphi-2M and other LLMs on longitudinal health-event generation (Fig. 2d). We selected 10,000 UK Biobank participants and used their health records before a defined prediction cut-off to generate future trajectories. Each trajectory consisted of predicted disease events and their estimated occurrence times. The evaluation included 77,564 recorded events after the cut-off, covering newly occurring inpatient diagnoses and death over a maximum follow-up window of 17 years. We evaluated each model using eight valid trajectories per participant, matching the sampling count reported for HealthFlux 28 . We assessed performance using the recall of event recovery, which measured the proportion recovered in at least one of the eight trajectories. HealthFound achieved a recall of 26.1%, compared to 13.3% for Delphi-2M and 24.5% for DeepSeek-V3.2 (Fig. 2d).

Inferring missing quantitative indicators from health profiles

We evaluated whether HealthFound could infer missing quantitative measurements from the remaining information in a health profile. Using approximately 1,900 held-out examples, we removed one observed measurement from each profile and asked the models to predict its value (Fig. 3a). Prediction accuracy was evaluated at relative-error tolerances ranging from 0.1% to 5%.

HealthFound consistently achieved the highest accuracy among the evaluated language models across all tolerance thresholds. At the primary 3% tolerance, HealthFound achieved an accuracy of 59.16%, compared with 53.23% for GPT-5.4, an improvement of 5.93 percentage points. At the stricter 1% tolerance, accuracies were 52.32% and 46.93%, respectively. At the 5% tolerance, they increased to 63.96% and 57.07%, corresponding to a 5.66percentage-point advantage for HealthFound (Supplementary Table 3).

We further compared HealthFound with conventional statistical approaches, including mean imputation and multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE). HealthFound achieved substantially higher accuracy than both methods across the evaluated tolerance thresholds (Supplementary Fig. 3). Together, these results demonstrate HealthFound's ability to infer missing quantitative measurements from complex health profiles, while highlighting differences in performance across indicator types.

Retention of general medical competence on public benchmarks

To assess medical competence after cohort specialization, HealthFound was evaluated on eight independent public benchmarks spanning medical examinations, clinical reasoning, literature comprehension and specialized medical knowledge (Fig. 3b-d).

HealthFound reached a mean accuracy of 84.70%, 7.23 percentage points above the next-best aggregate result of 77.47% from Kimi-k2.5 (Fig. 3b). The model ranked first on seven of the eight individual benchmarks. It scored 95.76% on MedQA, 94.48% on Medbullets-opt4, 93.18% on Medbullets-opt5, 88.94% on MedExQA and 43.55% on MedXpertQA; on MMLU-Med, Kimi-k2.5 scored 91.50% compared with 89.10% for HealthFound (Fig. 3d). These results show that quantitative specialization was compatible with broad medical benchmark performance.

Training stages support complementary capabilities

The sequential-stage evaluation demonstrated distinct contributions from each training stage (Supplementary Table 4). Curriculum supervised fine-tuning (SFT) increased UK Biobank disease-prediction accuracy from 57.1% to 61.9% and mean medical-benchmark accuracy from 66.3% to 80.0%, indicating improvements in both cohortbased prediction and medical reasoning. Rejection-sampled retention fine-tuning (RFT) further improved medical-benchmark accuracy to 84.3%, while UK Biobank accuracy increased to 62.8%. Finally, HealthFound Policy Optimization (HFPO) substantially improved disease-prediction accuracy to 68.9%, while maintaining medical-benchmark accuracy at 84.7%. Across the three stages, quantitative indicator-inference accuracy also increased progressively from 37.1% to 49.3%, while ROUGE-L F1 for literature-derived question answering improved from 0.0464 to 0.0814. These results suggest that the three stages contributed complementary improvements in quantitative prediction and medical reasoning.

To examine whether these gains resulted from increased training-data volume alone, we compared alternative configurations using matched data volumes (Supplementary Table 4). Standard UK Biobank SFT achieved 58.1% disease-prediction accuracy, while conventional SFT using data from all three stages reached 60.0%. Applying conventional GRPO after sequential SFT and RFT reduced prediction accuracy on UKB from 62.8% to 61.4%, suggesting that standard GRPO is not well suited to our disease-prediction task and may even degrade predictive performance, whereas our proposed HFPO provides a more effective optimization strategy. In addition, we compared SFT followed by Reply with SFT followed by RFT. The inferior performance of the former supports the effectiveness of our RFT design. These findings suggest that the sequential training strategy and task-specific optimization contributed to performance improvements beyond those attributable to training-data volume alone.

Literature-derived question answering

We evaluated answers to 3,574 held-out questions derived from biomedical publications. The questions covered basic and clinical medicine, biostatistics, bioinformatics and medical research methods. Each model answered the same questions under a common prompt, and its responses were compared with reference answers using BLEU, ROUGE and token-level precision. These measures quantify agreement with the reference wording rather than clinical correctness. Among the models shown in Fig. 3e, HealthFound achieved the highest mean scores on the four displayed lexical-overlap metrics, including mean ROUGE-L F1 of approximately 0.081, token-level F1 of 0.081, precision of 0.048 and BLEU-4 of approximately 0.001 (Fig. 3e).

Since lexical-overlap metrics do not directly assess scientific correctness, we therefore evaluated answer correctness separately using three independent LLM judges. HealthFound received the highest mean answercorrectness score (5.4/10, Supplementary Table 5), exceeding the next-best model by approximately 9.25% and supporting stronger agreement with reference scientific conclusions.

Disease representations reveal clinically coherent organization

We examined the clinical organization of disease representations by comparing HealthFound with the Qwen3-32B backbone. Clinically related diseases clustered in the representation space (Figure 4a), with clearer separation and more coherent clinical organization in HealthFound than in the Qwen3-32B backbone (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Input-masking experiments further showed that removing key biomedical variables reduced disease-prediction accuracy, supporting the predictive relevance of features identified from the model's reasoning. Overall, after removing the top-ranked PRS and diet/nutrition variables for blood and immune diseases, circulatory diseases and endocrine/metabolic diseases, prediction accuracy decreased by more than 0.3%. In particular, removing the topranked blood count and anemia variables for blood and immune diseases reduced prediction accuracy by approximately 0.35%, while removing the sleep variable for circulatory diseases resulted in an approximately 0.2% reduction (Figure 4b).

Qualitative clinical examples

We examined how HealthFound combined temporal and clinical information in individual predictions. Three representative cases illustrated prediction before disease onset, reasoning over a longitudinal record and treatment selection (Fig. 5). HealthFound and Gemini-3-Flash received the same record and question, and the comparison highlights the information emphasized in their responses.

In the hydrocephalus example, HealthFound predicted the reference high-risk label, whereas Gemini-3-Flash predicted low risk (Fig. 5a). Both models produced the reference high-risk label in the postmenopausal-bleeding example, and both models considered the history of breast cancer and its treatment (Fig. 5b). In the acetaminophen-overdose question, HealthFound selected N-acetylcysteine and described the time-sensitive antidote rationale, whereas Gemini-3-Flash selected activated charcoal (Fig. 5c).

To examine the contribution of temporal information, we perturbed the dates of existing clinical events while preserving their original chronological order and retaining the biomedical measurements. Disease-prediction accuracy decreased, with many previously correct predictions becoming incorrect. Some predictions remained correct after perturbation, consistent with temporal information contributing alongside other biomedical risk indicators (Supplementary Table 6).

Zero-shot transfer to independent datasets

We evaluated zero-shot transfer of HealthFound to two independent datasets (Fig. 6). MIMIC-IV provided longitudinal hospitalization records from 50,880 individuals and 408 incident disease endpoints; inputs were restricted to information recorded before the target diagnosis. NHANES provided cross-sectional examination and laboratory measurements from 1,534 survey participants for nine prevalent-disease tasks. HealthFound was evaluated without further training, reward optimization or prompt adjustment.

On 408 MIMIC-IV incident endpoints, HealthFound achieved the highest mean accuracy in every disease chapter and an overall accuracy of 76.1%. Gemini-3-Flash reached approximately 71.0%, and the remaining generalpurpose comparators reached 63.9% or lower (Fig. 6a). A total of 363 endpoints reached at least 70% accuracy with HealthFound, compared with 235 for Gemini-3-Flash, 109 for DeepSeek-V3.2, 105 for Qwen3 and 51 for GPT-5.4 (Fig. 6b). Representative comparisons with GPT-5.4 included 81.9% versus 71.0% for hypertensive renal disease, 76.3% versus 58.0% for Alzheimer disease and 72.5% versus 53.8% for respiratory conditions due to external agents. Supplementary Fig. 5 shows MIMIC-IV performance by prevalence stratum.

On the nine NHANES prevalent-disease tasks, HealthFound achieved the highest accuracy for every reported endpoint and a mean accuracy of 65.6% overall (Fig. 6c), compared with 58.0% for Gemini-3-Flash and DeepSeek-V3.2, and lower than 56.4% for the remaining general-purpose comparators. The tasks covered coronary heart disease, stroke, hypertension, diabetes, heart failure, kidney disease, arthritis, asthma and peptic ulcer. Performance was strongest on coronary heart disease and stroke and was lower for arthritis, asthma and peptic ulcer, but HealthFound remained the highest-performing model across these tasks.

Discussion

HealthFound demonstrates that longitudinal population cohorts can extend medical language models beyond medical knowledge retrieval towards quantitative reasoning over evolving health states while retaining broad medical competence. Whereas biomedical literature, clinical vignettes and examination questions primarily describe established diseases and their management 3,4,10 , prospective cohorts link measurements from apparently healthy or preclinical individuals to health outcomes observed years later 14, 15 . By integrating these complementary sources of information through a three-stage training framework, HealthFound supports basic LLM functions together with incident disease prediction, missing-measurement inference, future health-event trajectory generation and scientific question answering. Its advantages also extended to external datasets, demonstrating zero-shot transfer across different healthcare settings and data structures.

HealthFound differs from recent cohort-and record-based predictors in how longitudinal cohort information is incorporated into the model. Delphi-2M is trained from scratch on sequences of coded health events 20 , whereas GRASP uses an LLM to embed medical concepts before training a separate transformer for disease-risk prediction 21 . HealthFound instead updates a pretrained language model through cohort-derived supervision. The resulting model predicts 1,074 incident disease endpoints, infers missing biomedical measurements and answers scientific questions while achieving 84.70% mean accuracy across eight public medical benchmarks. Its ability to generate future health-event trajectories further extends these capabilities. HealthFound also outperformed Delphi-2M, XGBoost and logistic regression in incident disease prediction. These findings provide a practical basis for health world modelling, in which a single language model uses longitudinal health information to infer missing measurements, estimate disease risk and generate possible future health events.

Maintaining broad medical competence was an important part of this training strategy. Curriculum SFT introduced cohort-based tasks while retaining medical and general reasoning examples. RFT subsequently reinforced these abilities, increasing mean accuracy across eight public medical benchmarks while producing only a modest improvement in incident disease prediction. HFPO then increased disease-prediction accuracy while maintaining medical-benchmark performance. Moreover, conventional SFT using the combined training data reached 60.0% UK Biobank accuracy, compared with 68.9% for the complete framework. The improvement therefore depended on how the model was trained, beyond the amount of training data alone.

The representation analyses provide further evidence that cohort training changed how HealthFound organized disease-related information. Although clinical chapter labels were not included in the embedding inputs, clinically related diseases formed clearer groups in HealthFound than in the Qwen3-32B backbone. Input-masking experiments also showed that removing selected biomedical variables reduced disease-prediction accuracy, consistent with the clinical features identified in the model's generated reasoning. These findings suggest that HealthFound learned useful relationships among diseases and biomedical measurements.

External evaluations further tested whether these capabilities extended beyond UK Biobank. Without additional training or prompt adjustment, HealthFound ranked the top in mean accuracy across 408 MIMIC-IV endpoints and nine NHANES endpoints. These results extend HealthFound's performance to hospital records and population-survey data with different observation patterns and disease definitions, supporting its ability to apply cohort-derived quantitative reasoning beyond the original UK Biobank setting. Future work has three priorities. First, evaluation in more diverse longitudinal cohorts is needed to assess generalizability across populations and healthcare settings, including the effects of selection bias 29 , diagnostic coding and differences in follow-up. Second, richer modelling of health trajectories could better capture repeated measurements, irregular observation times, informative missingness and multimodal data beyond text-serialized derived features. Third, prospective studies should evaluate risk calibration under natural disease prevalence, the accuracy of temporal reasoning and explanations, and whether model predictions can improve clinical decisionmaking and patient outcomes 30.

In conclusion, HealthFound shows that longitudinal population data can train medical language models to analyse quantitative health profiles while retaining broad medical competence. By integrating cohort-derived longitudinal information with pretrained medical knowledge, HealthFound unifies disease prediction, missing-measurement inference, future health-event trajectory generation and scientific question answering within a single model. Its performance across thousands of endpoints, established medical benchmarks and two external datasets points towards a health world model that moves beyond static medical knowledge to reason quantitatively about evolving human health states and their possible future outcomes.

Methods

Natural-language training data construction

To express heterogeneous measurements in a common input format, we converted cohort records to text before constructing task-specific examples.

UK Biobank provided data from 501,936 participants recruited at ages 40-69 years in 2006-2010 14, 15 . Measurements included demographics, lifestyle and diet, physical measurements, blood biochemistry and haematology, polygenic risk scores (PRSs) (Supplementary Table 9), imaging-derived phenotypes and linked clinical records. Median cohort follow-up was 14.1 years, with a maximum of approximately 17 years. UK Biobank contributed approximately 3.8 million disease-prediction examples covering 1,074 endpoints, 944,000 natural-language health records and 186,000 masked-measurement examples.

We first converted the structured UK Biobank records into readable health profiles by mapping field identifiers to clinical names, data types, coding hierarchies and units. Missing values and ambiguous codes were omitted, while categorical codes were translated into their documented meanings and numerical values were retained with their units and assessment dates. These standardized profiles were then used to construct four types of training examples.

Disease-prediction examples:

For disease prediction, we identified incident cases and eligible controls for each of the 1,074 disease endpoints and sampled the two groups in balanced numbers. Participants with a pre-existing diagnosis of the target disease were excluded. Each input contained only observations available before the prediction cut-off, without summarization or information recorded after that point. To accommodate lengthy records, we divided the profiles into segments of up to 4,096 tokens, with an overlap of 300 tokens between adjacent segments. Each segment began with sex and age at recruitment and was paired with the corresponding endpoint-specific label. This process generated approximately 3.8 million disease-prediction examples, including multiple endpoint-specific examples and record segments from individual participants.

Natural-language health records:

To construct the 944,000 natural-language health-record examples, we instead divided the standardized observations into text blocks and used a locally deployed Qwen3 model to summarize each block. The summaries were subsequently merged into coherent health reports that grouped clinically related information and consolidated repeated descriptions while preserving measurement values, units, assessment dates and follow-up findings. Summarization was performed using vLLM 31 with temperature 0.6 and top-p 0.9.

Masked-measurement examples:

We constructed 186,000 masked-measurement examples by removing one observed measurement from a health profile and asking the model to infer it from the remaining information. The removed measurement served as the reference answer. These examples covered different indicator domains, including haematology, anthropometry, biochemistry and polygenic risk scores, with the expected answer format determined by the type of measurement.

Agent-generated task examples:

To extend cohort-derived supervision beyond predefined disease-prediction and measurement-inference tasks, we used an agent pipeline to generate 15,552 additional instructions from UK Biobank health profiles. The pipeline covered six task types: risk stratification, disease prediction, prognosis, estimation of missing-measurement ranges, lifestyle intervention and comorbidity. For each candidate, the agent generated a structured output containing the instruction, input health profile, reasoning, conclusion, task type and confidence. Its memory retained task specifications, operating rules, relevant context and previous validation errors to guide subsequent generation. We then applied structural and content checks to ensure that all required fields were present, reasoning and conclusions were relevant to the assigned task, and each generation batch covered all six task types. For diseaseprediction tasks, candidates were rejected if the input revealed the target diagnosis or included observations after the prediction cut-off, or if the reasoning used an already confirmed diagnosis to assign risk. Candidates that failed validation were allowed one targeted revision using the original health record and the identified errors. Only candidates that passed the complete validation procedure were retained for training.

In addition to UK Biobank examples, the training corpus also contained 72,000 scientific question-answer pairs and 7.4 million medical and general reasoning examples (Supplementary Table 10).

Medical and general reasoning examples:

To preserve the model's general reasoning and medical knowledge during cohort-specific training, we assembled 5.5 million general reasoning examples and 1.9 million medical reasoning examples. The general reasoning data included examples from GlaiveAI Reasoning-v1-20m, covering logical reasoning, multistep problem solving and instruction following. Medical reasoning examples covered diagnosis, differential diagnosis, risk-factor identification, test selection, treatment recommendations and clinical decision-making. Sources included Medical-O1-Reasoning 32 and tulu-3-sft-personas 33 . We converted the examples into a common Alpaca-style instruction format, retaining the question, reference answer and reasoning trace when available. After standardizing the fields and removing duplicate records and unnecessary special characters, we randomly subsampled the datasets to control their relative contributions to training. This process produced 7.4 million medical and general reasoning examples.

Scientific question-answer pairs: To train the model to interpret biomedical research evidence, we constructed scientific question-answer pairs from 10,500 curated publications based on UK Biobank. We used DeepSeek-V3.2 to process the article texts and extract structured study information, including research questions, data sources, sample sizes, variable definitions, statistical methods, quantitative findings and main results. We separately extracted the authors' interpretations, including proposed mechanisms, clinical implications and study limitations. We then paired the central research questions with their corresponding conclusions to construct question-answer examples grounded in the extracted evidence. This procedure generated 75,574 question-answer pairs, of which 72,000 were used for training and 3,574 were reserved for evaluation.

Model architecture and training

HealthFound was built on Qwen3-32B, a decoder-only language model. We retained its original architecture, tokenizer and vocabulary of 152,064 tokens, without adding task-specific prediction heads. All inputs, including instructions, health measurements and numerical values, were represented as text. Training sequences were limited to 4,096 tokens, while the inference context window was configured separately. We adapted the model through three successive training stages, each serving a different purpose.

Curriculum supervised fine-tuning (SFT):

We first trained the model to generate reference answers for cohort-derived, medical and general reasoning tasks. During training, we gradually increased the proportion of cohort examples while continuing to include medical and general reasoning data. This stage introduced longitudinal health-profile reasoning while maintaining exposure to the model's existing knowledge and reasoning tasks.

Rejection-sampled retention fine-tuning (RFT):

We next reinforced previously learned medical and general reasoning abilities. The model generated candidate responses to selected anchor prompts, and responses were retained if they passed correctness and formatting checks. We then combined the accepted responses with the original reference answers for further supervised training. The resulting model was used to initialize the final training stage and served as its fixed reference model.

HealthFound Policy Optimization (HFPO):

Finally, we improved task-specific performance by rewarding correct and consistent responses. For each prompt, the model generated multiple candidate answers, which were scored using taskspecific rules. In disease prediction, the reward primarily reflected final-answer correctness (weight 0.8), with additional contributions from reasoning-label correctness (0.1) and consistency between the reasoning and final answer (0.1). The model was updated to favour higher-scoring responses within each group, while limiting its divergence from the RFT reference model and retaining supervised training on anchor answers. The number of training examples used at each stage is reported in Supplementary Table 10. Task-specific reward components and their weights are listed in Supplementary Table 11.

Evaluation protocol and comparators

All language models were evaluated using the same task-specific prompts, input health records and required answer formats. Where supported by the model interface, we used a temperature of 0.6, top-p of 0.9 and a maximum of 16,384 generated tokens. Depending on the task, we extracted the final disease-risk label, numerical prediction or written answer and compared it with the corresponding reference.

We compared HealthFound with five general-purpose language models-GPT-5.4, Gemini-3-Flash, DeepSeek-V3.2, Kimi-k2.5 and MiniMax-M2.7-as well as the Qwen3-32B backbone. To assess performance against domain-specialized models, we additionally evaluated Llama3-Med42 (70B) 34 , HuatuoGPT2 (13B and 34B) 35 , MedGemma (4B-IT and 27B-Text-IT) 36 , HuatuoGPT-o1-8B 32 and Baichuan-M2-32B 37 (Supplementary Table 1).

For disease prediction, we also compared HealthFound with Delphi-2M 20 , XGBoost 38 and logistic regression. For numerical measurement inference, conventional baselines included mean imputation and multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) 39.

UK Biobank incident disease prediction

We constructed 1,074 incident-disease endpoints by adapting FinnGen Release 12 definitions 16 to UK Biobank. Disease ascertainment primarily used inpatient ICD-10 diagnoses and their first-recorded dates (fields 41270 and 41280), with death-cause records (fields 40001 and 40002) additionally used for mortality-related endpoints. We applied FinnGen inclusion, exclusion and composite rules, together with relevant age-and sex-specific criteria. ICD-10 subcodes were matched by prefix, and codes exceeding UK Biobank coding resolution were shortened to four characters. After manual review, duplicate or unsupported endpoint definitions were removed. All endpoints requiring unavailable surgical, medication, or reimbursement information, or unsupported disease characteristics, were excluded unless these features could be identified through diagnostic codes or implemented rules. Additional details on endpoint construction are provided in Supplementary Methods.

For each endpoint, participants with the target disease at or before baseline were excluded. Cases were first diagnosed during follow-up, whereas controls had no recorded target diagnosis; controls who died during follow-up were also excluded. Case inputs were restricted to observations before the first diagnosis, with all later records removed 40 . For each disease endpoint, we targeted 5,000 cases and 5,000 controls when at least 5,000 cases were available. Otherwise, all available cases were retained before feature filtering, with up to 200 additional controls selected relative to the case count, subject to availability. Within the training partition, cases and controls were balanced, with a maximum of 1,000 unique participants per class per endpoint. The training and test sets were disjoint, with all examples from each participant confined to a single partition.

The test set comprised 203,777 examples across 1,074 endpoints. We calculated accuracy, positive-class F1, sensitivity and specificity for each endpoint and summarized performance by endpoint and clinical chapter. We also evaluated lowprevalence settings using observed case proportions of 0.05%-5%. To assess how HealthFound's positive-case detection rate varies with the time interval before disease onset across disease chapters, we conducted a stratified analysis of UK Biobank disease prediction results. For each positive case, the latest date extracted from the input text was used as the prediction start date, and the interval to the target disease onset was calculated. We then divided intervals from 0 to 14 years into seven consecutive 2-year bins, calculated the positive-case detection rate for each disease within each bin, and averaged these rates equally across diseases within each chapter. The resulting chapter-level curves were plotted against the upper boundary of each interval.

Longitudinal health-event generation

We used HealthFound to generate possible future health-event trajectories from a participant's longitudinal health history. Rather than adding a separate trajectory-prediction head, we formulated this as a text-generation task: the model received a participant's historical health profile and generated a sequence of predicted future events and their occurrence times. We compared HealthFound with Delphi-2M 20 and other LLMs using 10,000 UK Biobank participants.

Construct the input history:

We sampled 10,000 unique participants and arranged their recorded diagnoses, assessment visits and measurements chronologically. Each participant's history was divided at a prediction cut-off, defined as the upper median observation date and no later than the second-to-last date. For HealthFound, the historical observations were represented as a natural-language health profile.

Generate future trajectories: Given the historical profile, HealthFound generated possible sequences of future disease events and their predicted occurrence times. Each trajectory represented one possible continuation of the participant's health history. We evaluated eight valid trajectories per participant for all models. For comparison, predicted events were represented using three-character ICD-10 categories and a separate death event.

Statistical analysis

To assess the statistical significance of HealthFound's performance relative to baseline models, we analyzed prediction records across 1,074 UK Biobank disease endpoints. Overall accuracy was calculated by first determining accuracy for each disease and then averaging equally across all endpoints. We employed a paired cluster bootstrap using 78,316 unique participant EIDs as resampling units, whereby each bootstrap replicate sampled participants with replacement while retaining all disease-specific records belonging to each selected participant, thereby preserving within-participant correlations. For each replicate, disease-specific accuracies and their equally weighted means were recalculated to estimate the accuracy differences between HealthFound and each baseline model. A total of 5,000,000 bootstrap replicates were performed, with 95% confidence intervals derived from the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distributions and two-sided empirical p-values calculated from the null-centered distributions. HealthFound achieved significantly higher accuracy than all baseline models, with empirical p-values reaching the Monte Carlo resolution limit of approximately 2.0 × 10⁻⁷ in every comparison.

Inference of missing measurements

We evaluated whether HealthFound could predict a missing biomedical measurement from the remaining information in a participant's health profile. The evaluation covered different indicator domains, including haematology, anthropometry, biochemistry and polygenic risk scores (PRSs). The test set contained approximately 1,900 held-out examples.

For each example, we first selected one recorded measurement and removed its value from the health profile. The original value was retained only as the reference answer, while the remaining observations provided the context for prediction. We then provided the incomplete health profile to the model and asked it to infer the missing measurement. The task instruction specified the target variable and the required answer format, which could be a numerical value, categorical label or value interval, depending on the variable type.

All models used the same answer-parsing and unit-conversion rules. For numerical measurements, we compared each prediction with its recorded value using relative error. A prediction was considered correct if its relative error fell within a specified tolerance of 0.1%, 0.5%, 1%, 2%, 3%, 4% or 5%. Supplementary Table 3 reports the comparisons at tolerances from 1% to 5%. Categorical predictions were evaluated by exact matching after code normalization, whereas interval predictions were scored by the overlap between predicted and reference ranges. These scores were evaluated separately from numerical accuracy. The scoring rules are defined in Supplementary Methods.

We additionally compared numerical predictions with conventional mean imputation and multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) 39 . Results by indicator domain are reported in Supplementary Table 12.

Training-stage comparisons

To assess the contribution of each training stage, we compared the original Qwen3-32B model with HealthFound after curriculum SFT, RFT and HFPO. The three stages used approximately 10.49 million, 0.089 million, and 1.86 million training examples, respectively. These models were evaluated on the same test sets using identical prompts, decoding settings and answer-parsing rules. We also compared alternative training strategies. Disease representations and feature masking

We investigated whether cohort-specific training changed how the model represented diseases and which clinical information it used for prediction.

We compared disease embeddings extracted from HealthFound and the original Qwen3-32B model, with both models kept fixed. Each disease was represented by its endpoint name followed by the text "UK Biobank disease endpoint". We used mean-pooled input-token embeddings from the input embedding layer to tokenize this text and averaged the corresponding token embeddings to obtain a 5,120-dimensional vector for each disease. The vectors were centred, projected to 96 dimensions, and then reduced to two dimensions using principal component analysis (PCA). The resulting plots were coloured by clinical chapter to examine whether related diseases formed coherent groups.

We further examined the predictive contribution of these features by removing selected biomedical variables from the input profiles and comparing disease-prediction accuracy with that obtained using complete profiles. These analyses characterized the model's learned representations and use of clinical information 48 . Supplementary Methods describes the representation and masking analyses.
