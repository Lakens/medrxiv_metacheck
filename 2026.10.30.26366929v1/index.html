No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

INTRODUCTION

Deep learning has enabled accurate detection of several eye diseases from color fundus photographs (CFPs) and optical coherence tomography (OCT), including diabetic retinopathy (DR), age-related macular degeneration (AMD) and glaucomatous optic neuropathy. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5] [6] However, developing and adapting these systems have traditionally required large amounts of labeled data. 6, 7 Several prominent systems, for example, were developed using tens of thousands to more than 100,000 expert-graded fundus photographs 1, 2 or thousands of labeled OCT volumes. 6 Expert annotation is time-consuming, 7 grader disagreement makes reference standards imperfect, 8 and public ophthalmic datasets are geographically concentrated, with incomplete reporting of patient demographics and labeling methods. 9 In addition, performance can vary substantially across algorithms and screening sites, which supports local validation before deployment. 10 Vision foundation models (VFMs), such as RETFound and VisionFM, may reduce the need for new task-specific annotations by learning from large image collections before being adapted to specific clinical tasks. [11] [12] [13] [14] RETFound, for example, was pretrained on 1.6 million unlabeled retinal images and, when adapted to ophthalmic tasks, outperformed comparison models while needing fewer labeled images. 13 Contrastive vision-language models (VLMs), such as CLIP, instead learn to match images with paired text, which allows them to classify an image by comparing it with written descriptions of each disease category. 15, 16 In ophthalmology, VLMs such as FLAIR and EyeCLIP have performed well when adapted with few labeled images, following pretraining on large ophthalmic imaging datasets. 17, 18 Generative multimodal large language models (MLLMs), a class of models distinct from VFMs and VLMs, 19 may reduce labeled data requirements even further. By connecting a vision encoder to a large language model, an MLLM can follow written instructions about an image and respond in free text. 20, 21 MLLMs can learn a new task from a few labeled examples in the prompt without retraining, an approach known as in-context learning. 22, 23 General-purpose MLLMs such as GPT-4o and Gemini can interpret ophthalmic images, although performance has varied by disease, imaging type and task, [24] [25] [26] [27] and adding examples to the prompt has improved it in some evaluations but not in others. 28, 29 Additionally, many of these evaluations used proprietary models accessed through the developer's cloud service. 23, 24, 27, 28 Sending images containing protected health information to an external cloud service for processing on behalf of a covered institution requires a business associate agreement and other applicable safeguards under the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA). 30, 31 Developers may also change or discontinue access to proprietary models after validation. 32 Open-weight MLLMs, whose model weights are publicly released, can instead run entirely on an institution's own hardware. 21, 32 Such locally deployed models keep both patient data and the model under institutional control, which may ease privacy and regulatory concerns about their clinical use. 21, 32 Evidence remains limited on whether locally deployed MLLMs match VFMs and VLMs across diverse ophthalmic tasks and datasets. Recent benchmarking studies have reported mixed findings on the value of ophthalmology-specific pretraining, with general-purpose VFMs matching or outperforming retina-specific VFMs on some tasks, 33, 34 and retina-specific VFMs retaining an advantage on others. 35

METHODS

This study was conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Washington University in St. Louis. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, which involved the analysis of previously collected clinical data, the IRB waived the requirement for informed consent. All analyses were performed on a secured HIPAA-compliant local GPU server.

We conducted a benchmark study to evaluate the performance of emerging generative MLLMs relative to established vision models (VFMs and VLMs) for ophthalmic image interpretation tasks across 7 imaging datasets (4 public and 3 private). Tasks included disease classification, disease risk and severity grading, anatomical measurements, and lesion localization in CFPs, posterior-segment OCT, and anterior-segment OCT (AS-OCT), as well as multi-image classification of real-world clinical OCT volumes.

A primary aim of this study was to assess how model performance changed with the number of labeled examples, such as images with known diagnoses, from which a model can learn a new task. First, models were evaluated without any labeled examples from the task's dataset (zero-shot evaluation). Second, models were evaluated after being given 10 labeled examples per class (10-shot evaluation). To ensure a fair comparison, the examples provided to MLLMs were the same examples provided to vision models, for each task and random seed (42-46). For a complete evaluation, vision models were also evaluated after being given all available labeled examples (full-data adaptation). This was not performed for MLLMs, as current models cannot accept thousands of example images within a single prompt.

MLLMs and vision models learned new tasks from these labeled examples in different ways. MLLMs received written task instructions and the labeled examples within a prompt, from which they generated predictions without any additional training, an approach known as in-context learning. In contrast, vision models underwent additional training on the labeled examples in one of three ways, which differ in how much of the pretrained model is changed: linear probing, in which the model's internal weights are kept fixed and only a simple final classification layer is trained; partial fine-tuning, in which the weights of the last few layers are also adjusted; and full fine-tuning, in which all weights are adjusted.

Models were compared with no labeled examples (zero-shot), with the same 10 labeled examples per class (10-shot), and, for vision models, with all available labeled data (full-data adaptation). DR Severity and DME Risk Grading

We evaluated five-grade DR severity and three-grade DME risk classification using IDRiD CFPs (Figure 2A-B), assessing recognition of retinal vascular abnormalities and macular hard exudates, respectively.

For DR grading (Figure 2A), GLM-5.3-Flash achieved the highest zero-shot quadratic-weighted kappa (QWK, 0.526; 95% CI, 0.384-0.652), compared with 0.437 for Qwen3.8-27B. With ten labeled examples per class (10-shot), GLM-5.3-Flash achieved the highest QWK (0.693; 95% CI, 0.592-0.774) using in-context learning without parameter updates, compared with 0.484 for DINOv3 and 0.482 for CLIP, the best VFM and contrastive VLM using linear probing, respectively. With full-data linear probing, DINOv3 achieved 0.652, improving to 0.714 with partial fine-tuning. SigLIP2 achieved 0.722 with full-data partial fine-tuning (Supplementary Appendix 1, A.13). Occlusion sensitivity analyses assessed clinically relevant image regions for both tasks (Figure 3a).

For DME grading (Figure 2B), GLM-5.3-Flash achieved the highest zero-shot QWK (0.776; 95% CI, 0.660-0.874). With 10-shot in-context learning, Qwen3.8-27B achieved the highest QWK (0.734; 95% CI, 0.611-0.841), compared with 0.514 for VisionFM and 0.570 for CLIP, the best VFM and contrastive VLM using linear probing, respectively. With full-data linear probing, DINOv3 achieved 0.724, improving to 0.745 with partial fine-tuning. SigLIP2 achieved 0.785 with full-data partial fine-tuning (Supplementary Appendix 1, B.13).

Overall, 10-shot MLLMs achieved substantial agreement with reference grades (QWK 0.69-0.73), approaching vision models adapted with substantially more labeled data (0.714-0.785). MLLM errors were predominantly adjacent-grade (adjacent accuracy 0.79-0.93), with high specificity, whereas supervised vision-model probes showed greater sensitivity for referable disease but more false positives among healthy eyes (Supplementary Appendix 1, A.13 and B.13).

Glaucoma Classification and Cross-Dataset Generalization of vCDR

We evaluated vCDR estimation and glaucoma discrimination using REFUGE CFPs, followed by cross-dataset evaluation on OHTS.

For vCDR estimation (Figure 2C), GLM-5.3-Flash achieved the best zero-shot mean absolute error (MAE, 0.104; 95% CI, 0.096-0.111), compared with 0.106 (95% CI, 0.099-0.113) for Qwen3.8-27B. With 10-shot in-context learning, Qwen3.8-27B achieved the lowest MAE (0.087; 95% CI, 0.082-0.092), compared with 0.140 (95% CI, 0.135-0.145) for RETFound and 0.195 (95% CI, 0.189-0.201) for SigLIP2, the best VFM and contrastive VLM using linear probing, respectively. Demonstrations did not consistently improve performance: GLM-5.3-Flash worsened from 0.104 to 0.163-0.169, whereas Gemma-4-31B-it improved from 0.150 to 0.122.

With full-data adaptation, DINOv3 achieved the lowest linear-probing MAE (0.074), while CLIP achieved the lowest overall MAE (0.050; 95% CI, 0.047-0.054) with full fine-tuning (Supplementary Appendix 1, C.1).

For glaucoma discrimination using predicted vCDR as a continuous screening score, zero-shot MLLMs achieved AUROCs of 0.810-0.828, highest for Qwen3.8-27B (0.828; 95% CI, 0.751-0.894). With 10-shot in-context learning, AUROCs ranged from 0.785 to 0.816, compared with 0.830 (95% CI, 0.783-0.872) for VisionFM and 0.679 (95% CI, 0.630-0.721) for CLIP using linear probing. Thus, zero-shot MLLMs approached the strongest 10-shot supervised probe without labeled examples. With full-data partial fine-tuning, RETFound achieved the highest AUROC (0.970; 95% CI, 0.936-0.993). VisionFM achieved the highest 10-shot probing AUROC despite higher vCDR MAE than RETFound (0.201 versus 0.140), indicating that measurement accuracy and discrimination were not directly aligned (Supplementary Appendix 1, C.2).

For cross-dataset evaluation, models were adapted using REFUGE examples and tested on OHTS without OHTS adaptation (Figure 2D). With 10-shot in-context learning, GLM-5.3-Flash achieved an MAE of 0.150 (95% CI, 0.144-0.157), compared with 0.164 (95% CI, 0.157-0.171) for Qwen3.8-27B. Among 10-shot linear probes, VisionFM achieved the lowest MAE (0.208; 95% CI, 0.202-0.214), followed by RETFound (0.320; 95% CI, 0.310-0.329) and SigLIP2 (0.382; 95% CI, 0.342-0.431). CLIP reached an MAE of 3.99, illustrating implausible unconstrained regression outputs under dataset shift. Under zero-shot evaluation, GLM-5.3-Flash achieved an MAE of 0.141 (95% CI, 0.135-0.147), compared with 0.152 for Qwen3.8-27B, outperforming both its 10-shot configuration (0.150) and the best 10-shot supervised probe (0.208) (Figure 4C).

Although zero-shot MLLMs achieved lower OHTS MAE (0.141-0.152) than the best 10-shot supervised probe (0.208), VisionFM showed a smaller change between REFUGE and OHTS (0.201 to 0.208) than 10-shot Qwen3.8-27B (0.087 to 0.164). Rankings also shifted: RETFound was the best 10-shot probe on REFUGE, whereas VisionFM performed best on OHTS. These findings indicate that task-specific demonstrations did not necessarily improve external-dataset performance.

To assess demographic robustness, vCDR estimation error was examined across OHTS subgroups stratified by age, sex, and race (Supplementary Appendix 1, D.2). MLLMs achieved lower MAE than 10-shot VisionFM across all subgroups. Across models, MAE was lower among Black than White participants (GLM zero-shot, 0.127 vs. 0.148) and among male than female participants (0.133 vs. 0.147), whereas no clear age-related trend was observed. Notably, the absolute Black-White MAE gap was 54.3% smaller for GLM zero-shot than for fully trained VisionFM (0.021 vs. 0.046), suggesting more consistent performance across racial subgroups.

These exploratory findings support the cross-dataset generalizability of MLLMs, although the underlying causes of demographic differences warrant further investigation.

OCT Macular Disease Classification

We evaluated four-class classification of normal retina, choroidal neovascularization (CNV), DME, and drusen using UCSD OCT (Figure 2E). Zero-shot MLLMs achieved macro-F1 scores of 0.843-0.877, highest for Gemma-4-31B-it (0.877; 95% CI, 0.857-0.897), compared with 0.100-0.156 for contrastive VLMs. With 10-shot in-context learning, Qwen3.8-27B achieved 0.938 (95% CI, 0.925-0.950), compared with 0.586 for DINOv3 and 0.402 for SigLIP2 using linear probing. With full-data linear probing, DINOv3 achieved 0.917 and SigLIP2 achieved 0.882. Full fine-tuning improved macro-F1 to 0.957 for VisionFM, 0.953 for RETFound, and 0.951 for DINOv3 (Supplementary Appendix 1, E.19). Occlusion sensitivity analyses assessed clinically relevant image regions (Figure 3d).

BPEI-DLS: Retinal Pathology Localization

We evaluated localization of drusen and double-layer sign (DLS) using BPEI-DLS (Figure 2F). Each B-scan was divided into eight vertical bands, with models predicting pathology presence in each region. Vision models used supervised spatial prediction, whereas MLLMs selected numbered regions through Set-of-Marks (SoM) prompting. 46 Under zero-shot evaluation, GLM-5.3-Flash achieved the highest MLLM lesion-level F1 (0.728; 95% CI, 0.722-0.735), compared with 0.709 for Qwen3.8-27B and 0.680 for Gemma-4-31B-it.

With 10-shot in-context learning, Qwen3.8-27B achieved 0.750 (95% CI, 0.744-0.756), compared with 0.656 for VisionFM and 0.696 for SigLIP2 using linear probing. With full-data linear probing, VisionFM achieved 0.813, improving to 0.832 with partial fine-tuning. DINOv3 achieved the highest overall F1 (0.856; 95% CI, 0.851-0.861) with full-data partial fine-tuning (Supplementary Appendix 1, F.16). Representative predictions are shown in Figure 3e. Thus, MLLMs localized lesions competitively with limited supervision, whereas supervised vision models achieved higher performance with more labeled data.

Anterior Chamber Angle Classification and Scleral-Spur Localization

We evaluated anterior-segment OCT (AS-OCT) interpretation through PACG and cataract classification and scleral-spur localization.

For multilabel classification of PACG and cataract, encompassing normal, cataract, PACG, and concurrent disease (Figure 2G), Gemma-4-31B-it achieved the highest zero-shot PACG macro-F1 (0.615; 95% CI, 0.535-0.691), compared with 0.583 for Qwen3.8-27B and 0.571 for GLM-5.3-Flash. With 10-shot in-context learning, Gemma-4-31B-it achieved 0.780 (95% CI, 0.721-0.832), compared with 0.670 for DINOv3 and 0.654 for SigLIP2 using linear probing. For additional tasks, Qwen3.8-27B achieved macro-F1 scores of 0.777 for cataract classification and 0.524 for four-class categorization with 10-shot in-context learning. With full-data partial fine-tuning, VisionFM achieved 0.912 for PACG and 0.804 for four-class classification (Supplementary Appendix 1, G.19).

For scleral-spur localization (Figure 2H), supervised vision models predicted Gaussiansmoothed spatial heatmaps, whereas MLLMs generated landmark coordinates from image instructions and marked few-shot demonstrations. Zero-shot GLM-5.3-Flash achieved the lowest MLLM localization error (442.7 µm; 95% CI, 407.3-481.3 µm). With 10-shot in-context learning, Qwen3.8-27B achieved 273.8 µm (95% CI, 251.9-297.8 µm), compared with 203.5 µm for RETFound and 408.7 µm for SigLIP2 using linear probing. With full-data linear probing, VisionFM achieved 127.9 µm, while SigLIP2 achieved 124.7 µm with full fine-tuning (Supplementary Appendix 1, H.1). Representative predictions are shown in Figure 3f. MLLMs performed competitively in classification, whereas fully adapted vision models achieved greater anatomical localization precision.

Extension to Volumetric Clinical OCT Classification (WashU-OCT)

We evaluated four-class classification of healthy retina, AMD, DME, and ERM using 1,422 heldout EHR-linked WashU-OCT volumes.

For zero-shot MLLM evaluation, each volume was represented by 25 B-scans submitted together in a single multimodal inference request, enabling volume-level classification without parameter updates. GLM-5.3-Flash achieved accuracy of 0.730 (95% CI, 0.707-0.752) and macro-F1 of 0.710 (95% CI, 0.686-0.733), compared with 0.608 (95% CI, 0.584-0.634) and 0.496 (95% CI, 0.476-0.516), respectively, for Qwen3.8-27B, and 0.660 (95% CI, 0.635-0.684) and 0.631 (95% CI, 0.604-0.656), respectively, for Gemma-4-31B-it. GLM-5.3-Flash achieved class-specific F1 scores of 0.854 for AMD, 0.709 for DME, 0.691 for ERM, and 0.586 for healthy retina. Qwen3.8-27B performed substantially worse for ERM (F1, 0.039), frequently misclassifying ERM as healthy.

As a volumetric baseline, OCTCube-M, a three-dimensional Vision Transformer pretrained using masked autoencoding, processed volumes resized to 60 × 256 × 256 voxels. With ten labeled volumes per class, linear probing achieved accuracy of 0.316 (95% CI, 0.304-0.328) and macro-F1 of 0.151 (95% CI, 0.145-0.156) across five random seeds. Partial fine-tuning of the final two transformer blocks and classification head improved accuracy to 0.588 (95% CI, 0.573-0.602) and macro-F1 to 0.502 (95% CI, 0.489-0.513). With full-data linear probing, OCTCube-M achieved an accuracy of 0.390 (95% CI, 0.364-0.416) and a macro-F1 of 0.230 (95% CI, 0.216-0.244), improving to 0.854 (95% CI, 0.835-0.872) and 0.822 (95% CI, 0.799-0.843), respectively, with partial fine-tuning (Figure 4D).

Zero-shot GLM-5.3-Flash exceeded OCTCube-M under both 10-shot adaptation strategies (macro-F1, 0.710 versus 0.151 with linear probing and 0.502 with partial fine-tuning), although OCTCube-M performed better with full-data partial fine-tuning (0.822). These findings demonstrate the potential for training-free MLLM classification of clinical OCT volumes while highlighting the benefits of supervised adaptation for dedicated volumetric models.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we built an evaluation harness to benchmark the performance of open-weight This study demonstrates several advances that build upon prior benchmark studies assessing MLLM performance on ophthalmic image interpretation. Notably, the Foundation Ophthalmic Cross-Dataset Understanding under Shift (FOCUS) study previously characterized MLLMs on binary fundus classification across 10 public datasets, 19 while the RetinaGroundBench study characterized zero-shot visual grounding of OCT biomarkers by open-weight MLLMs. 48 Our present study expands on these foundations by evaluating MLLM performance using an incontext learning framework, in which models received clinical definitions and labeled examples matched across model families. In addition, while prior studies have evaluated MLLM performance with CFPs and individual OCT B-scans, we expand the repertoire of assessed imaging modalities to include AS-OCT and whole OCT volumes. Likewise, our study extends beyond previously studied tasks, such as binary classification and bounding-box grounding, to additional complex tasks, including severity grading, anatomical measurement, and disease status classification. Whereas prior studies have shown a limited ability of MLLMs to perform bounding-box grounding of OCT biomarkers without low-rank adaptation (LoRA) fine tuning, 48 our work demonstrates that in-context learning allows MLLMs to perform region-level localization at a level similar to VFMs and VLMs trained on the same examples.

Altogether, our results mark a major progression in ophthalmic AI. Task-specific deep learning models have required training on thousands of graded two-dimensional images or annotated OCT volumes to achieve expert-level performance for a limited number of ophthalmic tasks. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5] [6] Moreover, despite a shift in much of this training burden to the pre-training phase for VFMs, our present study demonstrates that VFM performance still strongly depends on the amount of labeled training data provided for a given task. [11] [12] [13] [14] 17, 18 MLLMs offer a potential alternative to the heavy training requirements of other model architectures, since they can be directed to new tasks through prompting rather than training. Early evaluations of MLLMs in ophthalmology, however, found modest and variable diagnostic accuracy, with or without in-context examples. [24] [25] [26] [27] [28] [29] Our findings suggest that recent open-weight MLLMs, including Qwen3.8-27B, GLM-5.3-Flash, and Gemma-4-31B-it, can achieve substantially stronger performance on several ophthalmic imaging tasks than reported in earlier MLLM evaluations, particularly when provided with clinical definitions and a small number of labeled examples.

Several mechanisms may account for these results. Large-scale multimodal pretraining aligns visual features with language, 15, 16 which may enable MLLMs to apply written disease definitions to images. Prior work has linked zero-shot performance to the frequency of concepts in multimodal pretraining data. 49 This may help explain why common, well-described findings such as macular edema, drusen and optic disc cupping were recognized without examples, although we did not test this mechanism directly. In-context examples may then align predictions with dataset-specific labeling conventions, allowing rapid adaptation to a new task without retraining or developing a specialized model. Task format may also help explain why MLLMs localized lesions well but not the scleral spur. Selecting among numbered regions, as in Set-of-Marks prompting, 46 may reduce the spatial demands placed on MLLMs relative to generating precise coordinates as text. 48 Together, these properties allow a single general-purpose MLLM to be applied to diverse ophthalmic tasks with only a few labeled examples.

Figure 4 summarizes the principal comparisons of the study. Panels A and B illustrate the contrasting relationships between labeled data availability and model performance: MLLMs achieved strong zero-shot and few-shot performance across classification tasks (Figure 4A) and, with a different profile, across localization tasks (Figure 4B), while supervised vision models generally benefited from larger training sets. Beyond label efficiency, zero-shot evaluation offers a different perspective on cross-dataset generalizability: rather than transferring a model adapted on one dataset to another, MLLMs can be evaluated directly on independent datasets without task-specific training. Consistent with this distinction, zero-shot MLLMs achieved lower vCDR estimation errors on OHTS than vision models adapted using REFUGE examples (Figure 4C). Panel D extends this comparison to volumetric data: the strongest zero-shot MLLM classification of private clinical OCT volumes exceeded a volumetric vision foundation model adapted on ten examples per class.

Training-data contamination is a central concern in benchmarking MLLMs. Evidence of contamination with widely used multimodal benchmarks has been reported in both open-weight and proprietary MLLMs. 50 Public ophthalmic datasets are additionally susceptible to leakage between training and test splits. 51 Public datasets remain essential for reproducibility and comparison across studies, and recent benchmarks, as well as our own, have relied on them. 19, 48 To address this issue, we utilized three non-publicly released datasets to evaluate model generalizability, which include the controlled access OHTS as well as the institutionally curated BPEI-DLS and WashU-OCT datasets. On all three private datasets, MLLMs outperformed VFMs and VLMs at a matched label budget. Furthermore, for OHTS, BPEI-DLS, and WashU-OCT, zero-shot MLLMs outperformed VFMs and VLMs trained on 10 labeled examples per class. Therefore, the consistent performance between private and public datasets strengthens confidence that our public benchmark results reflect generalization rather than memorization.

Generalization also depends on whether performance holds across patient populations. In this study, the 10 labeled examples per class were sampled randomly, without stratification by age, sex, race, or ethnicity, so a small example set could in principle limit generalizability. However, zero-shot inference uses no examples, and as noted above, zero-shot MLLMs achieved the lowest vCDR error on OHTS, a multicamera US cohort in which 26% of participants were Black. 39 Moreover, 10-shot demonstrations drawn from REFUGE, a dataset of Chinese patients, 38 increased OHTS error only slightly relative to zero-shot inference (MAE, 0.150 vs 0.141). Across race, sex, and age subgroups on OHTS, MLLMs achieved lower error than vision models adapted with the same 10 examples, and no subgroup's error exceeded the overall error by more than 0.02 (Supplementary Appendix 1, D.2). Error was modestly lower among Black and male participants for all models, including the fully trained vision model, suggesting differences in image or optic disc characteristics between subgroups rather than model-specific bias. Nevertheless, these analyses reflect performance on a single task, and because the demographic composition of MLLM pretraining data is undisclosed, selecting representative examples and validating models on target populations continues to remain important before clinical use.

This study has several limitations. First, although the benchmark spanned multiple tasks, imaging modalities, and ocular diseases, it did not encompass all ophthalmic tasks. Second, all tasks in this study involved findings that clinicians can recognize. Fundus photographs also contain systemic information not readily quantified through routine clinical assessment. In the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities Study, standardized grading showed that retinal arteriolar narrowing was associated with current and past blood pressure, 52, 53 and deep learning has since been used to predict cardiovascular risk factors from fundus photographs. 54 Whether incontext learning can identify such signals was not tested here. Third, we did not assess semantic segmentation. MLLMs could represent segmentation masks as text, such as binary masks, but this approach has not been fully evaluated, so it remains uncertain whether dedicated segmentation models will remain better suited for this task. Fourth, we adopted standardized evaluation protocols, using primarily linear probing with partial fine-tuning for selected arms, for few-shot VFM and VLM adaptation and in-context learning without parameter updates for MLLMs. Although alternative fine-tuning strategies and prompt designs may influence performance, our objective was to compare model families under a matched label budget and consistent evaluation settings rather than optimize each model individually. Finally, the field is evolving rapidly, and our results represent a single point in time. With continued investment in general-purpose models, the advantage of MLLMs in label-limited settings may widen, although this will require reassessment as models and methods evolve. Our evaluation harness, including task prompts, labeled example sets, and inference settings, will be released as open-source software (https://github.com/wusmai/Ophthalmic-AI-with-Ten-Examples) to facilitate further development and evaluation of MLLMs for ophthalmic image analysis.

Our work suggests several directions for future research. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods such as LoRA may improve MLLM performance beyond in-context learning. 55 LoRA fine-tuning improved OCT biomarker grounding in RetinaGroundBench, 48 and open-weight MLLMs can be fine-tuned on local hardware. 21 Hybrid systems that combine MLLMs with dedicated segmentation models may also improve localization precision. Because open-weight models can be deployed within institutional infrastructure, they facilitate privacy-preserving clinical research, although HIPAA requirements continue to apply. [30] [31] [32] Broader external and prospective validation will be essential. In a randomized controlled trial, an ophthalmic foundation model used as a clinical assistant improved diagnostic and referral accuracy, 56 and comparable evidence will be required before MLLM-based tools enter clinical practice.

Developing task-specific ophthalmic AI has often required substantial annotation effort, computational resources, machine learning expertise, and a dedicated model for each application, which can limit development to centers with those resources. Because locally deployed MLLMs can be applied to new tasks using task-specific instructions and a small number of labeled examples, effort could shift from annotation and model training toward defining clinically meaningful questions, curating representative examples, and rigorously evaluating model outputs. This shift could democratize ophthalmic AI, allowing clinicians to develop and validate tools for the questions most relevant to their own patients, including rare diseases with few labeled cases and underserved populations, while keeping patient data within the institution.

In conclusion, locally deployed, open-weight MLLMs with 10 labeled examples per class outperformed VFMs and VLMs trained on the same examples in most of the ophthalmic tasks evaluated and generalized to new datasets without retraining. These findings highlight a broader opportunity: making advanced ophthalmic image analysis accessible without massive labeled 538 datasets or specialized model development.
