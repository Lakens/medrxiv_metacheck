Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. Abstract

Background: Antimicrobial resistance is a growing concern in long-term care facilities (LTCs), where residents are often older, have chronic health conditions, and are frequently prescribed antimicrobials. Historically, antimicrobial resistance data from Canadian LTCs have been limited. This study explores the use of lab-based surveillance data from the Public Health Agency of Canada's AMRNet program to describe antimicrobial resistance in LTCs.

Methods: Antimicrobial susceptibility data for clinical bacterial specimens were submitted from public and private laboratories across Canada. Where feasible, LTC patients were identified by submitting jurisdictions.

Data on urinary isolates from identified LTC patients were analysed using the first isolate per patient per species per calendar year. The data were described by distribution of age group, sex, species and antimicrobial susceptibility. Antimicrobials were classified by the World Health Organization' s 'Access, Watch and Reserve' (AWaRe) framework and a weighted-incidence syndromic combination antibiogram was constructed.

As part of AMRNet program development, in some jurisdictions, only certain specimen types or organisms were submitted. Not all jurisdictions participated every year. Due to privacy precautions, some values rely on estimations.

Results: From 2016-2024, AMRNet received data on 216,524 isolates from five provinces (BC, NL, ON, PEI, SK). Most isolates (98%) were from patients aged ≥60, with those ≥80 years representing 80%; females predominated across all age groups.

Urine represented >99% of submitted data. The most common organisms identified from urinary isolates were Escherichia coli (51%), Klebsiella pneumoniae (14%) and Proteus mirabilis (12%). Non-susceptibility varied across organisms: E. coli demonstrated higher non-susceptibility to several commonly used agents including fluoroquinolones (44%) and third-generation cephalosporins (18%) while K. pneumoniae displayed high non-susceptibility to nitrofurantoin (56%). Among P. mirabilis, 26% were non-susceptible to ciprofloxacin. Aminoglycoside and carbapenem resistance were low. When antimicrobials were grouped by WHO AWaRe categories, more than half of E. coli isolates were non-susceptible to at least one Watch antimicrobial.

Interpretation: AMRNet lab-based surveillance data can help describe national AMR patterns among LTC residents. Although limitations exist and identifying LTC patients in lab-based datasets can be challenging in some jurisdictions, these data provide a Unclassified / Non classifié feasible and cost-effective means of tracking national resistance trends among an atrisk population.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has emerged as a major threat to global health, reducing the effectiveness of antimicrobials used to treat both routine and severe infections (1) (2) (3) (4). As microorganisms develop mechanisms that enable them to withstand antimicrobial therapy, infections become harder to treat, resulting in longer illness duration, increased healthcare utilization and costs, and higher mortality worldwide (5) (6) (7).

Residents of long-term care (LTC) facilities are particularly vulnerable to the consequences of AMR due to a combination of factors, including advanced age, multimorbidity, high rates of antimicrobial use, and frequent interactions with healthcare services and on-site medical care, creating repeated opportunities for exposure to resistant organisms (4, (8) (9) (10). Residents in long term care (LTC) facilities experience a range of infections; however, urinary tract infections (UTIs) are consistently among the most commonly reported, accounting for approximately 20% to over 35% of all infections in some facilities (11) (12) (13) (14). UTIs are a major cause of antimicrobial use in this population, comprising more than one third of all antibiotic prescriptions (15) (16) (17) (18). Urine cultures are frequently obtained in the absence of clinical symptoms, leading to increased likelihood of misclassifying asymptomatic bacteriuria as UTIs and contributing to unnecessary antimicrobial use (19).

In Canada, national AMR surveillance has historically focused on acute-care facilities, with comparatively limited antimicrobial susceptibility data available from LTC facilities (20), representing an important gap in understanding resistance trends. Strengthening surveillance is essential to inform stewardship and infection-prevention efforts in these facilities. To address this gap, the Public Health Agency of Canada's (PHAC's) AMRNet surveillance program initiated a pilot project to analyse susceptibility data originating from LTC facilities. Established in 2021, AMRNet is a national laboratory-based AMR surveillance initiative led by the PHAC's National Microbiology Laboratory in collaboration with provincial and territorial public health organizations and laboratories across the country (21). As of April 2026, AMRNet collects both isolate-level and aggregate clinical antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) data from both public and private laboratories in eight participating provinces and territories. Building on this national infrastructure, the pilot project described here provides findings on antimicrobial non-susceptibility patterns in urinary bacterial isolates collected from LTC facilities across participating provinces and territories. As AMRNet data includes contributions from approximately 1,000 LTC facilities, representing about half of the approximately 2,000 LTC facilities in Canada, these results offer an unprecedented view of AMR in this sector (22).

Materials and Methods

Data sources and study population

This pilot project analysed clinical AST data from LTC facilities submitted to the Canadian AMRNet surveillance program by participating provinces and territories from 2016-2024. AMRNet, described elsewhere (21), includes antimicrobial susceptibility testing data from bacterial and fungal pathogens from public and private clinical laboratories across Canada. Along with susceptibility test results, AMRNet collects information on specimen type, as well as patients' sex, age group, setting and geographic location. Screening and surveillance specimens are excluded. LTC-associated isolates were identified by submitted jurisdictions where feasible. Identifiable LTC data were available from five provinces and included both isolate-level (4 jurisdictions) and aggregate submissions (1 jurisdiction).

As a developing surveillance system, not all jurisdictions participated in all years of surveillance. Participation in LTC surveillance increased from 3 jurisdictions for the 2017/18 datasets to 5 jurisdictions for the 2023/24 datasets. While AMRNet aims to collect all susceptibility test results from all geographic regions in a province, in 2/5 provinces, some regions were not yet onboarded during the study period, resulting in estimated coverage of approximately 60% and 85%, respectively.

Isolate-level data

Raw data submitted by participating provinces were standardized centrally by AMRNet using a custom R-based pipeline and the AMR R package (23) prior to analyses.

Aggregate data

One jurisdiction submitted aggregate rather than individual-level data. Aggregate data consisted of counts of susceptible (S) and non-susceptible (NS) results for each organism-antimicrobial combination, stratified by available covariates (specimen source, sex, age group, setting, region, and year). These data included only the first isolate per organism per patient in a calendar year and were limited to AST results reported to clinicians. For privacy, some small cell counts were suppressed and imputed with expected mean values.

Laboratory methods

Organism identification and AST were performed by laboratories according to routine diagnostic procedures in each jurisdiction. All jurisdictions report the routine use of CLSI methods and breakpoints in their jurisdictions although a small proportion of laboratories use alternative methods (e.g., EUCAST).

Data analysis procedures

Descriptive analyses were conducted to summarize organism distribution, demographic patterns, and antimicrobial susceptibility patterns. Duplicate isolates were removed according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidance (24) with only the first clinical isolate per patient per organism per calendar year included.

Antimicrobial non-susceptibility analyses, including AWaRe, and weighted-incidence syndromic combination antibiogram (WISCA) estimates were further restricted using AMRNet reporting criteria. To ensure comparability and reduce potential bias arising from selective testing and variation in laboratory reporting practices across jurisdictions, data from a jurisdiction were included for a given organism-antimicrobial combination only when ≥85% of isolates for that organism had a susceptibility result available for the antimicrobial (tested or reported). Following application of this jurisdiction-level criterion, organism-antimicrobial combinations were reported only when: 1) Data for the combination were contributed by at least three participating provinces or territories; and 2) At least 30 isolates were available for analysis (as per CLSI recommendations).

Data submitted that did not meet the AMRNet reporting criteria above were excluded from susceptibility estimates. Non-susceptibility was defined as intermediate or resistant susceptibility test results and was calculated as: (Number of non-susceptible results ÷ Total susceptibility test results reported) x 100 Because AST and reporting practices differed across jurisdictions, the number of isolates available for analysis varied by organism-antimicrobial combination.

Consequently, denominators differ between organisms and antimicrobials throughout the susceptibility analyses. Susceptibility estimates were based on data as submitted by participating jurisdictions; missing susceptibility results were not imputed, and no additional interpretative or expert rules were applied centrally.

To contextualize susceptibility patterns across antimicrobials, results were categorized according to the World Health Organization Access, Watch, and Reserve (AWaRe) classification. Only isolate-level data were included in this portion of the analyses.

A weighted-incidence syndromic combination antibiogram (WISCA) was constructed using the three most common urinary pathogens in the AMRNet dataset, representing over 77% of all urine isolates. The WISCA estimates probability that empiric antimicrobial therapy will have in vitro activity against a urinary pathogen and complements the organism specific susceptibility analyses by reflecting the clinical syndrome and pathogen frequency rather than individual organisms (25) Antimicrobialorganism combinations classified as intrinsically resistant according to CLSI M100 definitions were retained and considered resistant in the overall susceptibility estimates.

Ethics approval

As data were collected and analyzed for routine public health surveillance purposes at the Public Health Agency of Canada, research ethics board review was not required.

Results

Participating provinces and territories and isolate counts

Five jurisdictions (BC, NL, ON, PEI, SK) submitted data in which LTC facilities could be identified (Figure 1). In total, 216,524 urine isolates were reported from LTC facilities. Following CLSI-based deduplication and exclusion of isolates lacking susceptibility interpretations, 214,360 urinary isolates were included in the analysis.

Age and sex distribution of urinary isolates

Age and sex information was available for 97% of urinary isolates. The distribution of urinary isolates by age group and sex is presented in Supplementary Figure 1. Individuals aged ≥ 80 years accounted for the largest proportion of urinary isolates (80%) in long-term care facilities. Across all age groups, females consistently contributed the majority of isolates (75% of isolates overall).

Organism distribution

The distribution of organisms among urinary isolates is shown in Figure 2. Escherichia coli accounted for 51% of total isolates, followed by Klebsiella pneumoniae (14%) and Proteus mirabilis (12%). Together, these three Enterobacterales comprised >75% of urinary isolates. Enterococcus faecalis, other Enterococcus species and Staphylococcus aureus were among the most common gram-positive organisms and represented approximately 2-3% of urinary isolates each. Among S. aureus isolates, 32% were methicillin-resistant (MRSA) during 2022-2024.

Antimicrobial non-susceptibility among urinary isolates

The percentage of non-susceptible isolates for select antimicrobial-organism combinations is presented in Figure 3. As Enterobacterales comprised the majority of urinary isolates, non-susceptibility patterns were examined for the top three most frequently identified Enterobacterales species: E.coli, K. pneumoniae and P. mirabilis.

Among the three most common Enterobacterales, notable differences in nonsusceptibility patterns were observed. E. coli demonstrated much lower nonsusceptibility to nitrofurantoin (4.7%) than K. pneumoniae (>50%). In contrast, nonsusceptibility to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, amoxicillin-clavulanate, and fluoroquinolones was higher in E. coli than in K. pneumoniae (24.5%, 16.7%, and 43.0% vs. 8.4%, 4.4%, and 9.0%, respectively). Proteus mirabilis demonstrated intermediate non-susceptibility to ciprofloxacin (26.0%), higher than K. pneumoniae but lower than E. coli. Carbapenem non-susceptibility remained rare in all the three Enterobacterales (<1%), while non-susceptibility to aminoglycosides (gentamicin and tobramycin) was low across all organisms (≤10%). Among the three most common Enterobacterales, non-susceptibility to at least one third-generation cephalosporin (ceftriaxone, cefotaxime, or ceftazidime) was highest in E. coli (18.3%), followed by K. pneumoniae (12.0%). In contrast, non-susceptibility was rare in P. mirabilis (1.3%).

When grouped by WHO AWaRe categories, non-susceptibility differed among the three predominant Enterobacterales (Figure 4, Supplementary Table 1). E. coli showed the highest non-susceptibility to both Access (56.6%) and Watch (49.4%) antibiotics compared with K. pneumoniae (48.0% and 13.8%, respectively). P. mirabilis demonstrated intermediate levels of non-susceptibility to Access (35.8%) and Watch (36.6%) antibiotics. No Reserve antimicrobials met AMRNet's reporting criteria.

Weighted-incidence syndromic combination antibiogram

A WISCA was developed using the three most frequent organisms from urinary specimens (Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Proteus mirabilis) (25) (Table 1). Susceptibility among urine isolates was found to be highest for ceftriaxone (91.2%) and amoxicillin/clavulanic acid (89.0%). Susceptibility was approximately 80% for cephalexin (81.4%), trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (81.2%), and nitrofurantoin (80.6%). Lower susceptibility was observed for ciprofloxacin (65.9%) and ampicillin (47.5%).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this pilot project presents the first national-level description of antimicrobial non-susceptibility patterns among urinary bacterial isolates from Canadian LTC facilities, establishing initial benchmarks in this sector. Using routinely collected laboratory data submitted through AMRNet, this analysis demonstrates the feasibility and value of coordinated, multijurisdictional lab-based surveillance to inform stewardship and monitor resistance trends in LTC facilities. This predominance likely reflects the high burden of urinary tract infections, which are among the most common infections and indications for antimicrobial use in LTC populations. It may also reflect substantial variation in urine culturing and diagnostic practices that persists, in some studies, even after accounting for resident characteristics and may represent opportunities for antimicrobial stewardship (9, 13, 16-18 (26, 27). Together, these factors likely contributed to the predominance of urinary isolates in our dataset. These findings underscore the importance of antimicrobial stewardship efforts aimed at optimizing urine culture ordering and reducing unnecessary treatment of asymptomatic bacteriuria.

Consistent with prior reports, the Gram-negative organisms E.coli, K. pneumoniae and P. mirabilis were the most frequently identified organisms and represented more than three-quarters of all isolates, highlighting the predominance of Enterobacterales in this population (Figure 3) (11, 28, 29). Among gram-positive organisms, Enterococcus faecalis, other Enterococcus species and S. aureus represented a smaller proportions (2-3% each) of urine isolates; nearly one-third of S. aureus isolates were found to be methicillin-resistant among LTC isolates compared to one-fifth in overall AMRNet surveillance including all facilities (30). MRSA bacteriuria frequently results from colonization or contamination rather than true urinary infection, particularly among older adults and those residing in long-term care facilities (31). Studies have previously reported that S. aureus in urine is often nonspecific and may not reflect invasive disease, which is better assessed through bloodstream surveillance (32).

In this study, non-susceptibility patterns for E. coli, K. pneumoniae and P. mirabilis broadly aligned with published reports from LTC facilities in Canada and the United States. Although direct comparison are limited by differences in study design, laboratory practices and populations, these prior studies similarly report high susceptibility to nitrofurantoin and elevated non-susceptibility to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole and, notably, to fluoroquinolones in long-term care settings (11, 28, 29, 33). Consistent with known species characteristics, K. pneumoniae had relatively high non-susceptibility to nitrofurantoin in this study, reflecting its reduced susceptibility to this antimicrobial.

LTC facilities often have lower proportions of non-susceptible isolates compared with inpatient facilities (28, 34, 35) but higher proportions than community (36, 37). Consistent with this, we found a generally higher proportion of non-susceptibility among the isolates from LTC facilities compared to general AMRNet surveillance (all settings combined). For instance, 43% of E. coli urinary isolates from LTC facilities were nonsusceptible to ciprofloxacin compared with 30% in general AMRNet surveillance. This is likely due to the high proportion of isolates from outpatient/community settings in combined surveillance with lower rates of non-susceptibility.

Non-susceptibility to fluoroquinolones exceeded 40% in E. coli. This is consistent with prior studies from Canada and the United States reporting non susceptibility often exceeding 30%, with some LTC facilities reporting rates above 60% (11, 28, 35). This has direct implications for empiric therapy in these facilities. Fluoroquinolones are not recommended as first-line therapy for UTI treatment, yet they continue to comprise a substantial proportion of antibiotic use in LTC facilities, representing approximately one-fifth to one-third of all prescriptions despite reduced susceptibility and well documented risks that include, for example, development of Clostridioides difficile infection (9, 17, (38) (39) (40) (41) (42) (43) (44).

Non-susceptibility to third-generation cephalosporins varied among the predominant Enterobacterales and was highest in E. coli (18.3%), followed by K. pneumoniae (12.0%), while remaining uncommon in P. mirabilis (1.8%).. Increasing rates of nonsusceptibility to third-generation cephalosporins among Enterobacterales has been observed among urinary isolates in LTC facilities (33, 45) as well as in AMRNet data from all settings (30). Resistance to third-generation cephalosporins is often accompanied by resistance to other agents, such as fluoroquinolones, limiting empiric treatment options for complicated or more serious UTIs (46) (47) (48).

In contrast to fluroquinolones and third-generation cephalosporins, non-susceptibility to carbapenems and aminoglycosides remained low across E. coli, K. pneumoniae, and P. mirabilis. Although not restricted to long-term care facilities, this is similar to what has been reported from urine cultures in Ontario (28), from a surveillance study of Canadian hospitals (49) and from AMRNet data all settings (30). Carbapenems and aminoglycosides are not routinely used in LTC facilities; high susceptibility indicates that these agents remain largely preserved among the predominant urinary Enterobacterales.

The AWaRe classification, introduced by the WHO in 2017, was designed as a stewardship framework to support monitoring of antimicrobial use and emerging resistance (49, 50). When antibiotics were grouped by WHO's Access, and Watch AWaRe categories, a substantial proportion of urinary isolates demonstrated non-susceptibility to Access and Watch antibiotics (Figure 4). Non-susceptibility to Access antimicrobials was highest in E. coli and K. pneumoniae, while non-susceptibility to Watch antimicrobials was most pronounced in E. coli followed by P. mirabilis and was lower in K. pneumoniae. These findings highlight resistance to both first-line and broader-spectrum antimicrobials used to treat UTIs. No antimicrobials from the "Reserve" category met the reporting criteria for the selected organisms in the AMRNet's LTC dataset.

A weighted incidence syndromic combination antibiogram (WISCA) was also developed using the three most frequently isolated urinary pathogens, integrating organism prevalence with antimicrobial susceptibility results (Table 1). IDSA aligned guidance recommends nitrofurantoin, trimethoprim sulfamethoxazole, and fosfomycin as preferred empiric agents for uncomplicated urinary tract infections, with broader agents such as third or fourth generation cephalosporins or fluoroquinolones reserved for more complicated presentation (50, 51). The WISCA results broadly reflect these principles: nitrofurantoin (80%) and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (81%) demonstrate moderately high predicted susceptibility. When broader coverage is required, high susceptibility to third generation cephalosporins (91%) supports their reliability. These findings provide the first multi-jurisdictional overview of antimicrobial resistance in Canadian long-term care facilities, establishing a baseline for future surveillance. The AMRNet dataset represents approximately 1,000 LTC facilities across Canada, based on provincial and territorial LTC counts from the Canadian Institute for Health Information (CIHI), providing a strong foundation for future surveillance. As participation expands and LTC-specific data capture improves, AMRNet will enable more robust tracking of temporal trends, early identification of emerging resistance, and support for evidence-based decision-making and interventions. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, participation varied across jurisdictions and years, with not all jurisdictions contributing data throughout the entire study period. Second, while AMRNet aims to collect data on all organisms, specimen types and antimicrobial tested, as part of program development, not all jurisdictions were able to submit all combinations in every year. In addition, AMRNet can only collect data on what is tested and/or what is reported and antimicrobial susceptibility testing practices varied across jurisdictions and over time. To improve comparability and minimize potential bias, AMRNet applied predefined reporting thresholds, including minimum isolate counts and coverage criteria. Data were analysed as reported by jurisdictions. No centralized interpretation of susceptibility test results, including the application of breakpoints or expert rules, was undertaken, and no statistical imputation was performed for missing organism-antimicrobial combinations. Despite these limitations, this pilot establishes a critical foundation for national AMR surveillance in long-term care facilities and, highlights both the feasibility and value of coordinated multi-jurisdictional surveillance efforts.

Strengthening antimicrobial stewardship remains essential to addressing antimicrobial resistance in long-term care facilities. Efforts that support appropriate empiric antimicrobial selection, limit unnecessary urine culture testing, and discourage treatment of asymptomatic bacteriuria are particularly important in these facilities, where residents face high antibiotic exposure and are especially vulnerable to adverse effects and the consequences of resistance (43, 52). Sustained national surveillance efforts will be key for guiding stewardship initiatives and ensuring that Canadian long-term care facilities can respond effectively to the evolving antimicrobial resistance landscape. 1 on classifié.
