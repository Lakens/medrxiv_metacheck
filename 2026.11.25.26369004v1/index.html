How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Abstract

Precise delineation of stroke lesions on MRI scans provides essential quantitative information used routinely in stroke rehabilitation research. Manual annotation continues to be the gold standard for stroke lesion delineation but is subjective and labor-intensive. Our previous release of ATLAS v2.1 (N=1271) provided a large, open-source dataset of T1w stroke brain MRIs and manually segmented lesion masks, which facilitated the development of numerous lesion segmentation algorithms. However, fully automated algorithm performance is not yet adequate to replace manual protocols; existing lesion segmentation algorithms still lack accuracy and generalizability. As model architectures grow increasingly sophisticated, they demand large-scale, diverse training data. Here, we present ATLAS v3.0 (N=1815), an updated, larger dataset of T1w stroke brain MRIs with corresponding manually segmented lesion masks.

Background & Summary

Stroke is a leading cause of death and disability worldwide. 1 Brain imaging using noninvasive modalities such as Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) and Computed Tomography (CT) have become invaluable tools to support timely diagnosis by clinicians, define optimal treatment strategies, and evaluate prognostic outcomes based on stroke etiology. 2 Delineation of stroke-affected brain regions on imaging modalities (such as MRI scans) through lesion segmentation can provide important downstream spatial and quantitative information used in stroke rehabilitation research (e.g., tissue type classification and lesion load calculation, often complementing behavioral measures). 3 Stroke lesion delineation can be performed either manually by trained annotators, 4 or automatically by using trained algorithms. Manual annotation of stroke lesions remains the gold standard as it involves a trained annotator performing a thorough visual review to capture lesions of various sizes and patterns. However, it is a subjective and laborintensive process, requiring annotators to delineate lesions on individual brain MRI slices one at a time, or using semi-automated (e.g., filling) techniques with manual correction. 4 Automated methods are less time-consuming and more scalable, but face challenges in accurately delineating stroke lesions in particular. 3, 5, 6, 7, 8 Some unique, stroke-specific challenges include the heterogeneity in the appearance of lesions depending on the poststroke phase (e.g., acute, subacute or chronic) and type of imaging modality used (e.g. imaging, CT;T1-,T2-, or diffusion-weighted brain MRI), as well as the presence of other confounding pathologies and brain abnormalities that may be confused for stroke lesions. 3, 9 Additionally, stroke lesions often appear irregular and have poorly defined boundaries; distinct lesions may sometimes appear merged together or blurred on imaging. 3 To address these challenges, and to contribute to the development of accurate lesion segmentation algorithms, the ENIGMA Stroke Recovery Working Group has released some of the largest open-source datasets of T1-weighted (T1w) stroke brain MRIs with manually segmented lesion masks, including ATLAS v1.2 in 2018 10 and ATLAS v2.1 in 2022. 9 The ATLAS datasets have served as a key public benchmark, offering diverse and comprehensively annotated stroke lesion masks. 3, 11, 12 Both these datasets have been used to develop numerous automated lesion segmentation algorithms for nearly a decade using various machine learning and more recently, deep learning architectures. 13- 21 ATLAS v2.1 has been cited more than 270 times and downloaded upwards of 3600 times by trainees, faculty, and the research community globally (as of September 2026). 22 Notably, since the release of ATLAS v2.1, artificial intelligence (AI), and especially deep learning (DL) approaches, have undergone rapid transformations; this has resulted in marked improvements in medical image segmentation, as well as stroke lesion segmentation specifically. 23 Several research-based and translational clinical applications of the ATLAS v2.1 dataset leveraging AI and DL advancements are reported in the literature. [24] [25] [26] ATLAS v2.1 has continued to serve research purposes as a large-scale annotated dataset for algorithms, especially U-Net model architectures, used in automated stroke lesion segmentation. Importantly, ATLAS v2.1 has been used to develop "SynthSR", 24 an AI-driven tool that creates synthetic high-resolution (e.g., 1 mm isotropic) images from brain scans of any contrast and resolution, with the goal of overcoming sample size and power limitations in neuroimaging research while achieving spatial resolutions beyond those attained by conventional clinical scans. 24 Using ATLAS v2.1 data has enabled SynthSR to account for stroke lesions that would have otherwise prevented downstream analysis (e.g., due to spatial normalization), when upsampling clinical brain MRIs. Other research-based uses of ATLAS v2.1 have included the pretraining of newer self-supervised learning, or foundation, models 25 to capture innate, generalizable brain MRI representations, 26 and to serve as an evaluation target to test the robustness of anomaly detection models trained on healthy brain scans. 19, 27, 28, 29 Beyond research-based applications, the ATLAS v2.1 dataset has also been used to perform clinically relevant downstream prediction tasks, such as in time-to-stroke predictions, which could enable clinicians to optimize time-sensitive interventions for stroke patients that may otherwise be excluded due to unknown stroke onset information. 19 Despite this progress, the primary goal of the ATLAS v2.1 dataset release, to facilitate the development of robust and generalizable stroke lesion segmentation algorithms that match the performance of gold standard manual tracing, remains to be achieved at the time of this release.

Continued progress in DL model architecture development and prevention of model overfitting is dependent on large amounts of annotated training data. 23 Concurrently, deployment of successful DL models is also dependent on their ability to adapt to various sources of MR image variability such as differences in scanner types, acquisition parameters and protocols. 3, 35, 36 Such variability remains an inevitable aspect of realworld clinical datasets that must be adequately considered to ensure the training of robust, generalizable models. 36 More recent model architectures such as transformerbased or hybrid CNN-transformers are better-positioned to be generalizable as they are able to: (i) incorporate textural patterns (using CNN capabilities), (ii) capture relationships transformers). 12 However, these architectures tend to exhibit suboptimal performance in stroke lesion segmentation when compared to brain tumors, due to the immense variability in infarct size, shape, location and other presentation of stroke lesions. 12, 38 Moreover, effective implementation of such advanced models is again dependent on large, heterogenous, multi-institution training data. 39, 37 Expectedly, the top performing segmentation methods trained on ATLAS v2.1 (Table 1) adopted image augmentation techniques to introduce variation in original images or to simulate synthetic disruptions in structural anatomy. 34, 40 Various other data augmentation techniques including spatial transformations and intensity perturbations 41 have also been proposed. 42 While data augmentation techniques can improve model generalizability and accuracy by introducing additional variability into existing images, they cannot fully replicate the diversity of stroke lesion locations, sizes, and anatomical patterns captured by real patient data, underscoring the continued importance of expanding both the size and heterogeneity of training datasets like ATLAS to improve lesion segmentation performance.

To address the evident need for larger, more diverse training data for enhancing automated lesion segmentation algorithms, here, we present the ATLAS v3.0 dataset, containing 1,815 T1w stroke brain MRIs and accompanying manual lesion segmentations from 61 research cohorts across 10 countries. The release of ATLAS v3.0 comes with nearly 43% more data, replacing and expanding upon ATLAS v2.1. ATLAS v3.0 enhances valuable data properties of preceding ATLAS versions, 9, 10 including: (i) sizeable heterogeneity in lesion location across diverse brain regions, (ii) diversity in lesion size across research cohorts, representing a spectrum of multiple smaller, punctuate lesions to large lesions spanning various anatomical structures, and (iii) further variations in data acquisition protocols, sources and scanner parameters (see Data Characteristics and Supplementary Material, Tables 1 2 3; Figure 1). ATLAS v3.0 is intended to continue to support the technical growth in DL model architectures discussed above. Another key motivation for the release of ATLAS v3.0 is to support the translational applicability of segmentation methods to heterogeneous lesion contexts for enhanced research and clinical utility across the stroke recovery continuum. To that end, we retain our focus on providing T1w stroke brain MRIs, with data primarily from the subacute and chronic phases of stroke. 9, 10 T1w imaging was strategically chosen due to several important reasons. First, although diffusion weighted imaging (DWI) is the gold standard for visualizing the extent of acute stroke damage, 43 T1w images have been more commonly used in the subacute and chronic phases for post-stroke rehabilitation research to inform the prediction of functional outcomes. 44, 45, 46 Second, unlike DWI, T1w images preserve lesion visibility across varied timepoints and can be used to better characterize structural changes in the cortex with progressive chronicity. 47, 45 Third, T1w images can be used to quantify post-stroke secondary sequelae (e.g., global atrophy 48- 50 and white matter hyperintensities) 51 without the need for additional multimodal correction, which could be beneficial for longitudinal analyses. Fourth, newer hybrid CNN-Transformer architectures may be better suited to utilize the structural and anatomical granularity of T1w images, thereby enhancing the ability of CNNs to capture focal edge details with Transformers' ability to contextualize global intra-or extra-lesion relationships. 12 Finally, some recent approaches can be used to train a modality-agnostic model on T1w images (e.g., by generating augmented images using T1w MRIs that simulate other modalities), and subsequently test the trained model on real images from other modalities (DWI, T2, FLAIR, etc.), thus broadening the possible application of this work. 52 Consistent with previous releases, the ATLAS v3.0 public training dataset has been released in two formats. First, we provide a minimally preprocessed dataset with native space images as a default to ensure algorithm developers can (i) select their own method of image normalization and (ii) develop methods that use as much or as little preprocessing. Additionally, this ensures preservation of uncompressed, original voxel dimensions and avoids any anatomical disruptions or loss of resolution that may be caused from registering images into MNI space. We have attempted to preserve original image properties to represent real-world data acquisition properties more closely, and have performed only one standardized, minimal preprocessing step (i.e. brain extraction to retain anonymity of participant data). Second, we also provide a preprocessed dataset, with brain-extracted images; all subjects in this dataset have undergone additional intensity normalization and registration to MNI space. Notably, ATLAS v3.0 includes additional brain MRIs of stroke subjects with visible chronic lesions from the open-source Stroke Outcome Optimization Project (SOOP). 53 SOOP is a retrospective dataset of MRI scans acquired during acute stroke care that also reveal chronic lesions from previous strokes. Stroke lesions in the SOOP data were originally traced on diffusion weighted images, however, lesion masks were subsequently coregistered to and manually corrected in T1w space to preserve the spatial resolution benefits provided by T1w images, essential for accurate lesion boundary delineation. 54 This also enabled complementary information from both modalities to be used to determine lesion presence and extent, while maintaining consistency with the rest of the ATLAS dataset. Furthermore, we no longer retain the training/validation split of data from 955 subjects (training dataset = 655, validation dataset = 300) from ATLAS v2.1. Instead, we provide all 955 subjects as training data, including both T1w MRIs with lesion masks for all of these subjects. This should allow for wider validation of algorithms previously trained on ATLAS v2.1 and enable algorithm developers to choose their own validation approach. In addition, we have increased our total training data by 498 subjects (1453 from 955). Finally, we continue to retain the hidden generalizability dataset from ATLAS v2.1 and have added 46 new subjects from an unseen, independent research cohort to this dataset. This was done to increase the number of subjects available to test the generalizability of algorithm performance on unseen data (e.g., in the ISLES 2026 Challenge; see Data Characteristics below). Together, we anticipate that these enhancements will lead to the development of more improved lesion segmentation methods and enable robust research using our large, diverse dataset of stroke brain MRIs.

Identifying and tracing lesions

We followed the same lesion identification and tracing steps as previously outlined for the ATLAS v2.1 9 dataset for all subjects. For subjects in one cohort (i.e. SOOP), segmented lesion masks were made available by the contributors; trained ATLAS team members carefully reviewed and corrected lesion boundaries following our standardized manual lesion segmentation protocol. 4 This was done to ensure lesion masks were made as uniform as possible across the entire ATLAS v3.0 dataset. Briefly, tracers opened each individual T1w MRI in ITK-SNAP and carefully inspected them slice-by-slice, beginning at the posterior aspect of the brain (i.e., the cerebellum). Tracers were encouraged to scan for lesions across all 3 image planes (axial, coronal, sagittal), but trace only on the axial or coronal views. 10 Lesion tracing was performed using a stylus (e.g., on an Apple iPad) or a mouse. All identified lesions from each individual subject were saved together as a single segmentation mask, using a BIDS compliant naming scheme (further details in Data Records). Guidance on distinguishing between lesions and imaging-based markers of cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD; e.g., lacunes and white matter hyperintensities) was provided to tracers, in accordance with the STRIVE (Standards for Reporting Vascular changes on Neuroimaging) criteria 58 and further reviewed by a trained neuroradiologist with >10 years of experience (G.B.). A lacune was identified as bigger than perivascular spaces (3-15 mm), asymmetric, with irregular shapes (often triangular). Tracers were prompted to pay closer attention to subcortical structures such as the basal ganglia nuclei where lacunae are commonly found (Figure 1). Perivascular spaces were noted to be symmetric, smaller than 3 mm, and with regular tubular shapes following the course of blood vessels (Figure 1). Periventricular white matter hyperintensities were noted to be contiguous with the borders of lateral ventricles. On the other hand, tracers were guided to check throughout the course of the white matter to locate deep white matter hyperintensities. 58 Tracers were instructed to only capture stroke lesions in their annotation masks; CSVD markers were excluded. For the last small subset of subjects in ATLAS v3.0 (n=66), we introduced a modification to our lesion segmentation workflow. The primary goal of this modification was to test whether automated lesion segmentation outputs generated by training a DL model with transformer-based implementation could be used as a starting point, thus reducing the time associated with manual tracing while still involving human review and correction for accuracy (i.e., by adopting a human-in-the loop (HITL) approach) 35 . Detailed information regarding model architecture, training parameters, and model performance have been described in detail by Khan et al. 35 Briefly, we trained a publicly available nnU-Net model using ATLAS v2.1 data 35, 60 to automatically segment stroke lesions. This architecture was adopted for its ability to configure itself, including any training, pre-and post-processing steps involved, for new tasks and required limited manual intervention. Outputs generated by the automated segmentation method were subsequently corrected and quality checked for accuracy by two trained members with >3 years of manual lesion tracing experience (S.C. and M.H.K).

Preprocessing normalization, registration and defacing 601 602

To reduce any possibilities of re-identification that may occur with traditional defacing 603 approaches, 61 we used SynthStrip, 62 an open-source deep-learning based method 604 available with FreeSurfer (https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/docs/synthstrip/), to perform a brain-only extraction, thus anonymizing each image. SynthStrip also enables more robust brain extraction for brains impacted by pathology such as stroke. As an update from prior ATLAS versions, for ATLAS v3.0 registration, we used SynthMorph, 63 another open-source, deep-learning based method packaged with FreeSurfer to register images (https://martinos.org/malte/synthmorph/#tool). We implemented an affine-only registration (as opposed to a nonlinear warp) to the MNI152 template 64 ; this was done to avoid the lesion biasing spatial normalization, thereby preserving its volume, shape, and boundaries, as well as adjacent anatomy. The segmented brain mask was then coregistered to template space using nearest neighbor interpolation. Advantages of SynthMorph include its ability to register brain images of any volume, size, resolution or contrast without the need for prior preprocessing. After preprocessing, we visually assessed whether each subject's lesion mask registered properly to MNI space. Stroke lesion volumes (calculated as the total number of voxels within the lesion mask over anatomical regions of interest) were then obtained using our publicly available PALS toolbox (https://github.com/npnl/PALS). We note that the lesion volumes obtained from PALS were not released publicly with the training dataset (n=1453) and are only reported at a group-level across all subjects, in this paper. Technical Validation

We used our standardized and previously published lesion segmentation protocol in the preparation of ATLAS v3.0; details of the technical validation parameters of our protocol have been previously published. 4 Numerous automated lesion segmentation methods have been developed using the ATLAS v2.1 dataset, 17, 67, 68, 69, 70, 71 several of which have been summarized above (Table 1, Background and Summary). We performed manual quality checks for all subjects included in the ATLAS v3.0 dataset; segmented lesion masks in the native space were quality controlled by trained team members following our standardized protocol. After preprocessing, lesion masks were examined for appropriate registration to the MNI template space.

Lesion Segmentation Challenges

The provision of a hidden generalizability dataset to ensure fair performance evaluation of automated algorithms submitted to lesion segmentation challenges remains a key purpose of ATLAS v3.0, as done previously with ATLAS v1.2 and v2.1. Consistent with previous ATLAS dataset releases 9, 10 , the Medical Image Computing and Computer Assisted Intervention (MICCAI) Ischemic Stroke Lesion Segmentation Challenge (ISLES) 2026 edition (https://isles-26.grand-challenge.org/) for ATLAS v3.0 was launched. With this iteration of the challenge, we hoped to address the out-of-domain generalizability gaps that emerged from ISLES 2022. The ATLAS v3.0 training data for ISLES '26 was publicly released on April 24, 2026; the challenge concluded on August 19, 2026. The ISLES '26 challenge called for participants to develop automated stroke lesion segmentation algorithms using ATLAS v3.0; participants were ranked based on both technical proficiency and clinical impact. Performance evaluation metrics included the (i) DSC, which measures the overlap between the predicted and ground truth segmentation masks and normalizes the overlap based on the sizes of the combined regions, (ii) Absolute Volume Difference (a measure of the difference between the true and predicted lesion volumes), (iii) Absolute Lesion Count Difference (a measure of the difference between the number of ground truth and predicted lesions), (iv) Lesion-wise F1 score (which measures whether a singular voxel of a ground truth lesion is detected) and, (v) Area Under the Precision-recall Curve (which is computed as the weighted mean of precision values interpolated across operating points, given weightage based on the increase in recall). 72, 73 Further details on sample metrics can be found on GitHub (https://github.com/ezequieldlrosa/isles26/tree/main/utils). For this challenge, participants were encouraged to focus on algorithm development using only T1w images provided in the native coordinate system. The use of standardized or normalized versions of the data was not permitted. Detailed information regarding the ISLES '26 challenge has been described by de la Rosa et al. 73 Consistent with previous versions, this challenge was conducted in three phases. 74 In the training phase, along with the first batch of training data, a Python evaluation script was released, which could be used to compute the above-mentioned performance evaluation metrics, enabling participants to test and iterate models accordingly. The sanity-check phase required participants to submit a sample docker to test their algorithm's functionality on organizers' servers. The test phase required participants to submit a docker containing their final algorithm submissions which were evaluated to declare rankings. 74 We received 261 submissions for the Preliminary Docker Evaluation (sanity check) phase, and 65 submissions for the final test phase. Submissions for ISLES '26 were received from around the world, with the highest recorded submissions from China (60), India (53), United States (47), and Canada (14). The ISLES '26 official rankings and final performance metrics will be announced later in 2026. The widespread use of the ATLAS datasets highlights their impact on the stroke research and medical image segmentation communities. To that end, we aim to continue to provide as much data as possible for training and improving lesion segmentation algorithms. As previously outlined, the ATLAS datasets are compiled by an international consortium of stroke researchers (ENIGMA Stroke Recovery Working Group) 75 , which routinely receives stroke brain MRI data from all over the world. Future ATLAS releases may incorporate clinically relevant neuroimaging markers of CSVD such as perivascular spaces and white matter hyperintensities, making them available along with lesion masks. This may aid in the development of algorithms sensitive to delineating pathology involving CSVD markers and reducing mis-segmentations. 76 Further, successive lesion segmentation challenges may focus on developing automated segmentation methods that expansively capture relevant CSVD markers in addition to stroke lesions. To conclude, here, we release ATLAS v3.0, which includes a total of 1815 T1w stroke brain MRIs, with 1453 containing corresponding lesion masks as training data, and a hidden generalizability dataset (n=362) which is made available only for lesion segmentation challenges. The primary goal of the ATLAS v3.0 dataset release is to better represent the heterogeneity of lesion presentation across stroke brain MRIs. A nearly 43% increase in data from ATLAS v2.1, our dataset captures several sources of variation, closely representing real-world data (including lesion size, anatomical location, time of MRI acquisition etc.). Additionally, we anticipate that as model architectures like transformers 12 gain popularity, larger and more diverse datasets may be beneficial for effective model training and generalization, although human-in-the-loop approaches are likely to provide the optimal solutions.
