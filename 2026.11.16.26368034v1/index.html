How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Background

A defining characteristic of pregnancy is drastic changes to the maternal immune system, which has the dual mandate of protecting the mother and fetus from pathogens while also tolerating the semi-allogeneic fetus (1, 2). This involves dynamic changes in both innate and adaptive immune functions, including alterations in immune cell composition, cytokine signaling, and inflammatory activity across gestation (1). The proper orchestration of these changes is especially important given fetal plasticity during development and the potential impact of prenatal influences on offspring health (3).

Dysregulation of maternal immunological adaptations has been implicated in pregnancy complications such as preeclampsia, preterm birth, gestational diabetes, and offspring asthma (4) (5) (6).

In recent decades, vitamin D 3 (henceforth referred to as vitamin D) has emerged as an important immunomodulator in humans (7). While contained in some foods, the majority of vitamin D is synthesized in the skin via photochemical reactions by ultraviolet radiation. Vitamin D then undergoes 25-hydroxylation to form 25-hydroxyvitamin D 3 (i.e., 25(OH)D 3 and henceforth referred to as 25OHD), which is the primary circulating vitamin D metabolite found in humans and the primary metabolite of interest for this study. 25OHD subsequently undergoes 1 -hydroxylation to form 1,25(OH) 2 D 3 , the active form of vitamin D that affects transcriptional regulation by binding with the vitamin D receptor (VDR) transcription factor (TF). Upon binding with 1,25(OH) 2 D 3 , VDR, often as a heterodimer with retinoid X receptor (RXR), interacts with co-activators such as histone acetyltransferases to modify chromatin structure and gene expression (8).

Vitamin D affects both the innate and adaptive immune system (7). Most immune cells express VDR and are able to convert 25OHD into 1,25(OH) 2 D 3 (9). Antigen presenting cells (APCs), especially dendritic cells (DC), sit at the interface between innate and adaptive immune responses. Studies have shown vitamin D to suppress DC maturation (10, 11) and to promote T regulatory (Treg) cell development (12, 13). In general, these findings point towards a tolerogenic effect of vitamin D on the immune system. One potential mechanism by which vitamin D mediates these effects is the regulation of gene expression via epigenetic modifications, particularly DNA methylation (DNAm) (14, 15). DNAm, a heritable epigenomic modification with distinct effects on gene transcription, involves the addition of a methyl group to the 5-carbon position of a cytosine nucleotide, catalyzed by DNA methyltransferases. The vast majority of DNAm in humans occurs at cytosine-phosphate-guanine (CpG) dinucleotides (16). CpG-rich regions of the genome form "islands", often near promoter regions and characterized by hypo-methylation (17). There are studies suggesting that vitamin D is able to promote DNA demethylation, mostly in promoter regions of Vitamin D Receptor (VDR) target genes (14). This demethylation process may occur passively over several cycles of DNA replication, or it may be more active via the recruitment of ten-eleven translocation (TET) enzymes that induce DNA demethylation; however, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear (14).

Our understanding of vitamin D-associated changes to the methylome of pregnant populations is incomplete. We hypothesize that vitamin D may alter DNAm to promote a tolerogenic environment (i.e., one which induces immune tolerance). This . epigenome-wide association study (EWAS) aimed to explore associations between maternal DNAm and maternal 25OHD levels from blood samples of pregnant women enrolled in the Vitamin D Antenatal Asthma Reduction Trial (VDAART) (18). We then identified regions of vitamin D-associated methylation and functionally annotated them to genes, regulatory elements, and biological processes. We subsequently assessed the association between DNAm of CpGs and the expression of their annotated genes.

Additionally, we investigated the relationship between DNA methylation entropy, an information-theoretic measure that quantifies the heterogeneity in methylation status within a bulk sample, and vitamin D levels (see Figure 1 for an analysis overview).

Cohort characteristics

Epigenome-wide association study of maternal vitamin D levels reveals differentially methylated CpGs and genomic regions associated with 25OHD

To identify the epigenetic marks associated with maternal vitamin D levels during pregnancy, we conducted an epigenome-wide association study (EWAS) on DNA methylation data derived from maternal blood samples taken at two timepoints during pregnancy. Observations from both timepoints were included in linear mixed effect models to simultaneously capture both between-and within-subject (i.e., across time) effects. These probe-level models revealed two significant CpGs (Figure 2). Probe cg17097782_TC11, on chromosome 2, overlaps with several regulatory elements: the gene body of odd-skipped related transcription factor 1 (OSR1); a CpG island; a distal enhancer targeting the visinin-like 1 (VSNL1) and growth differentiation factor 7 (GDF7) genes; and a 25OHD-sensitive open chromatin region. Probe cg11176481_TC21, on chromosome 5, also overlaps with a distal enhancer which targets the forkhead box D1 (FOXD1) gene. The genes annotated to these differentially methylated probes (DMP) are enriched in pathways related to bone mineralization, SMAD protein signaling, transforming growth factor (TGF-) signaling, and embryonic organogenesis (Additional File 2). See Additional File 3, Sheets 1-4, for detailed DMP results. We did not observe any significant interaction effects between gestational age and 25OHD, suggesting that the effects of vitamin D on maternal DNAm are independent of gestational age. . To boost the epigenetic signal and increase statistical power (19), we next conducted a regional DNAm analysis (Methods), which resulted in 84 differentially methylated regions (DMRs), containing at least 2 CpGs, with an average region size of 116 bp. A sensitivity analysis shows that the method used to identify DMRs is fairly robust in terms of DMR count, average CpG count, and average region size when varying the distance cutoff and bin size (Additional File 4). CpG-level model results for CpGs constituting the identified DMRs can be found in Additional File 3, sheet 1. The heatmap in Figure 3.a reveals that most hypo-methylated regions are negatively associated with 25OHD, while meso-(intermediate) and hyper-methylated regions show positive associations with 25OHD. Figure 3.b further illustrates that methylation levels decreased and increased for hypo-and hyper-methylated DMRs, respectively, from the first to third trimester. These results suggest that an increase in vitamin D levels may stabilize existing methylation regimes in the vitamin D-sensitive parts of the epigenome by lowering DNAm in hypo-methylated DMRs and elevating DNAm in hyper-methylated DMRs.

Vitamin D-associated differentially methylated regions map to distinct genomic contexts

Providing genomic context is important to understand the functional implications of the identified epigenetic marks and regions. Figure 3.a (right) reveals numerous similarities and contrasts between hypo-, meso-, and hyper-methylated DMRs in terms of overlapping genomic features. All DMRs broadly overlap with gene bodies and co-factor binding sites, and to a large degree, enhancers. On the other hand, hypo-methylated DMRs (in blue) show broad overlap with CpG archipelago and promoter regions, whereas hyper-methylated DMRs (in red) lack any overlap with these regions. Hypo-methylated regions also show a modest overlap with VDR binding sites, in contrast to hyper-and meso-methylated regions. Additionally, meso-and hyper-methylated DMRs show little to no overlap with 25OHD-sensitive regulatory regions, whereas there is some overlap for hypomethylated DMRs, especially with 25OHD-sensitive open chromatin regions. Overall, these patterns may suggest that the downstream regulatory effects of vitamin D are primarily carried out by the hypo-methylated DMRs.

25OHD-associated regions are functionally enriched in innate and adaptive immunity, chromatin modeling and cross-presentation-related pathways

The identified DMRs were annotated to 342 unique genes (Methods). Of these, 35.4% were mapped via proximity and the rest were mapped via enhancer-target interactions.

These genes were significantly enriched in 42 pathways (FDR < 0.05) (Figure 4), which clustered into pathway groups that include MHC class I and II antigen processing and presentation, immune response, and ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling. Annotated genes related to antigen presentation include inhibitor of nuclear factor kappa B kinase subunit beta (IKBKB) and dynamin 2 (DNM2), as well as members of the human leukocyte antigen (HLA), transporter associated with antigen processing (TAP), and proteasome subunit beta (PSMB) gene families. For a complete list of enriched pathways and their associated DMRs and genes, see Additional File 3, Sheet 7.

An increase in vitamin D levels may promote methylation stability in the hypo-and hyper-methylated regions of the maternal epigenome

Our DMR analysis reveals that increased 25OHD levels are associated with decreased methylation in hypo-methylated regions and increased methylation in hyper-methylated regions, which suggests that 25OHD promotes methylation homogeneity (i.e., lower DNAm entropy) at vitamin D-associated regions across cell populations. Motivated by these observations, we hypothesized that the entropy of hypo-and hyper-methylated DMRs (Methods) would show negative associations with 25OHD levels.

Most hypo-and some hyper-methylated DMRs show lower average entropy relative to most hyper-and meso-methylated DMRs (Figure 3.a), indicating increased methylation homogeneity of cell populations across samples for the former group of DMRs versus heterogeneous methylation patterns for the latter group. As hypothesized, both hypo-and hyper-methylated regions show negative associations between entropy and 25OHD (Figure 3.a), which suggests that increased 25OHD levels may reinforce existing methylation regimes, thereby promoting methylation stability (lower entropy) in these regions. We also observe a broad decrease in entropy from first to third trimester for hypo-and hyper-methylated DMRs (Figure 3.c). The entropy-25OHD association was significant (FDR < 0.05) for 80 of the 84 DMRs (95%). This association reflects a combination of both between-subject and within-subject (i.e., across time) associations between 25OHD and entropy, which are not distinguished in the model. There were no significant interaction effects between 25OHD and gestational age on entropy. We observed significantly lower entropy for DMRs overlapping archipelago regions, VDR cofactor binding sites, promoters, and 25OHD-sensitive TADs, when compared to DMRs not overlapping these regions (Figure 5.a). However, no significant differences were observed for the effect estimates of entropy-25OHD associations when comparing DMRs that do or do not overlap various regulatory regions (Figure 5.b). Together, these findings suggest that while entropy of DMRs can help distinguish between different genomic regulatory contexts, this feature does not necessarily generalize to the potential effect of vitamin D on entropy.

To investigate whether our observations on entropy are more extreme than expected by chance, we constructed an empirical background of CpG sets that mimic several properties of our DMRs (Methods) and calculated regional entropy (averaged across samples), as well as entropy-25OHD associations, using these background CpG sets. Most of the observed entropy levels for DMRs fall in the low entropy range, although several fall in the high entropy range, whereas the background distribution reveals a bimodal distribution with the primary mode in the mid-entropy range and the secondary mode in the low-entropy range (Figure 6.a). For a more global view of the entropy of our identified DMRs, we take the average of the sample-wise averages of entropy over all DMRs and compare this to an empirical background distribution, which shows that it is extremely unlikely to observe by chance a set of 84 regions whose overall average entropy is as low or lower than our observed value (Figure 6.b). Finally, when we applied this bootstrap analysis on the estimated effect of 25OHD on regional entropy from linear mixed models, with coefficient estimates weighted by 1 minus the pvalue, we found that the majority of the entropy-25OHD effects for the DMRs are negative and fall in the left tail of the empirical distribution (Figure 6.c). When considering the average association between entropy and 25OHD across all DMRs, we observe a much stronger negative association than that of randomly chosen sets of 84 regions (Figure 6.d).

Discussion

In the present study, we found that 25OHD levels are associated with DNA methylation of sites and regions during pregnancy, which correspond to genes and regulatory regions related to components of maternal-fetal immune tolerance such as TGFsignaling, antigen processing and presentation, and lysosomal membranes. Increased 25OHD levels corresponded to decreased DNAm entropy of hypo-and hypermethylated regions, while entropy in these regions substantially decreased from the first to third trimester. Furthermore, the DNAm of many of these vitamin D-sensitive regions were found to be significantly associated with the expression of their annotated genes. These findings suggest a functional impact of vitamin D on immune processes, in part mediated by DNAm.

Perhaps the strongest signal from our regional DNAm analysis comes from genomic regions associated with antigen processing and presentation, a critical immune process involving antigen presentation from antigen presenting cells (APCs) along with costimulatory molecules and cytokines to direct T cell differentiation (24). Two DMRs associated with antigen presentation (DMR 10 and 40) show significant associations between their DNAm and the gene expression of their annotated genes: IKBKB and DNM2. IKBKB encodes for a kinase which phosphorylates an inhibitor of the NF-B pathway, causing the inhibitor to degrade (21, 22). The NF-B pathway is involved in the induction of pro-inflammatory genes in innate immune cells, as well as the development of inflammatory T cells (25, 26). Previous studies have shown that the inhibition of NF-B in dendritic cells can lead to down-regulation of MHC-II proteins, co-stimulatory molecules (e.g., CD80, CD40), and pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-12, TNF-), thereby promoting immune tolerance (27, 28). DMR 10, whose DNAm was found to have a negative association with 25OHD, lies in exon 1 of IKBKB, whose expression was positively associated with DNAm of DMR 10. Taken together, these associations suggest that vitamin D may promote immune tolerance by inhibiting NF-B activity via decreased methylation in IKBKB and a reduction in IKBKB expression. DMR 40, whose DNAm levels show a negative association with 25OHD levels in our cohort, lies in a distal enhancer that targets DNM2, a dynamin molecule involved in antigen presentation which facilitates endocytosis, whose expression is negatively associated with DNAm of the distal enhancer. Our data therefore suggest a positive association between vitamin D and DNM2 expression. In T cells, DNM2 has been shown to be critical for actin remodeling of the immunological synapse during antigen presentation, which promotes binding with APCs (29). Therefore, vitamin D may lead to prolonged binding between T cells and APCs, thereby limiting the APC's ability to detach and activate other T cells (28, 30). Consistent with this hypothesis, a key adhesion molecule, Intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM1) is also targeted by the distal enhancer overlapping DMR 40 and is negatively associated with DNAm, suggesting a positive relationship between 25OHD and ICAM1 expression. Moreover, consistent with the vitamin D-mediated inhibition of NF-B discussed above, inhibition of NF-B in dendritic cells has been shown to up-regulate ICAM1 (28).

The lysosomal membrane pathway was also significantly enriched for genes identified by our DNAm-25OHD analysis. Initially viewed as an intracellular compartment that facilitates autophagy and recycling of cellular waste, lysosomes have emerged as important signaling platforms, including at the interface between innate and adaptive immunity (31) (32) (33). The proteolytic activity of lysosomes is a crucial step for MHC class II antigen presentation and CD4 + T cell activation (34). We identified several genes-TMEM203, RAB2A, SLC44A2, and SLC12A9, annotated to DMRs 14, 17, 40, and 80, respectively-which participate in the lysosomal pathway in distinct ways (35) (36) (37) (38) and whose expression is significantly associated with the DNAm of their corresponding DMR. Our regional DNAm-25OHD analysis therefore implicates two closely related pathways-antigen processing and presentation, and lysosomes-which sit at the intersection of innate and adaptive immunity (39). The exact mechanisms by which vitamin D alters DNAm in these regions and how these alterations might confer immune advantages to mother and fetus during pregnancy remain to be further elucidated.

The TGFsignaling pathway, along with the related SMAD protein signal transduction pathway, was identified as being enriched in annotated genes from our initial probe-level EWAS, primarily the GDF7 gene, which encodes for a secreted protein that regulates the SMAD family of transcription factors involved in TGFsignaling. TGF-is a cytokine that plays critical roles in regulating the differentiation and function of various immune cell types, including the suppression of Th1/Th2 cell differentiation and the promotion of the generation of peripheral Treg cells (40), which help maintain tolerance towards the allogeneic fetus during pregnancy (6). A DMP (cg17097782_TC11) located on chromosome 2 lies in a distal enhancer targeting the GDF7 gene, which has been shown to positively regulate Treg cells via increased Forkhead Box P3 (FOXP3) expression (41). These findings may suggest that vitamin D promotes a tolerant immune environment via decreased methylation in a distal enhancer targeting the GDF7 gene, which may result in an increase in GDF7 expression and the positive regulation of Treg cells.

A major observation from this study is that higher vitamin D associates with decreased methylation in hypo-methylated regions and with increased methylation in hyper-methylated regions. These hypo-methylated DMRs in large part overlap CGIs and promoter regions. Furthermore, a substantial portion of hypo-methylated regions become accessible with vitamin D treatment in monocyte cell lines, which may potentiate treatment-specific epigenetic regulation in these regions. These findings are corroborated by our DNAm entropy analysis, which directly investigated the relationship between vitamin D and the heterogeneity of methylation status across the cell population. For both hypo-and hyper-methylated DMRs, entropy was significantly negatively associated with 25OHD while simultaneously showing large decrease from the first to third trimester. Taken together, these observations may point to a stabilizing effect of vitamin D on methylation patterns in these regions, wherein hypo-and hypermethylated regimes are reinforced through increased vitamin D levels.

Our additional entropy-based analyses also identified DMRs overlapping CGIs, promoters, VDR cofactor binding sites, and TADs as having significantly lower DNAm entropy compared to non-overlapping DMRs. These results align with previous studies, which found lower DNAm entropy values in CGIs and transcription start sites compared to other genomic regions such as exons, introns, and intergenic regions (42, 43). Our results show decreased DNAm heterogeneity (i.e., lower DNAm entropy) across cell populations for key regulatory elements involved in chromatin structure and gene transcription. Additionally, the DNAm entropy of identified DMRs was found to be lower than expected by chance when compared to random regions of comparable size and CpG density. These results indicate that genomic regions whose DNAm levels associate with 25OHD levels show significantly higher homogeneity in methylation status across cell populations.

The present study comes with several limitations. The Illumina V2 epigenome chip covers 935,000 CpG methylation sites or 3.3% of the human epigenome centered on CpG islands but certainly not the whole epigenome. Our blood samples were assayed in bulk, thus preventing a single-cell resolution and requiring deconvolution of cell type proportions to control for confounding in statistical models. Additionally, there are fewer than 100 samples with paired DNAm and RNA-seq data, which limits statistical power and the generalizability of observed DNAm-gene expression relationships. EWAS as a statistical methodology may be underpowered to detect all of the relevant CpG-25OHD associations. Further studies with whole-genome sequencing, single-cell resolution of DNAm, ideally coupled with data on gene expression and chromatin structure, would allow researchers to distinguish between immune cell types when investigating the effects of vitamin D on methylation dynamics during pregnancy.

Our study is of modest sample size and also lacks a replication cohort, which warrants caution regarding the generalizability of our findings to other populations of pregnant women.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we found that DNAm patterns in pregnant women associate with vitamin D levels, localizing to hypo-and hyper-methylated genomic regions associated with key processes at the intersection of the innate and adaptive immune systems, primarily antigen processing and presentation and lysosomal pathways. The expression of several genes corresponding to these pathways were also correlated with DNAm levels. Furthermore, these vitamin D-associated regions show significantly higher homogeneity in methylation status across cell populations. Our findings suggest that increased vitamin D may promote methylome stability in genomic regions associated with antigen processing and presentation, potentially promoting immune tolerance towards the allogeneic fetus during pregnancy.

Methods

VDAART Design

VDAART is a randomized, double blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial of vitamin D supplementation (4,000 IU plus a multivitamin with 400 IU daily) versus placebo (placebo pill plus a multivitamin with 400 IU daily) in pregnant women, given after baseline measurements were taken, to prevent adverse pregnancy outcomes including asthma and recurrent wheeze in their children. Eligible participants were women between the ages of 18 and 39 years with the estimated enrollment gestational ages between 10 and 18 weeks (singleton) who had a history of physician-diagnosed asthma or atopy, or a partner (biologic father of the child) with a history of physician-diagnosed asthma or atopy, and no history of smoking or current nicotine product use. Eligible participants were screened per VDAART protocol and enrolled if eligible. A participant flow chart is available in Additional File 6. Details of the trial design, protocol, and primary and secondary outcomes have been published previously (18, 44, 45). For blood sample collection and processing details and covariate assessment details, see (18, 44). VDAART was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of the participating clinical centers and the data coordinating center, with pregnant women signing informed consent at the enrollment visit covering both primary and secondary data analyses. In this study, we included pregnant women enrolled in VDAART who provided any postnatal data, up to the child's age of 3 years, and who had DNAm data that passed quality control steps (Table 1).

DNA methylation data processing

Blood samples were drawn from pregnant women participating in VDAART at the first (10-18 weeks of gestation) and third (32-38 weeks of gestation) trimesters (referred to as M1 and M2, respectively, throughout the text) and stored at -80°C. DNA was extracted from 947 blood samples from 477 individuals (477 samples from M1, 470 samples from M2, 470 overlap), using the EZ DNA Methylation-Gold Kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA). Samples were randomized across slides, while keeping samples from the same individual together on the same slide. The Illumina Infinium Methylation EPIC v2 array (Illumina, San Diego, CA) was used to perform genome-wide methylation assays of bisulfite-converted DNA samples across a total of 936,990 CpG sites. Preprocessing was performed using R programming language v4.5.1 (R Core Team, 2025) and the minfi package v1.54.1 (46). The sample quality control steps included removing technical replicates, samples with failed bisulfite conversion, samples with genotype or sex mismatches, and outlier samples. This resulted in the removal of 62 of 947 samples, for a total of 885 retained samples (Additional File 7). The probe quality control steps included removing probes with poor quality or non-unique mapping; probes with large detection p-values; probes labeled as "new variant" (without an rsID); and probes whose body, targeted CpG site, or single base extension overlapped with a common single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) with minor allele frequency 5%. This resulted in the removal of 124,320 of 936,990 probes, for a total of 812,670 retained probes (Additional File 7).

The normal-exponential out-of-band method (minfi::preprocessNoob function, following the package::function notation used in R) was applied for background correction and dye-bias normalization. The functional normalization method (minfi::preprocessFunnorm) was then used for between-array normalization. Finally, methylation values were logit-transformed (log ) with offset term ) to obtain socalled M-values, which were used as input for linear modeling.

DNA methylation data analysis

Linear mixed effects models were used to assess the relationships between probe-level DNAm M-values and 25OHD levels (ng/mL), with DNAm as the dependent variable. The models were adjusted for maternal age, BMI at first trimester, gestational age at sample collection, sample collection site, maternal asthma status, vitamin D treatment randomization group, season of sample collection, the first five ancestry principal components derived from VDAART genotype data (see (47) for details), and cell type proportion estimates. Illumina array slide and participant ID were included as random intercepts. Additionally, an interaction term between 25OHD and gestational age was included, to assess if the DNAm-25OHD relationship changes during pregnancy. Cell type proportions were estimated at each timepoint via EpiDISH::epidish in R (48), using robust partial correlation and a whole blood reference dataset of DNAm markers of 12 blood cell types for the EPIC array (49). The log ratios of cell type proportion estimates, with neutrophils as the referent, were included as covariates in linear mixed effects models. The function dream from the variancePartition package in the R programming language was used to fit the linear mixed effects models, using restricted maximum likelihood for model fitting and the Satterthwaite method for the estimation of effective degrees of freedom for hypothesis testing (50).

Linear mixed effects model results of adjacent CpGs were aggregated via the Enmix::ipdmr function in R, to identify differentially methylated regions (DMRs) (51).

Single probes were excluded. Default function arguments for Enmix::ipdmr were used, including a region-level FDR threshold of 0.05, to identify DMRs.

Differentially methylated region annotations

The CpG sets representing DMRs were annotated to genes in two ways: via proximity and via enhancer targets (Figure 1). For the proximity annotation, DMRs were annotated to genes if they overlap with either the promoter region or the gene body. For the enhancer annotation, enhancer-like regulatory elements and their putative gene targets were obtained from SCREEN v4 (52). DMRs overlapping with an enhancer region were annotated to the gene targets of that enhancer.

DMRs were further annotated to additional putative regulatory elements: promoter regions; CpG islands, shores, and shelves (henceforth denoted collectively as the "archipelago"); VDR bindings sites (BS); and VDR co-factor BS.

Putative promoter regions were obtained from the SCREEN database and are defined as regions located near transcription start sites (TSS) with high chromatin accessibility and H3K4me3 signal, with data restricted to those originating from blood-based cell types. CpG archipelago annotations were obtained from Illumina's manifest file on (EPIC v2.0 A2).

VDR and VDR co-factor binding sites were obtained from ChIP Atlas, while also restricting data to those originating from blood-based cell types (53). A transcription factor (TF) was considered a potential co-factor of VDR if it has an average colocalization score greater than 1 (more details at https://github.com/inutano/chip-atlas/wiki#colocalization_doc, accessed 2026/06/01).

The binding of VDR with 1,25OHD affects how VDR interacts with the genome and how it modifies chromatin structure. Therefore, data on 25OHD-sensitive regions from THP-1 cell lines (human monocyte) were obtained from publicly available sources to further investigate vitamin D-responsive regulatory elements. Genomic features showing significant differences between 25OHD-stimulated and control samples include H3K27ac and H3K4me3 histone marks (Table S2 in ( 54)), open chromatin (Supplementary Data File 008 in ( 55)), topologically associating domains (TADs)(Table S3 in ( 56)), VDR binding sites (Table S2 in ( 57)), and VDR target genes (Table S4 in (57)).

Gene expression data processing and quality control

Raw whole-blood mRNA-seq reads were processed using bcbio-nextgen (version 1.2.9). Read quality was assessed using FastQC (version 0.11.9), followed by adapter trimming and quality filtering with cutadapt (version 3.4). Processed reads were aligned to the GRCh38 human reference genome using STAR (version 2.7.10a).

Gene-level counts were generated using featureCounts from the Subread package (version 2.0.3), based on GENCODE version 46 gene annotations. Quality-control metrics included sequencing depth, alignment rate, duplicate rate, and ribosomal RNA content.

Sample-level expression profiles were examined for potential outliers using multidimensional scaling, implemented with the plotMDS function in the limma R package. Three clear outliers, one collected during early pregnancy (M1) and two collected during late pregnancy (M2), were identified and excluded. Samples without corresponding DNA methylation data were also excluded. The final analytic dataset included 152 samples from 89 participants, with 73 samples collected at M1 and 79 at M2.

Lowly expressed features were removed using filterByExpr with default settings in edgeR (version 4.6.3) (58), resulting in a final expression dataset comprising 14,899 expressed genes. TMM normalization factors were estimated using the edgeR calcNormFactors function to account for differences in library size and RNA composition.

Gene expression data analysis

The filtered RNA-seq count data and TMM normalization factors were supplied to voomWithDreamWeights in the variancePartition R package to estimate the meanvariance relationship and generate precision weights for log2 counts per million. Genewise linear mixed-effects models were subsequently fitted using the variancePartition dream function.

For each annotated CpG-gene pair, a gene-wise linear mixed-effects model was fitted using the variancePartition dream function, with gene expression specified as the outcome and DNA methylation as the primary predictor. Models included maternal age, BMI at enrollment, gestational age at sample collection, study site, maternal asthma status, randomized treatment group, the first five genotype principal components, and estimated blood-cell proportions as fixed-effect covariates. Illumina array slide and participant ID were included as random intercepts. The participant-specific random intercept accounted for the correlation between repeated M1 and M2 samples from the same participant.

Associations between circulating 25OHD concentrations and gene expression

were evaluated separately using linear mixed-effects models, with gene expression specified as the outcome and 25OHD concentration as the primary predictor. These models included the same covariates and a participant-specific random intercept as specified in the previous paragraph, but excluded Illumina array slide.

Pathway Analysis

Genes obtained from DMR annotation were assessed for their enrichment in biological pathways from Gene Ontology (GO), Reactome, and KEGG databases (59) (60) (61). Using annotated genes as the query set and all known genes (GENCODE v49 on genome build hg38) as the background set, over-representation analysis was performed using a hypergeometric test with an FDR threshold of 0.05. The R packages clusterProfiler and ReactomePA were used for pathway analysis (62, 63). To construct a network visualization of significant pathways, edges were created between two pathways if the Jaccard Index of their constituent genes is greater than or equal to 0.5, with node colors corresponding to clusters derived from the Leiden algorithm (64).

The standardized average entropy (referred to simply as "entropy" throughout the text) measures the degree of heterogeneity in methylation status for a set of probes across the cell population of a bulk sample, and it varies between 0 and 1 (65). Low entropy (i.e., low heterogeneity in methylation status across a cell population) represents DNAm values close to 0 or 1, whereas high entropy represents DNAm values close to 0.5.

Linear mixed effects models were used to assess the relationships between standardized average entropy of CpG sets and 25OHD levels, with entropy as the dependent variable. The modeling is analogous to the DNAm-25OHD models. The models were adjusted for maternal age, BMI at first trimester, gestational age at sample collection, sample collection site, maternal asthma status, randomization group, season of sample collection, the first five principal components of genotype data, and cell type proportion estimates. Illumina array slide and participant ID were included as random intercepts. Additionally, an interaction term between 25OHD and gestational age was included, to assess if the entropy-25OHD relationship changes during pregnancy.
