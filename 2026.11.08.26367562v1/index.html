Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Abstract

INTRODUCTION: Advanced glycation end products accumulate in Alzheimer's disease (AD) brain, but the clinical significance of circulating N ε -(carboxymethyl)lysine (CML) remains uncertain. We evaluated serum CML as a complementary peripheral biomarker of AD. METHODS: This observational case-control study enrolled 101 patients with clinically diagnosed AD and 91 healthy controls. Serum CML was quantified in 191 participants. Multivariable logistic regression and receiver operating characteristic analyses assessed association and discrimination. A randomly selected subcohort underwent ultrasensitive measurement of amyloid-, tau-, glial-, and neuroaxonal-related biomarkers. RESULTS: Serum CML was lower in AD than controls (median 15.26 versus 23.72 ng/mL; P<0.001). CML alone yielded an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.883. Five-fold cross-validation yielded AUCs of 0.727 for a clinical model and 0.911 after adding CML. Introduction

Alzheimer's disease (AD), the leading cause of dementia, is biologically characterized by amyloid-β (Aβ) deposition and tau neurofibrillary pathology [1, 2]. Contemporary diagnostic frameworks increasingly incorporate fluid and imaging biomarkers to characterize underlying AD pathology [3, 4]. Blood-based biomarkers, particularly phosphorylated tau217 (p-tau217), have demonstrated strong diagnostic performance in appropriately selected clinical populations [5, 6].

Nevertheless, AD involves broader biological disturbances, including oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and systemic metabolic dysregulation, which are not fully captured by amyloid-and tau-centered biomarkers [7]. Identifying complementary peripheral biomarkers may therefore provide additional insights into disease-associated systemic processes. Advanced glycation end products (AGEs) comprise chemically heterogeneous modifications generated through nonenzymatic glycation, glycoxidation, and reactive carbonyl chemistry [8]. Their circulating abundance reflects a dynamic balance among endogenous formation, dietary exposure, protein turnover, tissue retention, and renal clearance [9, 10]. N ε -(carboxymethyl)lysine (CML), a prevalent and extensively studied AGE modification, is widely regarded as an indicator of glycoxidative stress [11]. However, circulating CML concentrations are influenced by aging, oxidative injury, metabolic status, dietary intake, and kidney function [12, 13]. These multiple determinants complicate interpretation of circulating CML as a disease-associated biomarker and highlight the importance of accounting for potential confounding factors.

Neuropathological investigations have demonstrated substantial accumulation of CML in the aging and AD brain, although its distribution is anatomically and cellularly heterogeneous. Early studies identified CML immunoreactivity within neuronal perikarya and extraneuronal deposits, frequently associated with intracellular lipofuscin [14]. Subsequent investigations revealed CML deposition in neurons, astrocytes, and microglia, with particularly pronounced glial accumulation in AD [15, 16]. Regionally, CML immunoreactivity is prominent in the cerebral cortex and hippocampus but relatively limited in several subcortical structures. Within the hippocampus, CA3 and CA4 exhibit greater CML immunoreactivity than CA1 and CA2, while extraneuronal deposits are frequently associated with astroglial structures [15] [16] [17]. Furthermore, CML co-localizes with malondialdehyde, a lipid-peroxidation-associated product, suggesting an intersection between glycoxidative modification and oxidative lipid injury [18].

Importantly, cerebral CML deposition does not necessarily parallel conventional AD neuropathology. Neurons containing abundant neurofibrillary tangles may exhibit minimal CML immunoreactivity, whereas CML-rich neurons can occur in regions with comparatively limited tau pathology or neuronal loss. An inverse relationship between regional CML deposition and Braak stage has also been reported [15, 17]. At the molecular level, site-specific CML modification of Aβ42 at Lys16 or Lys28 reduces fibrillar aggregation while increasing cytotoxicity, potentially favoring persistent toxic oligomeric intermediates rather than enhanced reactive oxygen species generation [19]. These observations suggest that the biological consequences of CML depend on its molecular targets, modification sites, and cellular localization.

The relationship between cerebral and circulating CML remains incompletely understood.

Experimental evidence implicates age-associated intestinal barrier dysfunction and microbiotadependent CML exposure in cerebral accumulation, microglial oxidative stress, and mitochondrial impairment [20]. Clinical investigations have reported increased cerebrospinal fluid CML without a corresponding increase in serum concentrations [21]. Other studies identified elevated CML in albumin-free serum proteins during early AD but reduced extracellular-vesicle-associated CML in moderate disease [22], whereas increased plasma CML and sex-dependent variation have also been described [23]. Differences in biological matrices, molecular fractions, analytical methodologies, disease stages, and metabolic or renal characteristics may contribute to these apparently conflicting findings. Consequently, the direction and independent clinical significance of circulating CML alterations in AD remain unresolved.

In this observational case-control study, we measured serum CML in 101 clinically diagnosed AD patients and 91 healthy controls and examined its association with AD after accounting for demographic, renal, glycemic, and metabolic factors. We also evaluated its discriminatory contribution beyond routine clinical variables. In a randomly selected subcohort, amyloid biomarkers (Aβ42, Aβ40, and Aβ42/Aβ40) served as principal biological comparators, p-tau217 as the primary exploratory tau marker, and p-tau181, GFAP, and NfL as supportive measures. We hypothesized that circulating CML captures a systemic glycoxidative/metabolic dimension complementary to canonical AD pathology.

Methods

1.1 Study design and participants

This observational case-control biomarker study included 101 patients with clinically diagnosed AD and 91 healthy controls (HC) (n=192). Clinical and routine laboratory information was obtained from hospital examination records. Eligible participants were aged 60 years or older and included patients with clinically diagnosed AD and cognitively HC who voluntarily participated and provided written informed consent. Participants with non-AD cognitive disorders or other major neurological diseases, including stroke or vascular dementia, Parkinson's disease, and frontotemporal dementia, were excluded, as were individuals with documented CMV, EBV, HIV, HCV, or HBV infection according to the approved study protocol. Peripheral venous blood (5 mL) was collected and serum was separated for analysis. Exploratory subcohort analyses included biomarker group comparisons and combined ROC analyses evaluating CML with Aβ42/Aβ40 or p-tau217. 1.4 Measurement of serum CML

Serum CML concentrations were quantified using a commercially available Carboxymethyllysine (CML) ELISA Kit (abx575945; Abbexa, UK) according to the manufacturer's instructions. CML measurements were available for all 101 AD samples and 90 of 91 HC samples.

Because CML exists both as a free lysine-derived adduct and covalently attached to circulating proteins, we additionally characterized the molecular species detected by the commercial kit and by the in-house assay described below.

An in-house indirect competitive enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was established to provide an independent assessment of serum CML. The coating antigen was prepared by reacting bovine serum albumin (BSA; Absin, Shanghai, China) with glyoxal (Aladdin, Shanghai, China) in 0.2 M phosphate buffer (pH 8.0; Macklin, Shanghai, China) at 37 °C for 4 days in the dark. The reaction mixture was subsequently dialyzed against purified water for 24 h using a dialysis membrane with a molecular-weight cutoff of 8-14 kDa. For the competitive immunoassay, microplates were coated with the prepared CML-modified antigen and incubated with anti-CML primary antibody (ab125145; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) in the presence of CML standards or serum samples. Bound antibodies were detected using an anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), followed by color development with tetramethylbenzidine (TMB). Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a UV-VIS microplate spectrophotometer (SpectraMax; Molecular Devices, CA, USA).

The analytical target profiles of the two assays were further examined using free CML and CMLmodified BSA (CML-BSA) standards (Supplementary Figure 3). In the in-house competitive assay, increasing concentrations of either free CML or CML-BSA produced concentration-dependent decreases in OD450, demonstrating recognition of both free CML and protein-bound CML epitopes (Supplementary Figure 3A, B). In contrast, when CML-BSA was tested with the commercial CML kit, OD450 remained essentially unchanged across the tested CML-BSA concentration range, indicating little detectable response to protein-bound CML under these assay conditions (Supplementary Figure 3C). Accordingly, the commercial kit predominantly reflects a free-CMLsensitive serum pool, whereas the in-house assay detects a broader immunoreactive pool comprising free and protein-bound CML. Serum CML measurements obtained using the two assays were compared in 60 paired serum samples (30 AD and 30 HC), and their association was evaluated using Spearman rank correlation analysis. Because the assays quantify different molecular pools and use different calibration systems, their absolute concentrations and reported units are not directly interchangeable. The cross-assay comparison was therefore intended to assess concordance in relative biological variation rather than numerical equivalence (Supplementary Figure 2).

1.5 Single-molecule immunoassay biomarker subcohort

Serum samples were randomly selected from 31 patients with AD and 29 HC for ultrasensitive single-molecule biomarker analysis. Serum concentrations of AD-related biomarkers, including phosphorylated tau at threonine 181 (p-tau181), phosphorylated tau at threonine 217 (p-tau217), glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), neurofilament light chain (NfL), amyloid-β42 (Aβ42), and amyloid-β40 (Aβ40), were quantified using an ultrasensitive single-molecule array (Simoa) immunoassay platform (BioChip Reader, Iomics Bioscience Inc., Beijing, China). Serum samples were stored at -80 °C until analysis, and biomarker measurements were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions. For quantitative summaries, assay results reported below the lower limit of quantification were assigned the corresponding limit values. The Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio was calculated from the quantitative Aβ42 and Aβ40 values. Amyloid measures were prespecified as the principal biological comparator because they directly complement the CML-focused hypothesis. p-tau217 was retained as the principal exploratory tau marker because of its high performance for AD pathology. p-tau181, GFAP, and NfL were analyzed as supportive exploratory measures and were not used to redefine the primary study endpoint. Exploratory combined analyses paired CML with Aβ42/Aβ40 and, separately, with p-tau217 to assess potential complementarity across systemic glycoxidative, amyloid-related, and tau-related signals. Analyte-specific sample sizes are reported because the final analysis sets differed slightly across biomarkers. Vendor-supplied reference intervals that were not validated for serum were not used to classify participants as biomarker-positive or biomarkernegative.

1.6 Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as median and interquartile range (IQR) and compared using two-sided Mann-Whitney U tests. Categorical variables were summarized as n (%) and compared using χ² tests or Fisher's exact test when appropriate. The association between CML and AD was evaluated by logistic regression. Sequential sensitivity models expressed CML per 1-SD increase and adjusted for age and sex, then eGFR, and finally fasting glucose, HbA1c, BMI, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia. A parsimonious multivariable model was additionally fitted using CML in its original unit (ng/mL) together with age, sex, eGFR, and HbA1c to provide directly interpretable adjusted effects and to support the clinical-model ROC analysis. Complete-case analysis was used within each model. In the single-molecule subcohort, biomarker distributions were summarized as median (IQR) and AD-HC differences were assessed using two-sided Mann-Whitney U tests, with analyte-specific sample sizes reported in Supplementary Table 1 and Figure 5. To examine whether renal function could account for the CML difference, Spearman rank correlations between serum CML and eGFR were calculated separately in AD and healthy controls (Supplementary Figure 1).

For CML alone in the full CML cohort (n=191), the AUC and 95% confidence interval (CI) were estimated by bootstrap resampling. For comparison of the clinical model (age, sex, eGFR, and HbA1c) with the clinical model plus CML, analyses were restricted to 178 participants with complete data (101 AD and 77 HC). ROC curves for these fitted models were generated from out-of-fold predictions using stratified five-fold cross-validation; the corresponding AUCs were 0.727 and 0.911, respectively. In the single-molecule subcohort, two exploratory ROC analyses were performed using the complete cases available for each biomarker pairing: CML, Aβ42/Aβ40, and a CML + Aβ42/Aβ40 model (n=59; 30 AD and 29 controls), and CML, p-tau217, and a CML + p-tau217 model (n=60; 31 AD and 29 controls). 1.4 CML provides substantial discrimination and adds information to a clinical model

Given the robust independent association between serum CML and AD, we next assessed its ability to discriminate patients with AD from HC and whether it provided information beyond routinely available clinical variables. In the full CML cohort (n=191), CML alone yielded an AUC of 0.883 (95% CI 0.830-0.925; Figure 4A). We then compared a clinical model incorporating age, sex, eGFR, and HbA1c with the same model additionally including CML. This analysis included 178 participants (101 AD and 77 healthy controls) with complete data for all model variables. Using out-of-fold predictions from stratified five-fold cross-validation, the clinical model yielded an AUC of 0.727 (95% CI 0.649-0.799), whereas addition of CML increased the AUC to 0.911 (95% CI 0.861-0.953; Figure 4B). These cross-validated results indicate that CML contributed substantial discriminatory information beyond the selected demographic, renal, and glycemic variables in this cohort, while the generalizability of this improvement requires independent external validation.

1.5 Canonical AD-related biomarkers in the single-molecule subcohort

To place the CML finding in the context of established AD-related biological pathways, we next characterized amyloid-, tau-, astroglial-, and neuroaxonal-related serum biomarkers in the randomly selected single-molecule immunoassay subcohort of 31 AD participants and 29 HC. Aβ42 was lower in AD (P=0.000445), whereas Aβ40 was unchanged (P=0.9764), resulting in a markedly lower Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio in AD (P<0.0001) (Figure 5A-C; Supplementary Table 1). Consistent with a taurelated biomarker abnormality, p-tau217 and p-tau181 were both higher in AD (P<0.0001 and P=0.0003068, respectively; Figure 5D, E; Supplementary Table 1). NfL and GFAP were also higher in AD (both P<0.0001; Figure 5F, G; Supplementary Table 1).

Analyte-specific sample sizes are indicated beneath each panel and in Supplementary Table 1.

The Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio and NfL analyses each included 30 AD observations, whereas Aβ42, Aβ40, p-tau217, p-tau181, GFAP, and CML included 31 AD observations; all corresponding biomarker analyses included 29 healthy controls. Collectively, these measurements demonstrate that the subcohort exhibited the expected group-level differences across canonical amyloid-and tau-related biomarkers, together with supportive changes in markers of neuroaxonal injury and astroglial activation, thereby providing biological context for evaluating CML alongside established AD-related signals.

1.6 Exploratory ROC comparison in the biomarker subcohort

Finally, to explore whether the systemic glycoxidative information represented by CML might provide discriminatory information complementary to canonical AD-related biomarkers, we evaluated CML in combination with an amyloid-related marker and, separately, a tau-related marker (Figure 6). In the complete CML and Aβ42/Aβ40 dataset (n=59; 30 AD and 29 HC), the crossvalidated AUCs were 0.864 for CML, 0.794 for Aβ42/Aβ40, and 0.883 for the combined CML + Aβ42/Aβ40 model (Figure 6A). We next performed the corresponding analysis using p-tau217 as the tau-related comparator. In the complete CML and p-tau217 dataset (n=60; 31 AD and 29 HC), the cross-validated AUCs were 0.878 for CML, 0.844 for p-tau217, and 0.966 for the combined CML + p-tau217 model (Figure 6B). Model performance in both analyses was evaluated using stratified fivefold cross-validation. The slight difference between the two CML AUCs reflects the different complete-case analysis sets. Thus, CML showed only a modest increase in discrimination when combined with Aβ42/Aβ40 but a larger increase when combined with p-tau217. Given the limited subcohort size, however, these combined-model results should be considered exploratory and require independent validation.

Discussion

In this case-control biomarker study, serum CML was markedly lower in patients with clinically diagnosed AD than in healthy controls. The association remained strong after adjustment for age and sex and after further accounting for eGFR and major metabolic covariates, including fasting glucose, HbA1c, BMI, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia. CML alone showed good discrimination (AUC of 0.883). More importantly, in the 178-participant complete-case analysis, stratified five-fold crossvalidation yielded an AUC of 0.727 for the clinical model and 0.911 after CML was added. Thus, the lower serum CML signal was not readily explained by the measured demographic, renal, or metabolic differences and provided substantial additional discriminatory information beyond the selected clinical variables. Nevertheless, these results arise from a case-control cohort and still require independent external validation before they can be interpreted as generalizable clinical performance or used to define a diagnostic threshold.

The randomly selected single-molecule immunoassay subcohort showed lower Aβ42 and Aβ42/Aβ40, unchanged Aβ40, and higher p-tau217, p-tau181, glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), and neurofilament light chain (NfL) in AD. These patterns align with established amyloid-, tau-, glial-, and neuroaxonal-associated biomarkers [3-6, 24, 25]. Unlike amyloid and phosphorylated tau markers, which more directly reflect defining AD pathology, CML may capture systemic glycoxidative, carbonyl-related, and metabolic processes. Its potential value is therefore complementary rather than substitutive.

Reduced circulating CML appears paradoxical given reports of increased CML deposition in AD brain tissue. Neuropathological studies reveal heterogeneous cerebral CML distribution across neuronal and glial populations and anatomical regions [14] [15] [16] [17]. Immunoreactivity is prominent in cortex and hippocampus, including CA3/CA4, and in astroglial-associated extraneuronal deposits.

Intracellular deposits may accompany lipofuscin or vacuolar structures [14] [15] [16]. These distinct cellular and molecular pools may differ in formation, retention, and turnover. Thus, regional cerebral accumulation need not parallel serum CML concentrations. This distinction is central to interpreting the apparent discrepancy: immunohistochemical tissue deposition reflects localized modifications, whereas a serum assay measures an assay-dependent circulating fraction at a particular time.

CML deposition also differs from conventional AD pathology. Neurons with prominent neurofibrillary tangles may exhibit little CML, whereas CML-positive neurons can lack substantial tangles. Regional deposition has been inversely associated with Braak stage [15, 17]. Site-specific modification of Aβ42 at Lys16 or Lys28 slows fibrillar aggregation while increasing cytotoxicity, particularly at Lys28 [19]. This may reflect persistent toxic oligomeric intermediates rather than increased reactive oxygen species production. CML's consequences therefore depend on the substrate, modification site, aggregation state, and cellular environment.

CML may also reflect oxidative lipid injury. Its cerebral co-localization with malondialdehyde links glycoxidative modification to lipid peroxidation [18]. Reactive carbonyls arising from lipid oxidation, alongside carbohydrate-derived pathways, can contribute to CML formation. Local oxidative processes could therefore increase cerebral protein modification without raising circulating CML. The relative contributions of lipid-derived carbonyl chemistry, glycoxidation, and protein turnover in AD remain unresolved. These pathways are not mutually exclusive, and their relative importance may differ across cell types and disease stages.

Glial biology and peripheral trafficking may influence CML homeostasis. Astrocytes and microglia can accumulate CML-modified proteins through local formation, uptake, altered proteostasis, or inflammation [15, 16]. Mossad and colleagues described a gut-blood-brain pathway in which age-associated intestinal barrier changes increased CML exposure and CML impaired microglial mitochondrial function [20]. These findings support intercompartmental interactions but do not establish that cerebral uptake explains our observed serum decrease. Local glycoxidative and lipid-peroxidative formation offers another possibility [17, 18]. Microglial mitochondrial dysfunction is biologically relevant to AD, but the experimental gut-brain findings should not be extrapolated directly to human serum concentrations.

Prior clinical studies underscore the importance of specimen type and molecular fraction.

Increased cerebrospinal-fluid CML without a corresponding serum increase suggests partly independent central and peripheral regulation [21]. Increased CML in albumin-free serum proteins during early AD, reduced extracellular-vesicle-associated CML in moderate disease [22], and increased plasma CML with sex-related variation [23] have all been reported. Serum versus plasma, free versus protein-bound CML, extracellular-vesicle-associated CML, disease stage, analytical platform, renal function, metabolic status, and cohort composition may all contribute to heterogeneity. CML should not be treated as a homogeneous analyte across biological compartments and methods.

Our assay comparisons further support this distinction. The in-house competitive enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) responded concentration-dependently to purified free CML and CMLmodified bovine serum albumin, whereas the commercial assay showed little response to CMLmodified protein under tested conditions (Supplementary Figure 3). The in-house method therefore recognizes a broader immunoreactive pool, and the commercial method appears predominantly sensitive to a free-CML-associated fraction. However, absent response to CML-BSA does not prove absolute specificity for free CML across all modified proteins. Both assays yielded lower AD signals and a moderate positive rank correlation in 60 paired samples (Supplementary Figure 2). Their differing calibrations and detectable pools preclude direct comparison of absolute concentrations or units. Our primary finding concerns the commercial assay-derived signal. Thus, concordant betweengroup direction and rank-order correlation should be distinguished from analytical agreement, and neither immunoassay alone establishes absolute total CML content.

Several nonexclusive mechanisms could explain lower serum CML. CML-related modifications occur in circulating proteins, blood cells, extracellular vesicles, peripheral tissues, and brain [15, 22, [26] [27] [28]. Changes in protein turnover, tissue retention, uptake, or vesicle trafficking might redistribute immunoreactive CML. Diet, intestinal absorption, inflammation, systemic glycoxidative flux, and clearance could also alter circulating concentrations [8] [9] [10] [11] [12] [13] 20]. Lower serum CML need not imply lower whole-body formation or cerebral burden. These remain hypotheses because we did not quantify CML flux, identify modified proteins, or sample matched central and peripheral compartments.

Renal and metabolic confounding warrant attention. Impaired renal elimination generally increases circulating CML [12, 13], yet eGFR was modestly lower in AD despite markedly lower serum CML. Within-group CML-eGFR correlations were nonsignificant (AD: ρ=0.012, P=0.904; controls: ρ=0.202, P=0.060; Supplementary Figure 1). Adjustment for eGFR, glycemic indices, and metabolic variables did not materially weaken the association. Nevertheless, diet, medications, nutritional status, smoking, inflammation, and intestinal physiology could contribute residual confounding. These findings reduce the plausibility of a simple renal explanation but do not exclude more complex interactions among renal function, nutrition, and inflammation.

A compartment-dependent model may reconcile localized cerebral deposition with reduced circulating CML without assuming direct serum-to-brain sequestration. Regional formation, proteinspecific modification, cellular uptake, vesicular trafficking, and systemic clearance may change independently. Testing this model requires orthogonal quantification of free and protein-bound CML in serum or plasma, blood cells, extracellular vesicles, cerebrospinal fluid, and, where possible, brain tissue. Targeted mass spectrometry, identification of CML-modified proteins, and longitudinal sampling could distinguish formation from redistribution and clearance. Paired measurements would be especially informative if accompanied by detailed information on protein modification, sample handling, and renal function.

In our subcohort, the expected abnormalities in Aβ42/Aβ40 and p-tau217 confirmed canonical amyloid-and tau-related signals [24], supporting the idea that CML captures a biological dimension not identical to amyloid deposition, tau phosphorylation, astroglial activation, or neuroaxonal injury.

Exploratory combined-marker analyses suggested potential complementarity. CML plus Aβ42/Aβ40 yielded an AUC of 0.883 versus 0.864 for CML alone in 59 participants. CML plus p-tau217 yielded an AUC of 0.966 versus 0.878 for CML and 0.844 for p-tau217 individually in 60 participants.

Despite five-fold cross-validation, these small analyses are vulnerable to sampling variability and model optimism. Incremental value beyond validated p-tau217 and Aβ42/Aβ40 assays requires independent evaluation in cognitively unimpaired individuals, mild cognitive impairment, AD, and non-AD dementias. The apparent improvement should not be regarded as a validated diagnostic gain until its magnitude and uncertainty are reproduced prospectively.
