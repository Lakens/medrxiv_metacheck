Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 7 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer mortality worldwide, responsible for an estimated 1.8 million deaths in 2022 [1]. Prognosis is strongly stage-dependent: five-year survival is 68-92% for stage I disease but falls below 10% at stage IV [2]. Because a substantial fraction of tumors are still diagnosed after regional or distant spread, strategies that shift detection to earlier stages have considerable potential to reduce population mortality.

Randomized trials have established that annual low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) meaningfully reduces lung cancer mortality, by 20% in the National Lung Screening Trial (NLST) and by 24-33% in the NELSON trial [3, 4]. In 2021, the US Preventive Services Task Force broadened its recommendation to adults aged 50-80 years with a ≥20 pack-year history, expanding eligibility by approximately 6.4 million people [5]. As the screened population grows, the discriminative accuracy of nodule risk assessment becomes increasingly important to contain false positives without sacrificing sensitivity.

The American College of Radiology's Lung-RADS system, introduced in 2014 and refined in version 2022 [6], standardized nodule reporting and management. However, its risk assessment relies mainly on nodule diameter and attenuation; secondary features such as margins and spiculation are assessed subjectively, with inter-reader disagreement in 29% of reading pairs [7]. The consequences run in both directions. The positive predictive value of Lung-RADS 4B is only 22-37% [8], and transthoracic needle biopsy carries meaningful complication risk, including pneumothorax in approximately 15% of procedures (6.6% requiring chest-tube placement) and hemorrhage in 1% [9]. Conversely, malignant nodules assigned to low categories wait up to a year for their next evaluation, risking meaningful disease progression and prognostic consequence. Clinical risk calculators, including the Mayo [10] and Brock [11] models, and NELSON's volumetric approach, add structured probability estimation but rely largely on the same morphological inputs. AI-based nodule classification has emerged along two lines. Per-nodule characterization tools, including Optellum's LCP-CNN [12] and RevealDx's RevealAI-Lung [13], hold clearance for incidentally detected nodules. Scan-level forecasting models such as Sybil [14] predict future cancer risk but are not FDA cleared. To our knowledge, Median Technologies' eyonis LCS holds the only FDA clearance with a screening-specific indication [15]. We propose that the value of any such tool is best understood through the concept of an AI biomarker: an algorithmic measurement whose worth lies precisely in the information it contributes orthogonal to the variables already driving existing estimators.

Here we validate LungAI, a deep learning malignancy classifier producing per-nodule and per-examination risk scores, on a held-out NLST partition identical to the published test split of Sybil and Ardila et al. [14, 16]. We deliberately position LungAI as an adjunct to, not a replacement for, Lung-RADS v2022. The reclassification algorithm we propose preserves the familiar management ladder (12-month, 6-month, 3-month, biopsy) and shifts patients within it, requiring no change to radiology workflow or reporting infrastructure. We test four propositions: (i) LungAI outperforms Sybil and size-based measures on discrimination; (ii) it retains discriminative value after adjustment for diameter -and, on follow-up examinations, after adjustment for interval growth -enabling stratification within Lung-RADS categories; (iii) its score strata map monotonically onto clinically meaningful risk tiers; and (iv) performance is stable across patient and technical subgroups.

Methods

Study design and oversight

This retrospective diagnostic-accuracy study used de-identified NLST data and is reported per STARD 2015 [17] (flow in Supplementary Figure S1). We locked the LungAI model weights, follow-up risk adjustment configuration, and all reclassification rules before evaluating the held-out test partition. This secondary analysis used de-identified NLST data obtained from the NCI Cancer Data Access System under an approved data-use agreement; no investigator had access to identifiable information, and institutional review board review was therefore not required.

Cohort

The evaluation cohort was the NLST held-out test partition defined by Ardila et al. [16] and used as the published test split of Sybil [14]; it was never used in either LungAI or Sybil development. NLST enrolled high-risk current and former smokers aged 55-74 between 2002 and 2004 with annual LDCT screening and pathologically confirmed outcomes [3].

Within this partition, all LDCT examinations with ≥1 nodule detected by Coreline Soft's FDA-cleared aView LCS nodule-detection software [18] were considered (4,872 examinations, 1,817 patients, 128 cancers). Examinations were excluded if the cancerassociated nodule was not visible on the index LDCT (164 examinations), slice thickness exceeded 2.5 mm (257 examinations, 7 cancers), or no solid or part-solid nodule measured 4-30 mm (1,704 examinations, 32 cancers). We excluded ground-glass nodules a priori because ground-glass malignancies are often indolent and managed with surveillance rather than resection, making a malignancy score less decision-relevant. The analysis set comprised 2,747 examinations from 1,244 patients (89 cancers, 3.2%). Notably, only 7 examinations (0.3%) included part-solid nodules and none were malignant; the analyzed cohort is therefore de facto solid-nodule-only.

Index test

LungAI is a locked, commercial-grade, deep learning computer-aided diagnosis (CADx) model that analyzes LDCT volumes and returns a malignancy score (0.0 -10.0, in increments of 0.1) for each detected nodule and an aggregate examination-level score defined as the maximum across nodule scores. The examination-level maximum reflects the clinical decision unit: triage is determined by the most suspicious nodule while pernodule scores preserve interpretability and localization. LungAI scores are not probabilities; they are unitless scores with absolute-risk interpretation provided empirically through observed malignant fractions per score stratum (Table S1). This nonprobability score convention is common among cleared CADx devices [12]. The displayed scale is anchored to fixed operating points estimated on the NLST validation partition: integer scores 1.0 through 9.0 correspond, respectively, to thresholds operating at 95%, 90%, and 85% sensitivity and at 90%, 95%, 97%, 98%, 99%, and 99.5% specificity. A threshold at a given score therefore carries a locked operating interpretation; for example, a rule flagging examinations with scores at or above 2.0 operates at approximately 90% sensitivity in the validation population. This anchor mapping was frozen with the model before any evaluation of the held-out test partition. LungAI is investigational: a 510(k) premarket notification is pending with the US Food and Drug Administration, and the device is not cleared or available for sale in the United States.

Two locked configurations were evaluated. The standalone configuration, hereafter referenced as LungAI, operates on a single examination. The follow-up configuration, hereafter referenced as LungAI-FU, is a logistic regression on three inputs per nodule: the standalone-model raw output on the current examination, the nodule's inverse volume doubling time relative to the matched prior nodule, and an indicator for tracked versus new nodules. Inverse volume doubling time was expressed as log₂(Vcurrent/Vprior) / Δt (doublings per year). Untracked nodules carry no growth term, and tracked nodules whose prior detection has no measurement receive the tracking indicator with growth term set to zero (458 nodules, all benign). LungAI-FU is applicable when a prior examination exists, mirroring standard clinical practice in which screening studies are interpreted against priors. Nodule measurement on both examinations and inter-examination nodule matching were performed using Coreline's aView LCS. We fit and cross-validated fusion coefficients on the NLST validation partition, leaving the test partition untouched before evaluation; we report locked coefficients as odds ratios in Table S2. The follow-up analysis set comprised all post-baseline examinations (n=1,899; 45 cancers), coinciding with the screening-round stratification of Supplementary Figure S2.

Comparators

Lung-RADS v2022. NLST pre-dates Lung-RADS. Lung-RADS categories were therefore assigned retrospectively by a board-certified radiologist according to ACR v2022 rules, blinded to LungAI outputs and to outcomes, and without prior-scan information. Annotation covered a positive-enriched random subset of 256 examinations, of which 226 met analysis-set eligibility (78 cancers, 148 benign; sampling fractions 87.6% of cancers, 5.6% of benign examinations). All Lung-RADS-dependent quantities were projected to the full analysis set by contingency weighting (w⁺ = 89/78 ≈ 1.14; w⁻ = 2,658/148 ≈ 18.0; Table S3). We evaluated Lung-RADS's discriminative performance on the annotated subset with contingency weighting and performed inference on ΔAUROC and ΔAUPRC by patientclustered bootstrap. At the 3/4A decision boundary, we measured weighted sensitivity at 81.2% and specificity at 85.1%.

Sybil.

The publicly released Sybil model [14] was applied to identical examinations using its one-year risk score, with no nodule-coordinate input as designed. Comparison between LungAI and Sybil was only performed at the examination level, where both produce a score. We selected the one-year horizon as the most favorable to Sybil.

Size measures.

We studied mean nodule diameter and nodule volume as standalone discriminators. On follow-up examinations, interval change in diameter (Δdiameter) and volume (Δvolume) were used as comparators. These measures are defined only for tracked nodules with a measured prior and were evaluated on the 963 follow-up examinations with at least one such nodule (27 cancers).

Reference standard

An examination was labeled as malignant if a pathologically confirmed primary lung cancer was recorded for the corresponding nodule at any time within the NLST follow-up window. An examination was labeled as benign if the trial read was negative with no cancer diagnosis during follow-up.

Reclassification algorithm

The combined algorithm preserves the Lung-RADS management ladder and shifts patients within it by thresholding the displayed LungAI score: up-triage advances management by one rung (12-month LDCT → 6-month LDCT → 3-month LDCT/PET-CT → biopsy referral) and down-triage retreats by one. We evaluated two reclassification schemata, both were specified before evaluation, and we tested no other cutpoint configurations.

Schema A (primary): per-category cutpoints matched to Lung-RADS's own sensitivity profile.

Round cutpoints were fixed a priori within each Lung-RADS category. Up-triage cutpoints were fixed at 2.0 (empiric sensitivity ~90%) within Lung-RADS 2, 4.0 (empiric specificity ~90%) within Lung-RADS 3, and 6.0 (empiric specificity ~97%) within Lung-RADS 4A, roughly corresponding to published metrics for Lung-RADS 3, 4A, and 4B respectively. We chose these cutpoints to match our empiric estimate of Lung-RADS v2022's own performance at the 2/3, 3/4A, and 4A/4B decision boundaries on the annotated NLST subset, and consistent with published estimates for Lung-RADS version 1.0 (84.9% baseline sensitivity at the category-≥3 positivity threshold) [19]. Down-triage cutpoints were fixed at 1.0 in Lung-RADS 3 (empiric sensitivity ~95%), 1.5 in Lung-RADS 4A (arithmetic midpoint of 1.0 and 2.0), and 2.0 in Lung-RADS 4B/4X (empiric sensitivity ~90%). We chose these cutpoints to be highly conservative in down-triaging, reflecting the logic that an erroneous down-triage decision is significantly more costly than an erroneous up-triage one. The cutpoints were pre-selected and applied once without modification. Achieved within-category sensitivities and specificities are reported in Results and were not used to revise the cutpoints post-hoc.

Schema B (sensitivity analysis): a single operating point.

Up-triage at a single displayed score of 4.0 across all categories, with down-triage cutpoints unchanged. The cutpoint 4.0 (empiric specificity ~90%) was chosen as a round clinical operating point approximating the maximum-Youden threshold derived on the NLST validation partition. Results are in Supplementary Figure S3.

Robustness.

A threshold-perturbation analysis jointly scaled the operating thresholds of all six Schema A cutpoints across a four-fold range (×0.50 to ×2.00) and recomputed the principal reclassification metrics (Table S4).

Statistical analysis

Our primary endpoint was examination-level AUROC, which we compared against each comparator risk score with the paired DeLong test [20]. AUPRC differences were tested by paired patient-clustered bootstrap (2,000 replicates). All confidence intervals were obtained by the same patient-clustered bootstrap. We additionally assessed model discrimination at the nodule level, wherein each cancer examination contributed one nodule (the diagnosed target nodule) and each benign examination contributed all nodules meeting inclusion criteria. Since including multiple nodules per examination violates the independence assumption of the DeLong test, we used patient-clustered bootstrap for nodule-level inference. Sybil, which produces no nodule-level output, was omitted from nodule-level analysis (Table S5; Supplementary Figure S4).

Orthogonality was tested with nested logistic models and likelihood-ratio tests: at baseline, malignancy ~ restricted cubic spline of diameter (3 df) ± LungAI; on follow-up examinations, M0 = spline(diameter) + volume doubling time + new-nodule indicator versus M1 = M0 + LungAI standalone (6.4 events per parameter). Reclassification was tested by McNemar's exact test separately among cancers and benign examinations on the annotated subset, with weighted projection for cohort-level counts, and summarized by weighted categorical NRI [21]. Stage at diagnosis was taken from the NLST cancer data and assigned to every examination of the patient. Among annotated cancers we tabulated stage by direction of the LungAI-based management change (Table S6). Subgroup heterogeneity used Cochran's Q with I². Decision curve analysis [22] compared net benefit of Lung-RADS alone versus the combined algorithm over threshold probabilities of 1-20%, with stratumlevel observed malignant fractions from the weighted cross-tabulation as probability inputs. Since Lung-RADS categories exist only on the annotated evaluation subset, these risk estimates are necessarily in-sample, and the decision-curve comparison should be read as descriptive rather than out-of-sample (Supplementary Figure S5). The clinical-economic analysis is described in Figure 5. We use Medicare-anchored unit costs (LDCT $300; CT-guided biopsy $4,000; Table S7) [23], lead-time accounting netting delayed down-triaged cancers, and a real-world scenario applying published "defined adherence" rates per Lung-RADS v2022 category (Table S8). Adherence rates were taken as the unweighted mean of four studies [24] [25] [26] [27] identified in the meta-analysis of Lin et al. [28], under the assumption that patients on a 12-month pathway have, by definition, no opportunity for diagnosis within 1 year. All hypothesis tests were two-sided with alpha set to 0.05. Analyses were performed in Python 3.9.2 (numpy 1.26.4, pandas 2.3.2, scipy 1.13.1, scikit-learn 1.5.2, statsmodels 0.14.6).

Results

Cohort

The analysis set comprised 2,747 examinations (1,244 patients; 89 cancers, 3.2%); characteristics are in Table S9. Median age was 61 years (IQR 58-66), 62% were male, and median smoking exposure was 49.5 pack-years. Median maximal nodule diameter was 6.3 mm overall and 12.8 mm among cancers. The cohort was 93.2% White, reflecting NLST enrollment.

Discrimination (standalone configuration)

LungAI achieved an AUROC of 0.92 (95% CI 0.88-0.95) and an AUPRC of 0.47 (0.35-0.61) (Figure 1; Table S10). It outperformed Sybil (AUROC 0.85, ΔAUROC 0.07, 95% CI 0.04-0.09, AUPRC 0.25, ΔAUPRC 0.18, 95% CI 0.11-0.25), mean diameter (AUROC 0.78, ΔAUROC 0.14, 95% CI 0.08-0.19, AUPRC 0.13, ΔAUPRC 0.34, 95% CI 0.22-0.47), and volume (AUROC 0.79, ΔAUROC 0.14, 95% CI 0.09-0.20, AUPRC 0.12, ΔAUPRC 0.38, 95% CI 0.25-0.50) on this cohort. The AUPRC separation -nearly doubling precision-recall performance relative to the best comparator at 3.2% prevalence -is the most clinically salient difference. Lung-RADS v2022 itself, scored ordinally on the weighted annotated subset, achieved AUROC 0.89 (95% CI 0.82-0.94) and AUPRC 0.25 (95% CI 0.15-0.47). LungAI's AUROC advantage over Lung-RADS was not significant (ΔAUROC 0.03, 95% CI -0.04-0.10, P=0.20), while its AUPRC advantage was significant (ΔAUPRC 0.31, 95% CI 0.10-0.50, P=0.003). Nodule-level discrimination was concordant: the per-nodule score achieved AUROC 0.93 (0.89-0.96) and AUPRC 0.37 (0.23-0.57) at 1.5% nodule-level prevalence, exceeding diameter and volume on both metrics (all P≤0.001; Table S5; Supplementary Figure S4).

Orthogonality to nodule size

Adding LungAI to a flexible diameter model increased AUROC by 0.10 (0.06-0.18; likelihood-ratio χ²=161, P<10⁻³⁶). This suggests that LungAI is not simply recapitulating nodule size.

Follow-up configuration

Among 1,899 follow-up examinations (45 cancers), the follow-up configuration achieved AUROC 0.91 (0.83-0.97) and AUPRC 0.60 (0.45-0.74) (Figure 2), exceeding Sybil (ΔAUROC 0.06, P<0.001), Δdiameter (ΔAUROC 0.11, P=0.009), and Δvolume (ΔAUROC 0.09, P=0.021). Its advantage over the baseline configuration on the same examinations was confined to the precision-recall tradeoff (AUPRC 0.60 vs 0.41, ΔAUPRC 0.18, P<0.001); the AUROC gain was not significant (ΔAUROC 0.01, DeLong P=0.13, bootstrap P=0.078). This suggests that incorporating interval growth primarily sharpens the ranking of true cancers among highscoring nodules. Notably, against a model of size, interval growth, and nodule novelty, LungAI remained decisively informative (χ²=79.4, P<10⁻¹⁸, model AUROC 0.81→0.87). This suggests that the morphological signal it captures is independently predictive even where growth information is available.

Within-category stratification and reclassification

Across every Lung-RADS category, LungAI strata separated observed cancer cases substantially (Table S1; Figure 3). Within Lung-RADS 2, 0.2% of cases below a LungAI score of 2.0 were malignant versus 1.3% at or above; within Lung-RADS 3, 0.9% below a score of 1.0 were malignant versus 16.7% at or above 4.0; within 4A, 1.0% below 1.5 were malignant versus 46.2% at or above 6.0; within 4B/4X, 0% below 2.0 were malignant versus 48.3% at or above it. Achieved within-category up-triage sensitivities were 78%, 50%, and 68%, capturing a meaningful proportion of cases under-triaged by Lung-RADS v2022.

Includes contingency-weighted counts, observed malignant fractions, arm-level weighted sensitivity and false-negative rates, assigned management, and resulting effective Lung-RADS categories. The Schema B tree -a single up-triage operating point at a displayed score of 4.0, approximating the validation-partition maximum-Youden threshold -is shown in Supplementary Figure S3.

Applying the combined algorithm (Figure 4), cancers on the slowest, 12-month pathway fell by 46% (3.1→1.6 per 1,000 examinations) and cancers referred directly for tissue diagnosis rose 31% (18.3→23.9 per 1,000). On the other hand, benign biopsy referrals fell 20% (32.7→26.2 per 1,000) without meaningfully affecting total biopsy volume (51.0→50.1 per 1,000). Among cancers, up-triage dominated down-triage 20 to 3 (McNemar P<0.001). Among benign examinations, reclassification was approximately balanced (28 up-triaged versus 22 down-triaged, P=0.480), while invasive workup of benign patients nonetheless declined. The event NRI was +0.25 (95% CI 0.13-0.37) and the non-event NRI was -0.04 (95% CI -0.13-0.05), yielding an overall NRI of +0.20 (95% CI 0.04-0.36) (Table S11). The net reclassification benefit is positive even on the blunt overall metric, which weights a shift from 12-to 6-month imaging the same as a biopsy referral. These findings were robust to choice of clinical operating point. Across joint scaling of all six of the above decision thresholds over a four-fold range, the event NRI remained positive throughout (+0.16 to +0.27), cancers remaining on the 12-month pathway stayed at 1.6 per 1,000 examinations (versus 3.1 under Lung-RADS alone), and net lead time remained strongly positive (18-28 months). The direction of effect is stable across the threshold range, with magnitude varying as expected (Table S4). Stage at diagnosis, tabulated by direction of reclassification among annotated cancers (Table S6), was distributed similarly across groups: 80.0% of up-triaged cancers with known stage were stage I, versus 75.9% of unchanged cancers (exploratory Fisher exact for stage II+ versus stage I, up-triaged versus all others: OR 0.70, P=0.77). Two of the three down-triaged cancers were stage II+. Because stage reflects the care delivered in NLST, which predates modern nodule management, these analyses are purely descriptive.

Clinical and economic trade-off

Per 1,000 nodule-positive examinations, assuming 100% adherence to LDCT rescan recommendations, the algorithm resulted in 31.7 months of gross diagnostic lead time among accelerated cancer diagnoses, less 6.4 months of delayed diagnosis, for a net lead time of 25.2 months. This comes at a net saving of approximately $2,200, with 6.5 fewer net benign biopsies (Figure 5). Applying published transthoracic-biopsy complication rates [9] to the avoided benign biopsies, this corresponds to approximately 1.0 fewer pneumothoraces, 0.4 fewer chest-tube placements, and 0.07 fewer biopsy-related hemorrhages per 1,000 nodule-positive examinations. Assuming published follow-up adherence rates (Figure 5B) [24] [25] [26] [27] [28], 24.9 of 32.4 cancers per 1,000 nodule-positive examinations would be diagnosed within 1 year using Lung-RADS alone versus 26.5 of 32.4 using LungAI, netting 1.6 additional timely diagnoses while at a net cost saving. Against the stage gradient of lung cancer survival [2], months of lead time at the stage I/II boundary are a plausible mechanism by which triage reallocation could translate into mortality benefit.

Subgroup stability

AUROC was consistent across sex, age, scanner manufacturer, screening round, slice thickness, pack-year smoking history, and diameter bands (all Cochran's Q P≥0.058; Supplementary Figure S2; Table S12). Cancer stage showed nominal heterogeneity (stage I versus benign AUROC 0.95, 95% CI 0.92-0.98; stage II+ versus benign 0.83, 0.69-0.94; Q P=0.047), reflecting higher discrimination for stage I disease. Heterogeneity flagged for race is attributable to a degenerate small stratum with only 4 non-White cancers and is not interpretable as differential performance.

Discrimination by time to diagnosis

To assess whether LungAI's advantage is confined to cancers a radiologist would likely diagnose promptly unaided, we stratified discrimination by the interval from examination to cancer diagnosis (<1 year versus ≥1 year; Supplementary Figures S6-S7; Table S13). At baseline, LungAI retained useful discrimination among cancers diagnosed ≥1 year after the examination (AUROC 0.86, 95% CI 0.78-0.93), where mean diameter was close to chance (AUROC 0.63, 0.49-0.76; ΔAUROC 0.23, P<0.001). The follow-up configuration showed the same direction of effect (ΔAUROC 0.23, P=0.044), although with only 11 cancers in the ≥1year stratum its estimates are wide (AUROC 0.78, 0.59-0.96). AUPRC is not comparable across strata because prevalence differs and benign controls are shared.

Discussion

In a held-out NLST partition identical to the published test splits of the Sybil and Ardila et al. papers, LungAI discriminated screen-detected cancers significantly more accurately than Sybil, mean diameter, and estimated volume. Most significantly, LungAI contributed risk information orthogonal to the size and growth features on which Lung-RADS and clinical calculators principally rest. The practical expression of that orthogonality is the management redistribution of Figure 4. Integrated into the existing Lung-RADS algorithm without altering its structure, LungAI decreased the proportion of cancers on the slowest surveillance pathway from 9% to 5%, increased the proportion of cancers triaged directly to tissue sampling from 56% to 73%, reduced the rate of benign nodules triaged directly to tissue sampling by 20%, and increased the positivity rate of the Lung-RADS 4B/4X designation from 36% to 48%. An algorithm that merely re-ranked by size could not plausibly achieve both, as diameter's standalone AUROC (0.78) and the diameter-adjusted likelihood-ratio test make clear.

Our positioning is deliberately conservative: LungAI is intended to augment Lung-RADS rather than replace it. Radiologists first assign a Lung-RADS category and then use the LungAI score to determine whether to up-or down-triage the examination, requiring no new reporting infrastructure. Because the overwhelming majority of screen-detected nodules are benign and reside in lower Lung-RADS categories, a tool that confidently rules out such nodules can reduce interval imaging and invasive workup, while reliable up-triage of the minority of low-category malignancies shortens time to diagnosis. We believe that this is the realistic adoption path for screening CADx technology, and it frames the appropriate comparison set: not AI versus radiologist, but workflow-with versus workflowwithout the AI biomarker.

Two findings deserve emphasis beyond the headline discrimination. First, the follow-up configuration -a transparent, three-variable logistic regression using the standalone model's output, volume doubling time, and nodule tracking status -shows that where growth information exists, it should be used, and that the imaging biomarker remains independently informative after growth is accounted for (χ²=79.4). This fusion layer is interpretable, and the proprietary component is confined to the nodule-level score.

Second, our adherence-adjusted analysis quantifies a mechanism usually discussed qualitatively: patients told that their scan looks benign and to "return in 12 months" frequently do not [24] [25] [26] [27] [28], and up-triaging elevated-risk Lung-RADS 2 patients to a 6month pathway both shortens the interval and concretizes follow-up. Accounting for published re-scan adherence rates, we estimate that LungAI would yield 1.6 additional early diagnoses per 1,000 examinations while generating a modest net cost saving, a favorable profile relative to accepted costs of screening-based early cancer detection [29]. The avoided benign biopsies also carry a direct harm benefit independent of cost: at published complication rates [9], roughly one pneumothorax and 0.4 chest-tube placements are averted per 1,000 nodule-positive examinations. A formal costeffectiveness analysis with staged treatment costs and quality-adjusted outcomes is warranted, and the figures here should be read as bounding estimates under stated assumptions (Table S7). Future screening will not rest on imaging alone. Blood-based assays -ctDNA, protein panels, methylation signatures -capture molecular shedding, a biological axis orthogonal to both radiologist reads and imaging AI. The natural evolution of lung cancer screening is Lung-RADS alone → Lung-RADS plus imaging AI (this work) → a unified risk model integrating Lung-RADS, imaging AI and circulating biomarkers, each contributing orthogonal signals. The AI-biomarker framing introduced here generalizes directly: each modality earns its place by the information it adds conditional on previous modalities.

Limitations

First, this is a single-cohort held-out validation; no external cohort is included, and NLST scanners date to 2002-2004. Multi-site external validation on contemporary data is underway at several institutions. Second, although part-solid nodules were eligible, only 7 (0.3%, no cancers) entered the cohort; conclusions apply to solid nodules, and groundglass disease is out of scope. Third, stage at diagnosis reflects care delivered in NLST, which precedes modern lung cancer screening, not the hypothetical reclassified pathway. Thus, the stage breakdown by reclassification direction (Table S6) is purely descriptive and cannot establish the effect of reclassification on stage at diagnosis.

Conclusion

LungAI supplies malignancy-risk information orthogonal to the features underlying Lung-RADS. Deployed as a within-category stratifier on the existing management ladder, LungAI reallocates screening patients so that cancers move toward earlier evaluation and benign patients away from invasive workup, all at unchanged biopsy volume and an estimated net cost saving. External validation will determine whether these operating characteristics transport to other settings.
