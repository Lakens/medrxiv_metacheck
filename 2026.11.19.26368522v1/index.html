How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Objective: Risk stratification for major depressive disorder (MDD) remains limited by clinical heterogeneity and modest genomic prediction. We examined whether integrating family history, polygenic risk scores (PRS), childhood trauma, and background factors improves prediction of lifetime MDD diagnosis and its severity, relative to models including only subsets of these predictors.

Methods: Analyses included 9,927 cases and 4,452 controls from the Genetic Links to Anxiety and Depression Study and NIHR BioResource (GLAD+) and 40,667 cases and 70,755 controls from UK Biobank (UKB). Lifetime MDD was defined using DSM-5 based online assessments. Predictors included family history of multiple psychiatric disorders (mFH), multi-trait PRSs (mPRS), childhood trauma, and background factors (sex and ten genetic principal components). Nested elastic net regression and XGBoost were used to predict MDD within and across cohorts.

Results: Multivariable models integrating mFH, mPRS, childhood trauma, and background factors demonstrated strong risk classification (AUC=0.83 in GLAD+; 0.74 in UKB). Family history consistently outperformed multi-trait PRS (GLAD+: 0.74 vs. 0.66; UKB: 0.65 vs. 0.60).

External validation showed preserved classification (AUC=0.71-0.82), with attenuation reflecting differences in case severity across cohorts. Models explained up to 27% of the variance in the number of depressive episodes.

Conclusions: Multivariable integration of familial, environmental, and genetic factors yields robust and transferable prediction of MDD. Family history and childhood trauma consistently outperform current PRS for both case classification and severity prediction, underscoring the continued importance of integrating clinical data into prediction models in the genomic era.

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is one of the most common psychiatric disorders worldwide, with a lifetime prevalence of 15-18%(1), and 35-75% of individuals with a first episode go on to have recurrences (2). MDD impairs social and occupational functioning, reduces health-related quality of life, and elevates suicide risk (3, 4). Many biological, environmental, and sociodemographic risk factors have been identified (5, 6), and clinicians already weigh family history, early adversity, and demographic factors informally when judging risk and prognosis. Yet reliable tools that combine these factors for individualized risk stratification remain limited (7, 8).

Family history of depression is a consistently robust predictor. The offspring of those with MDD are two-to-three times more likely to develop MDD (5), and family history of other psychiatric disorders, including anxiety disorders and bipolar disorder, is also predictive (6).

Family history reflects a combination of common and rare genetic effects together with familial environments and exposures (6, 9). Its accuracy, however, depends on the number and age of relatives, the reporter's knowledge, and whether relatives have received or disclosed a diagnosis; stigma and historical under-diagnosis mean that familial risk is unknown or incomplete for many individuals (10). Genome-wide association studies (GWASs) have identified over 600 loci associated with depression (11), and polygenic risk scores (PRSs) for depression now explain a modest but increasing proportion of variance (5-6%) (11). PRSs capture only the common-variant component of inherited risk, and integrating them into clinical prediction remains a difficult challenge in psychiatry (12). One approach is multi-polygenic scoring (mPRS), in which PRSs for several genetically correlated disorders and traits are combined using supervised machine learning (13) (14) (15). Because PRSs do not depend on relatives' diagnoses being known, they may add most where family history is unavailable (10).

Childhood trauma and sex are also well-established risk factors. Childhood trauma is associated with increased risk of depression (16) (17) (18) and with earlier onset, greater chronicity, and treatment-resistant depression (17). MDD occurs approximately twice as often in females as in males (19). We tested whether prediction of MDD could be improved by integrating family history of multiple psychiatric disorders with mPRS, childhood trauma, and sex. We used supervised machine learning, which optimises predictive accuracy from multivariable data without requiring prespecified assumptions about causal relationships between predictors and outcomes (14, 20); elastic net regression handles correlated predictors and permits comparison across models using a common predictor set. We used two large UK cohorts, a clinically enriched sample (the Genetic Links to Anxiety and Depression Study and other NIHR BioResource cohorts; GLAD+) and a population-based sample (UK Biobank; UKB), training and testing models within each cohort and validating them externally in the other.

We examined: (1) the relative and incremental contributions of family history, mPRS, childhood trauma, and background factors to classification of lifetime MDD and its severity; and (2) the generalizability and calibration of prediction across cohorts.

Methods

Study sample

GLAD+ combines two UK cohorts: the NIHR BioResource Genetic Links to Anxiety and Depression (GLAD) Study (www.gladstudy.org.uk) (21), which recruits participants with lifetime depression and/or anxiety, and healthy volunteers from other NIHR BioResource (https://bioresource.nihr.ac.uk/) cohorts. Both groups were recontacted during the COVID-19 pandemic for the COPING Study, through which the measures used here were collected.

Participants provide demographic, environmental, and genetic data and consent to medical record linkage and recontact (Supplementary Methods).

UKB is a population-based cohort of over 500,000 UK residents aged 40-69 years at recruitment (2006) (2007) (2008) (2009) (2010). Online Mental Health Questionnaires were completed in 2016-17 (MHQ1; over 157,000 respondents) (22) and 2022 (MHQ2; over 169,000), with more than 206,000 participants completing at least one. Family history of psychiatric disorders among first-degree relatives was collected in MHQ2. All participants provided informed consent.

Measurements

Major depressive disorder (MDD)

Lifetime MDD was assessed in both cohorts with the adapted Composite International Diagnostic Interview-Short Form (CIDI-SF) based on DSM-5 criteria (22). Cases reported at least five symptoms, including depressed mood or loss of interest, nearly every day for at least two weeks. Controls did not meet these criteria and reported neither a diagnosis of depression nor any depressive episode. In UKB, cases met criteria in either MHQ1 or MHQ2 (Table S1). After exclusions for depressive episodes among controls, genotype availability and quality, non-European genetic ancestry, participation in UKB (identified by duplicate genotypes), and missing predictor data, the final GLAD+ sample comprised 9,927 MDD cases and 4,452 controls (Figure S1). UKB included 40,667 cases and 70,755 controls (Table S1).

Three severity measures were available. The number of depressive episodes lasting two or more weeks was reported in categories from "1" to "13 or more" in GLAD+ and as a count in UKB, which was mapped onto the GLAD+ categories (Supplementary Methods). Age of onset was the self-reported age at the first two-week period of depression. In GLAD+ only, the degree of treatment-resistance was assessed as a composite score (0-14) combining the 4-item Maudsley Staging Method (23) and the Patient Health Questionnaire-9(24) (Table S2).

Family history (FH)

In GLAD+, participants reported whether any family member had ever been diagnosed with each of 22 psychiatric disorders (Table S3; optional survey). In UKB, participants reported whether any first-degree blood relative had been diagnosed with each of nine disorders (Table 1; MHQ2). Each disorder was coded as a binary indicator of at least one affected relative. Multiple family history (mFH) therefore denotes a set of disorder-specific indicators entered simultaneously into the model, not a count or composite. Main analyses used the nine disorders assessed in both cohorts; the full 22 GLAD+ items were used only in a sensitivity analysis.

Polygenic risk scores

PRSs were calculated with the GenoPred pipeline(25) using MegaPRS (26) under the LDAK heritability model, with the best-fitting model selected by pseudovalidation on the GWAS summary statistics, and were standardised to an ancestry-matched reference sample (Supplementary Methods). In both cohorts we generated PRSs for MDD, anxiety, ADHD, bipolar disorder, ASD, PTSD, and BMI from European-ancestry GWASs that excluded UKB samples (Table S4). Following the multi-polygenic score approach(15), these seven PRSs were entered as separate standardised predictors rather than combined into a single score.

PRSs for 15 further traits, several derived from GWASs that include UKB participants, were generated in GLAD+ and used only in a GLAD+ sensitivity analysis (Table S4).

Childhood trauma (ChT)

Childhood trauma was assessed in both cohorts with the five-item Childhood Trauma Screener (27), covering emotional, physical, and sexual abuse and emotional and physical neglect; participants reporting any trauma type were classified as exposed (Table S5).

Childhood trauma was the environmental exposure selected because it is robustly replicated, was measured identically in both cohorts, and precedes the typical age of onset; adult exposures were either not harmonised across cohorts or were measured after onset.

Analytical approach

MDD status was predicted using multivariable nested elastic net regression (28), which mitigates overfitting and handles multicollinearity (15). Nested cross-validation used five randomly assigned outer folds for model evaluation and ten inner folds, stratified by case status, for tuning α and λ by grid search (caret default grid) on classification accuracy (binary outcomes) or root mean squared error (continuous outcomes) (Figure 1). Performance was quantified by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), computed from pooled out-of-fold predictions across the outer folds, and by mean squared error for continuous outcomes. AUCs of predictor groups were compared using DeLong's test for paired ROC curves on identical outer folds, with FDR and Bonferroni correction; because DeLong's test may be miscalibrated for nested models (29), comparisons with the full model were interpreted alongside the magnitude of the AUC difference. Class weights adjusted for case-control imbalance (Supplementary Methods).

As a sensitivity analysis for non-linear effects, we applied eXtreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) with the same predictors. A planned hyperparameter grid search did not complete, so prespecified hyperparameters were used (Table S6). XGBoost was evaluated within each cohort by stratified 5-fold cross-validation and, for external validation, trained and class-weighted on one full cohort and applied unchanged to the other (Supplementary Methods).

Predictors were grouped into four domains: (1) mFH for nine psychiatric disorders, (2) mPRS for seven disorders and traits, (3) childhood trauma, and (4) background factors (sex and ten genetic principal components), all entered as penalized predictors in the elastic net and as standard features in XGBoost. Age was not included (see Limitations). Predictors were harmonised across GLAD+ and UKB (Table 1), and analyses used complete cases (Supplementary Methods; Figure S1). Predictor contributions were summarised as standardised coefficients (coefficient × predictor SD), averaged across the five outer-fold models, and as the number of outer folds in which each predictor had a non-zero coefficient.

Models predicting MDD diagnosis, number of depressive episodes, and age of onset were trained within each cohort and evaluated in the other. For external validation, elastic net models trained within the outer folds (80% of the training cohort) were applied to the other cohort; GLAD+-trained outer-fold coefficients were compared with those of a model refit on the full GLAD+ sample (Tables S7-S7a). As a sensitivity analysis of case-mix effects, UKB MDD cases were resampled to match the GLAD+ episode distribution, together with randomly selected controls (n = 2,442, 1,597 cases and 845 controls), with approximate 95% confidence intervals for AUC and R² derived from the estimates and sample sizes (30). We also examined whether the model trained for MDD diagnosis predicted the three severity measures (treatment-resistance in GLAD+ only).

Observed-scale R² was converted to the liability scale(31) assuming a population prevalence of 5% in GLAD+, reflecting its clinically enriched, recurrent case definition, and 15% in UKB, reflecting lifetime MDD in the general population (1, 32); because this conversion depends on the assumed prevalence, estimates across 5-20% are given in Table S8 alongside observed-scale R² and AUC. Positive and negative predictive values (PPV, NPV), sensitivity, and specificity were calculated at prediction-score cut-offs of 0.3-0.7, and calibration plots compared predicted probabilities with observed outcomes.

Results

GLAD+ cases were more severe: 89% had recurrent episodes and 28% reported 13 or more, whereas 41% of UKB cases had a single episode and 72% had three or fewer (Figure 2).

Compared with controls (GLAD+ = 4,452; UKB = 70,755), participants with MDD (GLAD+ = 9,927; UKB = 40,667) were younger, more often female, more likely to report a family history of depression and anxiety, and more likely to have experienced childhood trauma (Table 1).

Prediction of MDD status

In GLAD+, the model integrating family history, mPRS, childhood trauma, and background factors achieved an AUC of 0.83 under nested cross-validation (Figure 3A). Family history was the strongest single domain (AUC=0.74), whereas mPRS (0.66), childhood trauma (0.67), and background factors (0.67) each showed moderate performance alone. In UKB, discrimination was lower but followed the same pattern (Figure 3B): the combined model achieved an AUC of 0.74, and family history (0.65) outperformed mPRS, childhood trauma, and background factors (0.60-0.62; Table S9). The single-trait MDD PRS performed similarly to the mPRS (AUC 0.65 vs 0.66 in GLAD+; 0.59 vs 0.60 in UKB; Table S10), and excluding mPRS from the full model reduced discrimination only slightly (0.82 vs 0.83 in GLAD+; 0.73 vs 0.74 in UKB; Table S11). Each domain improved discrimination beyond background factors alone, with the largest gain from family history (Table S12). Childhood trauma, family history of depression, female sex, MDD PRS, and family history of anxiety had the largest standardised coefficients and were retained in all outer folds in both cohorts (Figure S2).

Extending the GLAD+ predictors from the harmonised set to all 22 family history items and 22 PRSs yielded only small gains (full model AUC 0.836 vs 0.833; Table S13).

External validation and calibration

Models transferred across cohorts (Figure 3C-D). UKB-trained models validated in GLAD+ achieved AUCs of 0.82 (elastic net) and 0.81 (XGBoost); GLAD+-trained models validated in UKB achieved 0.74 and 0.71. Models without mPRS performed similarly (elastic net AUC 0.81 in GLAD+; 0.72 in UKB; Table S11). Coefficients of the GLAD+-trained outer-fold models were highly concordant with the full-sample refit (Pearson r = 0.993-0.998; Tables S7-S7a), and liability-scale R² was higher in GLAD+ than in UKB at every assumed prevalence (Table S8).

Calibration differed by learner and setting (Figure 3E-F, Table S14). Elastic net had calibration intercepts closer to zero in all five train-test settings, whereas XGBoost had slopes closer to one and lower Brier scores in four of five. In external validation, both learners departed clearly from ideal calibration: UKB-trained models underestimated risk in GLAD+ (slope 1.40 for both), and GLAD+-trained models overestimated risk at higher predicted probabilities in UKB (slope 0.79 for elastic net and 0.85 for XGBoost).

At a cut-off of 0.5, the UKB-trained model validated in GLAD+ had a PPV of 0.86, sensitivity 0.77, specificity 0.72, and NPV 0.58; the GLAD+-trained model validated in UKB had an NPV of 0.76, specificity 0.79, PPV 0.60, and sensitivity 0.56 (Table S15). These differences largely reflected MDD prevalence (69% in GLAD+ vs 36% in UKB): applying either model's sensitivity and specificity to the other cohort's prevalence reproduced its observed predictive values (Table S16). Within-cohort models showed similar patterns (Table S17). The four predictor domains were weakly intercorrelated (r = 0.15-0.17; Table S18).

Prediction of MDD severity

Applied to severity outcomes (Table S19), the diagnosis-trained model's predicted risk increased on average across episode categories, although distributions overlapped substantially and the gradient was weaker in UKB (Figure 4). It explained 27% of the variance in the number of episodes in GLAD+ and 11% in UKB, 9% and 6% of the variance in age of onset, and 0.5% of the variance in treatment-resistance (p=1.18x10-5). The diagnosis-trained model therefore carries information relevant to the number of episodes and age of onset, but little for treatment resistance.

Models trained directly on the number of episodes explained 26.5% (GLAD+-trained) and 24.8% (UKB-trained) of the variance in GLAD+, and 12.1% (UKB-trained) and 10.1% (GLAD+-trained) in UKB (Table S20). The same predictors dominated (Figures S3-S4). For the number of episodes, the largest standardised coefficients were for childhood trauma (1.34 episodes per SD in GLAD+; 0.29 in UKB) and family history of depression (0.89; 0.37); for age of onset, childhood trauma (-1.71 and -2.03 years per SD), female sex (-1.75; -1.23), and family history of depression (-1.18; -1.53) predicted earlier onset.

Despite the attenuated cross-cohort R², observed episode counts increased consistently across prediction deciles in both cohorts (Figure S5), indicating preserved ranking; differences were mainly on the raw scale (Figure S6), consistent with calibration shifts.

Resampling UKB cases to match the GLAD+ episode distribution improved the cross-cohort performance of the GLAD+-trained model for both MDD status (AUC 0.78, 95% CI 0.762-0.798 vs 0.74, 95% CI 0.737-0.743 in the full UKB sample) and the number of episodes (R²=14.5%, 95% CI 11.9-17.1% vs 10.1%, 95% CI 9.8-10.4%; Table S20), consistent with case-mix differences contributing to attenuation.

Discussion

In two large UK cohorts spanning clinically enriched and population-based samples, integrating familial, genetic, and environmental risk factors gave consistent classification of lifetime MDD and of the number of depressive episodes. Family history and childhood trauma, the two risk factors a clinician can elicit in a single consultation, were stronger predictors than current PRSs, which contributed complementary but more modest information.

Family history outperformed mPRS in both cohorts (AUC 0.74 vs 0.66 in GLAD+; 0.65 vs 0.60 in UKB; Table S9), and the single-trait MDD PRS performed as well as the mPRS, so for depression the gain from combining PRSs across traits was small (Table S10). PRSs capture only common variants identified by GWASs, whereas family history reflects a broader set of familial risk factors: rare variants such as neurodevelopmental copy number variants and damaging coding variants (33, 34), non-additive genetic effects, geneenvironment correlation, and shared family environment. Larger GWASs and sequencing studies will improve PRSs and may narrow the gap with SNP-based heritability (35), but a substantial difference between measured polygenic liability and overall familial risk remains.

Family history of depression and the depression PRS were only weakly correlated (Pearson's r = 0.10-0.15, Table S18), consistent with their being complementary rather than interchangeable markers of inherited susceptibility (10); combining them captures partially distinct components of inherited risk.

Predictors of diagnosis and of illness course were largely shared: childhood trauma and family history of depression were the strongest predictors of case status, number of episodes, and earlier onset in both cohorts (Figures S2-S4). The contribution of childhood trauma relative to family history was greatest for episode count in GLAD+, consistent with evidence linking trauma exposure to earlier onset and treatment-resistant depression (17).

The diagnosis-trained model explained 27% of the variance in the number of episodes in GLAD+, equivalent to a correlation of 0.52, a very large effect by the benchmarks of psychological research (36), although estimates were lower in UKB. Prediction of treatment resistance was minimal, so current multivariable risk prediction cannot yet identify individuals at risk of poor treatment response.

Models trained in GLAD+ generalized to UKB, with attenuation that partly reflects differences in case mix; harmonizing the episode distribution partially restored performance (AUC 0.78 vs 0.74; Table S20). External validation also revealed reciprocal miscalibration, with UKBtrained models underestimating risk in GLAD+ and GLAD+-trained models overestimating it in UKB, in keeping with the difference in prevalence (69% vs 36%) and severity. Relative risk ranking is therefore transportable across cohorts, but accurate absolute risk estimation requires recalibration to the target population.

Clinically, these findings indicate that systematic assessment of family history and childhood trauma outperforms current PRSs for depression risk stratification; even without PRSs, the models retained strong performance (AUC 0.73-0.82, Table S11). Family history aggregates genetic and shared environmental risk, retrospectively reported childhood trauma is a powerful vulnerability marker, and both are readily obtained in routine practice. This supports a biopsychosocial view of risk in which inherited liability, early adversity, and demographic factors act together, and which is best captured by combining them rather than relying on any one. mPRS alone showed modest but above-chance discrimination (AUC 0.66 in GLAD+; 0.60 in UKB), which may become relevant where genomic data are held in medical records but family history is unknown or incomplete. The incremental gain from adding current PRSs was modest, however, and these results do not support routine clinical implementation of PRSs for depression at this stage, although their performance is likely to improve substantially (35). Because the models were developed and validated for lifetime MDD, their performance for incident MDD should be established in prospective cohorts before prognostic clinical application.

Threshold-based metrics add clinically interpretable information beyond AUC. In the clinically enriched GLAD+ sample, the high PPV suggests value for identifying likely cases but limited rule-out capacity; in the population-based UKB sample, the higher NPV suggests greater usefulness for identifying lower-risk individuals (Table S15). These contrasts primarily reflect the difference in prevalence (Table S16). In practice, the model would first be recalibrated to local prevalence through intercept adjustment(37), with the decision threshold set by the clinical goal and the relative cost of false-positive and false-negative results (38): for example, a high-NPV threshold for primary care triage, or a high-PPV threshold for case identification in specialist settings. Several limitations should be considered. Analyses were restricted to participants of European genetic ancestry. Family history, childhood trauma, age at onset, and number of episodes were self-reported and subject to recall bias and misclassification. Family history was collected through an optional survey in GLAD+ (any relative) and in MHQ2 in UKB (firstdegree relatives only), with differing participation biases, and missing family history was the main reason that 16% of otherwise eligible GLAD+ participants were excluded under complete-case analysis (Figure S1); information on the number, type, and degree of affected relatives could further improve prediction. GLAD+ and UKB differed in recruitment design, age structure, case prevalence, and severity; GLAD+ controls were older than cases, so age was not included as a predictor in either cohort, and the higher case proportion in GLAD+ may have influenced calibration. Case-control imbalance was addressed with class weights and case-mix differences with an exploratory resampling analysis. Genetic principal components were entered as penalized predictors; entering them unpenalized or regressing them out of the PRSs would adjust more robustly for population stratification. Family history and childhood trauma consistently outperformed PRSs, which added modest complementary information, and accurate absolute risk estimation required recalibration for prevalence and severity. These findings highlight both the promise and the current limitations of genetically informed prediction in depression: systematic assessment of family history and early adversity remains central to clinically meaningful risk stratification, independent of future genomic advances.
