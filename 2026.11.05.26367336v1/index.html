Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

P-Value Text
p>.05 Most of the classifiers cannot discriminate the gender and neither a feature set nor a classification algorithm or a training setup has a distinct advantage over the other (all p>.05).

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Text Sentence
p>.05 Most of the classifiers cannot discriminate the gender and neither a feature set nor a classification algorithm or a training setup has a distinct advantage over the other (all p>.05).

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Abstract

Gait analytics is utilized for a wide range of health-related applications, ranging from fitness tracking to medical diagnostics, and beyond. Inertial measurement units that are ubiquitously available in mobile devices provide an accessible means of gait analysis but differences in sensor specifications, placement, and recording protocols limit comparability across studies.

This study developed and evaluated an analysis-centered data warehouse and standardized quality-assessed processing pipeline to facilitate the integration and joint analysis of heterogeneous gait accelerometer data. Six publicly available datasets including a total of 935 persons were integrated into a star-schema data warehouse, and feature comparability was assessed across datasets and sensor locations. Gender classification served as a proofof-concept downstream analysis. Gait-cycle extraction, which serves as foundation for feature extraction, was successfully achieved with an accuracy above 99% for the dataset that provides ground truth information. Comparability varied across datasets and sensor configurations, with correlation coefficients between average gait cycles ranging from 0.5 to close to 1.0. Centrally positioned sensors generally show greater correspondence than peripheral sensors, although differences in statistical features persist even between sensors at nominally identical locations. Gender classification performance remains largely near chance level, and no consistent benefit from pooling datasets could be established. These findings demonstrate that heterogeneous gait accelerometer data can be integrated and processed within a common analytical framework, but standardized processing alone does not ensure comparability of the resulting features. The proposed open-source framework implemented in Python provides a foundation for reproducible cross-study gait analysis and for investigating residual heterogeneity associated with datasets and sensor configurations.

Introduction

Quantification of the human gait is of interest to a variety of disciplines. In the wider context of health, gait features can on the medical side support the diagnostic procedure and monitoring of disorder or therapeutic progress [1] in orthopaedics [1] [2] [3], neurology (e.g., Alzheimer's and Parkinson's disease [4, 5] or stroke [6]), and psychiatry (e.g., schizophrenia, anxiety, depression, and other mood disorder [7] [8] [9] [10]). On the performance side, fitness tracking for well-being purposes [11] [12] [13] as well as analytics in recreational and competitive sports are increasingly utilizing gait tracking and similar measurements [14]. Unrelated to health applications, gait features are harnessed in further use cases such as biometric authentication [15] [16] [17] [18]. The gold standard for gait analytics has long been data from motion capture systems, force plates, and similar laboratory equipment [19, 20]. Those systems are challenged by mobile sensors such as inertial measurement units (IMU) comprising accelerometers and gyroscopes [19, 21, 22]. Those sensors are versatile, lightweight, easily available, cheap, and ubiquitous due to their integration in standard smartphones [23] and other smart devices. Their usefulness in gait analytics has been demonstrated by several studies showing in general a good correspondence between characteristics extracted from video-or force sensor-based methods and from smartphone IMU data [14, 24, 25]. The characteristics assessed in those studies are predominantly spatio-temporal, i.e., step cycle features such as step length, step time, or cadence [24], or additionally kinematic features [14].

Depending on the use case, different features are extracted from the raw gait time series.

They include spatial, temporal, spatio-temporal, kinematic, and kinetic features [14, 19].

Besides general changes in spatio-temporal features such as slowing or reduction of step length [5], specific changes in kinematic features such as smoothness, or the symmetry in spatio-temporal feature are characteristics of interest in a wide range of health applications [6]. Gait asymmetry in particular has been found to be associated with balance instability at older age [26] [27] [28], compromised mental health status [29], and is also prevalent in individuals diagnosed with motor-related neurological disorders such as Parkinson's disease [4, [30] [31] [32] [33], post-stroke conditions [34] [35] [36] [37], and Multiple Sclerosis [38] [39] [40]. Given its clinical relevance, the reduction of gait asymmetry is a targeted outcome in therapeutic interventions for those disorders [41].

Challenges for application of IMU data in gait analysis and related decision support systems cumulate in the lack of robustness and generalisability of the features and models used for those purposes [5, 14, 20]. Diversity in sensor specifications, recording setups, sensor locations [24, 42], data quality, and study designs [20] hamper the comparability and replication of research findings [43]. On top of those variations stemming from recording conditions is the lack of standardized format for data storage and processing [20, 44], which leads to laborious data harmonization efforts whenever data from different studies or simply from different sensors needs to be processed in a comparable fashion. Gait analytics can be improved by leveraging large and diverse datasets [20, 44], and with replication of results with demarcable, and if possible, publicly available, datasets [45]. The demand for such datasets and study designs is aggravated by the application of increasingly complex and therefore data-greedy algorithms for analysis purposes, especially in the light of increasingly widespread use of deep learning [5, [46] [47] [48], and the demand for translating research findings into practise [20, 44]. The possibility to collect large research datasets with IMUs is easy to realize yet storage, processing, and comparison of the data is still laborious due to the diversity on several ends. This study presents 1) an analysis-centered data scheme (data warehouse (DWH) with star schema [49] [50] [51]) for accelerometer data from gait tasks including participant-and studyspecific additional data, 2) a quality-assessed data processing pipeline for data harmonization, pre-processing, feature extraction, and analytics, and 3) a proof-of-concept analysis on the combined dataset. The quality of the gait cycle extraction algorithm is assessed with a quality metric and additionally evaluated against ground truth for one of the datasets included. As feature sets, we provide 1) statistical features from the gait cycles including asymmetry features and 2) the normalized full gait cycle from the Euclidean norm of the jerk. For evaluation purposes, the features were systematically compared across body . sides, sensor locations, and datasets. Furthermore, the feature sets were subjected to 116 exemplary analyses on gender classification with different configurations of included 117 datasets. Data scheme and extract-transform-load (ETL) processes are based on the data of 118 six publicly available datasets comprising gait accelerometer data from both standalone 119 IMUs and sensors built into smartphones [52] [53] [54] [55] [56] [57] [58]. However, data scheme and processing 120 pipeline are designed to be extendable to incorporate further data formats, additional 121 information, and further processing steps such as additional data quality checks or additional 122 features. Both technical artifacts are provided in an open-source toolbox with accompanying 123 technical documentation. 124 125.

Results

Data Storage

The data is stored in a star schema comprised of central fact tables each containing specific parts of recorded data or their derivates. Accelerometer data is stored raw, preprocessed, in gait cycles, and as statistical features. Additional fact tables contain raw electromyography (EMG), gyroscope, and magnetometer data. The dimension tables connected to the fact tables contain additional information that provide the analytical context for the fact data, such as participant characteristics, or sensor positions and specifications.

Quality Management

We verified the gait cycle extraction process with the Marea [52] dataset that provides a ground truth of the gait cycles based on force sensitive resistors. The average dominant peak detection accuracy for the left and right ankle sensor was 99.3±2.1% and 99.9±0.2%, respectively. The heel strike is recorded 56.7±18.6ms and 58.9±65.4ms before the detected peak across all data samples for the left and right foot, respectively.

In order to evaluate the data quality independent of a ground truth, the quality of the extracted gait cycles (n=343,431) from the accelerometer data was assessed by correlating each individual uniform cycle with the corresponding average cycle. In order to be included in further analyses, we set a threshold of 0.7.255,542 cycles, i.e., 74.4% of the cycles, fulfilled this criterion.

Statistical Evaluation of Harmonized Data

In order to pool data across datasets or sensors, the features used for analyses need to be comparable. We first evaluated comparisons of exemplary sensors where the data stem either from different datasets, sensor locations, or body sides for illustrative purposes. The comparison between two sensors on the right waist (same location) from the datasets OU-ISIR-SAID [55] and Vajdi [53] (different datasets) shows only a moderate overlap in the average gait cycle (r=0.82; Fig 1, top). Along this line, most of their statistical features were not comparable (Fig 1, bottom). In contrast, the pairing of two other datasets (Vajdi [53] and DBHuGa [56]) even with similar but different sensor locations (left front pocket vs. left thigh) shows better harmonization results. The average gait cycles correlate higher (r=0.96; Fig 2 top) and the differences between the statistical features were slightly less although still prevalent (Fig 2, bottom). There is a very good correspondence for sensors of the same dataset in the same location but for opposite body sides (DBHuGa [56] shin (Fig S1) and OU-ISIR-SAID [55] waist (S6)), decreasing for sensors with increasingly different locations (DBHuGa [56] comparison of left shin to left thigh (Fig. S3) and left wrist (S5)).

A less detailed but more comprehensive picture emerges from the full correlation matrix between all sensors in all datasets (Fig 3). The sensor data from the center waist electrodes correlate very highly independent of their dataset of origin (Fig 3, small triangle). Waist sensor placed on one side of the body follow closely regarding their correlation strength. We find a general tendency for the sensor placed on the far end of the extremities (ankle and wrist) to have a lower correlation with those placed more towards the bodies center of weight. The sensors from the OU-ISIR datasets have a visibly lower correlation with sensors from other datasets then the rest of the comparisons (Fig 3). In order to put the correspondence across sensor positions and datasets in perspective and assess the variability of gait within participants, we also calculated each sensor's within and across subject correlations (Fig 4). The across subject correlations of the McGill [57], Marea [59], and DBHuGa [56] sensors are in a similar order as the higher correlations across sensors and databases and consistently higher than the within subject correlations. The across subject correlations in the Vajdi [53] and OU-ISIR datasets [54, 55] are markedly lower and in the range of the within subject variability.

Gender Classification on Harmonized Data

The ultimate test for harmonized data is its utilization in a downstream classification task, comparing performance of individual datasets with joint performance as well as generalization across datasets. We chose a gender classification task since this information is available in all but one datasets and the gender of a person can be detected in its gait [60, 61]. In order to match the dimensionality across feature sets we condensed the 200 time points of the unified gait cycle with a principal components analysis (PCA) to the ten first components that explain a variance of 75.2±11.1%.

Using again the two sensors on the right waist (same location) from the datasets OU-ISIR-SAID [55] and Vajdi [53] (different datasets) (cf. Fig 1) we have to conclude that the features we used for this task do not seem well suited for gender discrimination, at least not from the right waist sensors of these two datasets (Fig 5). Most of the classifiers cannot discriminate the gender and neither a feature set nor a classification algorithm or a training setup has a distinct advantage over the other (all p>.05). The features from sensors in the left front pocket and left thigh, respectively, from the Vajdi [53] and DBHuGa [56] datasets (cf. Fig 2) seem to be similarly inadequate for gender classification (Fig 6). However, here the statistical features tend to perform better in general (F1,96=15.052, p<0.001) albeit with marked differences across training setups (F5,96=2.416, p=0.041). The classification results based on the remaining sensor pairings (Figs. S2, S4 and S7) present a similar picture. Given the overall around chance level classification performance, an advantage of harmonizing and pooling the data can neither be confirmed nor refuted.

Discussion

This study developed and evaluated an analysis-centered data warehouse and processing pipeline for harmonizing heterogeneous accelerometer data acquired during walking. The technical framework integrates participant-, study-, sensor-, and recording-specific information with standardized processing steps for gait-cycle extraction, quality assessment, and feature generation. Across six publicly available datasets, the results demonstrate that such a framework can substantially reduce technical heterogeneity and enable joint analysis of data originating from different studies. At the same time, the analyses show that technical harmonization does not necessarily result in comparable resulting gait representations.

Residual differences related to dataset, sensor location, and body side remain evident, both for normalized gait cycles and for derived statistical features. This distinction between harmonization of data processing and comparability of the underlying measurements presents important implications for cross-study gait analysis.

The results from the statistical evaluation demonstrate that data can be represented in a common analytical format but still lack analytical comparability. For example, accelerometer recordings obtained from the right waist in the OU-ISIR-SAID [55] and Vajdi [53] datasets were processed using the same pipeline and nominally represent the same sensor location, yet the resulting average gait cycles show only moderate correspondence, while several statistical features differ substantially. This finding indicates that differences between studies cannot be attributed solely to data format or preprocessing. Rather, sensor characteristics, recording protocols, participant populations, and other study-specific factors may remain embedded in the resulting features even after standardized processing. Therefore, technical harmonization is a necessary first step for creating a common representation in which residual heterogeneity can be quantified and investigated, rather than a procedure that completely removes heterogeneity. This issue is well known in other modalities, e.g., batch effects in genomics data [62, 63] or scanner effects in magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [64] [65] [66] [67] [68]. Adjustment methods such as Combating Batch (ComBat, [62]), originally developed for genetic batch effects but transferred successfully to features derived from a variety of MRI modalities [64] [65] [66] [67], provide tools for aligning the distribution characteristics of the features while preserving differences in participant characteristics of interest. While those approaches may be suitable for the statistical features derived from the accelerometer data, the partially distinct forms of the unified gait cycles suggest that this feature might not be fit for combination across datasets. These considerations are particularly relevant for multi-study analyses, where apparently standardized inputs may still contain systematic study-or devicespecific information.

The results furthermore suggest that harmonization strategies have to explicitly retain sensor location information as an important characteristic of the signal. Data recorded at different body locations are influenced by different biomechanical components of body motion [69, 70] and are therefore not expected to exhibit identical temporal or statistical characteristics even after normalization. Sensors mounted at the waist or other central locations capture a more global representation of the whole-body movement because they are located closer to the body's center of mass, whereas peripheral sensors are more strongly influenced by limbspecific motion and attachment characteristics [71] [72] [73]. Our correlation analysis indicates generally stronger correspondence among centrally positioned sensors, even across datasets, whereas sensors located toward the extremities, such as the ankle and wrist, tend to show lower correspondence with sensors positioned closer to the body center or with sensors from other datasets. This renders the former especially suitable for analyses across datasets. However, depending on the use case, the former or latter is better suited to capture the characteristic of interest [72].

The pairwise comparisons demonstrate that some sensor-position combinations have substantially stronger correspondence, indicating that harmonization can facilitate meaningful comparisons under appropriate conditions. The across-and within-subject analyses provide additional evidence that the magnitude of variability depends on both the dataset and the sensor configuration. In several datasets, across-subject correlations were higher than the corresponding within-subject variability, whereas other datasets showed the opposite pattern. This heterogeneity suggests that the relative contributions of subject-specific gait characteristics and recording-specific factors are not constant across datasets.

Consequently, pooled analyses should account for the individual structure of the data and only opt for a combination of datasets when the conditions are met.

The gait-cycle extraction procedure provides an important foundation for our harmonization approach. When evaluated against annotated gait events in the MAREA dataset [52], dominant-peak detection showed high accuracy for both left and right ankle sensors, with mean accuracies over 99%. These findings support the feasibility of extracting a consistent gait-cycle representation directly from single sensor accelerometer recordings, even when explicit gait-cycle annotations are unavailable. However, this validation should be interpreted within the scope of the available ground truth. The algorithm was evaluated against event annotations from a single dataset and only for ankle-mounted sensors; therefore, these results do not establish equivalent performance across all sensor positions represented in the data warehouse. This limitation is particularly relevant for peripheral and non-peripheral sensors, for which the relationship between local acceleration patterns and conventional gait events may differ. Future validation using independently annotated datasets and multiple sensor locations would therefore be important to establish the generalizability of the extraction algorithm. Along this line, we decided for a unified processing of the data from each sensor individually instead of a global gait cycle extraction across sensors because of the heterogeneity in number of sensors and their locations across datasets. An extension of the preprocessing pipeline might be useful for specific sets of sensors when a global gait cycle with temporally aligned sensor cycles is necessary for addressing specific research questions.

The quality-control procedure offers a complementary safeguard against unreliable cycle extraction. Of the 343,431 extracted gait cycles, 74.4% met the predefined correlation threshold of 0.7 with the corresponding mean gait cycle and were retained for downstream analysis. This demonstrates the practical value of an automated quality-assessment step when integrating data from heterogeneous sources. At the same time, the finding that approximately one quarter of all extracted cycles did not meet the criterion indicates that the choice of quality threshold has a potentially substantial effect on the resulting analytical dataset. The threshold of 0.7 was selected for the present analysis and is configurable in the pipeline. Nevertheless, the current study does not establish whether this threshold is optimal across datasets, sensor locations, or gait conditions. The within-subject correlations demonstrate substantial differences for sensor positions and datasets suggesting that it might be recommended to adapt individual thresholds. Importantly, however, a low correlation with the mean cycle is not necessarily equivalent to poor data quality, as atypical but genuine gait cycles may also differ from the recording-specific mean. Especially in clinical populations, this variability can even be an important feature of interest [74] [75] [76].

The rational for the gender-classification analysis was to provide a proof of concept for the use of the harmonized data in downstream analysis, rather than to develop an optimized gender-classification model. Classification performance was generally close to chance for the examined sensors, and a consistent benefit from combining data across datasets or sensor locations could therefore not be established. These findings do not demonstrate that the harmonization framework is ineffective. Rather, they illustrate that successful technical harmonization does not automatically translate into improved performance in a downstream machine-learning task. Furthermore, the configuration of the six training setups provides a suitable strategy for proper evaluation of joint data usage. Comparing the power of the increased data size achieved by pooling with the performance of the individual datasets and the pooled data while keeping dataset size provides a comprehensive test for improvement via data pooling. Training on one dataset and testing on the other provides a test unbiased to preprocessing for the generalizability across studies.

The strength of this study is the use of multiple publicly available datasets with substantial variation in participant numbers, recording duration, sensor type, sensor location, and study characteristics. This heterogeneity reflects the conditions under which a general harmonization framework ultimately needs to operate. The data warehouse additionally retains contextual information alongside the sensor measurements, providing a basis for investigating these sources of variation rather than discarding them during preprocessing.

The framework is also designed to accommodate additional processing steps, sensor modalities and further datasets, while the technical artifacts are provided in an open-source toolbox with accompanying documentation. These endeavours support reproducibility and provide a basis for extending the framework beyond the datasets evaluated here. Some limitations, nevertheless, need to be considered. First, the available datasets differed substantially in participant characteristics, recording duration, sensor configuration, and study protocol, and these factors are often intrinsically confounded. For example, an observed dataset effect cannot necessarily be attributed to the dataset itself because it may reflect differences in hardware, placement, participant population, or recording conditions. Second, several potentially informative comparisons could not be performed because the required metadata or features were unavailable. Consequently, the pairwise comparisons should be regarded as representative examples rather than a complete characterization of all possible sources of heterogeneity. Future studies might explicitly model contextual factors such as age, footwear, walking surface, and other recording conditions in order to disentangle their contributions [77].

Overall, the present study demonstrates that heterogeneous accelerometer datasets can be integrated into a common analytical framework with standardized gait-cycle extraction, quality management, and feature engineering. At the same time, the results show that technical harmonization alone does not remove systematic differences associated with datasets and sensor configurations. The principal contribution of the framework is therefore to provide a reproducible infrastructure in which the differences across datasets can be identified, quantified, and incorporated into subsequent analyses. Such an approach facilitates more transparent cross-study comparisons, reproducible feature engineering, and ultimately more robust development and validation of gait-analysis methods across heterogeneous real-world datasets.

In this structure, one or more central fact tables store the primary observational data while multiple dimension tables provide contextual information relevant to those observations. Each dimension table is directly associated with one or more fact tables, and the dimensions are independent from each other. In contrast to normalized relational designs, the star schema intentionally permits a certain degree of data redundancy to simplify query operations. By reducing the number of required joins, this approach improves computational efficiency at data read-out and is therefore well suited for analytical environments that demand rapid processing of large and complex datasets. The implemented scheme includes four initial fact tables: Raw Accelerometer, Raw Gyroscope, Raw Magnetometer, and Raw EMG. Only the raw accelerometer data is further processed. They store the respective raw sensor readings together with timestamps to maintain temporal order. Additional fact tables, introduced during feature extraction, are the preprocessed accelerometer data, extracted gait cycles in different normalization steps as well as statistical features extracted from the gait cycles. Eight dimension tables provide complementary information on activities (activity), ground conditions (ground), participants (person), recording metadata (recording), technical sensor information (sensor), sensor placement (sensor_location), step events (step_event), and rotation information for harmonization of sensors (world_coordinate_system).

A comprehensive description of the data model, including a detailed data dictionary, is available in the accompanying technical documentation. SQL scripts for setting up the data scheme with a postgreSQL (The PostgreSQL Global Development Group) database are provided in the code repository.

Cycle Extraction

The analysis focusses on gait cycles derived from the accelerometer data during walking.

Because most available datasets do not explicitly provide gait cycle annotations, we designed and tested an algorithm to extract gait cycles directly from the sensor signals.

Each accelerometer provides tri-axial measurements (𝑥, 𝑦, and 𝑧 components), which inherently depend on the sensor's orientation, as each axis captures acceleration along a specific spatial direction. To mitigate this orientation dependency and obtain a signal representation that reflects only the underlying dynamic behaviour, the Euclidean norm of the jerk is used for further processing.

To estimate the global step frequency, we identified the first prominent peak in the frequency spectrum. For sensor placements near the body's center of mass, this peak typically appears around 2 Hz, whereas peripheral sensor locations yield frequencies closer to 1 Hz.

For non-centered sensor placements, a gait cycle is defined as the concatenation of the swing phase of the limb bearing the sensor, resulting in a dominant peak, followed by the contralateral swing phase, which yields a non-dominant peak. Dominant peaks are first identified by locating local maxima that exceed the global mean, while enforcing a minimum inter-peak distance determined by the estimated step frequency. To suppress non-dominant events among these candidates, we apply an amplitude threshold derived from the mean of the candidate peak amplitudes, again subject to the same distance constraint. Assuming an alternating dominant/non-dominant structure, we subsequently examine the inter-peak intervals with respect to the step period to infer and insert missing dominant peaks whose amplitudes may fall below the mean-based threshold.

Non-dominant peaks are then identified by segmenting the signal between successive dominant peaks under the assumption that each segment contains exactly one non-dominant event. Because this event lies between two flanking minima, it is designated as the maximum bounded by the pair of 'major minima' (Fig 9). To determine these minima, we first detect all local minima within the segment, excluding those within a small neighbourhood of dominant peaks to avoid false detections. The absolute minimum in the segment is taken as the first major minimum. For each remaining candidate minimum, we compute the highest signal value along the path connecting it to the absolute minimum. Minima that lie in the same valley as the absolute minimum yield lower connecting maxima, whereas those positioned across the opposing valley share the amplitude of the non-dominant peak. Among the minima belonging to this opposing valley, the one with the lowest value is selected as the second major minimum. The non-dominant peak is then identified as the highest local maximum located between these two major minima. The gait cycle is then defined as the major minimum preceding the dominant peak until the major minimum preceding the next dominant peak.

Fig 9 Major minima detection for non-dominant peak detection.

All detected minima are marked with a circle. The first minimum is simply the lowest value (hot pink). The second minimum is not the second lowest value since this can be on the same side of the non-dominant peak. The algorithm described in the text finds the true second minimum (dark purple).

For centrally mounted sensors, the extraction of individual gait cycles is accomplished by segmenting the signal at every second minimum between successive peaks. This approach is feasible because contributions from the left and right leg appear with comparable prominence in the signal, resulting in a symmetric pattern that allows reliable cycle delineation.

Ground Truth

To evaluate the performance of the cycle extraction algorithm, we used the ankle-mounted sensors from the MAREA dataset, which provide annotated step-event labels. Heel-strike events are expected to correspond, with a temporal offset, to the dominant and non-dominant peaks identified within the extracted gait cycles. However, because the dataset occasionally contains misordered event annotations, only walking sequences with correctly ordered step-event labels are included in the analysis. Wrist-mounted accelerometer data are excluded, as the dominant peak observed in wrist motion during a gait cycle is not temporally aligned with the heel strike and therefore does not constitute a reliable reference event.

Because heel-strike annotations and signal-derived peak positions do not coincide perfectly, a tolerance threshold was defined to account for minor temporal deviations. Based on typical step frequencies and the dataset's sampling rate, a maximum difference of 123 milliseconds between the detected peak and the annotated ground-truth event was established as an acceptable margin of error.

Quality Management

To identify gait cycles unsuitable for inclusion in further analysis including the calculation of statistical features, a quality-assessment procedure was implemented. A mean gait cycle was computed for each recording, each participant, and each dataset, separately for each sensor location. In datasets where only a single recording was available per participant, the mean gait cycle at the recording and participant levels was identical. Cycles exceeding a predefined correlation threshold with their respective mean gait cycle were retained for further analysis, whereas those falling below the threshold were excluded. In this study, a correlation coefficient of 0.7 was selected as the cutoff threshold for inclusion.

Classification of Gender

As a proof of concept for the data harmonization, we perform a gender-classification task, as gender information is available for most datasets included in the comparative analyses.

Classification performance was evaluated for the same combinations of conditions used in the statistical comparisons (Table 2), enabling a direct assessment of how differences in dataset, sensor location, and body side affect predictions.

For the classification analyses, we used two machine learning algorithms: logistic regression as a linear classifier and random forests as a nonlinear classifier. The same two feature sets as in the statistical analysis were evaluated: (i) the ten statistical features and (ii) ten features derived from the uniform gait cycles (see below). Six training-testing strategies were examined. These included training and testing on data from a single sensor; training and testing on combined data from both sensors, either preserving the dataset size of a single sensor or doubling it; and training on one sensor while testing on the other. For all six strategies, the amount of training data was matched to the size of the smaller dataset to ensure comparability across conditions. The data was sampled once in the beginning of the analysis, so all six strategies relied on the same participants.

To harmonize the dimensionality across feature sets, we used the first ten principal components derived from a principal component analysis (PCA) calculated on ten randomly chosen gait cycles per participant and condition.

Classification performance was evaluated using stratified cross-validation. To ensure statistical reliability, each fold contained a minimum of five samples, with the total number of folds capped at ten. For comparisons that yielded less than five folds we only report the balanced accuracy over all folds, for the remaining comparison we calculated mean and standard deviation across folds. Stratification was performed primarily with respect to gender, and in cases when data from two sources were combined also with respect to the originating dataset, to the extent permitted by the available sample distributions. To assess whether classification performance exceeded chance level, we conducted one-sided t-tests against chance level of 0.5. This test was skipped if the data was partitioned in less than five folds.

A conservative significance threshold of α = 0.01 was chosen to account for the multiple comparisons inherent in the evaluation across classifiers, feature sets, and training strategies. For completeness, results achieving only a threshold of α = 0.05 or even exceeding α = 0.001 are also indicated. In cases where the number of available folds was insufficient to support a t-test, we instead reported the weighted mean of the fold-wise accuracies as a descriptive summary measure.

To evaluate the influence of classification algorithm, feature set, and training strategy on model performance, we conducted mixed-effects analyses of variance (ANOVA) on the accuracy outcomes, with classification algorithm as within-subject factor and feature set as well as training-testing strategy as between-subject factors.
