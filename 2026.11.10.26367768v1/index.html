How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. 1. Introduction

A negative control outcome (NCO) is an outcome believed not to be causally affected by the exposure of interest [1, 2]. Because an NCO shares the bias structure of the outcome of interest but carries no true effect, any systematic departure from the null in its estimate may indicate residual confounding, selection bias, or information bias [1, 2]. This property underlies empirical calibration, in which effect estimates for a large set of NCOs are used to derive an empirical null distribution and to calibrate the p-values and confidence intervals for the outcome of interest [3, 4]. Yet in a scoping review of pharmacoepidemiologic studies that used negative controls, 81% used them to detect bias but only 9% to correct bias and 4% to calibrate p-values [5]. Nearly half (46%) relied on a single negative control [5]. Calibration requires a large set of NCOs to capture a diverse mix of biases and to stabilize the null distribution, with 50 to 100 suggested as a rule of thumb [6]. NCOs must be selected not only accurately but also in sufficient numbers.

In practice, however, NCOs are still selected largely by hand. The main automated resource is the Common Evidence Model (CEM) [7], developed by the Observational Health Data Sciences and Informatics (OHDSI) community. It proposes candidates for which no evidence of association has been documented. However, absence of evidence does not establish a plausible null, and CEM does not account for the data available at a given institution. Large language models (LLMs) can read drug information and reason about causal plausibility, but applying them to NCO selection raises three design requirements. First, because LLMs are prone to hallucination, their judgments should complement, not replace, the evidence that CEM integrates from product labels, spontaneous reports, and literature. Second, extended deliberative reasoning is computationally costly [8], so deliberation should be applied selectively to the candidates that need it. Third, selection should reflect local data availability, . so that the resulting set is both usable at the institution and large enough for calibration.

Statement of Significance 105

Problem or Issue

Selecting negative control outcomes (NCOs) is labor-intensive. Empirical calibration requires dozens of NCOs [6], yet extensively verifying every candidate is impractical, and the resulting selections vary across investigators and studies.

What is Already Known

The Common Evidence Model (CEM) automates the previous manual search for NCO candidates, but it relies on counting co-occurrence evidence and does not assess semantic plausibility. It also does not consider institutionspecific data characteristics, so many proposed outcomes may be unusable at a given institution.

What this Paper Adds

MENTOR, a multi-agent system that combines CEM evidence collection with LLM-based semantic reasoning, achieving a higher positive predictive value than CEM while proposing more NCOs. Costly deliberative reasoning is reserved for candidates on which CEM and the first-pass judgment disagree, and an iterative loop searches for a target number of NCOs.

Who would benefit from the new knowledge in this paper

Investigators conducting observational studies who use NCOs to detect residual confounding and other biases and to correct for them through empirical calibration. observational studies of glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists found that the stated rationale for selection rested most often on clinical or subject-matter reasoning, and no study discussed the assumptions behind its negative controls [11]. This approach is labor-intensive.

It also offers limited reproducibility across investigators and studies. More fundamentally, the defining assumption of an NCO is that no causal relationship exists between the exposure and the outcome. This assumption must be checked for every candidate, which is rarely feasible by hand across the full space of potential outcomes.

2.2 Knowledge-based approaches to NCO selection

To alleviate this burden, OHDSI developed CEM [7], a knowledge base that extends an earlier automated repository of drug-adverse reaction evidence [12]. This precursor was used to identify the 76 NCOs of a multinational antihypertensive study [13]. CEM integrates evidence from product labels, spontaneous reporting systems, and the published literature, and proposes exposure-outcome pairs lacking documented evidence of association as NCO candidates [7, 12]. Even so, the final list is still curated by hand. Earlier studies reviewed prevalence-ranked candidates manually [14] or had two physicians reduce 690 automatically filtered pairs to about 100 [15].

Two aspects of NCO selection lie outside what CEM was designed to do, and both are currently left to manual review. The first is plausibility. CEM screens by the presence or absence of documented evidence. This narrows the candidate space efficiently but does not by itself establish that a null relationship is plausible. Absence of evidence may reflect an unstudied pair, and a single piece of evidence excludes an outcome even when that evidence arises from confounding by indication or from a loosely related parent concept. Because CEM aggregates co-occurring index tags, such as Medical Subject Headings, rather than reading the source text, this judgment falls to the reviewer. The reviewer must also account for under-and over-reporting in the underlying sources [16, 17]. The second is data availability. CEM is a database-independent knowledge base, so it does not consider whether a candidate outcome is sufficiently observed at a given institution. OMOP databases differ in coding granularity and practice despite shared standardized vocabularies, and institutions differ in patient characteristics and size [18, 19]. An NCO can be analyzed only if enough patients in the study cohorts experience the outcome, so a proportion of proposed NCOs may be unusable in a particular database. Multi-institutional studies typically fix a single NCO set for comparability across sites, and candidates with too few events are dropped at each site. Smaller institutions are therefore left with fewer NCOs for calibration.

2.3 LLMs in drug safety and multi-agent systems

LLMs are increasingly applied in medicine [20]. They can read and interpret drug information [21], judge whether a causal relationship is plausible [22], and invoke external tools to retrieve structured evidence [23]. Recent examples include AgentMD [24] and agents that construct OMOP concept sets [25]. However, LLMs can generate plausible but unsupported content [26], and how they weigh supplied evidence depends on how it relates to their parametric knowledge [27]. This argues for pairing LLM reasoning with evidence-based screening rather than replacing it. Extended reasoning improves performance on difficult problems but spends excessive computation on simple ones [28].

Multi-agent LLM systems divide a task among specialized agents to make such selective allocation possible. However, they fail in characteristic ways, such as flaws in system design, misalignment between agents, and missing or incorrect verification [29]. Long inputs . add a further risk, because LLMs use information in the middle of a long context less reliably than at its beginning or end [30]. Evaluating such systems at scale increasingly relies on LLM judges. They can agree with human preferences about as well as humans agree with each other [31] but tend to favor their own generations [32]. This motivates using a judge from a different model family and validating it against human experts.

3.1 Multi-agent system

3.1.1 Overview and design principles

MENTOR generates institution-specific NCOs from a database in the OMOP CDM. Its inputs are the ingredient-level OMOP concept IDs of the target and comparator drugs. Its output is a set of concept IDs for valid NCOs with rationales, exported as an ATLAS concept set expression and a CohortGenerator negative control outcome table (Supplementary S6). Four agents (Curator, Suggester, Adjudicator, and Diagnostician) are orchestrated as a directed graph (Figure 1). Candidates are drawn from a Concept Pool built from the institution's own patient counts. Each candidate is assessed by two independent lines of evidence, CEM evidence and LLM reasoning over drug information, and labeled concordant or discordant according to whether the two agree. Only discordant candidates are passed to the Adjudicator. The Diagnostician then lowers the patient-count threshold (the minimum number of patients with the outcome; min_patients in the code) stepwise until a target number of NCOs is reached.

3.1.2 Curator

The Curator verifies that each input concept ID is a standard ingredient concept in the OMOP vocabulary and retrieves its name for label lookup. It then queries the openFDA drug labeling application programming interface (API) of the U.S. Food and Drug Administration [33] for . single-ingredient labels whose substance or generic name matches the ingredient, falling back to all matching labels when no single-ingredient label exists. Because label text often differs across manufacturers, the Curator takes the text variant shared by the largest number of manufacturers. The sections used are indications, mechanism of action, contraindications, and four adverse-effect sections (Supplementary S1). An LLM, instructed not to add information absent from the labels, condenses this text into a structured drug context of standardized medical terms (Supplementary S2). This drug context informs all subsequent LLM judgments.

3.1.3 Suggester

The Suggester first constructs the Concept Pool from the institution's CDM by counting, for each standard condition concept, the distinct patients with at least one record of that concept or any of its descendants. Overly broad and pregnancy-related concepts are removed with a predefined exclusion list. The pool is built once per institution and reused across drug pairs. Candidates meeting the current patient-count threshold are assessed in two ways. In the evidence-based assessment, the two ingredients are queried in CEM through the OHDSI WebAPI, and a candidate is proposed by CEM if no CEM source reports an association with either drug. Candidates not covered by CEM are outside the system's scope. In the plausibility assessment (first pass), the LLM receives the drug context, the candidate's name, and its ancestor concepts up to three levels. It returns a valid/invalid judgment with a two-to foursentence rationale. All judging prompts share one NCO definition. An outcome is valid only if, for each drug, (1) it is not an indication, (2) it is not a contraindication or known adverse effect, and (3) it has no causal pathway through the drug's mechanism, metabolism, or pharmacokinetics. The LLM may draw on its own pharmacological knowledge for criterion (3), but absence from the drug context alone is not treated as evidence of no relationship. A candidate is labeled concordant if the two assessments agree and discordant otherwise.

3.1.4 Adjudicator

The Adjudicator resolves every discordant candidate with the model's reasoning mode (thinking mode in Qwen3) enabled. It also resolves any candidate whose first-pass call returned no parsable judgment. Besides the first-pass inputs, it receives the CEM verdict and the firstpass judgment and rationale. It also receives the CEM evidence counts by source (publications, product labels, and spontaneous reports) and by matching level (exact, descendant, parent, and ancestor), and the CEM flags, such as indication or contraindication, drug-induced, or too broad (Supplementary S3). It is instructed to identify which source is likely wrong without default preference. Exact or descendant matches in product labels, and other exact or descendant evidence with a plausible pathway, are treated as disqualifying. Evidence found only at the parent or ancestor level, or attributable to confounding by indication, does not preclude acceptance. When uncertainty remains, the candidate is rejected, because an invalid NCO biases calibration more than an omitted one (Supplementary S2.3).

3.1.5 Diagnostician

The Diagnostician checks whether the current NCO set has reached a target number (100 by default). Because a parent concept and its children, or near-synonymous concepts, may all be judged valid while largely capturing the same patients and bias, the raw number of NCOs overstates the number of independent NCOs. The Diagnostician therefore receives the names of the current NCOs and is instructed to estimate the effective number that would remain if each group of parent-child or near-synonymous concepts were counted as one. The LLM makes this estimate from the concept names rather than computing it from the vocabulary hierarchy. The effective number, not the raw number, is compared with the target. It is also instructed to judge diversity, that is, whether the set spans many organ systems, disease types, and clinical domains, and to treat a concentrated set as insufficient even if it is large. It returns . a binary decision with a rationale (Supplementary S2.4). If the effective number falls short of the target, the threshold is lowered by one step, and the Suggester and Adjudicator process the newly eligible candidates. The loop ends when the target is reached or when the next threshold would fall below a predefined floor.

3.2 Experimental setup

3.2.1 Data source and target drugs

We used the Ajou University School of Medicine (AUSOM) database, which contains the electronic health records of approximately 2.9 million patients with visits from January 1994

3.2.2 CEM baseline

We obtained CEM-based NCO candidates through the WebAPI of OHDSI ATLAS [34]

3.2.3 Candidate pool construction

The exclusion list is fixed and shared across institutions. It contains 15,951 concepts that are unsuitable as NCOs regardless of the drug pair. These are overly broad or nonspecific concepts and pregnancy-related concepts, whose occurrence is governed by reproductive status rather than drug exposure (Supplementary S5).

In AUSOM, 19,231 standard condition concepts were recorded for at least one patient, and 13,009 remained after exclusion. To evaluate all methods on a common candidate set, the experiments used a single fixed patient-count threshold of 10,000 without the thresholdlowering loop, which yielded 186 condition concepts. After excluding those not covered by CEM, 117, 115, and 117 candidates remained for the antidiabetic, anticoagulant, and antihypertensive pairs, respectively (349 in total; Supplementary S5).

3.2.4 Models and experimental instantiation

MENTOR was implemented with LangGraph [35] (Supplementary S6). All agents used Qwen3.6-27B with FP8 quantization, served by vLLM 0.23.0 [36] on two NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPUs with tensor parallelism, at a temperature of 0. In the main configuration, reasoning mode was enabled for the Adjudicator only. Each configuration was run once per drug pair with the threshold and the floor fixed at 10,000, so that all configurations shared the same candidates and the Diagnostician was invoked once per run. The threshold-lowering loop from 70,000 down to 10,000 was reconstructed offline from these runs. The hyperparameters and prompts are given in Supplementary S1 and S2. The prompts were developed iteratively by the authors and frozen before these experiments.

3.2.5 Reference standard

The primary reference standard was an LLM judge, Claude Opus 5 (Anthropic; model identifier claude-opus-5), from a different model family from the MENTOR model to mitigate . self-preference bias [31, 32]. The judge was blinded to the CEM verdict and all MENTOR outputs. It received the candidate outcome, the drug context, and the same NCO definition as MENTOR. It was instructed to check the three conditions for each drug and to draw on its own pharmacological knowledge, because the drug context may be incomplete (Supplementary S2.5). Each candidate was judged once.

As a secondary reference, 90 candidates (30 per drug pair) were drawn from the evaluation set by stratified random sampling with proportional allocation. Three epidemiologists with 7-10 years of experience each reviewed one drug pair. Reviewers were blinded to the CEM verdicts, MENTOR outputs, and LLM-judge labels and followed a written labeling guideline (Supplementary S7). They were given only the drug pair and the outcome name but could consult pharmacological references, product labels, and the literature. They classified each candidate as a valid NCO, not a valid NCO, or indeterminate, with a brief reason.

Reviewers were instructed not to use generative AI for the labeling decisions, although its use for translating or summarizing literature was permitted.

3.2.6 Performance metrics and statistics

Each candidate was treated as a binary classification instance with a valid NCO as the positive class. Because invalid NCOs contaminate the empirical null distribution, positive predictive value (PPV) is the key safety metric, whereas sensitivity determines how many usable NCOs are obtained. We report the number of proposed NCOs, accuracy, PPV, negative predictive value (NPV), sensitivity, specificity, and Cohen's κ [37], with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) from percentile bootstrap (2,000 resamples, stratified by drug pair for pooled estimates). Paired differences in correctness were tested with the exact McNemar test.

Estimates are reported against the judge labels on the full evaluation set and against the human labels on the human-labeled sample, excluding indeterminate ratings. Consistency . between the two references was assessed by the Spearman rank correlation of accuracies across all methods and configurations and by the direction of each configuration's difference from the main configuration. The judge was validated against the human labels by percent agreement, Cohen's κ, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV, with an exact McNemar test for asymmetric disagreement. In sensitivity analyses, indeterminate ratings were treated as valid or not valid.

3.2.7 Secondary analyses (a) Component analysis.

We compared four decision rules for discordant candidates, namely CEM only, first-pass LLM only, the Adjudicator without reasoning mode, and the Adjudicator with reasoning mode (the main configuration). Configuration ablations changed one factor at a time. They enabled reasoning mode in all agents, removed the drug context (ingredient names only), used raw instead of summarized label text, or removed the concept hierarchy. Agreement between configurations was summarized by the Jaccard index of the final NCO sets. Reproducibility was assessed by comparing first-pass outputs between the two Adjudicator configurations, which share identical Curator and Suggester settings.

(b) Discordance analysis, adjudication load, and cost.

We partitioned the MENTOR output into concordant and discordant candidates and counted false positives (FPs) in each group against the judge labels. For concordant candidates, which bypass adjudication, the error rate was also estimated from the human-labeled sample.

Adjudication load was the number and proportion of candidates routed to the Adjudicator. LLM calls, tokens (input, output, and reasoning), and wall-clock time per agent were extracted from the run logs. Time spent waiting for the CEM WebAPI was recorded separately.

(c) Availability and threshold sensitivity.

. We counted the outcomes in the CEM candidate list obtained through the WebAPI that had at least one, 1,000, 5,000, and 10,000 patients in AUSOM (with descendants, without the exclusion list). Because candidates are judged independently of the threshold, the NCO sets the loop would produce at higher thresholds are subsets of the set at 10,000. We therefore reconstructed from the main runs the numbers of NCOs proposed by MENTOR and by CEM, and the MENTOR PPV, at thresholds from 70,000 to 10,000 in steps of 1,000.

(d) Error analysis.

All misclassified candidates in the main configuration (FPs and false negatives [FNs] relative to the judge) were coded with a fixed codebook (Supplementary S8, S9). The codes covered decision path, error type (whether the evidence needed for the correct decision was in the drug context), and failure mode in the deciding agent's rationale. Codes were assigned by Claude Opus 5.5 (claude-opus-5-5), a model different from both the MENTOR model and the judge, and were not verified by human reviewers.

3.2.8 Reporting and ethics

This study is reported in accordance with the TRIPOD-LLM checklist [38] (Supplementary S10). The Institutional Review Board of Ajou University Hospital reviewed the study and granted an exemption (AJOUIRB-EX-2026-412), waiving the requirement for informed consent because only de-identified data were analyzed retrospectively. Patient-level data were accessed only within the institutional environment. The self-hosted LLM received only concept names, drug information, and aggregate patient counts, and external services (openFDA, the CEM WebAPI, and the LLM APIs used for the judge and error coding) received no patient data.

4.4 Component analysis

Two runs with identical Curator and Suggester settings produced identical drug contexts and first-pass judgments, including rationales, for all 349 candidates (Supplementary S14). Among the discordance-handling strategies, the Adjudicator with reasoning mode achieved the highest accuracy (Figure 2b; Supplementary S15), although its gain over the first pass alone was not statistically significant (0.017 [0.000 to 0.037], P=0.11). The gain came from sensitivity (0.021 [0.004 to 0.042]) and NPV (0.037 [0.009 to 0.072]), with no difference in PPV (0.007 [-0.009 to 0.022]) or specificity (0.009 [-0.028 to 0.045]; Supplementary S15). Without reasoning mode, the Adjudicator rejected more candidates and performed worse than the first pass . (-0.032 [-0.060 to -0.003], P=0.04). Reasoning mode therefore added 0.049 [0.020 to 0.077] (P=0.002). Among the 154 discordant candidates, accuracy was 0.18 when following CEM, 0.82 when following the first pass, 0.86 for the Adjudicator with reasoning mode, and 0.75 for the Adjudicator without reasoning mode (Supplementary S15).

In the ablation analysis, reasoning mode in all agents increased accuracy (0.054 [0.017 to 0.092], P=0.008) at 2.6 times the wall-clock time. Ingredient names only (-0.023 [-0.057 to 0.009], P=0.23), raw label text (P>0.99; 3.4-fold token usage), and no concept hierarchy (P=0.23) had no significant effect. Against the expert labels, the configurations ranked in the same direction relative to the main configuration, except that removing the concept hierarchy was slightly better (by two candidates) and using ingredient names only was tied. The final NCO sets overlapped substantially across configurations (Jaccard index, 0.83-0.90; Supplementary S16).

4.5 Concordance, false positives, and adjudication load

CEM and the first-pass judgment agreed on 195 candidates (55.9%) and disagreed on 154 (44.1%). The proportion of discordant candidates was 22.2%, 59.1%, and 51.3% for the antidiabetic, anticoagulant, and antihypertensive pairs, respectively (Supplementary S17, S18).

Discordance was dominated by candidates that CEM rejected but the first pass accepted (136 of 154), of which the Adjudicator accepted 133 (97.8%). Of the 19 such candidates that the judge rated not valid, the Adjudicator rejected only 3. Of the 18 candidates that CEM accepted but the first pass rejected, the Adjudicator accepted 7 (38.9%).

Of the 35 FPs, 17 (48.6%) were concordant acceptances, which bypass adjudication.

Of the 26 outcomes CEM proposed that the judge rated not valid, the first pass rejected only 9.

The PPV was 0.83 (86/103) for concordant and 0.87 (122/140) for adjudicated acceptances. Of . the 92 concordant rejections, 21 were valid according to the judge. Among the 63 expertlabeled candidates, 4 of 21 concordant acceptances and none of 23 adjudicated acceptances were rated not valid.

The main configuration used 512 LLM calls and 1.37 million tokens and took 158 minutes for the three pairs (53 minutes per pair, of which 2.6 minutes in total were spent waiting for CEM). The Adjudicator accounted for 30.1% of calls, 52.0% of tokens, and 82.3% of wallclock time, largely because of reasoning tokens. Per proposed NCO, the system used 5,626 tokens and 38 seconds (Supplementary S19, S20).

4.6 Availability of usable NCOs

CEM, queried through ATLAS, proposed 227, 169, and 170 outcomes for the antidiabetic, anticoagulant, and antihypertensive pairs. Of these, 149 (65.6%), 106 (62.7%), and 100 (58.8%) had at least one patient in AUSOM, and only 20 (8.8%), 14 (8.3%), and 12 (7.1%) had at least 10,000 (Figure 3a; Supplementary S21). These are the raw WebAPI counts, before restoring optimization-removed descendants and applying the exclusion list, and thus differ from the 121 proposals evaluated above. As the threshold was lowered from 70,000 to 10,000, MENTOR reached 50 proposed NCOs (before collapsing related concepts) at 16,000, 16,000, and 14,000 patients, whereas CEM reached 50 only for empagliflozin-sitagliptin and at most 30 and 17 for the other pairs (Figure 3b). The MENTOR PPV was 0.69, 1.00, and 0.88 at 20,000 patients and 0.77, 0.95, and 0.84 at 10,000 (Supplementary S21). In all 18 runs, the Diagnostician judged the set at 10,000 (55-100 NCOs; estimated effective counts, approximately 40-60) insufficient for the target of 100.

4.7 Error analysis.

The main configuration produced 35 FPs and 25 FNs (Supplementary S18, S22). FPs arose about equally from adjudication and concordant acceptance (n=18 vs. 17). Most were labelgrounded, that is, inferable from the drug context (n=24, 13 through two or more inference steps). None involved an outcome named verbatim in the context. The remaining 11 rested on established (n=5) or speculative (n=6) evidence outside it (Supplementary S9).

Among the 18 adjudicated FPs, the most frequent failure mode was mechanism-first filtering (n=7), in which an outcome was accepted because no direct mechanistic pathway was found, although a listed adverse effect could cause it indirectly. All seven involved rib fracture concepts, across all three drug pairs. Among 17 concordant FPs, 4 reflected taxonomic mismatch, in which a broader listed term (e.g., upper respiratory tract infection for acute sinusitis) was not linked to the outcome despite appearing in the hierarchy input. Three reflected label dismissal, in which a listed adverse effect was acknowledged but judged noncausal. Most FNs (18 of 25) reflected over-extended mechanistic reasoning, such as rejecting gastritis for warfarin-apixaban because of bleeding risk.

5. Discussion

In this study, a multi-agent LLM system informed by CEM evidence proposed about twice as many NCOs as CEM from the same institution-specific candidates, with higher accuracy, sensitivity, and PPV. The conclusion held under expert labels. Deliberative adjudication restricted to discordant candidates added a modest gain over the first-pass judgment, but only when the adjudicating model was allowed to reason. Finally, at the 10,000-patient threshold used here, only 7%-9% of CEM proposals were usable in the institution's data.

CEM's conservative yield follows from its screening rule. It rejects an outcome whenever any source reports evidence at any matching level, without distinguishing a causal . effect. Most discordance (136 of 154) arose from such exclusions, and the Adjudicator accepted 133 of them, in agreement with the first pass. Concordance, however, did not guarantee correctness. The first pass identified only 9 of CEM's 26 invalid proposals, and concordant acceptances accounted for about half of all FPs. First, selective deliberation concentrates reasoning where the two sources conflict and reduces computation relative to reasoning in all agents. It improves accuracy little, because the Adjudicator rarely overrode the first pass. Its contribution lies instead in producing rationales that cite the source and level of evidence, which a reviewer can audit. Second, enabling reasoning in all agents improved accuracy, but concordant acceptances still bypassed adjudication. Directing additional review to them is one way to improve the system.

The system is intended to support, not replace, the final review by investigators. The system automates much of this curation and returns, for every candidate, the CEM evidence by source and matching level and the first-pass rationale. For discordant candidates, it also returns the Adjudicator's decision and reasoning. Investigators can therefore confirm the final list by checking the stated evidence and reasoning rather than reviewing every candidate from scratch. This review should include concordant acceptances, which contained about half of the FPs.

The availability analysis supports institution-specific selection. Of CEM's proposals, 34%-41% did not occur in our database and only 7%-9% reached 10,000 patients. These counts precede the washout or time-at-risk restrictions that further reduce the events available in an actual study cohort. Because MENTOR operates on each institution's own Concept Pool, it could run locally at each site of a distributed network and share only the resulting concept sets. Such local selection could complement a common network set and help restore sufficient numbers at sites where the common set falls short. At the 10,000-patient floor, no configuration in our data reached 100 effective NCOs, the upper end of the 50-100 suggested for calibration. [6]. Whether that target is attainable at lower thresholds, and at what cost in PPV, remains to be examined.

Apart from the final investigator review, the system requires no manual steps. From ingredient concept IDs, it produces outputs that can be loaded directly into ATLAS or Strategus pipelines. A run took about 53 minutes per drug pair on a single self-hosted model, or 12 minutes with the Adjudicator's reasoning mode disabled, but with lower sensitivity (0.80 vs.

0.89; Supplementary S15).

Most remaining errors were failures of reasoning over information the system already had, and four patterns suggest remedies. First, the Adjudicator ruled out downstream consequences, such as fractures from listed causes of falls, on mechanistic grounds without consulting the listed adverse effects. An explicit check of listed effects before mechanistic reasoning would address this. Second, some FPs dismissed an acknowledged adverse effect as non-causal, contrary to the NCO definition, which an explicit prompt rule could prevent. Third, concordant FPs missed broader listed terms present in the hierarchy input. Although limited to three levels, that input listed a median of 24 (up to 69) terms per candidate, including synonyms, grouping terms from other vocabularies, and very broad concepts such as 'Infectious disease'. This may also explain why removing it had no significant effect. Fourth, most FNs rejected a valid outcome through a long or speculative causal chain. Bounding the length of admissible chains is one possible remedy. These remedies were not evaluated, because revising prompts on the same drug pairs would overfit.

That neither removing nor expanding the drug labels changed accuracy suggests that the model relied largely on its own pharmacological knowledge. This is consistent with reports that LLMs adopt supplied evidence depending on how it relates to their parametric knowledge. [27] and use long inputs unevenly [30]. Whether the drug context matters more for less familiar drugs than the six studied here remains open.

The evaluation relied on an LLM judge, which raises the question of circularity. To mitigate it, we used a model from a different family, blinded it to the system outputs, and validated it against domain experts. Agreement was substantial (κ=0.63), and the judge's PPV was high (0.95). Its disagreements tended to be conservative, which would bias the system's PPV downward, and the main conclusions held on expert labels. This study has further limitations. In terms of generalizability, it was conducted in a single institution with three drug pairs. Applicability to other institutions and drug classes remains to be shown. The drug context depends on U.S. product labels, so drugs or indications not approved in the United States are not covered. For experimental design, we restricted candidates to concepts with at least 10,000 patients and an existing record in CEM so that all methods could be compared on the same candidates. We also reconstructed the thresholdlowering loop offline instead of running it. Performance on rarer outcomes and on outcomes outside CEM coverage, and the behavior of the loop below 10,000 patients, are unknown. Each configuration was run once with a single LLM. The architecture does not depend on a specific model, but other models were not evaluated. Because the prompts were developed on the same drug pairs, the results may favor the system. In terms of the reference standard, the system and the judge share the NCO definition and the drug context. The judge validates adherence to that definition rather than an independent conception of validity. Agreement was also weakest for lisinopril-hydrochlorothiazide (κ=0.42). The human sample was small, contained 30.0% indeterminate ratings, and had one reviewer per pair, so inter-rater agreement could not be assessed. Reviewers were instructed not to use generative AI, but individual use could not be fully controlled. How much the system shortens investigator review remains to be evaluated.

. The error analysis was coded by a third model with a fixed codebook but was not verified by human reviewers. Finally, the Diagnostician only judges whether the current set is sufficient.

It does not remove redundant concepts or optimize the final set for diversity, which is left to investigators.

6. Conclusion

We developed a multi-agent LLM system that selects institution-specific negative control outcomes. It combines CEM evidence with LLM reasoning over drug information and accounts for data availability in each institution's OMOP CDM. In a single-institution evaluation of three drug pairs, the system proposed substantially more NCOs than CEM with higher sensitivity and PPV. The system is intended as a decision-support tool whose final list investigators confirm against the evidence and rationales it provides. Adjudication of discordant candidates helped only when reasoning mode was enabled. The system requires only standard OHDSI resources and a locally served open-weight LLM. It could therefore be run at other institutions with an OMOP CDM, including each site of a network study, without patient-level data leaving the site. Future work should evaluate the system across multiple institutions and in real-world study workflows..

In this pair, CEM proposed 30 outcomes (sensitivity, 0.31) and MENTOR proposed 87 (sensitivity, 0.92), with PPVs of 0.93 and 0.95, respectively. On the 63 human-labeled candidates, MENTOR outperformed CEM (accuracy, 0.79 vs. 0.44; PPV, 0.91 vs. 0.79). Across the eight systems compared (CEM, the first pass, and six MENTOR configurations), accuracy rankings under human labels correlated with rankings under LLM judge labels, both on the same 63 candidates (Spearman ρ=0.98, P<0.001) and on the full set of 349 (ρ=0.84, P=0.009).
