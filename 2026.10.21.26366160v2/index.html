Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

What we found

We found 27 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module retrieves information from repositories.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Chronic pain affects most individuals with spinal cord injury (SCI) and frequently impairs daily function, and nearly half experience neuropathic pain. Neuropathic pain is commonly thought to develop months after injury and to persist once established. These views rest on classifying each patient as having neuropathic pain or not, although patients often report several pains. The same binary label is the usual target of prognostic and biomarker studies that aim to predict life-limiting pain. We tracked up to three of the worst pains per interview from injury to one year in 140 TRACK-SCI participants, using the DN4 interview (DN4i, threshold 3). Within three days of injury, half of all pain areas and three quarters of participants met the threshold, and pain-area prevalence changed little over the year. Individual pains, however, often changed. Only a third remained above the threshold throughout, one third resolved or fluctuated, and pains in the same person often followed different courses. At more than half of interviews with several pains, one pain met the threshold while another did not, so that the patient's classification depended on the pain considered. We therefore derived a threshold-free Pain Composite Score from the DN4i items and pain intensity. The items formed a single dimension, and the composite was associated with sleep and mood interference whereas the binary classification was not. Neuropathic pain after SCI thus appears early, does not invariably persist, and differs between a person's pains, and graded pain-level measures may offer a more stable target for predicting life-limiting pain.

1. Introduction

Chronic pain affects up to 80% of individuals with spinal cord injury (SCI) and reduces their quality of life [14, 37, 38], and about half of all individuals with SCI develop neuropathic pain [9, 36]. Neuropathic pain arises from a lesion or disease of the somatosensory nervous system [41]. The International Spinal Cord Injury Pain (ISCIP) classification divides it into at-level and below-level pain relative to the neurological level of injury (NLI), a distinction thought to reflect different mechanisms [8, 15, 37, 38].

Current understanding of neuropathic pain after SCI rests on two assumptions. The first is that it develops over the months following injury, which is why it is usually assessed in the subacute and chronic phases, even though it can be detected within days [34] and is preceded by altered pain modulation in the first weeks [12, 19]. The second is that neuropathic pain persists once it is established, a view supported by its persistence between one and six months in pooled cohorts [44] and by its stable prevalence during inpatient rehabilitation [10]. Both assumptions derive from dichotomous classifications of the patient or of the single most bothersome pain, the pain that clinical studies usually assess [46], and group estimates built on such classifications may conceal change in individual pains. Pain intensity follows distinct trajectories during rehabilitation [7], and individuals often report several pains that may each follow their own course, so that the pain a patient ranks as worst may not be the one with the most neuropathic features.

How neuropathic pain is measured may in turn shape what is concluded about its course. In research settings, it is usually identified with screening questionnaires such as the Douleur Neuropathique en 4 Questions (DN4), whose seven-item interview version (DN4i) retains acceptable accuracy at a threshold of 3 when the examination items cannot be obtained [6], and which reached an area under the curve of 0.86 in SCI [20]. Such questionnaires were designed to support rather than replace a clinical diagnosis based on the description, location, and examination of pain [8, 16, 29, 31], and were validated almost exclusively at their cutoff [6, 13, 30, 31], where specificity can be modest [13]. Used as binary classifications, they place pains that differ by a single item on opposite sides of the decision, so that small fluctuations appear as new or resolved pain and statistical power is lost in prognostic and biomarker studies [1]. A continuous score would avoid these problems, but whether the DN4 items reflect a single dimension, the property that justifies summing them, has rarely been examined [39], and instruments that grade symptom severity [5, 49] are seldom collected in acute cohorts. Moreover, neither a binary classification nor a graded symptom score indicates whether a pain limits daily life, which is what matters most to patients. These limitations matter most for studies that aim to predict neuropathic pain. Prognostic models and biomarker studies require an outcome that is defined consistently across patients and over time, and they have generally labeled patients as developing or not developing neuropathic pain, on the assumption that the condition persists once present [45]. When patients report several pains with different characteristics, and when a change in a single item, such as a newly reported tingling, can move a pain across the threshold between interviews, such a label may depend on which pain is assessed and when. A model trained to recognize it may then learn the instability of the classification rather than differences in the underlying pain. Prediction may therefore be better served by a graded target that reflects both the neuropathic character of a pain and its intensity, the features most likely to determine whether a pain limits a patient's life.

To our knowledge, no study has followed each pain separately from injury. We therefore tracked each reported pain, up to three per interview, in the Transforming Research and Clinical Knowledge in SCI (TRACK-SCI) study from the hyperacute phase to one year, to ask whether early onset and persistence hold for individual pains rather than for patients. We then derived a threshold-free Pain Composite Score from the DN4i items and pain intensity and examined whether the items form a single dimension. Finally, we asked whether the pain that participants ranked as worst was the pain with the most neuropathic characteristics, and related the composite score, the binary classification, and pain intensity to interference with sleep, mood, and day-to-day activities.

2. Methods

>Methods

2.1. Study design and participants

The data were collected in TRACK-SCI, an ongoing multicenter prospective observational study conducted by the Brain and Spinal Injury Center (BASIC) at the University of California San Francisco (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04565366) [42]. The study was approved by the institutional review board of the University of California San Francisco (protocol 15-16115, Transforming Research and Clinical Knowledge in Spinal Cord Injury), and participants or their legally authorized representatives provided informed consent. Only participants enrolled at Zuckerberg San Francisco General Hospital and Trauma Center (UCSF/ZSFG) routinely received in-hospital pain assessments, and the present analysis was therefore restricted to that site. Participants were assessed with the DN4 during the acute hospital stay, on the same days as the International Standards for Neurological Classification of SCI (ISNCSCI) examinations (days 0 to 7 and discharge) [35, 42], and at 3, 6, and 12 months after injury by structured interview. A comprehensive list of TRACK-SCI measures has been published previously [42]. Age at injury, gender, race, ethnicity, and mechanism of injury were taken from the TRACK-SCI database, and the American Spinal Injury Association Impairment Scale (AIS) grade and the NLI were taken from the ISNCSCI examinations. Table 1 reports the AIS grade and the level of injury at the last in-hospital IS-NCSCI examination. Participants were included if they had at least one DN4 assessment in which all seven interview items were answered, irrespective of the examination items.

2.2. Pain assessment

Pain was assessed with a modified version of the International SCI Pain Basic Data Set [46] in structured interviews, conducted in person during the acute hospitalization and almost always by telephone at follow-up, apart from occasional interviews conducted in person at a follow-up ISNCSCI examination. At each interview, participants described up to three pain problems, hereafter called pain areas, numbered 1 to 3, and ranked them from worst to least severe. The number followed the pain across interviews (Section 2.4), independently of its rank. For each pain area, they rated the average pain intensity over the previous week on a 0 to 10 numeric rating scale and completed the DN4. At each follow-up interview, participants also rated how much their pain had interfered with day-to-day activities, overall mood, and sleep in the previous week, each on a 0 to 10 scale. Current pain treatments and their drug classes were recorded at follow-up.

For pain area 1 at the 6-and 12-month interviews, pain location relative to the NLI was classified as above, at, or below level by applying the ISCIP definition [8] to the body regions recorded on the pain location checklist. The reference level was the NLI from the ISNCSCI examination at the same time point, or the estimated NLI recorded when the examination could not be performed (one interview). The at-level window comprised the NLI dermatome and the three dermatomes below it. Each body region was mapped to its approximate dermatome range, and a pain area was classified as below-level when any region lay more than three dermatomes below the NLI, even if the pain also involved the at-level zone, as at-level when its regions fell within the window, and as above-level when all regions lay rostral to the NLI. Pain areas with a region straddling the lower edge of the window could not be placed unambiguously and were classified as at-level.

2.3. DN4 interview scoring and prevalence

The DN4 comprises seven symptom descriptors, such as burning, tingling, and numbness, and three items that require a physical examination [6]. Because most assessments were performed by telephone, the examination items could not be obtained reliably, and all analyses used the seven-item interview version (DN4i). In patients after fracture surgery, a DN4i administered by telephone gave the same classification as an earlier in-person DN4 in 94% of cases [43]. Each item was scored 1 (yes) or 0 (no), giving a total of 0 to 7, and a score of 3 or more was considered screen-positive [6]. A pain area with a screenpositive score is referred to as DN4i-NP, a term that denotes the questionnaire classification rather than a clinical diagnosis of neuropathic pain.

Prevalence of DN4i-NP was calculated for pain areas and for participants (any pain area positive) in five time bins (0 to 3 days, 4 to 14 days, 3, 6, and 12 months). Because some pain areas were assessed more than once during the hospital stay, each pain area was counted once per time bin and considered positive when any of its assessments in that bin were positive. The 95% confidence intervals were exact binomial intervals for participants and, to account for the nesting of pain areas within participants, bootstrap intervals that resampled participants (5,000 resamples) for pain areas. At interviews with more than one pain area, we also recorded whether all, some, or none of the pain areas were DN4i-NP, and the participant-level classification (any pain area positive) was followed across time bins in participants assessed in at least two bins.

2.4. Pain-area trajectories

Trajectories were defined for individual pain areas. Because the order in which participants reported their pains could change between interviews, each pain area was matched across interviews by its reported location, so that a trajectory followed the same pain over time. For each pain area with at least one in-hospital assessment (0 to 14 days) and at least one follow-up assessment, we constructed a status sequence across the five time bins used for prevalence, in which a bin was screen-positive when any assessment within it was screen-positive. A sequence with no change in status was classified as Persistent DN4i-NP (all positive) or No DN4i-NP (all negative), a single change as Resolved DN4i-NP (positive to negative) or Delayed-onset DN4i-NP (negative to positive), and two or more changes as Fluctuating DN4i-NP. Trajectories were defined by these rules rather than by data-driven clustering because the number of pain areas and of assessments per pain area was too small to estimate latent trajectory classes reliably. Because pain areas assessed more often had more opportunities to change, we also report how often the status changed from one assessed time bin to the next, a measure that does not depend on the number of assessments. In a sensitivity analysis, the status of each in-hospital bin was taken from its last assessment. A second analysis classified all pain areas with at least two follow-up assessments, using the same rules without the in-hospital status.

2.5. Pain Composite Score and dimensionality of the DN4i

Because the DN4 was designed as a binary screening tool and a binary classification is poorly suited to prognostic and biomarker models, we derived a threshold-free score. Pain intensity was included alongside the seven items so that the score would reflect how severe each pain was as well as its neuropathic character. The seven DN4i items and average pain intensity were standardized and entered into a principal component analysis (PCA), following the syndromic workflow previously described for neurotrauma data and implemented in the syndRomics package [40]. We used linear rather than nonlinear PCA because a binary item admits only one quantification, so that nonlinear PCA yields the same solution for the seven items [28], whereas its rescaling of pain intensity would depend on the number of components extracted. The PCA was fitted on the 528 pain-area assessments with a recorded intensity, and scores were computed for all 596 assessments by setting a missing intensity to the sample mean. This applied to 68 assessments, most of them at follow-up interviews that also lacked interference ratings, and to only 2 assessments in the interference models. The number of components to retain was determined with the permutation test implemented in syndRomics [40]. In an approach analogous to Horn's parallel analysis [22], this test compares the variance accounted for (VAF) by each component with its distribution after 10,000 permutations of the data, with Benjamini-Hochberg-adjusted p-values across all components [3]. Loadings were standardized for interpretation. The first component (PC1) was oriented so that DN4i items loaded positively, scaled to unit standard deviation, and retained as the Pain Composite Score. The relationship between PC1, the DN4i total, and pain intensity was described with Spearman correlations.

Because combining the DN4i items into a single score assumes that they reflect one underlying dimension, the PCA and permutation test were repeated with the seven DN4i items alone, using all pain-area assessments with complete items. To address the nesting of pain areas within participants, this analysis was also performed with one pain area per participant (the first assessment of the worst-ranked pain). The internal consistency of the summed items was then estimated with the Kuder-Richardson formula 20 (KR-20).

2.6. Worst-ranked pain and neuropathic characteristics

At interviews in which more than one pain area was reported, we calculated the proportion of interviews at which the participant's worst-ranked pain had the highest PC1, DN4i total, and pain intensity among that participant's pain areas, counting ties as concordant. The expected proportion under random ranking was the mean of 1/k across interviews, where k is the number of pain areas.

2.7. Association with pain interference

Associations with pain interference were tested at the 3-, 6-, and 12-month interviews for the pain that participants themselves ranked as worst. Because interference was rated for pain overall, this is the pain most likely to limit daily life, and it is identified independently of its neuropathic characteristics. Selecting the pain with the highest PC1 would have made the analysis circular, and averaging across pain areas would have diluted the contribution of the pain that limits the patient most. For each interference domain (sleep, mood, day-to-day activities), a linear mixed model was fitted with PC1 as the outcome, with interference, time point, and their interaction as fixed effects, any current pain treatment and the AIS grade at the time point as covariates, and a random intercept per participant [2]. The interference effect was tested with a Type III F-test. With the interaction in the model, this test evaluates the interference slope averaged across the three time points, and it does not depend on the coding of the time factor. Averaged and time point-specific slopes with 95% confidence intervals were estimated with estimated marginal trends [27], and the time point-specific slopes are presented descriptively. To display the data underlying each model, predicted PC1 values across the interference range were estimated at each time point, averaging over the covariates in proportion to their frequency. P-values for the three domains were adjusted for multiple testing as described in Section 2.9.

Four further analyses characterized these associations. First, a model without the interaction served as a sensitivity analysis. Second, each participant's interference score was decomposed into the participant mean (between-participant component) and the deviation from it (within-participant component), which were entered together [11]. Third, to compare scoring approaches, the models were refitted in a common sample with standardized outcomes and standardized interference, using in turn PC1, the DN4i total, the binary DN4i classification (score of 3 or more), and pain intensity as the outcome. Finally, interference was modeled as the outcome with standardized intensity and PC1 entered together, with and without the number of pain areas. Because PC1 incorporates pain intensity, this model was repeated with the DN4i total, which does not, in place of PC1.

2.8. Opioid use

Opioid use at each follow-up interview was classified as present when an opioid was recorded for any pain area and absent only when the medication record documented no opioid use. Interviews at which the medication list could not be obtained were treated as unknown and excluded. Because few treated participants had confirmed absence of opioid use, this comparison is reported descriptively (Fig. S6B).

2.9. Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean and standard deviation or median and range, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Prevalence, trajectories, the concordance between the worst-ranked pain and its neuropathic characteristics, and the analyses of pain location and opioid use were descriptive. Inferential analyses comprised the permutation tests of the principal components (Section 2.5) and the linear mixed models of pain interference (Section 2.7). Mixed models were fitted by restricted maximum likelihood, and fixed effects were tested with Type III F-tests using Satterthwaite degrees of freedom [25]. Because participants contributed several pain areas and several time points, this dependence was accounted for by the participant random intercept in the mixed models and by resampling participants rather than assessments for the bootstrap confidence intervals. Each model used all assessments with complete data for its variables, and the only imputation was the setting of a missing pain intensity to the sample mean when PC1 was computed (Section 2.5). Model residuals and random effects were inspected for normality and constant variance, and the influence of individual participants was assessed by refitting each model with one participant omitted at a time. Benjamini-Hochberg adjustment [3] was applied across components in the permutation tests and across the three interference domains in the primary mixed models, whereas the sensitivity and secondary analyses were not adjusted for multiple testing. Two-sided p-values below 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed in R 4.6.0 with the packages syndRomics 0.1.0 [40], lme4 2.0-6 [2], lmerTest 3.2-1 [25], emmeans 2.0.4 [27] and ggplot2 4.0.3.

3. Results

>Results

3.1. Participants and pain assessments

Of the 226 participants enrolled in TRACK-SCI at UCSF/ZSFG, 16 withdrew and 70 did not complete a DN4 questionnaire, leaving 140 participants with at least one DN4 assessment (Fig. 1). They contributed 596 pain-area assessments from 330 interviews. One pain area was reported at 140 interviews, two at 114, and three at 76. The pain assessment was offered at every interview, and because the DN4 characterizes a reported pain, only 6 of the 596 assessments recorded no current pain. Forty-two participants were assessed only in hospital, 56 only at follow-up, and 42 in both phases. During the hospital stay, 112 pain-area assessments from 51 participants fell within 0 to 3 days of injury and 135 from 57 participants within 4 to 14 days, and at follow-up 133, 117, and 99 assessments were obtained from 74, 68, and 56 participants at 3, 6, and 12 months. Cohort characteristics are summarized for all participants and by assessment phase in Table 1. The cohort was predominantly cervical (77.9%) and incomplete, with AIS D the most common grade at the last in-hospital examination (60.7%), and the grade could not be determined for 11 participants. The groups contributing to each phase were similar in age and gender, whereas AIS D was more frequent among participants assessed in both phases (81.0%) than among those assessed only in hospital or only at follow-up (50.0% and 53.6%), consistent with the greater participation of less severely injured patients in TRACK-SCI follow-up.

3.2. Prevalence of DN4i-NP from the hyperacute phase

To examine whether neuropathic characteristics are already present early after injury, contrary to the view that neuropathic pain develops over months, we determined the proportion of pain areas and of participants with DN4i-NP at each time point (Fig. 2A). Within 0 to 3 days of injury, 48 of 98 pain areas (49.0%, 95% CI 40.6 to 57.4) met the DN4i threshold, and 38 of 51 participants (74.5%, 60.4 to 85.7) had at least one pain area that did. The proportion of pain areas with DN4i-NP changed little over the following year, ranging from 46.6% to 59.1% at the later time points and reaching 52.5% (40.4 to 63.8) at 12 months. The proportion of participants with at least one DN4i-NP pain area declined modestly, to 60.7% (46.8 to 73.5) at 12 months. Participant-level prevalence exceeded pain-area prevalence at every time point because participants often reported pains of different character, and at 109 of the 190 interviews with more than one pain area (57.4%) at least one pain area met the threshold while another did not (Fig. 2B). More than a quarter of all assessments (26.7%) scored 2 or 3, within one item of the threshold. DN4i-NP was therefore present from the hyperacute phase, and a considerable share of assessments lay close enough to the threshold for a single item to change their classification.

3.3. Trajectories of individual pain areas

To examine whether DN4i-NP persists once present, we followed each pain area from the hospital stay through the first year. Sixty-six pain areas from 40 participants had both inhospital and follow-up assessments (Table 2, Fig. 2C), and the assessments of each participant are shown in Fig. S1. Persistent DN4i-NP was the most frequent trajectory (30.3% of pain areas), followed by No DN4i-NP (24.2%), Fluctuating DN4i-NP (19.7%), Resolved DN4i-NP (15.2%) and Delayed-onset DN4i-NP (10.6%). Resolved and fluctuating courses together accounted for one third of pain areas (34.8%). The distribution was broadly similar in a sensitivity analysis in which the status of each in-hospital time point was taken from its last assessment rather than from any assessment. Because a pain area assessed at more time points has more opportunities to change, and therefore to be classified as fluctuating, we also examined each step from one assessed time point to the next. A pain area that met the threshold at one time point fell below it at the next in 30% of cases (23 of 77), and a pain area below the threshold met it at the next time point in 31% (20 of 65). Trajectories also differed between the pains of the same person. Of the 22 participants with more than one pain area, 20 had pain areas that followed different trajectories. Most pain areas came from participants with cervical injuries (55 of 66) and AIS grade D (55 of 66), which precluded a comparison of trajectories across injury characteristics.

Among 104 pain areas from 63 participants with at least two follow-up assessments, the same trajectories were present, with Persistent DN4i-NP again the most common (35.6%), Resolved DN4i-NP in 12.5% and Fluctuating DN4i-NP rare (2.9%) (Table 2, Fig. 2D). Twelve of the 13 Fluctuating DN4i-NP pain areas also had at least two follow-up assessments, and eight of these were classified as Delayed-onset DN4i-NP when only follow-up interviews were considered. These pain areas met the threshold in hospital, fell below it at the first follow-up interview, at 3 months in six of the eight, and met it again thereafter. Classifying participants rather than pains obscured much of this turnover. Of 87 participants assessed at two or more time points, 46 (52.9%) had at least one DN4i-NP pain area at every time point and would have been classified as having per- sistent neuropathic pain, whereas 30 (34.5%) changed classification at least once. Overall, the stable prevalence concealed considerable change in individual pain areas, one third of which resolved or fluctuated.

3.4. Pain Composite Score and dimensionality of the DN4i

To obtain a threshold-free measure of both the neuropathic character and the severity of each pain, we entered the seven DN4i items and pain intensity into a PCA. 3.5. Neuropathic characteristics of the worst-ranked pain

Because pain is usually assessed and treated through the pain that the patient ranks as worst, we asked whether this pain was also the pain with the most neuropathic characteristics. At the 190 interviews in which more than one pain area was reported, the participant's worst-ranked pain had the highest PC1 in 58.9% of interviews and the highest DN4i total in 62.6%, compared with 43.3% expected under random ranking (Fig. S3A). It was the most intense pain at 76.6% of the 184 interviews with intensity ratings for every pain area. Results were similar at follow-up alone (58.6%, 61.6% and 72.9%). When the worst-ranked pain did not have the highest PC1, the difference was substantial (median 1.03 standard deviations), and at 60 of the 190 interviews (31.6%) another pain area scored at least 0.5 standard deviations higher (Fig. S3B). Participants therefore ranked their pains more by intensity than by neuropathic character, and at almost a third of interviews a more neuropathic pain was present but not ranked first.

3.6. Association with pain interference

To examine whether neuropathic characteristics relate to how pain affects daily life, we modeled PC1 of the worst-ranked pain at follow-up against interference with sleep, mood, and day-to-day activities. The interference models included 136 to 138 worst-ranked pain assessments from 73 to 74 participants, and the participant random intercept accounted for 37% to 41% of the variance in PC1. Higher PC1 was associated with greater sleep interference (averaged slope 0.082 standard deviations of PC1 per interference point, 95% CI 0.033 to 0.131, F = 11.38, p < 0.001) and mood interference (0.085, 0.029 to 0.141, F = 9.40, p = 0.003), whereas the association with interference with day-to-day activities did not reach significance (0.054, -0.009 to 0.117, F = 2.94, p = 0.089) (Table 3 and Fig. 4A, with the individual assessments in Fig. S4). Both significant associations remained after Benjamini-Hochberg adjustment (p = 0.003 and 0.004) and when any single participant was omitted (all p < 0.01), and the model diagnostics showed no relevant departures from normality or constant variance (Fig. S5). Neither any pain treatment nor AIS grade was associated with PC1 in these models (p = 0.72 to 0.99 and 0.47 to 0.69). The interference-by-time point interactions were not significant for sleep and mood (p = 0.23 and 0.075) and were nominally significant for day-to-day activities (p = 0.042). Descriptively, slopes were close to zero or negative at 3 months and positive at 6 and 12 months for all three domains (Fig. 4A).

In the model without the interaction, the sleep and mood associations were unchanged (0.080, p = 0.001, and 0.078, p = 0.006) and the day-to-day activities association remained short of significance (0.052, p = 0.11). Decomposition of interference showed that the associations were carried by differences between participants (sleep 0.107, mood 0.119 and day-to-day activities 0.102, with p = 0.002, p < 0.001 and p = 0.016, Fig. 4B), whereas changes in a participant's interference across interviews were not associated with changes in PC1 (sleep 0.047, mood -0.004 and day-to-day activities -0.018, with p = 0.20, 0.93 and 0.71). Participants whose pain had more neuropathic characteristics therefore reported more sleep and mood interference, whereas changes in interference within a participant were not accompanied by changes in PC1.

3.8. Pain location and opioid use

Because at-level and below-level pain may arise from different mechanisms and opioid use may modify pain reports, we explored PC1 by pain location and opioid use. Pain location relative to the NLI could be classified for pain area 1 at 65 of 70 follow-up interviews at 6 and 12 months (49 of the 65 were the worst-ranked pain). Thirteen pain areas straddled the lower edge of the at-level window and were classified as at-level. At 6 months, 5 pain areas were above, 19 at, and 15 below level, and at 12 months 3 were above, 12 at, and 11 below level. At 12 months, below-level pain areas tended to have higher PC1 than at-level areas (median 0.56 and 0.08), whereas at 6 months the two were similar (0.23 and 0.19). Above-level areas were too few for comparison, and the small numbers precluded formal testing (Fig. S6A). Among participants receiving any pain treatment, confirmed absence of opioid use was recorded for only 1, 1, and 2 participants at 3, 6, and 12 months, respectively, opioid use for 16, 12, and 11 participants, and unknown medication exposure for 40, 37, and 31 participants. Pooled across time points, the median PC1 was -0.50 in the 4 participants without opioid use and 0.18 in the 29 with opioid use (Fig. S6B). These exploratory data suggest that below-level pain areas may carry more neuropathic characteristics at 12 months, whereas the medication records were too incomplete to support conclusions about opioid use.

4. Discussion

In this prospective SCI cohort, half of all pain areas met the DN4i threshold within three days of injury, and this proportion changed little over the first year. This stability was only apparent, however, as individual pains followed persistent, resolved, delayed-onset, and fluctuating courses, and in 20 of the 22 participants with more than one pain area, the pains of the same person followed different courses. The DN4 interview items formed a single dimension, and the threshold-free Pain Composite Score derived from them was associated with sleep and mood interference whereas the binary classification was not. At the same time, pain intensity was the strongest correlate of interference and largely determined which pain participants ranked as worst, so that the most neuropathic pain was often not the one ranked first. These findings call into question two widely held views of neuropathic pain after SCI and have implications for how neuropathic pain is defined in prognostic and biomarker studies.

Our own work on this cohort began as an effort to identify blood biomarkers that predict the development of neuropathic pain, a design that appeared straightforward under the assumption that neuropathic pain persists once it has developed. Participants could then be labeled as developing or not developing neuropathic pain, and early molecular profiles compared between the two groups. Our data show why such a label is difficult to assign after SCI. At more than half of the interviews at which participants reported several pains, one pain met the DN4i threshold while another did not, so that the participant's classification depended on which pain was considered. Followed over time, a third of participants changed classification at least once, and assessments close to the threshold could cross it between interviews without a meaningful change in the pain itself. A model can predict only as well as its outcome is defined, and an outcome that changes with the pain chosen and the interview selected limits what any biomarker can achieve.

4.1. Early onset and variable course of neuropathic characteristics

The view that neuropathic pain develops over the months after SCI has been challenged by prospective work showing that it can be detected in the worst pain within days [34], is present in about 30% of patients on admission to rehabilitation [10], and is preceded by altered pain processing in the first weeks [12, 19]. Outside SCI, acute neuropathic pain is recognized after surgery and trauma, where it is often overlooked although it may progress to persistent pain, and screening questionnaires have not been validated in this early phase [18]. By assessing every pain area rather than the worst pain alone, we found that half of all pain areas and three quarters of participants met the DN4i threshold within three days, even with the interview items alone. Neuropathic characteristics may therefore be more common early after injury than worst-pain assessments suggest, and the later onset reported in earlier studies may partly reflect when patients were assessed rather than when their pain changed.

The stable prevalence among pain areas over the first year and the predominance of persistent courses appear to support the second view, that neuropathic pain persists once established, as in pooled cohort data [44]. Following individual pains, however, revealed considerable turnover beneath this stability, much as reported for pain intensity [7]. Classifying the patient rather than the pain amplified the apparent stability, as more than half of the participants assessed at several time points had at least one DN4i-NP pain area at every time point, whereas only about a third of individual pains remained above the threshold throughout. One third of pain areas resolved or fluctuated. Together with the differing courses of pains in the same person, these findings suggest that the assumption of persistence, on which our earlier analysis of this cohort also relied when it combined the 6-and 12-month assessments [17], deserves to be revisited for individual pains.

The typical fluctuating course, above the threshold in hospital, below it at 3 months, and above it again thereafter, may reflect transient early symptoms, masking by nociceptive pain [34], or the limited validity of self-report in the first days after injury. Because at-level and below-level pain differ in their time course [15, 37, 38], some of these courses may also correspond to distinct mechanisms. The higher composite scores of below-level areas at 12 months in our data also deserve examination in larger cohorts.

Part of this turnover, and perhaps part of the views it challenges, may arise from the threshold itself. More than a quarter of all assessments lay within one item of the cutoff, where a change in a single item can register a little-changed pain as resolved or new. When the participant rather than the pain is classified, a person whose at-level pain resolves while a new below-level pain develops would be counted as unchanged, although both pains have changed. A graded description of each pain over time may give a more faithful account of its course.

4.2. A threshold-free score of neuropathic characteristics

The psychometric evaluation of the DN4 has focused almost exclusively on its accuracy at a threshold [4, 6, 30, 31], and prognostic studies, including our earlier analysis of this cohort, have used it in the same way [17]. The only exploratory factor analysis we identified, performed with the full ten-item version in a mixed chronic pain population, found four factors and low internal consistency [39]. In our SCI cohort, by contrast, the interview items formed a single component and were internally consistent, possibly because the examination items, two of which formed a separate factor in that study, were not included. This unidimensionality justifies summing the items and supports, at least in this cohort, the use of the DN4i total as a graded measure.

The empirically derived Pain Composite Score was nearly identical to the unweighted DN4i total (ρ = 0.98), because all items loaded positively and pain intensity contributed little. Rather than making the composite redundant, this convergence indicates that a simple item count captures the graded information in the items, and the unweighted total offers fixed weights for prediction and biomarker models applied to new cohorts. The high loading of numbness nevertheless calls for caution, because this item may reflect sensory loss rather than neuropathic pain. Item response modeling, as has been applied to painDE-TECT [33], would establish whether the total also behaves as an interval-level measure.

Graded scoring also related more closely to daily life, as continuously scored neuropathic characteristics were associated with sleep and mood interference, whereas the binary classification of the same items was not. In SCI, where neuropathic, nociceptive, and possibly nociplastic mechanisms frequently coexist [23], a threshold that treats a pain with three neuropathic features in the same way as a pain with seven appears to discard clinically meaningful information.

4.3. Pain intensity and interference beyond neuropathic classification

Pain intensity was associated with interference two to four times as strongly as the neuropathic character of the pain scored with PC1, although the neuropathic association with sleep interference persisted, at roughly a third of the strength of intensity, once intensity was accounted for. Participants also ranked their pains largely by intensity, as the worst-ranked pain was the most intense pain at three quarters of interviews but the most neuropathic pain at only 59%. These findings agree with a large SCI Model Systems survey, in which neuropathic pain was more interfering across participants but, within individuals who had both pain types, differed only in sleep interference [14].

In our data, too, the associations reflected differences between participants rather than changes within them, although few participants contributed enough interviews to assess responsiveness to change. The associations were also weakest at 3 months, when interference may reflect the injury and early recovery more than the character of pain. Although intensity and interference are both self-reported and may share reporting tendencies, the results suggest that how much pain hurts matters most for daily life, that its neuropathic character makes a smaller but independent contribution to sleep, and that treatment directed at the most neuropathic pain may miss the pain that limits the patient most. The association between the number of pain areas and mood interference further suggests that the overall burden of pain, rather than any single pain, shapes how patients feel.

4.4. Implications for prognostic and biomarker studies

The instability of the binary label led us to a different target. Rather than asking whether a patient has neuropathic pain, a prognostic or biomarker study may ask to what extent a pain bears neuropathic features, how intense it is, and how much it limits daily life, and treat these as graded rather than binary quantities. The Pain Composite Score was derived with this aim, to combine neuropathic characteristics and intensity into a threshold-free measure that reflects the pain patients care about, and, as discussed above, it related to daily life where the binary classification did not. Graded outcomes also retain the statistical power that dichotomization discards [1], which matters in cohorts of the size typical of acute SCI research. The need for such patient-centered targets is underlined by the earlier find-ing that quality of life in this cohort was associated with depression and social roles rather than with injury severity [21], which shows that the classifications used to stratify patients can capture poorly what matters most to them.

Biomarker discovery in SCI pain has so far targeted mainly the presence or mechanistic phenotype of neuropathic pain [45]. Early blood transcriptomic and routine laboratory profiles already carry information on injury severity and outcome in TRACK-SCI participants [26, 32], and a circulating microRNA has been associated with neuropathic pain severity modeled as a graded outcome [24], which suggests that threshold-free pain phenotypes are a feasible target for such studies. The Pain Composite Score is an initial step toward a measure of life-limiting pain, by which we mean pain that interferes with daily activities, mood, or sleep. In its current form, however, it is dominated by neuropathic characteristics and will require refinement, for example through greater weighting of intensity and the inclusion of interference, and validation in independent cohorts.

4.5. Limitations

This study has several limitations. Because most assessments were performed by telephone, the three DN4 examination items, hypoesthesia to touch and to pinprick and pain evoked by brushing, could not be obtained, and the DN4i has not been validated in SCI against a clinical reference standard such as the grading system of the Neuropathic Pain Special Interest Group [16], a role the patient-reported ISCIP pain type could not fill. The analysis was restricted to a single site, a third of the participants who remained in the study did not complete a DN4 questionnaire, and many contributed assessments in only one phase, so that the cohort may not represent all patients with SCI. The modest number of pain areas followed from the acute phase means that the less frequent trajectories should be interpreted with caution, and matching pain areas across interviews by their reported location may occasionally have linked different pains. In addition, pain areas were assessed at different numbers of time points, and a pain assessed more often had more chances to change and therefore to be classified as fluctuating. For this reason, we also reported how often a pain changed status from one time point to the next, a measure that does not depend on the number of assessments. Because the DN4 characterizes a reported pain, the participant-level prevalence refers to participants with pain at each time point rather than to the whole cohort. Interference was rated for pain overall rather than for each pain area, which may have weakened associations with the characteristics of a single pain, a limitation that version 3.0 of the International SCI Pain Basic Data Set now addresses [47]. Finally, medication use was recorded at every follow-up interview, but participants differed in how precisely they could report it, and opioid exposure remained uncertain at many interviews. Although opioid administration early after SCI may influence later pain in experimental models [48], too few treated participants had confirmed absence of opioid use to examine such effects.

4.6. Conclusions

In this cohort, neuropathic characteristics of pain after SCI were present from the first days after injury, did not invariably persist, and often differed between the pains of the same person, so that whether a patient was classified as having neuropathic pain depended on which pain and which interview were considered. A threshold-free composite of neuropathic characteristics and intensity was associated with sleep and mood interference where the binary classification was not, although pain intensity remained the strongest correlate of interference. Graded, pain-level measures that combine the character, intensity, and course of each pain may therefore offer a more stable and more informative target for prognostic and biomarker studies of lifelimiting pain than a binary classification of the patient.
