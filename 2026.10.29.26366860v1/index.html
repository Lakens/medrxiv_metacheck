Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Abstract

Drinking water biofilms host diverse communities spanning all realms of life. However, despite the known interactions between opportunistic pathogens and microbial eukaryotes such as amoebae, most studies focus only on the prokaryotic populations in these systems.

As a result, the degree to which microbial eukaryotes shape biofilm communities and the persistence of opportunistic waterborne pathogens, such as Legionella spp. is still largely unknown. Here we leveraged a set of 128 non-disinfected drinking water shower hose biofilms collected across Switzerland to characterize both communities and quantify eukaryotes' role in shaping prokaryotic communities and Legionella persistence. We found both communities to present a conserved set of core taxa dominated by oligotrophic bacterial families and amoebae. The prevalence of taxa was mainly driven by the phylogenetic diversity among their sequence variants rather than their number alone. We detected prokaryotic and eukaryotic communities to show similar patterns in their richness and composition, influencing each other more than abiotic building-level factors.

Legionellaceae bacteria were detected in 82% of samples presenting a diversity extending known reference species and no clear dominant taxa. Legionellaceae richness did not correlate with the richness of known amoebal hosts, but rather other amoebae, indicating that eukaryotic taxa relevant for Legionella persistence extend beyond the classically described hosts. These findings highlight the importance of integrating the eukaryotic communities to understand the microbial ecology in drinking water biofilms and recognize the conditions which allow opportunistic waterborne pathogens such as Legionella to persist.

Introduction

Biofilms are structured communities of microorganisms embedded in a self-produced matrix of extracellular polymeric substances which favour strong cell-to-cell interactions. This environment also offers protection from environmental stresses, such as desiccation and disinfection, and retains nutrients from the water phase (Flemming & Wingender, 2010). In drinking water systems, biofilms can grow inside pipelines and outlets, in which prokaryotic and eukaryotic communities can establish (https://paperpile.com/c/2HcMWJ/uoAo6. Liu et al., 2016).

Cooperation and competition shape the drinking water biofilm communities. Among the interactions between prokaryotes and eukaryotes, grazing by protists is a strong selective pressure on the bacterial community: many are consumed and digested, while others, called amoebae-resistant bacteria (ARB), survive and replicate inside their host, finding in this interaction an additional protection (Fan et al., 2024). In some instances this latter interaction also protects the eukaryotes, for example in amoebae, where some symbionts limit the invasion from other parasites (https://paperpile.com/c/2HcMWJ/95xL. König et al., 2019). Across the ARB, waterborne pathogens such as Legionella, Nontuberculous Mycobacteria (NTM) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa can be found (Greub & Raoult, 2004). The amoebal host can protect them from biocides and other stresses, favouring their persistence and dispersion in drinking water distribution systems (DWDSs). This increases the risk exposure for the user and the healthcare-associated infections related to water (Cateau et al., 2014).

Most of the drinking water biofilm studies focus solely on prokaryotic (i.e.https://paperpile.com/c/2HcMWJ/35hK1 bacterial and archeal) diversity under different conditions such as temperature, disinfection treatment and seasonality (Healy et al., 2024; Mi et al., 2015; Moreno et al., 2024) , while eukaryotic communities remain very poorly characterized, despite their role in the ecology of opportunistic pathogens. Eukaryotic diversity in DWDSs is shaped by both management and climatic factors and highly related to prokaryotic diversity, suggesting a close link between the two kingdoms (Gabrielli et al., 2023). The composition of eukaryotic communities is substantially different between water and biofilms, even though presenting in both cases a large fraction of unclassified sequences. Biofilms from different locations were different but functionally similar, representing also a hotspot for pathogenic amoebae (Li et al., 2021).

Biofilms are, in particular, hotspots of diversity for eukaryotic species known to host ARBs (Inkinen et al., 2019). It is then critical to improve our understanding of the role of eukaryotes in DWDSs to improve current microbiological management strategies and protect public health (Gabrielli et al., 2023).

Despite different studies on drinking water microbiota, prokaryotic and eukaryotic communities together are rarely characterized in the same samples, and the taxa considered as relevant hosts for waterborne pathogens are largely limited to a small number of amoebae described in literature (Boamah et al., 2017). Discussion

In this study, we investigated the prokaryotic and eukaryotic communities of non-disinfected drinking water shower hose biofilms collected across Switzerland. We studied their . composition (Fig. 1 and Fig. 2), their cross-kingdom association (Fig. 5) and the presence of potential opportunistic waterborne pathogens, such as Legionella (Fig. 1 and Fig. S2).

Despite originating from geographically separated locations, the shower hose biofilms shared a group of prokaryotic families detected across all samples, suggesting consistency in community membership at the family level in these environments. Several of these families have also been reported in biofilms from other non-disinfected drinking water systems, including distribution pipes (https://paperpile.com/c/2HcMWJ/PBGA. Liu et al., 2020) and shower hoses (Cavallaro et al., 2023; Proctor et al., 2018). Sphingomonadaceae, for example, has previously been identified among the dominant families in non-disinfected drinking water communities (https://paperpile.com/c/2HcMWJ/s3hF. Liu et al., 2014). These observations suggest similarities across non-disinfected systems, although they do not necessarily establish the presence of an overall common core community. Differences were also apparent, including the non-detection of members of the phylum Patescibacteria in our samples, which have frequently been reported in non-disinfected drinking water systems (Dai et al., 2020).

The disinfection regime is one of the main factors shaping drinking water microbiome composition, supporting the observations of Foucault and colleagues (Foucault et al., 2026).

Comparison of our data with the global drinking water genome cataloguehttps://paperpile.com/c/2HcMWJ/8byFo which is largely derived from disinfected systems (Sudarshan et al., 2024) , revealed notable differences in the representation of several genera. Sphingomonas, Sphingopyxis, Bradyrhizobium, OLB17, Brevundimonas, Phenylobacterium, Caulobacter, Afipia, and Ga0077523 were abundant in our shower hose biofilms but were either absent or present at only low abundance in the reference catalogue. Converselyhttps://paperpile.com/c/2HcMWJ/8byFo UBA4765, proposed as Raskinella (Sudarshan et al., 2024) , was not detected in our shower hose biofilms. However, these differences cannot be attributed directly to disinfection practices, as the compared datasets also differ in sample type and analytical approach. In particularhttps://paperpile.com/c/2HcMWJ/s3hF the predominance of bulk water samples in the reference catalogue (Sudarshan et al., 2024) may contribute to the observed differences, given the established compositional differences between planktonic and biofilm-associated communities (https://paperpile.com/c/2HcMWJ/s3hF. Liu et al., 2014) , as well as primer biases.

The eukaryotic core community was dominated by amoebae (Fig. 2)https://paperpile.com/c/2HcMWJ/Hpcb+HRkq+hzYZ including taxa described as hosts for waterborne pathogens, such as Vermamoebidae and Vannellidae (Boamah et al., 2017; Crull et al., 2025; Delafont et al., 2018) , and taxa proposed as potential hosts for Legionella spp., such as LKM74-lineage and Vahlkampfiidae (Margot et al., 2024). LKM74-lineage was also previously reported as highly abundant in drinking water biofilms (Cavallaro et al., 2023; Margot et al., 2024) and with a high transcriptional activity (Inkinen et al., 2019). The eukaryotic dataset had a relatively low sequencing depth and under-developed reference databases that limited taxonomic assignment. An example thereof is the clear misclassification of Cyanidiales_X, which phylogenetic analysis revealed.

to belong to the amoeboid genus of Angulamoeba. This limited taxonomic resolution is a recognized issue: in a similar dataset, 85% of the eukaryotic community remained unclassified at genus level, against 64% of the bacterial one (Cavallaro et al., 2023). These evidences highlight the need for a deeper eukaryotic sequencing and improved taxonomic databases to fully characterize the community in drinking water biofilms and their ecology, as pointed out also by other authors (Gabrielli et al., 2023; Inkinen et al., 2019).

Microdiversity analysis revealed that prokaryotic OTUs with greater internal phylogenetic diversity were detected across a larger proportion of samples (Fig. 1b), with total phylogenetic distance emerging as a stronger predictor of environmental prevalence than the effective number of ASVs. This suggests that phylogenetic differentiation among variants within an OTU, rather than variant richness alone, may be associated with their occurrence across different building water systems. Such a pattern is consistent with previous work linking microbial microdiversity to ecological differentiation and persistence across different buildingshttps://paperpile.com/c/2HcMWJ/KtOP such as institutional and residential buildings (He et al., 2025) , and in different environmental conditions and ecosystems (García-García et al., 2019; Orta-Ponce et al., 2025). Notably, OTUs belonging to the core prokaryotic families also exhibited high within-OTU phylogenetic diversity, suggesting that microdiversity may contribute to their widespread occurrence across drinking water biofilms. These results show that shower hose biofilms from non-disinfected systems share a conserved core of prokaryotic and eukaryotic taxa and that phylogenetic diversity inside an OTU appears to contribute to the persistence of prokaryote taxa across non-disinfected drinking water biofilms.

The statistical associations between prokaryotic and eukaryotic communities (Fig. 3 and Fig. 4) showed a clear trend towards the concept of so-called diversity-begets-diversity. This concept was proposed by Calcagno and coworkers (Calcagno et al.https://paperpile.com/c/2HcMWJ/Dc8H 2017) , demonstrated across multiple ecological niches (Hamilton et al., 2020; Madi et al., 2020) and previously observed at global scale in drinking water metagenomes (Gabrielli et al., 2023). Going in-depth of the cross-kingdom network (Fig. 5)https://paperpile.com/c/2HcMWJ/Hpcb the eukaryotic families showing the strongest associations included both well-known and poorly characterized taxa: free-living amoebae such as Vermamoebidae and Echinamoebidaehttps://paperpile.com/c/2HcMWJ/k8uE already described as hosts for waterborne pathogens (Boamah et al., 2017) , Rotosphaeridae and Nucleariida, which are known to harbour endosymbiotic proteobacteria (Dyková et al., 2003) , Kinetoplastea_XX, which includes flagellates that can be important human pathogens such as Trypanosoma and Leishmania (Kostygov et al., 2021) and Limnofilidae, bacterivorous cercozoan protists (Bass et al., 2009). On the prokaryotic sidehttps://paperpile.com/c/2HcMWJ/8hwU taxa with different ecological strategies were detected, for example Weeksellaceae, including the genus Chryseobacterium, in which some species are able to survive amoebal digestion (Greub et al., 2004) , Legionellaceae, which replicate within amoebal hosts (Boamah et al., 2017; Greub et al., 2004) and Micavibrionaceae, . predatory epibiotic bacteria (Wang et al., 2011). On a fundamental level, this raises important questions on how drinking water microbes interact across trophic levels. Three relatively well-known interactions would influence cross-kingdom diversity. Firsthttps://paperpile.com/c/2HcMWJ/7Rp5+iAQ9+rSAt many protist species graze directly on prokaryotes as their primary food source, and several studies showed species-specific grazing habits (Bornier et al., 2021; NandaKafle et al., 2023; Weger et al., 2024) , suggesting that both the act of selective grazing and defensive traits against grazing may contribute to diversity in co-existing populations. Secondhttps://paperpile.com/c/2HcMWJ/hl9X+7z4f+MqPP a variety of symbiotic bacteria co-evolve with specific eukaryotes (Horn & Wagner, 2004; Leitsch et al., 2010; Steele et al., 2023) , adding to the prokaryotic diversity in a variety of niches occupied by the host. Third, multiple bacterial species such as Legionella spp. requires an eukaryotic host for replication, where host specific infection (Declerck et al., 2007; Shaheen & Ashbolt, 2021) as well as defensive mechanisms of some hosts, would likely contribute to environmental diversity of both kingdoms. It is recognised that many of the possible relationships remain unresolved, and a mere statistical association is not sufficient to demonstrate a causative interaction.

Notwithstanding, these results indicate that the associations between prokaryotic and eukaryotic communities reflect different types of ecological relationships, from resistance to grazing to shared bacterial prey, rather than host-pathogen interactions only. This broader context is relevant for interpreting the relationships of Legionella, which have so far been described mainly in terms of a small number of amoebal hosts.

Legionella is a bacterial genus of considerable public health relevance (https://paperpile.com/c/2HcMWJ/iSM6 of which several sequence variants were detected in nearly all of our biofilm samples, often co-occurring with each other and in association with various eukaryotes (Fig S2, Fig. 7 and Fig. S9). Three aspects of Legionella diversity were particularly noticeable. First, L. waltersii was by far the most prevalent identified species (present in 59% of the samples). This species was first isolated from cooling tower water (Benson et al.https://paperpile.com/c/2HcMWJ/Owr9 1996) , and is only rarely associated with clinical outcomes in humans (https://paperpile.com/c/2HcMWJ/yKOj. König et al., 2005). Second, the species with by far the highest clinical relevance, L. pneumophila, was only detected in 5% of samples, suggesting that shower hoses may not necessarily be a primary niche for Legionella in building plumbing systems. This is seemingly corroborated by a recent source attribution study in Switzerland, which linked only a small fraction of clinical cases to samples collected from patients' showers (Bigler et al., 2026). While some of the other species such as L. birmighamensishttps://paperpile.com/c/2HcMWJ/XrHU+QIRN+qTO6 L. erythra and L. rubrilucens have rarely reported clinical associations (Matsui et al., 2010; Papadakis et al., 2018; Wilkinson et al., 1987) , these are generally regarded as considerably less relevant for public health than L. pneumophila (ECDC, 2026). Thirdly, our data showed co-occurence of different Legionella species in the same biofilm samples, following similar previous observations in drinking water biofilm samples (Cavallaro et al., 2023; Foucault et al., 2026; Margot et al., 2024). The majority of.

the Legionella ASVs remain, in fact, unassigned at species level, and phylogenetic analysis places most ASVs outside the clades of known cultivated species used as references. This reflects both the high intra-genus diversity of the 16S rRNA gene within Legionella (Hassler et al., 2022) and the incomplete coverage of reference databases for environmental Legionella. As an example, a recent study demonstrated that the inclusion of metagenome-assembled genomes nearly doubles the number of recognized Legionellaceae species with many novel species forming clades distant from currently isolated taxa (Gabrielli et al., 2024). Legionella require hosts for replication (Declerck et al., 2007; Shaheen & Ashbolt, 2021). Yet, no clear correlation was found with amoebae described in the literature as Legionella hosts, or specifically with the prevalent Vermamoeba, despite this being the one of the relationships most studied in drinking water (Delafont et al., 2018).

Positive associations were found with other amoebae such as LKM74-lineage, Parafumarolamoeba and Echinamoeba. LKM74-lineagehttps://paperpile.com/c/2HcMWJ/H6d0 a poorly characterized amoebozoan family, has been proposed as a potential Legionella host in drinking water biofilms (Margot et al., 2024) , and the high prevalence in the dataset suggests that its role, in particular the relation with waterborne pathogens, deserves further investigations. Our results indicate that a broader range of eukaryotic taxa may have a role in Legionella persistence in non-disinfected shower hose biofilms than the ones classically described.

However, considerable further research is needed to link the environmental observations from studies like our own with the potential ecological interactions to untangle observed associations. As discussed abovehttps://paperpile.com/c/2HcMWJ/EvgA multiple cross-kingdom interactions can occur between Legionella species and protists (Hammes et al., 2025) , and these interactions will depend on both the Legionella species present (Buse & Ashbolt, 2011) and the protist species present (Shaheen & Ashbolt, 2021). Potential outcomes can include co-existence without uptake (Shaheen & Ashbolthttps://paperpile.com/c/2HcMWJ/sI2g 2021) https://paperpile.com/c/2HcMWJ/iAQ9 uptake without replication (Bornier et al., 2021) , replication where the protist host survives (Horn & Wagner, 2004) and replication where the protist hosts are destroyed (Shaheen & Ashbolt, 2021).

In conclusion, our work shows that prokaryotic and eukaryotic communities of non-disinfected shower hose biofilms are structured around a conserved core of ASVs and are associated with each other, even if the exact ecological associations remain unresolved.

Eukaryotic taxa relevant for Legionella are likely to extend beyond those described in the literature as known hosts. Hence, characterizing the eukaryotic fraction of drinking water biofilms is therefore essential to understand the ecological context in which opportunistic pathogens persist. Ultimately, a deeper understanding of Legionella diversity and its relationship with protists in drinking water systems could support the development of better monitoring and management strategies in building plumbing systems.

Methods

Methods 128 samples and controls were obtained from participants at the SwissLegio project (Fischerhttps://paperpile.com/c/2HcMWJ/CpB1f Bigler, et al., 2023) , previous approval.

Sampling and biofilm collection

Shower hoses were collected and kept in cooled boxes during the transport, to be refrigerated at 4 °C until processing. Biofilms were collected by cutting the shower hoses and scraping the material with the backside of a sterile 1 mL pipette tip. 2 mL of biofilm from each sample were frozen at -80 °C until processing.

DNA extraction and quantification

The DNA was extracted using the FastDNA SPIN kit (MP Biomedicals), following an adapted protocol. 0.8 mL of scraped biofilms were incubated for 1 h at 37°C and 300 rpm with 294 µL of 1 × Tris-EDTA pH 8.0 (100 mM Tris Base, 10 mM EDTA, G-Bioscience) and 6 µL of lysozyme (50 mg/mL, Thermo Scientific). After incubation, 300 µL of CLS-TC buffer (FastDNA SPIN kit, MP Biomedicals) and 30 µL of proteinase K (20 mg/mL, Thermo Fisher Scientific) were added to the samples. Samples were then incubated for 30 min at 56°C, 300 rpm. Lysing matrix and 630 µL chloroform/Isoamyl alcohol (ratio 24:1, Brunschwig) were added to the incubated samples. After 5 min of vortexing, samples were centrifuged for 10 min at 4 °C and 14,000 × g and 750 µL of the Binding Matrix (FastDNA SPIN kit, MP Biomedicals) added to the upper aqueous phase. The next steps followed the manufacturer's protocol.

Negative and positive controls were processed during each extraction batch. Negative controls were clean filters, while positive were filters previously used for L. pneumophila culture filtration. Mock communities for both prokaryotes and eukaryotes were added using ZymoBIOMICS Microbial Community DNA Standard II Log Distribution (Zymo Research), ABRF-MGRG 10 Strain Even Mix Genomic Material (ATCC) and Mycobiome Genomic DNA Mix (ATCC).

Amplicon sequencing and processing

Prokaryotic and eukaryotic libraries were sequenced at the Next Generation Sequencing (NGS) Platform of the University of Bern on a PacBio Revio instrument. Reads processing was performed on the Euler high-performance computing cluster at ETH Zürich, Switzerland.

Prokaryotic community profiling.

Maximum likelihood phylogenetic trees were constructed for both datasets. For the prokaryotic rRNA dataset, ASV sequences were aligned using MAFFT v.7.525 with automatic algorithm selection (--auto) (Katoh et al., 2002) and trimmed using trimAl (-automated1) (Capella-Gutiérrez et al., 2009). The phylogenetic tree was built using IQ-TREE2 v2.2.2.6 with ModelFinder Plus (-m MFP, -mset GTR), 1000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates (-B 1000) and branch length optimization (-bnni) (Minh et al., 2020). For the eukaryotic rRNA dataset, the same alignment and trimming procedure was applied and the phylogenetic tree was constructed using FastTree v2.1.11 with -nt -gtr parameters (Price et al., 2009). ITOL v.7.5.1 (Letunic & Bork, 2024) was used to visualize both trees. Further phylogenetic analyses were performed for specific clades of interest. Eukaryotic ASVs classified as Cyanidiales_XX in the eukaryotic dataset were selected to understand the presence of this taxa. Sequences were extracted and compared using BLASTn (Zhang et al., 2000) against NCBI (accessed on: March 2026).

Since the BLAST results highlighted a high similarity of the Cyanidiales_XX ASVs with Angulamoeba sequences, taxa belonging to the family Cyanidiales (i.e., genera Cyanidium, Cyanidioschyzon, Cyanidiococcus) and from the genus Angulamoeba were selected for further phylogenetic analyses. Galdieria maxima, the previous classification of Cyanidiococcus yangmingshanensis, was also included in the selected taxa, while Saccharomyces cerevisiae was included as an outgroup. 18S rRNA sequences of the selected taxa were either downloaded from NCBI or, where no sequences were available, barrnap was used to predict the 18S rRNA on the whole genome sequence and the best predicted sequence was retained. Further phylogenetic analyses were also conducted on ASVs belonging to the genus Legionella to verify species-level taxonomic assignment obtained through GTDB R220 database. Full-length 16S rRNA sequences of known Legionella species (n=65) were retrieved from NCBI and used as reference sequences for phylogenetic comparison with the dataset ASVs. In addition, the 16S rRNA sequence of Coxiella burnetii was selected as an outgroup, being the closest taxa outside

Legionellaceae family (Legionellales order). Sequence alignment and tree construction for Cyanidiales_XX and Legionellaceae ASVs were carried out as performed for the eukaryotic ASVs.

Core communities assessment

ASVs belonging to the core eukaryotic and prokaryotic communities were calculated as proposed previously (Shade & Stopnisek, 2019). Briefly, ASVs-level taxa were ranked by a weighted occupancy-abundance index, and taxa were added iteratively to calculate the mean of Bray-Curtis similarity across samples. To identify the membership in the core community, a cut-off was defined using the last 2% increase method, identifying the point where additional taxa contribute marginally to the community dissimilarity. Below the point, ASVs were classified as belonging to the satellite community.

Diversity analyses

Alpha diversity was estimated using observed richness and Shannon diversity indices, using the estimate_richness function from phyloseq. Beta diversity was estimated using Bray-Curtis and Jaccard dissimilarities calculated using the distance function from phyloseq.

The presence of effects due to distinct water sources on community composition was tested similarly to what described by Badra et colleagues (Badra et al., 2025).

Network analysis

Network analyses combining both prokaryotic and eukaryotic datasets were performed using SPIEC-EASI v.2.0.0 (Kurtz et al., 2015) using the Meinshausen-Bühlmann (MB) neighbourhood selection method and the following parameters: lambda.min.ratio = 5e-2, nlambda = 40, selection criterion = bstars, 100 repetitions and random seed = 10010.

Network visualization was performed using the visNetwork R package. ASVs were filtered based on total reads count in the ASV table. Different thresholds were applied to each dataset, 5 reads for the prokaryotic dataset and more than 3 reads for the eukaryotic, to balance the lower sequencing depth of the eukaryotes dataset.

Microdiversity and OTU-level phylogenetic diversity

.

Microdiversity was performed on both datasets at OTU level (98.7% similarity), similarly to what described before (García-García et al., 2019). Each OTU was rarefied at 5000 reads using the rrarefy function from vegan R package retaining 631 prokaryotic OTUs and 34 eukaryotic ones. Microdiversity was then calculated as the exponential of the Shannon diversity of the ASVs within each OTU, obtaining the effective number of ASVs, while the total cophenetic distance was computed as the sum of pairwise phylogenetic distances between all the ASVs. OTU prevalence was defined as the proportion of samples in which the OTU was detected after rarefaction. Microdiversity and total cophenetic distance were tested as predictors of OTU persistence using a Random Forest regression model (Breiman, 2001) with randomForest v.4.7.1.2 (ntree = 1000). Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to assess differences of the prevalence of OTUs with microdiversity above and below the threshold of 2 effective ASVs.

Eukaryotic community profiling

Long-read amplicon HiFi sequencing of the 18S-ITS-28S region was performed using the TAReuk454FWD1 (CCAGCASCYGCGGTAATTCC) and 21R (GACGAGGCATTTGGCTACCTT) primers (Latz et al., 2022).

Raw reads were processed using the NextITS pipeline v.0.5.0 (Mikryukov et al.https://paperpile.com/c/2HcMWJ/zqSV 2026) , omitting the ITS extraction step (--its_region none). Denoising and clustering were performed using the UNOISE3 algorithm (--unoise true). The resulting ASVs were further curated by identifying 18S rRNA sequences using barrnap v.1.10.5 (Seemann, n.d.) and discarding sequences with none or more than one predicted rRNA. ASVs taxonomy was assigned based on the predicted 18S rRNA sequences using the Naive Bayes classifier algorithm implemented in DADA2 (Callahan et al., 2016) relying on the PR2 database v.5.1.1 (Guillou et al., 2013). Sequences not corresponding to Eukarya have been removed. A final decontamination was performed using the decontam v.1.32.0 (prevalence method) based on the sequenced negative controls (Davis et al., 2018). The Clusterize function from DECIPHER was used to group the ASVs in OTUs with 98.7% of similarity. The eukaryotic dataset was, then, rarefied using the function rarefy_even_depth at 3000 reads.
