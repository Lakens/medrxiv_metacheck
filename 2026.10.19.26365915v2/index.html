To fix Exact P-Values

What we found

We found 1 imprecise p value out of 32 detected p values.

Show details

Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

P < 0.05 Data were RMS-normalized in SCiLS Lab and analyzed by PCA and OPLS-DA (200 permutations; differential features: VIP > 1 and P < 0.05), with regional comparisons across the cortex, outer stripe of the outer medulla (OSOM), inner stripe of the outer medulla (ISOM), and inner medulla (IM).

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 12 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

How Replication Check works

This module checks references and warns for citations of original studies for which replication or reproduction studies exist in the FLoRA database.

The Replication Check module compares the reference list against studies in the FLoRA (FORRT Library of Replication Attempts) database based on the DOI. If a study in the database is found, a reminder is provided that a replication or reproduction of the original study exists, and should be cited. Abstract

Introduction: Chronic kidney disease (CKD) generates metabolically heterogeneous stress across circulating, microbial, and renal compartments. Trace element supplementation may engage redox and metabolic pathways and selectively modulate responsive metabolic programs.

Objectives: To define the metabolic branch selectivity, renal regional distribution, and tissue correlates of responses to a trace element cocktail (TEX) through an integrated clinical-to-mouse multi-omics framework, and to organize these findings into an experimentally testable model.

Methods: In a double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, 80 adults receiving maintenance hemodialysis received standard care plus TEX (20 mL/day; n = 40) or standard care plus matched placebo (n = 40). Longitudinal clinical outcomes were analyzed using participant random-intercept models. Endpoint plasma metabolomics and fecal metagenomics included healthy controls (n = 10) and voluntary trial subsets (placebo, n = 12; TEX, n = 20). Mice with 5/6 nephrectomy underwent targeted tryptophan metabolite profiling, renal spatial metabolomics, histopathology, and molecular analyses.

Results: Longitudinal monitoring showed no overt treatment-related laboratory safety signal. Plasma metabolomics identified treatment-associated differences (false discovery rate <0.05), including directional shifts in phospholipid and amino acid modules toward healthy reference profiles. Integrated microbial and targeted metabolic analyses resolved branch-selective responses, with TEX reducing 3-hydroxykynurenine, kynurenic acid, and xanthurenic acid and shifting the host kynurenine-branch score toward the sham profile in CKD mice. Spatial metabolomics mapped region-specific renal responses, and tissue analyses identified reduced collagen I accumulation together with increased AQP1, SIRT3, SOD2, and NRF2 signals.

Conclusion: This study establishes branch selectivity and renal regional specificity as defining features of TEX-associated metabolic remodeling in CKD. The integrated evidence defines a spatially organized, kynurenine-centered metabolic-renal response architecture comprising host kynurenine-branch remodeling, anatomically patterned renal metabolic responses, and selective structural, tubular, and mitochondrial/redox phenotypes, with kynurenine-mitochondrial/redox coupling as the central mechanistic proposition.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a progressive systemic disorder and a major global health burden, with substantial morbidity and mortality despite advances in disease management and kidney replacement therapy. [1] Beyond declining renal filtration, CKD is characterized by persistent metabolic stress involving altered nutrient handling, redox imbalance, mitochondrial dysfunction, [2] inflammation, and accumulation of retained metabolites. [3, 4] The kidney is particularly vulnerable to metabolic stress because tubular epithelial cells have high energetic demands and rely heavily on mitochondrial metabolism to sustain solute transport and specialized cellular functions. [2, 4] Metabolomics is therefore valuable not only for biomarker discovery but also for defining systems-level pathway organization and treatment responsiveness. [5, 6] This systems perspective motivates investigation of whether metabolic organization can be modified under persistent renal stress.

Trace elements provide a potential entry point to this question. Essential minerals serve as cofactors or structural components of enzymes involved in redox defense, mitochondrial function, intermediary metabolism, and cellular adaptation, while their systemic homeostasis is frequently altered in CKD and further influenced by dialysis. [7] [8] [9] Clinical studies of individual trace elements have produced heterogeneous biochemical and clinical responses. [10] [11] [12] [13] Mechanistic studies of selenium biology further show that selenoprotein H can regulate MFN2-dependent mitochondrial quality control in experimental kidney injury. [14] A defined trace element cocktail therefore provides an opportunity to determine how combined elemental exposure engages selective metabolic programs across interconnected compartments, without assigning the response to any single element in advance.

The response to metabolic stress may extend across interacting systemic and tissue compartments. The gutkidney axis links renal dysfunction, intestinal microbial ecology, and circulating metabolism, with bidirectional interactions between kidney disease, the microbiota, and microbe-derived metabolites shaping host homeostasis. [15] [16] [17] Tryptophan metabolism provides a useful framework for resolving these interactions because circulating tryptophan metabolites arise from both host and microbial pathways, including the host kynurenine pathway and microbial indole metabolism. [18] [19] [20] Kynurenine metabolites add further biological relevance through their participation in immune, inflammatory, and metabolic regulation. [21] At the same time, metabolic vulnerability within the kidney is inherently spatial: distinct anatomical regions operate within different oxygen, substrate, and energetic environments and maintain specialized metabolic programs. [22, 23] Spatially resolved metabolomics can determine how these responses are distributed across anatomically defined compartments. [24] Using a problem-oriented multi-omics strategy [25], we integrated longitudinal clinical assessment in maintenance hemodialysis patients with endpoint plasma metabolomics and fecal metagenomics, followed by targeted tryptophan-pathway profiling, renal spatial metabolomics, histopathology, and molecular analyses in an independent CKD mouse model. Rather than treating each platform as an independent endpoint, we used each layer to resolve a question raised by the preceding analysis: circulating metabolomics identifies responsive modules, metagenomics clarifies the microbial context, targeted profiling resolves branch selectivity, spatial metabolomics localizes renal responses, and tissue analyses define accompanying phenotypes. We hypothesized that TEX elicits selective metabolic responses that can be resolved progressively from circulating modules to tryptophan branches, renal regions, and tissue phenotypes. This design was intended to establish the direction, anatomical distribution, and tissue correlates of TEXassociated remodeling while generating mechanistically testable priorities.

Material and methods

Ethics statement

The clinical trial was approved by the Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee of Yanbian Hospital, Yanbian University (Approval No. 2023266) on 16 February 2023, before enrollment. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment, and the study followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Recruitment occurred from 1 March to 1 June 2023, and follow-up ended on 1 September 2024. The trial was registered with the International Traditional Medicine Clinical Trial Registry (ITMCTR2026000263; https://trialsearch.who.int/Trial2.aspx?TrialID=ITMCTR2026000263) on 3 February 2026, after recruitment and follow-up had been completed. Registration was therefore retrospective and did not meet the requirement for prospective registration. Ethics approval preceded enrollment but did not constitute prospective trial registration. Animal experiments were approved by the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of the Institute of Materia Medica, Peking Union Medical College (Approval No. IMM-N-26-0139) and were conducted in accordance with institutional animal care and use guidelines.

Randomization and masking

In this randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, parallel-group trial, 80 adults (18-75 years) receiving maintenance hemodialysis were randomly assigned (1:1) to standard care plus oral trace element supplementation (experimental group [EG], n = 40) or standard care plus matched placebo (control group [CG], n = 40). An investigator who was not involved in participant recruitment, intervention assignment, or outcome assessment generated a computer-based random allocation sequence using permuted blocks of four without stratification. Allocation was concealed using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes prepared in advance and opened only after eligibility was confirmed, written informed consent obtained, and baseline assessments completed. Enrolling clinicians had no access to the allocation sequence. After enrollment, a research coordinator opened the next envelope in numerical order and assigned the corresponding intervention; the coordinator could not access the sequence before opening the envelope. Participants and investigators were blinded to treatment allocation, and the placebo was matched to the active formulation. The trace element cocktail (TEX) was obtained from Beijing Jinshan Ecology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China; food production license No. SC23221078200017) and administered orally at 20 mL/day for up to 12 months after a 1-month run-in period. Clinical laboratory parameters were collected during routine dialysis visits. A voluntary subset additionally provided endpoint blood and fecal specimens at Yanbian University Hospital for multi-omics analyses (healthy controls [HC], n = 10; CG, n = 12; EG, n = 20). Because multi-omics sampling was performed only at endpoint in a voluntary subset, these molecular analyses were interpreted as exploratory.

Participants and follow-up

This single-center study was conducted at Yanbian University Hospital. Eligible participants were adults (18-75 years) with CKD diagnosed and staged according to KDIGO criteria who were receiving regular maintenance hemodialysis for end-stage renal disease. Body weight, dialysis prescription, and major medications were required to be stable during the month before screening, and all participants provided written informed consent. Key exclusions were acute dialysis or intermittent kidney replacement therapy, planned kidney transplantation, recent severe cardiovascular events, uncontrolled hypertension, clinically important bleeding, malignancy, severe infection, HIV infection, active autoimmune disease, severe gastrointestinal disease or malnutrition, allergy to study materials, participation in another trial, and pregnancy or breastfeeding.

Follow-up visits were scheduled every 2-3 months with a ±14-day window. The protocol specified recording of adverse events and concomitant medications and assessment of adherence using returned packaging.

Clinical outcomes, sample size, and protocol amendments

The primary clinical objective was to evaluate safety and tolerability. Longitudinal treatment-by-time slopes were analyzed for creatinine, BUN, cystatin C, eGFR, and parathyroid hormone; other clinical laboratory measurements were secondary indicators. The planned sample size was 40 participants per group, with no documented formal calculation based on an assumed effect size, significance level, or statistical power. Endpoint omics sampling, originally planned in a random subset of approximately 20 EG and 10 CG participants, was ultimately conducted in voluntary subsets of 20 EG and 12 CG; together with absent baseline omics measurements, this warranted exploratory interpretation. The study design was finalized before recruitment began on 1 March 2023, and subsequent amendments did not alter the implemented trial design. Post-initiation changes comprised removal of platelet count, uric acid, carbon dioxide, total protein, prealbumin, and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) from the final reporting panel; addition of calcium-phosphorus product; reclassification of eGFR as a primary indicator; and specification of 12-month follow-up. The first five laboratory variables were absent from the final panel, whereas hs-CRP was measured only in a subset and had incomplete data. The registry listed creatinine, BUN, cystatin C, parathyroid hormone, and hs-CRP, but not eGFR, as primary outcomes. These amendments and discrepancies preclude claims of complete prospective alignment among the original study plan, registry record, and final manuscript; eGFR analyses were therefore treated as exploratory. Calcium-phosphorus product was derived and retained in Table S3 but was not analyzed as a treatment-effect outcome.

Animal study

Male C57BL/6N mice (SPF, 8 weeks old) were obtained from Charles River Laboratories (Cat #213). The animals were group-housed under standard laboratory conditions, with temperature and humidity maintained within the animal facility's routine ranges, adequate ventilation, and ad libitum access to standard chow and water. After 1 week of acclimatization, the mice were randomly allocated to Sham, CKD, and CKD-TEX groups using a random number generator. Chronic kidney disease was induced in the CKD and CKD-TEX groups by 5/6 nephrectomy, whereas mice in the Sham group underwent sham surgery. CKD-TEX mice received TEX diluted 300-fold in drinking water ad libitum for 8 weeks. Investigators performing histological scoring and image quantification were blinded to group allocation. At endpoint, blood and kidney tissues were collected for renal function assessment, histopathology, immunostaining, inflammatory profiling by RT-qPCR and ELISA, targeted plasma metabolite quantification, and spatial metabolomics.

Elemental analysis by ICP-OES

The elemental composition of TEX was determined by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (Agilent 5800). Three independent digestions of the same composite solution were analyzed (2 mL digested and diluted to a final volume of 10 mL); Fe, Mg, Al, Zn, K, and Li received an additional 20-fold dilution, whereas the other elements received no additional dilution. Full-element screening, operating conditions, replicate measurements, means, standard deviations, and coefficients of variation are provided in Table S1. Elements below the limit of detection in all replicates were excluded. Ag detected in only one replicate was excluded as an analytical artifact, as documented in Table S1. These measurements characterize the administered formulation and do not represent circulating or tissue elemental exposure in participants.

Untargeted plasma metabolomics

Plasma samples were extracted with acetonitrile:methanol (1:4, v/v) containing internal standards, and pooled quality-control samples were analyzed alongside study samples. Profiling was performed on a Q Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled to an ACQUITY Premier HSS T3 column (Waters), acquiring full-scan MS and data-dependent MS/MS over m/z 75-1,000 in both ionization modes. Data were processed in R and MetaboAnalystR using PCA, hierarchical clustering, and PLS-DA (999 label permutations); group comparisons used Kruskal-Wallis and Mann-Whitney U tests with Benjamini-Hochberg correction, metabolites were annotated against the KEGG Compound database, and effect sizes were summarized as Cohen's d.

Fecal metagenomic sequencing and analysis

Fecal genomic DNA was quality-controlled, fragmented, and subjected to shotgun library preparation and sequencing on an MGI DNBSEQ-T7 platform (BGI, Shenzhen, China). Reads were quality-filtered with fastp, host-depleted and mapped with Bowtie2, assembled with MEGAHIT, and gene-predicted with MetaGeneMark; a nonredundant catalog was built with CD-HIT, with taxonomic annotation by DIAMOND/MEGAN against the NCBI nonredundant database and functional annotation against KEGG. Abundance profiles were CLRtransformed, and microbial tryptophan biosynthetic capacity was summarized as a composite module score across seven KEGG orthologs (trpE-trpB).

Targeted tryptophan metabolite analysis

Mouse plasma tryptophan and its metabolites were quantified by targeted LC-MS/MS on a QTRAP 6500+ system (SCIEX) using scheduled multiple reaction monitoring, with data acquired in Analyst 1.6.3 and quantified in MultiQuant 3.0.3; metabolite-by-metabolite MRM transitions were individually optimized.

Spatial metabolomics

Frozen kidney sections (10 μm) were mounted on ITO-coated slides, coated with 2,5-dihydroxybenzoic acid matrix, and imaged on a timsTOF fleX mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics) in positive ion mode over m/z 50-1,300 at 50 μm spatial resolution, with MS/MS for structural confirmation. Data were RMS-normalized in SCiLS Lab and analyzed by PCA and OPLS-DA (200 permutations; differential features: VIP > 1 and P < 0.05), with regional comparisons across the cortex, outer stripe of the outer medulla (OSOM), inner stripe of the outer medulla (ISOM), and inner medulla (IM).

Renal function, histology, immunostaining, ELISA, and RT-qPCR

Mouse Scr and BUN were measured with commercial assay kits (Cat #ADS-W-FM034 and Cat #ADS-W-N013-96; Jiangsu Aidisheng Biological Technology Co., Ltd.). Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded kidney sections (3 μm) were processed for H&E, PAS, IHC, and immunofluorescence using primary antibodies against AQP1, SIRT1, SIRT3, SOD2, CD38 (CST), IDH2 (Abcam), collagen type I, α-smooth muscle actin, NRF2, and F4/80 (Proteintech), with Alexa Fluor-or CoraLite-conjugated secondary antibodies (Abcam, Proteintech). Images (Olympus DP26) were quantified with Fiji/ImageJ (version 2.14.0) and QuPath under consistent settings. Renal IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-10 were measured by ELISA (Elabscience Cat #E-EL-M0044; R&D Systems Cat #VAL601 and #VAL605) and normalized to total protein (BCA assay). RT-qPCR was performed with SYBR Green chemistry on an ABI 7500 system using β-actin as the reference gene and the 2-ΔΔCt method; primer sequences are provided in Table S2.

Statistical analysis

Analyses used R (version 4.4.1), GraphPad Prism (version 10.4.1), Python (version 3.13.13), and MetaboAnalystR. Data are presented as mean ± SD, mean ± SEM, or median with interquartile range, as specified in the figure legends; tests were two-sided with P < 0.05 considered significant, and the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure controlled the false discovery rate where applicable. Clinical analyses followed randomized allocation and used all available non-missing observations without imputing missing values. Linear mixed-effects models were fitted by restricted maximum likelihood, with participant-specific random intercepts and fixed effects for treatment, elapsed time, and treatment-by-time interaction. The interaction coefficient represents the between-group slope difference (EG minus CG); Wald 95% confidence intervals were calculated from model-based standard errors. Outcome-specific models used 236 observations from 80 participants for Scr, eGFR, and BUN, 200 observations from 79 participants for cystatin C, and 218 observations from 79 participants for PTH. The longitudinal clinical dataset and outcome-specific data availability are provided in Tables S3 and S4. Targeted metabolite, histological, cytokine, and biochemical data were analyzed by one-way ANOVA with Tukey's test. Spatial metabolomics used PCA/OPLS-DA; zonematched comparisons used Friedman tests followed by paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, while betweengroup comparisons used Kruskal-Wallis and Mann-Whitney U tests, with Holm correction for multiple comparisons. Individual participants or animals were the biological replicates; repeated assay measurements were not treated as independent biological replicates. Experiment-specific tests, sample sizes, and comparisons are provided in the figure legends. Effect-size distributions and recovery indices were treated as descriptive summaries and not as substitutes for direct between-group significance testing.

Results

A defined trace element cocktail establishes the clinical exposure framework

We first characterized exposure to the trace element cocktail in 80 maintenance hemodialysis patients randomized to standard care plus matched placebo (CG, n = 40) or standard care plus TEX (EG, n = 40) and followed longitudinally (Fig. 1A). The baseline characteristics presented were age, sex, and body mass index (Fig. 1B). The formulation comprised 20 mineral elements, with iron, magnesium, aluminum, zinc, potassium, and lithium representing the predominant constituents (Fig. S1 and Table S1). At 20 mL/day, it provided approximately 46.1 mg iron, 39.5 mg magnesium, 29.3 mg aluminum, 14.5 mg zinc, 11.2 mg potassium, 5.14 mg lithium, 2.66 mg manganese, and 463 μg selenium per day; the nominal aluminum exposure corresponded to 205.1 mg/week. Zinc, manganese, copper, and molybdenum exposures remained below general adult upper intake levels, whereas nominal iron and selenium exposures exceeded commonly cited general-population values. [26, 27] Aluminum exposure was considered in relation to the general-population tolerable weekly intake framework. [28] These reference values provide general-population context rather than dialysis-specific thresholds. Together with the formulation analysis, they define the administered exposure framework for interpreting the subsequent longitudinal and molecular findings.

Longitudinal monitoring establishes the clinical and laboratory context

The clinical dataset comprised 236 observations (CG, 119; EG, 117): 77 participants contributed three records, 2 contributed two records, and 1 contributed baseline data only. The median participant-level maximum recorded follow-up was 6.55 months (IQR 5.48-8.10; range 0-10.9). Longitudinal monitoring showed no overt treatment-related laboratory safety signal. Hepatic biomarkers (cholinesterase, alanine aminotransferase, and aspartate aminotransferase), parathyroid hormone, phosphate, calcium, hemoglobin, and albumin showed no significant treatment-by-time interactions (all Pinteraction ≥ 0.142; Fig. 1C and Fig. S2). Standardized estimates were generally small, with 95% confidence intervals spanning the null (Fig. 1C). These findings support a reassuring laboratory safety profile within the monitored assessments during the observed followup.

The serum creatinine slope difference between EG and CG was -6.17 μmol/L/month (95% CI -17.32 to 4.99; Pinteraction = 0.28; Fig. 1D). The corresponding eGFR slope difference was +0.12 mL/min/1.73 m²/month (95% CI -0.02 to 0.27; Pinteraction = 0.094; Fig. 1E). Confidence intervals for the integrated renal-parameter estimates remained compatible with the null (Fig. 1F). Slope differences for BUN and cystatin C were +0.18 mmol/L/month (95% CI -0.28 to 0.63; Pinteraction = 0.45; Fig. 1G) and +0.003 mg/L/month (95% CI -0.060 to 0.066; Pinteraction = 0.92; Fig. 1H), respectively. The PTH slope difference was +3.45 pg/mL/month (95% CI -8.01 to 14.91; Pinteraction = 0.55; Fig. S2D). In maintenance dialysis, these circulating trajectories provide a longitudinal clinical context rather than a direct measurement of residual kidney function, thereby motivating molecular phenotyping of treatment-associated responses.

Plasma metabolomics identifies treatment-associated metabolic modules

Untargeted plasma metabolomics showed pronounced separation between healthy controls (HC) and CG, consistent with broad CKD-associated metabolic differences, [29] [30] [31] whereas EG remained within the CKDassociated metabolic space with a descriptive displacement from CG (999 permutations, P = 0.0010; R²Y = 0.88, Q² = 0.43; Fig. 2A). Median absolute Cohen's d was 2.09 for CG versus HC and 0.25 for EG versus CG (Fig. 2B). The direct EG-versus-CG comparison identified metabolites in both directions exceeding the FDR = 0.05 threshold (Fig. 2C), and the effect-size distribution indicated selective remodeling across metabolic features. Enrichment analysis of metabolites differing between HC and CG identified multiple interconnected pathways (Fig. 2D), and representative metabolites illustrated heterogeneous endpoint patterns across groups (Fig. 2E). Module-level recovery indices relative to the HC reference were highest for phosphatidylethanolamine and phosphatidylcholine, followed by amino acids (Fig. 2F); selected metabolites likewise showed directional shifts toward the HC reference (Fig. 2G). These human molecular findings identify phospholipid and amino acid modules as priority candidates for longitudinal metabolic studies.

Metagenomics refines the microbial context of metabolic remodeling

Principal coordinate analysis based on Aitchison distance showed separation between HC and CG (PERMANOVA, P < 0.001), whereas EG did not differ significantly from CG (P = 0.14) and remained distinct from HC (P < 0.001; Fig. 3A). Shannon diversity differed across the three groups (P = 0.0072; Fig. 3B). At the taxonomic and functional levels, TEX-associated effect sizes were descriptively smaller than diseaseassociated differences (Fig. 3C-F). All seven KEGG orthologs in the microbial tryptophan biosynthesis pathway (trpE, trpG, trpD, trpC, trpF, trpA, and trpB) were depleted in CG, whereas their TEX-associated effect sizes were close to the null (Fig. 3G). The composite module score was lower in CG than HC (P < 0.001) and remained lower in EG than HC (P = 0.0050), with no significant CG-versus-EG difference (P = 0.18; Fig. 3H). These findings distinguish community-wide microbial composition from the systemic metabolic response and motivate branch-resolved assessment of host and microbial tryptophan metabolism.

TEX selectively modulates host kynurenine metabolism in CKD mice

To resolve the host and microbial components of tryptophan metabolism, we examined the two major branches of circulating tryptophan metabolism, which generate bioactive metabolites implicated in kidney disease, inflammation, and systemic uremic toxicity, [19, 32, 33] using an independent 5/6 nephrectomy-induced CKD mouse model (Fig. 4A). Targeted profiling identified coordinated CKD-associated changes across both branches (Fig. S3A-C). The host kynurenine branch showed increased KYN/TRP and circulating KYN, 3hydroxykynurenine (3-HK), kynurenic acid (KYNA), and xanthurenic acid (XA) (Fig. 4B-F). Supplementation shifted this profile toward the sham state, with significant reductions in 3-HK, KYNA, and XA relative to untreated CKD, whereas KYN and KYN/TRP showed similar directional changes without reaching significance. Recovery analysis showed attenuation of CKD-associated elevations in 3-HK, KYNA, and XA (Fig. S3D), and the kynurenine branch metabolite score likewise shifted toward the sham profile (Fig. 4G). The coordinated downstream response was therefore more pronounced than the change in the upstream KYN/TRP ratio, focusing interpretation on metabolite handling within the branch.

In contrast, metabolites within the microbial indole branch showed less consistent responses. CKD increased circulating indole-3-propionic acid (IPA) and indole-3-lactic acid (ILA), but neither was significantly reduced following supplementation, while indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) showed no clear treatment-associated change (Fig. 4H-J). Indoxyl sulfate was elevated in CKD, but the CKD-versus-CKD-TEX comparison was not significant (Fig. 4K). Because circulating indoxyl sulfate is strongly influenced by renal clearance, it was not interpreted as a direct indicator of microbial pathway activity. [19, 34] Consistently, the composite indole branch metabolite score showed no clear restoration with TEX (Fig. 4L). The response in mice was therefore branch selective, with movement toward the sham phenotype in the host kynurenine arm. Together with the human metagenomic findings, these observations prioritize host-associated kynurenine metabolism for mechanistic investigation of TEX-associated renal responses.

Spatial metabolomics resolves region-specific renal responses to TEX

MALDI imaging mass spectrometry-based spatial metabolomics was performed on kidney sections from sham and CKD mice, with four anatomically defined regions-the cortex, outer stripe of the outer medulla (OSOM), inner stripe of the outer medulla (ISOM), and inner medulla (IM)-analyzed separately (Fig. 5A). Principal component analysis revealed pronounced spatial structuring, consistent with the established regional organization of renal metabolism, [22, 35, 36] and CKD produced substantial displacement of these regional profiles (Fig. 5B). Regional deviation was heterogeneous across zones (Friedman test, P = 0.00090), with the inner medulla showing the greatest deviation (Fig. 5C). The global measure of renal metabolic zonation showed an overall group difference (Kruskal-Wallis test, P = 0.042), but no pairwise comparison remained significant after Holm correction, including CKD versus CKD-TEX (Fig. 5D). Representative metabolites displayed distinct zone-enriched distributions under physiological conditions-triglyceride species in the cortex, kynurenine in the OSOM, acetyl-L-carnitine in the ISOM, and glycerophosphocholine in the IM-with corresponding ion images supporting their regional distributions and CKD-associated remodeling (Fig. 5E-G).

Descriptive TEX-associated effect-size patterns varied by anatomical location and metabolic class rather than showing a uniform shift (Fig. 5H). After centering to the global median, all displayed metabolic classes had positive values in ISOM and negative values in IM, whereas the cortex and OSOM showed class-dependent mixtures of positive and negative values. No significance testing was performed for these centered effect-size patterns. Together, the regional deviation and centered effect-size maps distinguish the anatomy of CKDassociated disruption from the anatomy of TEX-associated responsiveness. These findings define regional heterogeneity as a central feature of renal metabolic remodeling and identify anatomical compartments for focused tissue analyses.

TEX engages selected renal structural and tubular markers

To place the regional metabolic patterns in a tissue context, we examined selected renal structural and tubular markers (Fig. 6A). PAS staining showed prominent tubular injury in CKD kidneys (Fig. 6B). CKD increased collagen I deposition and α-SMA-positive area; TEX significantly reduced collagen I accumulation but not α-SMA area (Fig. 6E and F). [30] AQP1 fluorescence intensity was reduced in CKD kidneys and was significantly higher following TEX supplementation (Fig. 6E and F). Endpoint serum creatinine and BUN did not differ significantly between CKD and CKD-TEX, and the CKD-versus-CKD-TEX difference in tubular injury score was not significant (Fig. 6C and D). The principal tissue signal was therefore concentrated in selected structural and tubular markers, providing phenotypic context for the metabolic findings.

Exploratory interaction analyses further examined whether circulating kynurenine-branch metabolites showed group-dependent associations with BUN. Significant metabolite-by-group interactions were observed for 3-HK and KYNA (Pinteraction = 0.026 and 0.0050, respectively; Fig. S3E). These group-dependent associations provide a quantitative link between branch metabolites and renal phenotypic variation, nominating kynureninebranch metabolites for controlled studies of metabolic-renal phenotype coupling.

TEX enhances selected antioxidant and stress-response markers CKD was associated with marked reductions in renal SIRT3 and SOD2 fluorescence intensity, together with a more modest reduction in IDH2, consistent with alteration of mitochondrial antioxidant-related markers. [37, 38] Representative images are shown in Fig. 6G and quantification in Fig. 6H. TEX significantly increased SIRT3 and SOD2 signals relative to untreated CKD, whereas IDH2 showed a directional but nonsignificant difference. NRF2 fluorescence intensity was significantly higher following supplementation than in untreated CKD, while CKD alone did not differ significantly from sham (Fig. S4A and B). [39] Together, these changes identify a selective antioxidant-related molecular response centered on SIRT3, SOD2, and NRF2, providing a tissue-level complement to the metabolic remodeling observed in CKD mice. SIRT1, CD38, and F4/80 showed no treatment-associated differences: although SIRT1 and CD38 were altered in CKD relative to sham, neither differed significantly between CKD and CKD-TEX (Fig. S4C-E), and F4/80positive area did not differ across groups (Fig. S4F and G). Renal IL-6, IL-10, and IL-1β transcript levels and corresponding cytokine concentrations likewise showed no consistent treatment-associated changes (Fig. 6I and J). This marker specificity reinforces the selective nature of the tissue response and narrows the molecular model to a defined mitochondrial/redox module rather than a uniform change across all measured pathways.

Discussion

This study identifies branch-selective and region-specific metabolic remodeling as the central feature of TEXassociated responses in CKD. The clinical cohort establishes the human context, with longitudinal laboratory monitoring showing no overt treatment-related safety signal and plasma metabolomics resolving treatmentassociated molecular differences. The mouse experiments then progressively refine this signal: targeted profiling identifies the host kynurenine branch as a responsive pathway, spatial metabolomics maps renal responses to anatomical compartments, and tissue analyses reveal accompanying structural and antioxidantrelated phenotypes. The resulting evidence chain moves from systemic metabolic differences to pathway selectivity, renal localization, and tissue context, providing a coherent model of how a multi-element intervention engages metabolic organization in CKD. Because creatinine and eGFR trajectories do not directly quantify residual kidney function in maintenance dialysis, [40, 41] molecular phenotyping adds a complementary dimension to conventional clinical assessment.

The formulation provides a biologically plausible basis for this selective response. Essential trace elements participate in redox regulation, mitochondrial and intermediary metabolism, and cellular homeostasis, while their availability in CKD is influenced by absorption, tissue distribution, renal handling, and dialysisassociated removal. [7, 9, 42] The formulation also contains elements not classified as essential trace elements. Previous clinical studies have generally evaluated individual elements and reported heterogeneous biochemical and clinical responses. [10] [11] [12] [13] The present findings extend this work by evaluating a defined multi-element formulation as a coordinated exposure whose responses can be resolved across metabolic branches and tissue compartments. Experimental evidence linking selenoprotein H to mitochondrial quality control illustrates how elemental biology can be translated into specific renal mechanisms, [14] while the present systems-level phenotype provides a basis for future component-resolved analysis. This shifts the emphasis from singleelement endpoints toward the organization and selectivity of the metabolic response.

Within the clinical cohort, plasma metabolomics provides the strongest human molecular evidence of treatment-associated differences. CKD is characterized by extensive disruption of circulating metabolic networks, [5, [43] [44] [45] and the healthy-control and CKD comparisons reproduced that broad metabolic background. Against this background, the EG-versus-CG comparison identified FDR-significant metabolites in both directions, with selected phospholipid and amino acid modules and individual metabolites shifting toward the healthy reference. The smaller treatment-associated effect-size distribution relative to the diseaseassociated comparison further supports selectivity: the response is concentrated in defined modules rather than dispersed uniformly across the metabolome. Phosphatidylethanolamine and phosphatidylcholine recovery also connects the intervention-associated signature to membrane and lipoprotein-related lipid organization. Detailed HDL lipidomics in CKD has shown that molecular lipid species provide pathophysiological information beyond conventional lipid measurements, [46] supporting the value of resolving lipid responses at the species and module levels. Recovery indices quantify the direction and magnitude of these shifts and provide a practical framework for evaluating their temporal durability in baseline-controlled studies of metabolic resilience. [47] Accordingly, the clinical metabolomic signature serves as a pathway-selection layer for the mechanistic analyses that follow.

The fecal metagenomic findings refine this model by distinguishing microbial community structure from metabolic pathway responses. CKD markedly altered microbial composition and tryptophan biosynthetic potential, whereas TEX-associated community-level effects were more circumscribed. This pattern directs attention to selective microbial functions and host metabolic handling, rather than requiring broad community restructuring as an explanation for systemic metabolic differences. Fecal metagenomics captures genetic and functional potential, and its integration with microbial activity, substrate availability, metabolite flux, and localized intestinal interactions will further resolve the gut-kidney metabolic interface. [48] [49] [50] [51] Integrated human metagenomic and metabolomic studies have linked gut microbial and circulating metabolic variation to CKD severity, [52] while defined microbial pathways can generate circulating indole metabolites with host biological activity. [17, 20] In hemodialysis, gut microbial features have been linked to indole metabolism and circulating uremic solutes, while kidney function itself can influence microbial metabolic output. [53, 54] Accordingly, the metagenomic results serve as a mechanistic filter that motivates branch-resolved analysis of tryptophan metabolism. A useful mechanistic comparison is provided by a JAR study in which microbial IPA alleviated diabetic kidney disease through mitochondrial protection involving SIRT1 stabilization. [55] The present data show a different response architecture: the indole-branch score and SIRT1 were not treatmentresponsive, whereas host kynurenine metabolites and SIRT3, SOD2, and NRF2 markers changed in a coordinated manner. This contrast narrows the model from a generalized microbial metabolite-sirtuin explanation to a branch-and node-specific framework.

The host kynurenine branch is closely linked to inflammatory and metabolic features of CKD. [33, 56] Kynurenines are bioactive regulators of immune and metabolic processes, [21] and kynurenine-3monooxygenase represents a regulated step connecting upstream kynurenine handling to 3-HK and downstream metabolism. [57] Within this pathway, targeted mouse analysis identifies the clearest pathwaylevel response to TEX. Reductions in 3-HK, KYNA, and XA, together with the shift in the kynurenine-branch metabolite score toward the sham profile, form a coherent response across related metabolites. The different behavior of microbial indole metabolites reinforces this branch selectivity. Host kynurenine metabolism and microbial indole production are interconnected but distinct arms of tryptophan metabolism in CKD, [18, 19, 58] and kynurenine-pathway metabolites have been associated with inflammation, renal dysfunction, and subsequent kidney failure. [58] [59] [60] Recent mechanistic studies of quinolinic acid further demonstrate the value of connecting kynurenine-pathway metabolites with renal spatial metabolic phenotypes and systemic outcomes. [61, 62] Against this broader pathway context, the coordinated 3-HK, KYNA, and XA responses narrow the TEX-associated signature to specific downstream metabolites. Their stronger response relative to KYN and KYN/TRP raises the possibility that downstream production, utilization, or transport may be particularly relevant, while the group-dependent associations of 3-HK and KYNA with BUN add phenotypic relevance. These alternatives can now be distinguished directly using isotope-resolved flux analysis together with enzyme-and transport-focused measurements. [63] Renal spatial metabolomics localizes this pathway-selective response within the kidney. CKD-associated disruption was anatomically heterogeneous, with the inner medulla showing the greatest deviation from sham. Kynurenine itself displayed a zone-enriched distribution in the OSOM, while TEX-associated effect sizes varied across regions and metabolic classes. Spatial metabolomics is specifically suited to resolving such regional alterations, which can be diluted when kidney tissue is analyzed as a single homogenate. [24, [64] [65] [66] [67] Human studies combining spatial metabolomics with clinical and molecular phenotyping have likewise demonstrated regional differences in renal oxidative metabolism, [36] reinforcing the importance of anatomical context. The observation that the IM showed the greatest CKD-associated deviation while centered TEXassociated class effects differed across the ISOM and IM further distinguishes the spatial distribution of disease disruption from that of treatment responsiveness. The maps therefore convert whole-organ metabolic averages into anatomically resolved phenotypes and provide spatial coordinates for examining how circulating pathway responses relate to renal tissue metabolism. Global zonation and regional analyses offer complementary views of renal metabolic organization, [68, 69] with the regional profiles identifying priority compartments for mechanistic investigation in the context of their distinctive oxygen and substrate environments. [23] The tissue analyses add structural and molecular significance to this spatially resolved model. Reduced collagen I accumulation and increased AQP1 signals identify extracellular-matrix and tubular marker responses, while increased SIRT3, SOD2, and NRF2 signals support a mitochondrial and antioxidant-related component. AQP1 has an established role in tubular water handling, [70] making its increased signal a useful tubular phenotypic correlate for future transport-focused validation. Tubular cells have substantial energetic requirements and depend heavily on mitochondrial metabolism, [37, 38, 71] and SIRT3 integrates metabolic and oxidative signals relevant to tubular adaptation. [72] Mitochondrial ROS can function as both signals and amplifiers of cellular injury, [73] providing a biologically coherent point of convergence for the observed metabolic and redox-related responses. Recent JAR studies have shown how selenoprotein H-MTCH2-MFN2 signaling and ANXA1-UCP1 signaling can be experimentally resolved into defined mitochondrial qualitycontrol and homeostatic mechanisms in kidney models. [14, 74] The present findings nominate a complementary SIRT3-SOD2-NRF2-associated axis for this level of mechanistic analysis. The convergence of kynurenine-branch remodeling, region-specific metabolic responses, and antioxidant-related marker changes therefore defines a kynurenine-mitochondrial/redox framework as a testable mechanism linking systemic and renal responses, with collagen I and AQP1 extending the framework to extracellular-matrix and tubular phenotypes.

Across these complementary experimental layers, each analysis resolves a question raised by the preceding one, consistent with a problem-oriented rather than platform-oriented use of multi-omics. [25] Human metabolomics establishes systemic treatment-associated differences; metagenomics clarifies the microbial context; targeted profiling identifies branch selectivity; spatial metabolomics provides renal localization; and tissue analyses add phenotypic context. Human and mouse findings are complementary rather than interchangeable: the former anchor clinical relevance, while the latter provide pathway, regional, and tissue resolution. This evidence chain creates a coherent and experimentally tractable model with three explicit predictions. First, if TEX preferentially alters host kynurenine handling, isotope-resolved studies should detect branch-specific changes in metabolite flux rather than relying on abundance alone. [63] Second, region-and cell-resolved analyses should determine whether kynurenine metabolism and mitochondrial/redox markers coincide within defined renal compartments; single-cell metabolomics and integrated kidney spatial studies provide directly relevant methodological precedents. [75, 76] Third, component-resolved formulations coupled with blood and tissue element measurements should establish which exposure features account for the response. Together, these predictions convert the kynurenine-mitochondrial/redox model into a practical research program with defined readouts and experimental endpoints.

These findings also define a focused agenda for confirmation and translation. The human omics analyses were conducted at endpoint in voluntary, unequally sized subsets without baseline molecular measurements, and one participant contributed baseline clinical data only; baseline-controlled longitudinal multi-omics in adequately powered cohorts will therefore be important for establishing temporal persistence and clinical generalizability. [77] Urine-based measures of residual kidney function, direct blood and tissue element measurements, absorption and retention analyses, and component-resolved formulations will further refine clinical and exposure attribution. Participant-level adverse-event counts and returned-packaging adherence records were unavailable for quantitative analysis, and general-population intake reference values are not dialysis-specific safety thresholds; future studies should integrate systematic harms and adherence reporting with direct exposure assessment. In mice, larger groups, individual dose assessment, isotope tracing, and cellresolved kidney analyses will strengthen causal and mechanistic resolution. [78] The trial was registered retrospectively after completion of recruitment and follow-up, and post-initiation changes were made to the outcome panel and classification. These factors limit assessment of complete prospective alignment and are therefore disclosed explicitly. Ethics approval preceded enrollment but did not constitute prospective trial registration.

Conclusion

In conclusion, TEX-associated metabolic remodeling in CKD is defined by branch selectivity, renal regional specificity, and selective tissue involvement. Human circulating phospholipid and amino acid modules provide the clinical anchor, while the independent CKD mouse experiments resolve host kynurenine-branch remodeling, anatomically patterned renal metabolic responses, and coordinated collagen I/AQP1 and SIRT3/SOD2/NRF2 phenotypes. These findings establish a specific response architecture-kynureninebranch remodeling accompanied by spatially organized renal metabolic, mitochondrial/redox, and structural changes-rather than a generalized metabolic or microbial signature. The kynurenine-mitochondrial/redox connection is thus the central mechanistic proposition emerging from this study.
