Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Abstract

The experience of comfort, dignity, and safety during the use of transfer technologies remains poorly understood despite evidence supporting their effectiveness in reducing work-related musculoskeletal injuries among healthcare professionals. This study explored how older adults and healthcare professionals understand and experience comfort, dignity, and safety during technology-assisted transfers.

A qualitative descriptive study guided by reflexive thematic analysis was conducted across three healthcare facilities in Ottawa, Canada. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with 15 older adults and long-term care residents requiring technology-assisted transfers, and four focus group discussions were conducted with 15 healthcare professionals. Data were analyzed using Braun and Clarke's reflexive thematic analysis.

Findings revealed an overarching theme: the relational nature of the transfer experience.

Comfort, dignity, and safety were experienced as interconnected and reciprocal rather than as distinct outcomes. Three dimensions were identified: (1) comfort, which was physical, emotional, and relational; (2) dignity, which was restored through how staff treated the person; and ( 3) safety, which depended on trust in the person operating the device and, for professionals, on clinical judgment and adaptability. Participants also highlighted the importance of staff training, equipment customization, communication, and adequate staffing to enhance transfer experiences. Conclusion. Comfort, dignity, and safety during technology-assisted transfers are not inherent properties of transfer devices but are co-constructed through interactions among older adults, healthcare professionals, and technologies. Transfer quality depended on relational practices, such as communication, privacy, and staff continuity, alongside technical competence. Training that combines technical and relational skills, stable staffing, and adaptable equipment may improve transfer experiences.

1. Introduction

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), the number of people aged 60 and over is expected to increase from one billion in 2020 to 2.1 billion by 2050. The population aged 80 and over is expected to nearly triple during this same period (WHO, 2025). The natural aging process inevitably leads to a decline in functional abilities, which can result in older adults being admitted to hospitals and long-term care facilities more frequently. In these facilities, mobility impairments become more prevalent, and the risk of falls and related injuries increases accordingly (WHO, 2021). Falls are the leading cause of injury among older adults, and assisting them with movement exposes healthcare professionals to a comparable risk of musculoskeletal injury. Prevalence estimates for low back pain exceed 40% among nurses and nursing aides in long-term and home care settings (Davis & Kotowski, 2015; Martimo et al., 2008). Therefore, the use of assistive technologies, such as lifts (floor, total, mechanical, and ceiling), to facilitate mobility and transfers (e.g., from a bed to a wheelchair or toilet, and vice versa), has become common in these settings. There is evidence supporting the effectiveness of these technologies in reducing musculoskeletal injury among healthcare professionals (Hegewald et al., 2018; Martimo et al., 2008). However, little is known about how their use is experienced in terms of comfort, dignity, and safety. The existing literature on transfer technologies primarily considers the perspectives of healthcare professionals and employers. These studies evaluate transfer technologies based on factors such as load reduction, injury rates, and cost-effectiveness (Hegewald et al., 2018; Koppelaar et al., 2009; Martimo et al., 2008). The few studies that have addressed patient-level outcomes have generally focused on transfer time and general comfort ratings. For example, ceiling lifts have been associated with shorter transfer times and higher comfort ratings than floor-based lifts (Alamgir et al., 2009a; Alamgir et al., 2009b). The deeper relational, emotional, and identity-related dimensions of the transfer experience have received comparatively little attention. A scoping review mapping how comfort, dignity, and safety are conceptualized and measured across technology-assisted literature confirmed this pattern 1 : safety was the most rigorously conceptualized construct, comfort occupied an intermediate position, and dignity was the least defined and measured construct. It was mainly addressed through qualitative or ethical lenses rather than through dedicated instruments or tools, which limits their evaluation and comparison across settings.

Comfort (Kolcaba, 1994), dignity (Nordenfelt, 2003), and safety (Larsson et al., 2023) are central dimensions of person-centred care, yet they are difficult to operationalize and measure consistently in real-world contexts such as technology-assisted transfers, because they are subjective, multidimensional, and shaped by context (Clancy et al., 2021; Šaňáková & Čáp, 2019). The patient perspective is particularly underrepresented. This gap is especially significant for older adults with mobility impairments in hospitals or longterm care facilities. For this population, assisted transfers are frequent, and issues of vulnerability, functional dependence, and loss of autonomy are heightened (Heggestad et al., 2015; Liu et al., 2025). Qualitative research on dignity in long-term care settings suggests that older residents primarily define and experience dignity in relational terms, such as privacy, respectful communication, and preserving self-worth, rather than through clinical or technical criteria alone (Fuseini et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2025; Nordenfelt, 2003).

Little is known about how older adults and healthcare professionals jointly understand and experience these concepts during technology-assisted transfers. Informed by the Person-Centered Practice Framework (McCormack & McCance, 2006 , 2017), this study aimed to explore how older adults and healthcare professionals understand and experience comfort, dignity, and safety during technology-assisted transfers.

2.2 Settings, Participants, and Sampling

Using a purposive convenience sampling strategy, participants were recruited from three healthcare facilities in Ottawa, Ontario, Canada: Élisabeth Bruyère Hospital, Saint-Vincent Hospital (Bruyère), and Perley Health. These sites were selected because they provide continuing and long-term care and rely extensively on technology-assisted transfers. This provided a rich and varied context in which to examine everyday transfer practices. Perley Health, one of the largest long-term care facilities in Ontario, provided access to a wide variety of residents and transfer situations. Transfers at these sites were performed with ceiling lifts, sit-to-stand lifts, and floor lifts, within facility policies such as two-person transfer requirements.

Two groups of participants were included. The first group consisted of older adults and LTC residents with mobility impairments (n = 15) who required at least two assisted transfers per day using a transfer device. Individuals with cognitive impairment were not included, as this study was not designed to adapt its consent process and data collection methods to this population.

The second group included healthcare professionals directly involved in patient transfers (n = 15). This group included physiotherapists, physiotherapy assistants, registered nurses, registered practical nurses, and personal support workers (PSW) or personal care attendants (PCA).

All individuals who met the eligibility criteria were invited to participate during the recruitment period, to ensure the inclusion of those with direct, regular experience with technology-assisted transfers. Those who expressed interest and were available were enrolled, and sampling prioritized availability and willingness rather than deliberate variation in participant characteristics. Recruitment procedures varied slightly by site. At Saint-Vincent Hospital, clinical or unit managers distributed study information to eligible staff members, who were invited to contact the research team; at Élisabeth Bruyère Hospital, the same approach was attempted for staff without success (see Section 2.3). The research team received contact information for interested patients or residents and then contacted them for further screening and potential inclusion in the study. At Perley Health, staff were approached via an institutional recruitment email, and residents were approached via a recruitment flyer posted in common areas. The flyer outlined eligibility criteria and study procedures. Interested individuals contacted NT directly who screened them for eligibility, obtained written informed consent, and scheduled interviews or focus groups.

2.3 Data Collection

Between January and August of 2025, NT collected data through in-person focus group discussions (FGDs) and individual semi-structured interviews with both groups of participants.

All 15 patients and LTC residents across the three sites were interviewed individually. To accommodate the schedules of healthcare professionals, four focus group discussions (FGDs) were conducted: two at Saint-Vincent Hospital (one with RN/RPNs, n = 3; one with PCAs, n = 3) and two at Perley Health (one with PSWs, n = 4; one with a mixed group of PTs/PTAs and RNs/RPNs, n = 5) in conference rooms at the respective facilities. No healthcare professionals were recruited at Élisabeth Bruyère Hospital, and no FGDs were held there, because site managers were unavailable to respond to the recruitment emails needed to facilitate staff recruitment; only patients and residents were interviewed at this site. Interviews lasted between 30 and 45 minutes, and FGDs lasted between 45 and 60 minutes. Each participant took part in a single session; no repeat interviews were conducted.

The interviews and focus group discussions (FGDs) followed pre-established, pilot-tested guides that addressed the following: (1) experiences with transfer technologies, (2) perceived comfort, dignity, and safety during technology-assisted transfers, and (3) suggestions for improving the transfer experience. Separate guides were used for patients/residents and for healthcare professionals, following recommended practices for developing semi-structured interview guides (see Supplementary Files 2 and 3; Kallio et al., 2016). All sessions were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. Field notes were also taken to capture nonverbal cues not evident in the recordings. No one other than the participant(s) and NT was present during data collection. Data collection took place within the participating healthcare facilities, either in patients' or residents' rooms or in a conference room for staff FGDs.

Prior to participation, sociodemographic data was collected (age, sex, and length of stay for patients/residents; age, sex, professional role, and years of experience with technology-assisted transfers for professionals) through a written form. All transcripts and verbatim excerpts were anonymized using unique participant identifiers (e.g., P01 and S01 for patients and staff, respectively) before analysis and reporting. Transcripts were not returned to participants for comment or correction.

2.4 Research Team, Reflexivity, and Analytic Rigor

NT, a woman physiotherapist and PhD candidate with over six years of experience in qualitative health research, conducted all interviews and focus group discussions (FGDs). NT had no prior clinical or professional relationship with participants and was introduced as an external researcher. Participants were informed that the study is part of a broader doctoral project aiming to identify a way of evaluating the comfort, dignity, and safety of transfer technologies; the analysis reported here focuses on participants' own understandings and experiences of these concepts. Participants were informed verbally and in writing about the study purpose, procedures, and voluntary nature of participation prior to providing consent.

Consistent with the principles of reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006 , 2021), reflexivity was maintained throughout data collection and analysis. NT kept a reflexive journal and analytic memos to document evolving assumptions, methodological decisions, and interpretations. During interviews and FGDs, participants' accounts were summarized back to them by NT to verify her understanding of their experiences and meanings. This in-session verification did not constitute formal member checking, as transcripts and interpretations were not returned to participants. Regular analytic discussions among the research team (NT, JJ, and MB) encouraged critical engagement with the data and exploration of alternative interpretations. Differences in interpretation were viewed as analytically valuable opportunities to deepen understanding rather than discrepancies requiring consensus. Analytic rigour and transparency were further supported through maintenance of an audit trail documenting methodological decisions, refinements to interview guides, and the development of codes and themes throughout the study.

2.5 Data analysis

All transcripts were anonymized using unique participant codes (P01 for patients and residents and S01 for healthcare staff, for example). The analysis combined deductive and inductive approaches. Deductive coding was informed by the predefined domains and concepts embedded in the interview and focus group discussion (FGD) guides. Consistent with the study's aim of exploring participants' understanding of and experiences with comfort, dignity, and safety, data were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006).

NVivo was used for data management and for initial coding; because the coauthors did not have access to NVivo, coding was then continued in Word tables so that all team members could review it. The analysis followed Braun and Clarke's six phases. NT led the coding, and the research team members (JJ and MB) engaged in regular analytic discussions to refine and deepen interpretation throughout.

1. Familiarization with the Data

NT transcribed all recordings and immersed herself in the corpus by repeatedly reading the transcripts. Early analytic notes captured first impressions, emerging patterns, and sensitizing concepts drawn from the interview and focus group discussion (FGD) guides.

2. Generating Initial Codes

NT systematically coded the entire dataset, identifying semantic and latent meanings related to the participants' experiences with transfer technologies and their perceptions of comfort, dignity, and safety. Deductive codes, informed by the interview and focus group discussion (FGD) guides, were combined with inductive codes that emerged directly from the data.

3. Constructing Initial Themes

Codes were organized into preliminary themes that reflected patterned meanings across interviews and focus group discussions (FGDs). This stage involved grouping related codes and exploring their conceptual connections to person-centered transfer experiences.

4. Reviewing and Refining Themes

Themes were developed through an iterative process of reflexive interpretation, discussion among the research team (NT, JJ, and MB), and engagement with the data.

5. Defining and Naming Themes

Themes and subthemes were clearly defined and named to reflect their conceptual essence.

A coding record was maintained to support transparency of theme development. Coding tables (categories, codes, descriptions, and illustrative extracts) were used as working documents to organize the data; they were shared with JJ and MB for comment and revised iteratively and were not applied as a fixed codebook or used to assess coding agreement. 3.2 The Relational Dimension as a Cross-Cutting Thread 251

A core finding of this study is that comfort, dignity, and safety are not purely technical aspects of the transfer, but rather, they are influenced by the quality of the relationship between patients and staff throughout the process. Participants in both groups consistently described a "human factor": communication, eye contact, and verbal "check-ins" performed by staff during the procedure. These factors determine the quality of the transfer, regardless of the equipment used. When this relationship is strong, patients report feeling "in charge", even while suspended in a mechanical device. For example, P02 said, "The effectiveness of the staff is 100% related to how safe I feel." Conversely, even technically adequate equipment fails to produce a positive experience when the relational dimension is absent, as noted by P02 regarding dignity: "I don't think there's anything about the equipment that lends to dignity. There is nothing. There is nothing. There is no dignity in being lifted naked in the sling! There is no dignity." This relational thread connects the three dimensions : comfort, dignity and safety, examined below.

3.2.1 Perceptions and Definitions of Comfort : Comfort as a Relationally Mediated State

Within this relational frame, comfort encompassed physical, emotional, and personalizationrelated dimensions for both patients/residents and healthcare professionals.

The Physical Dimension of Comfort

For patients and residents, comfort is primarily defined as the absence of pain. P05 illustrates this idea: "When there's no pain involved [...] I feel comfortable when getting moved. No pain or anything." Proper positioning and careful equipment handling were also identified as contributing to physical comfort. This includes adjusting slings, preventing skin pinching, and avoiding sudden movements. For example, P02 noted that discomfort during transfers was mostly due to mechanical defects, substitute equipment, or improper sizing. Furthermore, technical failures, such as rapidly draining lift batteries, prolonged physical suffering, as illustrated by this quote from P02: "The battery runs out quickly. I'm in agony for five minutes instead of two". The same participant also noted that standardized equipment often failed to accommodate complex anatomical profiles, such as limb loss or severe obesity. This forced patients to endure ill-fitting devices. "Nothing is made to accommodate multiple amputations." The idea that personalized equipment should be considered to meet individual needs instead of standardized ones is supported by P09 (bariatric care needs), who emphasized the difference between a ceiling lift with two prongs and one with four prongs, the latter of which is more suitable for bariatric individuals (see Figure S1, Supplementary File 4). "This four-pronged lift is more comfortable than the two-pronged lift at the general hospital. It cradles you without causing pain."

Healthcare professionals agreed that physical comfort requires pain prevention and conceptualized this through clinical execution, ensuring precise sling placement, anatomical alignment, and pressure relief, as reported by S9 and S3, respectively.

S9: "Not having any physical discomfort and not being in poor positioning that would make you feel awkward... Make sure that the skin is not pinched, pulled, or twisted in any way when using a device." S3: "When you're talking about comfort, you have to position the patient properly in the chair." Sometimes, you put the patient too low, and they slide on the chair. You have to make sure the patient sits all the way back in the chair! That is comfort."

The Emotional and Psychological Dimension of Comfort

Beyond physical comfort, technology-assisted transfers can evoke feelings of profound psychological vulnerability. Patients described a grieving process associated with losing functional independence and experiencing transfers as a state of helplessness or melancholy. P01 said, "Well, I guess I will say that I feel out of control. When you are not in control, it is a state of helplessness. That's how I would describe it." [...] It's depressing to lose the ability to stand and walk for a long time. I call it a state of melancholy, meaning I can't do anything on my own.

"

Patients and residents reported that emotional comfort was restored when they developed a deep sense of trust in their caregivers' competence. For example, P15 said, "I feel comfortable because I know what they're doing. They're trained for this job, so I'm comfortable with them doing it. I trust them. "

From the perspective of healthcare professionals, emotional comfort was defined as proactively mitigating resident anxiety through step-by-step communication, seeking consent, and reading nonverbal distress indicators throughout the transfer process, as mentioned by S1 and S15. S1 said, "They like to be talked to before being moved. It makes them feel respected and less anxious." S15: "It's pretty much communication. Like letting them know, step by step, what you're doing. We look at their facial expressions and read their body language. So, if they make a facial expression, grunt, or move, those little cues help us determine whether we should change something."

Personalization and Relational Consistency

For both groups of participants, comfort was linked to tailoring the transfer approach to the specific needs and preferences of each patient and resident, as well as maintaining care continuity. On one hand, patients and residents feel more secure when the same person performs the transfer consistently. They believed that relief or "float" staff who were unfamiliar with their routine caused acute pain, as reported by P5: "A couple of them don't know how to use this thing properly, and I'm in pain when they move me! The regular PSW at night in the morning has no problem. But the one who replaces her when she's off is painful. Just because they don't know how to put the sling on properly."

On the other hand, professionals confirmed that relational familiarity is an essential prerequisite for psychological ease, as reported by S4: "Knowing their baseline, knowing triggers, knowing a whole set of things, and having familiarity that breeds comfort and trust."

Overall, both groups agree that comfort requires the absence of physical pain, but they emphasize different mechanisms for achieving it: patients focus on the physical fit and technical reliability of the equipment itself (sling size, battery performance, customization for amputation or skin sensitivity), while professionals focus on the relational conditions surrounding the transfer (a calm environment, personalization, and staff familiarity). This suggests that comfort is produced by a combination of adequate equipment and relational quality, rather than by either factor alone.

3.2.2 Perceptions and Definitions of Dignity

The clearest illustration of the relational thread introduced above concerns dignity. Across patient and resident accounts, transfer technology is consistently described as having no inherent dignity. Dignity during a technology-assisted transfer is not guaranteed by the technology (device) itself. Instead, it must be actively restored moment by moment through the attitude, empathy, and communication of the staff performing the transfer. This tension between mechanical execution and interpersonal care is present in the three sub-dimensions examined below: Personhood and respect, environmental privacy and modesty, and the technological boundary between human and the technology.

Personhood and Respectful Care

Patients and residents associated dignity with being recognized as whole human beings rather than being treated like objects or clinical tasks. They experienced violations of dignity when staff displayed impatience, handled them roughly, or dismissed them verbally, as illustrated by the following quotes: P05: "I keep telling her I'm not a dog! I'm a human being! Treat me like one. "

P15 :" By dignity you just like, kind and careful... You don't make a big deal out of anything. "

Similarly, healthcare professionals defined dignity as preserving patients and residents' identities and self-worth.

S9: "Preserving their sense of self. That would be my best stand... making sure that they feel respected as a person. "

Privacy, Modesty, and Environmental Control

Conversely, the exposure of private body parts during mechanical transfers was reported by residents as the most severe threat to dignity. For example, suspension in a lift during commode or shower transfers exposed extreme bodily vulnerability: P02 "There is no dignity in being lifted naked in the sling! Yeah, there is no dignity. If you're naked, going to the toilet, you're in a sling... everything is always just out there, exposed to the world. " This was feeling supported by another resident P04 who said: "Well, when they move me down the hall with no clothes on and find that rather awkward. " Professionals viewed environmental control, closing room doors, drawing privacy curtains, covering residents with blankets, and respecting cultural or religious modesty preferences as the baseline of dignified care, as reported by S3 and S15 during a FGD : "Privacy is number one. You don't leave the door open... you close the door. You close the curtain for the dignity of the patient ". They also emphasized the importance of respecting patients'/residents' cultural sensitivities, e.g., providing female-only care providers for Muslim female residents, as reported by S8 : " Culturally, you have to be respectful... we had one lady on our unit that are Muslim, right? So it's a religion thing. They don't want men to take care of them... So culture thing. "

The Technological Boundary: Machine vs. Human Relationship

Across the interviews and focus groups, patients and residents consistently emphasized that transfer technologies do not inherently confer dignity. They described being lifted and suspended in midair as an unavoidably mechanical and dehumanizing act, regardless of the device used. As noted by P05: "There is nothing about the equipment that lends dignity". However, patients also stressed that a staff member's attitude has the power to transform an otherwise undignified procedure. P02 captured this contrast succinctly: "There is no dignity in being moved via a lift. However, I feel dignified by the attitude of the staff ". This theme was particularly salient among participants who were completely dependent on others. For them, dignity was not inherent in the technology, but rather, it was conferred through interpersonal care. As P06 explained: "When you're incapable of doing anything yourself and someone has to do it for you there? Kind, considerate, and they understand what you're going through, that is feeling treated with dignity." Participants also described specific behaviors that help preserve dignity during transfers, such as making eye contact, being patient, and checking in moment to moment. As P06 put it, "Patience. How are you treating the person? Are you maintaining eye contact? Are you stopping to check in?" Healthcare professionals echoed this sentiment, noting that maintaining eye contact and providing continuous verbal guidance are essential to counteracting the unsettling experience of mechanical suspension. As one nurse, S1, explained: "We always tell them, "Look at our eyes. Look at our eyes." We maintain eye contact and read their nonverbal cues and comfort level". S9 further emphasized that, "since being hoisted leaves residents "floating in human's hand", preserving dignity requires an active, relational presence; consent; and keeping the resident covered to preserve their "sense of self" rather than processing them as a mechanical task."

Taken together, these cross-stakeholder accounts reveal a central conceptual distinction: Transfer equipment introduces a mechanical and potentially dehumanizing baseline, but dignity is restored or withheld through human interaction. The technology establishes the physical conditions of the transfer, but the staff's relational conduct determines whether the experience is dignified.

3.2.3 Safety: Physical Protection, Clinical Judgment, and Relational Trust

Safety emerged as the third nested dimension of the overarching relational thread and was universally recognized as the non-negotiable baseline of technology-assisted transfers. While institutional policies usually define safety in terms of systems-based criteria, such as equipment load limits, maintenance routines, and fall-prevention protocols, participant accounts revealed that safety is experienced and enacted relationally. For patients and residents, feeling safe depended less on the mechanical reliability of the device and more on interpersonal trust and confidence in the operator's technique. For healthcare professionals, safety required dynamic clinical judgment, continual adaptation to residents' changing conditions, and navigation of structural constraints, such as staffing shortages. Together, these perspectives highlight a central conceptual distinction: Systems-based safety establishes the technical conditions of a transfer, but relational safety determines how safe the transfer feels and unfolds in practice.

Physical Protection and Fall Prevention

Patients consistently defined safety in experiential terms, primarily as an absence of falling, pain, or collision during transfer. As one resident, P12 explained: "Falling! You can feel unsafe if you think you are going to fall". Another, P02 emphasized the importance of staff competence: "For me personally, safety is 100% related to how safe I feel, in that I won't sustain further injuries because the staff doesn't know what they're doing."

In contrast, healthcare professionals articulated safety through a clinical and procedural lens, emphasizing correct equipment selection, routine maintenance, and proper body mechanics. As one staff member S15, summarized: "Safety equals the right equipment for the right patient for the right reasons, causing no harm". These accounts illustrate how systems-based safety provides the structural foundation for preventing harm while patients rely on relational cues to assess their protection.

Clinical Judgment, Adaptability and Adaptability

Healthcare professionals emphasized that physical safety is not a static baseline but a dynamic clinical process that requires ongoing evaluation and real-time adaptability. Instead of treating a resident's ability to transfer as a fixed attribute, staff explained that medical status, physical weakness, and diurnal fatigue necessitate continuous clinical adjustments. As one nurse explained: S9: "If they're having medical issues where they're weaker, we would accommodate whatever transfer is necessary. You have to use your clinical judgment. "

One PT noted that physical fluctuations often follow daily patterns or medication cycles, especially in patients with neurodegenerative conditions like Parkinson's disease. S5 : "Technically, the way it's meant to work is there is supposed to be one transfer status, but knowing that we're all human, we have ups and downs. I often use the example of Parkinson's where medication can be extremely effective in certain windows of times, and then you come back an hour later... it seems like a totally different person... ".

To safely accommodate these shifts, a resident's transfer protocol may evolve throughout the day. For example, it may transition from manual side-by-side assistance in the morning to a sitto-stand lift in the afternoon and ultimately to a total ceiling lift in the evening when exhaustion sets in. As one nurse, S9, explained: "If they're having medical issues where they're weaker, we would accommodate whatever transfer is necessary. You have to use your clinical judgment".

To maintain safety across shifts and communicate dynamic adjustments seamlessly to incoming care teams, staff relied on visual signage, door logos, and pictograms posted outside residents' rooms. Together, these adaptive practices demonstrate how clinical judgment and visual signage bridge the gap between rigid institutional safety protocols and residents' fluctuating, real-time needs.

Systemic Constraints and Staffing Shortages

Both patients, residents and professionals identified staffing shortages as the primary structural barrier to safety and dignity during transfers. Policies requiring two-person assistance for mechanical lifts ("No-Lift Policy") often resulted in delays when staff were unavailable. As one professional, S13 explained, "When you want to do a transfer, we need two people... We wait! If the patient is wet and needs to be changed, they have to wait. The equipment is good, but we need more staff" . These accounts underscore how system-level constraints directly shape the relational experience of safety. They reveal that even well-designed equipment cannot compensate for insufficient staffing.

Overall, although both groups prioritize safety, professionals tend to focus on the prevention of harm through clinical risk-adjustment protocols, such as teamwork ratios and situational escalation, whereas patients tend to prioritize trust in a specific, known operator. Similarly to the tension identified for dignity, professionals describe safety as a system-level property (technique, staffing ratio, protocol), while patients experience it as a relational property (familiarity and competence of the individual operator).

3.3 Participant Preferences and Recommendations

Participants from both groups went beyond articulating the conceptual and ethical dimensions of technology-assisted transfers to offer pragmatic, experience-based recommendations for improving clinical practice and care delivery. These insights bridge the gap between mechanical execution and person-centered care. They reflect a shared desire to ensure that transfer technologies support, rather than undermine, patient's and resident's comfort, dignity and safety.

Rather than viewing device design in isolation, patients, residents and healthcare professionals emphasized that effective technology integration requires alignment across operator competence, equipment functionality, communication workflows, and organizational support. The following sections examine the participants' recommendations across four key domains: training and operator expertise; equipment selection and technical design; communication and coordination protocols; and staffing and relational care continuity.

3.3.1 Training and Expertise

Both residents and healthcare professionals emphasized a foundational recommendation: reforming educational practices to ensure that operator competence aligns with experiential and situational care needs. Patients often cite a lack of specialized training as a primary source of pain and anxiety. One common recommendation is to implement standardized training to ensure that all staff, especially "floats" and temporary workers, handle equipment with the same level of competence as regular staff.

P06 " We need standardized training for them. Why do I say that so quickly? Because I've seen it at the rehab centre [...] Standardized training for the staff is lacking here. "

P05 "Couple of them don't know how to use this thing properly, and I'm in pain when they move me! [...] The one that replaces her [regular PSW] when she's off, it's painful [...] Just because they don't know how to put the sling on properly. " One resident specifically suggests that staff should be required to get into a sling themselves during training to understand the physical and emotional sensation of being lifted. P03 : " I also think that one of the things that should happen is that the people operating this sling should be made to get into it and feel the differences. " Healthcare professionals emphasize this recommendation by pointing out that, while general training is available, it is often not "just-in-time." They recommend a training-on-demand structure, in which staff receive a refresher on a specific device, such as a sit-to-stand lift, when a new resident requiring that technology arrives on the unit. S5: "[...] I think perhaps a better structure for better availability to training on demand for specifically what is needed in the moment is probably the best. [...] we get plenty of training, but we don't always get the training that we need in the moment that we needed. " They also advocate for "refresher courses" to prevent the development of "bad practices" over time. S15 "Refresher courses are very good because some of the nurses who have been here for so many years, they're using their old ways of doing things. And then we get rusty, sometimes we keep on doing things that on old ways and yeah, so refresher courses for sure ".

S5 "Example: if my colleague works like in the unit where they haven't used the Sit-to-stand lift in three months and all of a sudden there's a new resident who requires that the sit-to stand lift, the colleague doesn't need to be trained on all the different transfer techniques! He needs to be refreshed on the sit-to-stand lift at that time. " Both groups converge on the need for more frequent training, but their focus differs: patients seek standardization to ensure safety regardless of the operator, while professionals seek specificity and timing to ensure clinical competence for the diverse needs of their current patients and residents. While experiential and on-demand training enhance operator competence and establishes the human skill set necessary for safe transfers, participants emphasized that staff expertise must be supported by appropriately designed, ergonomically sound equipment.

3.3.2 Equipment Choice and Technical Design

To support the staff's expertise, patients, residents, and healthcare professionals expressed their technical preferences and suggested hardware refinements. These refinements aimed to optimize mechanical performance, minimize physical discomfort, and reduce operator strain.

Patients and residents nearly universally prefer ceiling lifts to floor-based mechanical lifts.

Patients and residents prefer ceiling lifts because they allow for more precise positioning.

Standalone lifts, on the other hand, can make patients and residents feel "scrunched in a ball" or "hovering" awkwardly over a chair.

P02: "I would however say ceiling lifts would be 100% better than... mechanical standalone lifts.

For me, a ceiling lift is almost critical because it can position me directly above where I need to be and then just drop me down. " showing that ceiling lifts are associated with shorter transfer times, higher patient comfort ratings, and lower implementation barriers when integrated early into care routines compared to floor lifts (Alamgir et al., 2009a (Alamgir et al., , 2009b;; Koppelaar et al., 2009).

Overall, there is strong alignment among stakeholders regarding the superiority of ceilingmounted technology for patient comfort and staff ergonomics.

However, an optimized design and technical functionality of the equipment are insufficient if the transfer procedure lacks active and respectful communication between the operator and the resident.

3.3.3 Communication and Engagement Protocols

Acknowledging that mechanical transfers have the potential to compromise a resident's sense of autonomy, participants underlined the critical role of structured verbal and visual communication protocols in preserving that autonomy and maintaining consistency across shifts. Patients and residents recommended a "checklist" approach to communication, where staff would explain every technical step (e.g., which loop of the sling is being attached). They value being treated as partners in the transfer process.

P06: "At the rehab centre... whatever they're doing and they're moving you, whether it's OT or it's PT, it does not matter... they're always giving feedback on what they're doing. So if they're about to put the sling, they're gonna tell you: OK, we're going to start here. We're going to do X, we're doing Y, we're going to do Z... It gives you a checklist of what you've done or haven't done". One patient noted that if they sense the staff is inexperienced, they will "direct them" to ensure their own safety. P01: "My priority is I hope the person who is doing it has done it before.

When you sense that this person is doing it for the first time, that is when you feel a little bit apprehensive. But what I do, is I direct them. I say: 'you do this; you do that... strap.' If they have not done it before, then I direct them, I am in charge".

Healthcare professionals recommended standardizing visual aids and door signage (logos) to communicate real-time transfer protocols across shift changes. They emphasized that visual signage is an essential cognitive aid for incoming staff, allowing them to anticipate fluctuating patient needs before initiating a transfer. S5: "Having those multiple logos on a wall... alerts the staff who are coming into the room that this person might not be as straightforward... so they can have a better read from the get-go in determining how they set up for a given transfer ".

They also acknowledged using pictograms or "logos" on room doors to communicate a patient's current transfer status (e.g., "Two-person pivot") across shifts. S8 : "Sometimes you might have the logos on the wall. Day shift: two staff pivot-disk transfer. Evening shift: two staff -Sara Lift... that way it at least alerts the staff who are coming into the room that this person might not be as straightforward... and that there will be these fluctuations so they can have a better read from the get-go." They emphasize that continuous verbal check-ins are vital for assessing a patient's "readiness" and recognizing non-verbal indicators of distress. S8: "Pretty much communication.

Like letting know step by step what you're doing... We look at their facial expression, we read their body language... Talking through the process, making sure they feel comfortable with you as a staff. Asking permission".

Both groups prioritize communication. Patients and residents view it as a way to regain control over a "helpless" situation, while professionals use it as a clinical monitoring tool to ensure safety during shift changes.

However, even the most effective communication strategies and technical skills cannot be maintained without organizational structures that support staff familiarity and relational continuity.

3.4.4 Staffing and Relational Consistency

The final domain of recommendations addresses the systemic and organizational conditions required to foster relational continuity and promote dignified transfer practices. These conditions include staffing ratios and care assignment stability.

Patients and residents emphasize that consistency in staffing is a priority need. They report that safety and dignity are best maintained by regular staff who "know the file" and the patient's specific pain points. P06: "It's um, you gotta have a nurse knows the file, OK or PCA or wherever. If they don't know you from Jacob, they're going to make mistakes. They're going to assume that you can walk. They're going to assume that you can support yourself, right? So what factors are important to me is that they know what they're doing with me well before they come in ". P02 : "When you have your regular staff, there's no issues, they know how to handle you... when you get staff who are floats or who are temporaries, they come in and then everything's off the table. And then it's an awful experience because they don't know what they're doing ".

Healthcare professionals explicitly state that staffing shortages are the primary barrier to a good transfer experience. They recommend increasing staff levels so residents do not have to wait in an uncomfortable or unsafe situation because a required two-person transfer cannot be performed immediately. S12 said : "At the same time, if I have any recommendation to say: we need more staff. What happens is because when you want to do a transfer, we need two people, your partner. And in the evening you have 11 patients, the other one has 11 patients, we have 22 for two. If you have to transfer this patient by two... you won't finish! [...] We wait! That means, if the patient is wet, and needs to be changed, the patient has to wait. The equipment is good, but we need more staff."

Overall, there is a relational tension where the "No lift policy," which requires two people, is technically sound but is hindered by staffing levels in practice. This leads to a compromise in the dignity and comfort that the policy was designed to protect. Taken together, participants' recommendations converged on four conditions for comfortable, dignified, and safe transfers: situation-specific training, adaptable equipment, structured communication, and stable staffing.

>Discussion

4.1 Revisiting the Conceptualization of Comfort, Dignity, and Safety

The principal finding was that comfort, dignity, and safety were experienced as fundamentally relational rather than solely technological outcomes. Participants consistently described these dimensions as emerging through interactions among residents, healthcare professionals, and transfer technologies. These findings echo the call in the dignity literature to move beyond single-attribute definitions of dignity towards multidimensional, relational accounts (Clancy et al., 2021; Jacobson, 2009).

Comfort is redefined as a holistic state that involves an absence of physical pain and a presence of emotional ease. Patients and residents often express this as being "pain-free," while professionals describe it as a state of "reassurance." Dignity is conceptualized as preserving personhood through privacy and respect. It is maintained through small actions, such as covering the patient or knocking before entering. Consistent with qualitative findings, residents' dignity in long-term care depends on everyday relational gestures rather than formal policy (Fuseini et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2025). Safety is clinically defined as "causing no harm" and experientially defined as the absence of falling, or any event that could cause injury. Taken together, the findings suggest the following preliminary working definition of these concepts as a triad of patient-centered security in the context of technology-assisted transfers: a state in which physical harm is prevented (safety), physical and psychological distress are absent (comfort), and the individual's sense of self and privacy remains intact (dignity).

4.2 The Primacy of the Relational Dimension: A Person-Centered, Dignity-Informed Perspective

Participants rarely attributed dignity to the technology itself. Instead, dignity arose through the way technology was introduced, explained, and used by healthcare professionals. Patients and residents explicitly stated that their comfort and safety depend on staff conduct rather than the mechanical device itself.

The technology is repeatedly described as merely a "machine" or a "tool," while eye contact, verbal check-ins, and patience redeem the inherent vulnerability of being "hoisted in the air."

The Person-Centered Practice Framework (McCormack & McCance, 2006 , 2017) is the primary interpretive lens applied to the findings, situating this relational dimension within a coherent structure. Staff competence and familiarity are repeatedly identified across the comfort and safety themes (Sections 3.2.1 and 3.2.3) and correspond to the framework's prerequisites.

Environmental features, such as closing doors and curtains during transfers and maintaining adequate staffing levels (Sections 3.2.2 and 3.3.4), correspond to the care environment.

Communicative practices, such as verbal check-ins, explanations, and shared pacing of the transfer, correspond to care processes (Sections 3.2.1-3.2.3). Comfort, dignity, and safety occupy the position of person-centered outcomes. They are not properties of technology but rather products of the interaction between prerequisites, environment, and process.

Considering the salience of dignity-related tensions in the dataset, particularly between technology and interpersonal care, findings relating specifically to the dignity theme require further interpretation through a relational dignity framework (Nordenfelt, 2003; Jacobson, 2009; Heggestad et al., 2015). This framework addresses how dignity is threatened or restored through the care relationship, rather than residing solely within the individual or the device (i.e. the technology). The data illustrate this distinction starkly (Section 3.2.2): a technically perfect ceiling lift transfer can result in a total loss of dignity if the operator is impatient or aggressive. This echoes the central claim of the Relational Dignity Framework that dignity is co-produced in interaction and is never guaranteed by equipment or environment alone.

Together, the two frameworks suggest that technology-assisted transfers are best understood as instances of person-centered care delivery, in which the device is one contributing element among several, and where dignity remains fundamentally relational.

4.3 Positioning relative to existing literature

Current literature on transfer technologies primarily focuses on biomechanical criteria and reducing work-related musculoskeletal disorders (WMSDs) among staff (Bernal et al., 2015; Caponecchia et al., 2020; Hegewald et al., 2018; Martimo et al., 2008). Similarly, systematic reviews of safe patient handling interventions evaluate success primarily in terms of staff injury and implementation outcomes rather than patient experience (Wåhlin et al., 2022). While these technical advancements are important, they often overlook the 'melancholy' or 'helplessness' experienced by patients during the process. Even the limited evidence that examines patient-level outcomes generally focuses on proxy measures, such as transfer time or single-item comfort ratings (Alamgir et al., 2009a (Alamgir et al., , 2009b)), rather than providing an in-depth account of the patient's experience. This study shifts the focus from professional biomechanical safety to the patient's experience. It highlights that 'safety' for patients is as much about trust in the operator as it is about strap integrity or battery life. This conclusion is consistent with, and extends, qualitative dignity research in long-term care more broadly (Fuseini et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2025; Šaňáková & Čáp, 2019). This aligns with the wider literature on assistive technology, which has identified a lack of human interaction as a significant barrier to the sustained adoption of technology among older adults, alongside concerns related to privacy, usability, and stigma (Malden et al., 2025).

4.4 Implications for Evaluating Comfort, Dignity, and Safety

These concepts require evaluation using frameworks that incorporate the patient's perspective.

Non-verbal metrics: Professionals described reading facial expressions and body language during transfers (Section 3.2.1), which suggests that evaluation tools should not rely solely on verbal self-report.

Real-time feedback is essential for accurately capturing these cues. Evaluations should not be static; they must include "rolling consent" and real-time pain assessments (e.g. 10/10 scales) to account for daily fluctuations in patient strength or medical stability.

Stakeholder triangulation: Evaluations must involve a 'team approach', integrating the professional's clinical assessment with the patient's subjective feeling of 'reassurance', in line with person-centred outcome indicators that combine clinical and experiential data (McCormack & McCance, 2017).

4.5 Implications for Clinical Practice and Implementation

Implementing transfer technologies involves more than just installing devices. The findings suggest that comfort, dignity and safety are co-constructed through the interaction between technology, healthcare professionals and care recipients. This highlights the importance of person-centered implementation approaches.

Shared decision-making and patient involvement.

Participants consistently emphasized the importance of receiving information, being listened to and being involved in decisions related to transfers. Wherever possible, patients and residents should be actively involved in choosing transfer methods, communicating their preferences, and identifying strategies to improve their comfort and dignity. This ensures that a patient's unique physical, cultural and/or personal requirements are always taken into consideration.

Person-centered implementation. The findings suggest that the success of transfer technologies cannot be evaluated based on technical performance alone. Effective implementation requires attention to relational continuity, familiarity between staff and residents, adequate staffing levels, customized equipment, and organizational processes that support personalized care. Therefore, technology should be implemented as part of a broader person-centered care strategy, rather than as a stand-alone technical solution.

5. Strengths and Limitations of the Study

>Strengths and Limitations of the Study

5.1 Strengths

This study has several methodological and theoretical strengths that advance the current understanding of technology-assisted transfers in long-term and complex continuing care. Firstly, it benefits from a robust dual-stakeholder and multi-site design that integrates the lived experiences of care recipients with the frontline perspectives of a multidisciplinary care team, including registered nurses, personal support workers, and physiotherapists, across multiple facilities. This provides a comprehensive understanding of transfer practices. Secondly, this study demonstrates analytical rigour by applying reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke) and adhering to COREQ-guided reporting. Reflexive journaling, analytical discussions and maintaining an audit trail supported transparency, reflexivity and methodological coherence throughout the research process. Thirdly, in a research domain that has historically centred on caregiver biomechanics and injury prevention, this study gives sustained primacy to the subjective voices of older adults. It illuminates how mechanical transfers directly affect their dignity, autonomy, and emotional security. Finally, the rich contextualized qualitative data provided an in-depth account of the transfer experience, informed a relational interpretation of technology-assisted transfers, highlighting how technologies, interpersonal care practices, and organizational conditions interact to shape comfort, dignity, and safety. This demonstrates how mechanical or technical tools, human relational practices, and organizational constraints intersect in daily care delivery.

5.2 Limitations

Despite these strengths, a few methodological limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, the use of convenience sampling within healthcare facilities located in a single urban region (Ottawa, Ontario) may limit the direct transferability of the findings to rural settings, home care environments or different institutional policy contexts. In addition, as sampling prioritized availability rather than deliberate variation in characteristics such as mobility, diagnosis, or functional status, the range of profiles captured may not be fully representative. Secondly, individuals with cognitive impairment were not included, as the consent process and interview format were not adapted for this population; their perspectives therefore remain to be explored with adapted methods. Thirdly, although the study was conducted in a bilingual region, only English-speaking participants took part in the study, which may have resulted in an underrepresentation of non-English-speaking participants from diverse ethnocultural backgrounds. Additionally, while nurses and personal support workers were heavily represented, physiotherapists, who are responsible for initial transfer assessments, were underrepresented in focus groups. Finally, participants who agreed to take part may have been more attentive to transfer-related issues than the broader population of residents and staff, which may have shaped the perspectives shared. Formal member checking was not undertaken, so participants did not have the opportunity to comment on the interpretation of their accounts. Comparative implementation studies are particularly needed to address the tension between mandatory two-person lift policies and chronic staffing shortages, which result in delayed or suboptimal care.

7. Conclusion

Technology-assisted transfers should be understood not merely as technical procedures but as relational care activities. Comfort, dignity, and safety emerged through the interaction of technology, healthcare professionals, and residents, suggesting that person-centered implementation may be as important as the technology itself.

6.

Implications for Future Research Based on the empirical findings, the Person-Centered Practice and Relational Dignity frameworks and the methodological boundaries of this study, several coherent research priorities emerge. Firstly, future studies should design and evaluate over time "just-in-time" educational interventions that integrate relational etiquette, such as maintaining eye contact, providing stepby-step narration and decoding non-verbal cues of distress, with biomechanical instruction. Secondly, comparative clinical trials are needed to evaluate the effectiveness of specialised transfer accessories, such as pressure-relieving "Comfy slings", customised sling dimensions for individuals with limb loss or bariatric needs, and gentler fabric materials, on skin integrity, shear force reduction and perceived comfort. Finally, implementation science research should investigate how institutional policies, staffing models and shift-to-shift communication tools, such as room-door signage and visual logos, shape transfer outcomes in various care environments, including home care, rural long-term care and acute rehabilitation units.
