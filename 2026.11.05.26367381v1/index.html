Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. Full text

Abstract

Infectious disease experts exchange practical knowledge through clinical discussions, but identifying relevant information across years of conversations is difficult. These discussions often contain differing opinions and emerging observations that are not yet reflected in published evidence. We present EIN-RECAP, an AI-assisted platform developed with Emerging Infections Network (EIN) experts and deployed in beta to summarize and search historical discussions. The system combines retrieval-grounded summarization, citation validation, and hybrid search while distinguishing published evidence from community experience. We evaluated summarization methods across 3,185 threads and conducted blinded expert review on a recent subset. Although a multiagent workflow achieved stronger automated scores, experts favored a simpler generation pipeline and identified repetition, inaccuracies, and overconfident recommendations in the more complex system. This feedback motivated an ensemble that improved discussion coverage and key clinical points on nine high-disagreement threads, although retrieval relevance remained a limitation. Our findings demonstrate the importance of expert involvement in clinical AI development and show why automated evaluation alone is insufficient to guide system selection.

Introduction

In 1992, the Institute of Medicine published a report (Oaks Jr, Shope, and Lederberg 1992) that identified a lack of communication between clinical medicine and public health as a threat to identifying and controlling emerging and reemerging pathogens. At the time, surveillance was only performed for specific diseases. One of the recommendations suggested the creation of multiple sentinel networks for disease surveillance. Consequently, in 1996, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) launched the Emerging Infections Network (EIN) to help detect and respond to infectious disease threats by connecting frontline professionals who work to treat, prevent, and control the spread of emerging and re-emerging infectious diseases to each other and to members of the public health community. Over time, the EIN has evolved into a flexible, nationwide network of ID clinicians with members from all 50 states, the District of Columbia, and Puerto Rico. Members primarily include ID physicians, but the network has expanded to include Phar-mDs and other Advanced Practice Providers (APPs) who work in the field of infectious diseases. In addition to frontline healthcare professionals, the network's listserv also includes CDC investigators and other members of the public health community. As of 2014, it included more than 3,000 members of the infectious-diseases community (Pillai et al. 2014).

Through its restricted-access listserv, EIN members exchange clinical questions and experiences that may not yet be represented in guidelines or peer-reviewed publications. The data used in this work contains 6,085 threads spanning February 11, 1997 , to March 4, 2026, each consisting of an initial post or announcement and its associated replies. Naively querying relevant discussions from the sheer volume of data evolving over three decades faces two primary challenges.

First, finding and bringing together discussions from the distant past requires accounting for differences in terminology and clinical context. Across an archive spanning nearly three decades, the same condition may appear under different names or abbreviations. Keyword matching can miss clinically related threads that use different terminology while returning discussions that share terms but address different questions. A recurring clinical question may also be spread across discussions from several periods. Even after locating a relevant thread, one may need to read a detailed case description and exchange to understand its conclusions. In such scenarios, summaries would help members assess relevance before committing to a full review.

Second, EIN administrators prepare monthly digests that require reviewing discussions and their replies. The work involves clinical interpretation as well as reading, since recommendations may reflect different institution-specific practices. Precomputed thread summaries could provide a consistent starting point for digest preparation. They could also support administrators in tracking recurring concerns and identifying questions that warrant formal surveys. Their value for digest preparation remains to be evaluated.

These needs call for both retrieval and summarization. Appropriately adapted large language models (LLMs) have shown strong performance on several clinical summarization tasks (Van Veen et al. 2024), while query reformula-tion and semantic representations can complement lexical retrieval when related questions use different terminology. Summarizing expert discussions nevertheless requires care: a generated account may omit minority viewpoints, combine recommendations made under incompatible assumptions, or present anecdotal experience as established evidence. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) can ground outputs in identifiable sources, but retrieved material still requires relevance screening and citation validation (Lewis et al. 2020; Zakka et al. 2024). A useful summary must distinguish published evidence from community observations and preserve the uncertainty needed to interpret recommendations.

We present EIN-RECAP, an Emerging Infections Network Retrieval and Evidence-grounded Clinical Analysis Platform for summarizing and searching historical EIN discussions. Its Multi-Agent Summarizer produces structured summaries that record clinical claims and recommendations together with discussion limitations. Claims are labeled according to support from retrieved publications, prior EIN discussions, or neither, and cited identifiers are validated against retrieved sources. Within this framework, Similar-Thread Retrieval supplies related earlier discussions as context, with temporal restrictions intended to prevent later discussions from being used to support an earlier thread.

The Multi-Agent Search Tool retrieves summaries and original discussions from natural-language clinical queries using lexical and semantic matching with clinical terminology normalization. This supports access to differently worded discussions, while the summaries help users decide which complete threads to review. Summarization and evidence validation occur offline, allowing the resulting structured outputs to be reused for online search and potential administrative workflows. Developed in collaboration with EIN experts, EIN-RECAP is deployed in beta within the network.

Our primary contributions are as follows: • We introduce EIN-RECAP, an applied AI platform for searching historical EIN discussions through structured summaries linked to original threads and supporting evidence.

• We integrate evidence-grounded summarization and retrieval of related earlier discussions with terminologyaware search, separating offline summary generation and evidence validation from online retrieval. The precomputed summaries support relevance assessment and provide reusable material for archive review.

• We evaluate summarization pipelines on 3,185 threads with at least three replies and conduct blinded expert assessment on 30 recent threads, where the pipeline with the strongest ROUGE and BERTScore results received the lowest expert ratings. Expert feedback informed an ensemble redesign that improved discussion coverage and key clinical point scores on nine high-disagreement threads, while publication relevance remained a limitation across pipelines.

Proposed System Design

Our overall framework consists of two primary components, namely the Multi-Agent Summarizer and Multi-Agent Search Tool. While the search tool enables access to the processed discussion archive, the primary methodological contribution of our framework is the Multi-Agent Summarizer. We will elaborate on the two components in detail next.

Multi-Agent Summarizer

EIN threads often contain information that typically comes from varied perspectives from members with different levels of expertise. A single language model's response may omit less prominent viewpoints or may sometimes highlight clinician experience as widely accepted clinical evidence even when it may simply be subjective opinions about a clinical topic. To address these limitations, our proposed frameworks assign distinct responsibilities to specialized agents and aggregate all the important information in producing a grounded summary. We will describe the two versions of this part of the overall framework, with a focus on Version 1 (V1) in the main text, while Version 2 (V2) will be described in detail in the Appendix.

Version 1 (V1)

V1 is a retrieval-grounded summarization pipeline with explicit evidence tracking and deterministic post-generation validation. We adopt the general retrievalaugmented generation (RAG) framework (Lewis et al. 2020) with a single generation agent, chosen deliberately for this deployment, since fewer model calls make an error attributable to a specific stage, retrieval or generation. Additionally, as prior work (Xiong et al. 2024) has demonstrated the potential value of RAG in medical applications, we curate the retrieval corpus instead of searching the unrestricted literature. V1 consists of five connected modules, namely PubMed Retrieval, Similar Thread Retrieval, Grounded Generation, and Output and Provenance Recording. The complete processing path and the outputs at each stage are shown in Figure 1. PubMed retrieval establishes the available scientific evidence, similar-thread retrieval contributes historical community experience, grounded generation synthesizes the two without collapsing their roles, deterministic validation checks the resulting attributions, and provenance recording preserves the complete processing history. Preferred PubMed Retrieval Module: This module provides the information from published works related to the thread being summarized. Instead of retrieving from unrestricted biomedical literature (Fiorini et al. 2018), we first construct a local PubMed collection from a set of journals selected in consultation with our EIN collaborators. The detailed list of the journals is presented in Table 6 in the Appendix. This decision was made to obtain information from credible and relevant sources. However, this approach comes with a tradeoff, wherein potentially relevant research articles from venues outside the preferred list will not be included.

For each thread, the query builder (LLM agent) generates a set of retrieval queries. Additionally, we limit the supplied context (i.e., number of PubMed articles), as LLMs are known to use information inconsistently as the input context becomes longer (Liu et al. 2024). The resulting queries are then used to search a vector database constructed from the preferred PubMed collection. For real-time summarization, for threads originating in year Y i , eligible PubMed articles are restricted to publications from years Y i -11 through Y i -1. This, in turn, means that the works published after the discussion cannot be used to explain or retrospectively strengthen its claims. The resulting set is then passed to the generation model. Similar-Thread Retrieval Module: Although PubMed provides access to peer-reviewed evidence, the published works may not address time-sensitive clinical questions that arise during an emerging outbreak. Clinical observations and studies typically require some time before being indexed into PubMed. In this situation, the experience of EIN members is extremely crucial as they have real-time knowledge of emerging conditions and topics through observation (Pillai et al. 2014), and prior discussions between EIN members in threads can provide a valuable source of clinical information that may not yet be represented in the published literature. To account for this scenario, this module retrieves semantically similar historical EIN threads and provides them to the summarization model as a separate source of communitypractice evidence. Similar to PubMed retrieval, the temporal restriction prevents information from later discussions from being introduced into summaries of earlier threads. Grounded Generation Module: This module synthesizes three explicitly separated inputs, namely the resolved text of the current thread, the eligible PubMed articles, and the eligible historical EIN discussions. The module's output contains a concise thread summary, key clinical points, evidencesupported claims, community-supported observations, uncertain claims, recommendations, limitations, cited PubMed PMIDs, and cited EIN thread identifiers.

Evidence-supported claims must cite one or more PubMed articles supplied by the retrieval module. Similarly, community-supported observations must cite eligible historical EIN threads. Statements that cannot be adequately supported by either source are assigned to the uncertain-claims category. The framework also computes summarylevel grounding and retrieval-confidence metrics.

These serve two goals: 1: They make grounding failures quantifiable during evaluation. 2: They provide diagnostic signals for identifying whether a weak summary originated from insufficient retrieval or from generation behavior. Output and Provenance Recording: The final module converts the validated outputs from the individual modules into two outputs. First, the module creates a human-readable markdown summary. Second, the system records metadata that was used to generate the final human-readable artifacts. This allows us to audit the specific modules of the overall framework in case of inaccuracies.

Version 2 (V2) Instead of generating a single summary directly from the thread, V2 first produces multiple candidate interpretations of the same discussion, evaluates them, and synthesizes the final structured output.

It has a set of perspective-specific Generation Agents. Each agent receives the same discussion thread, but is assigned a different clinical objective. The agent outputs are then passed to a Reflection Agent, which evaluates whether each candidate is faithful to the source conversation and other aspects.

In addition, the Proximity Agent identifies related prior EIN discussions and detects similarity among generated recommendations, and the Ranking Agent compares recommendations according to their relevance. These comparisons are aggregated to produce an ordered recommendation list. Finally, an Evolution Agent refines the highest-ranked recommendations, and a Meta-review Agent integrates the outputs of the preceding agents into the final structured summary. The overall framework is presented in Figure 6 in the Appendix.

Multi-Agent Search Tool

The Multi-Agent Search Tool provides an interface for retrieving historical EIN discussions from the structured summaries produced by the Multi-Agent Summarizer. The tool accepts a natural-language clinical query and returns archived discussions whose summarized content and original thread text are most relevant to the query. In the current implementation, the user interface supports both direct search and hybrid search. Direct search uses the literal query terms, while hybrid search applies LLM-assisted planning and multi-view search.

During offline index construction, each generated summary is parsed into structured fields. These fields are combined into a searchable document representation. The index stores tokenized text for BM25 retrieval (Robertson and Zaragoza 2009), and dense embeddings for semantic retrievalAt query time, hybrid search first invokes a local Ollama-based query-planning agent that normalizes clinical terminology, corrects likely misspellings, extracts clinical terms, and generates multiple short query variants. If the agent is unavailable or returns invalid output, the system falls back to deterministic local extraction and query-variant generation. Queries without plausible medical or infectiousdisease content are rejected to avoid returning misleading nearest-neighbor results.

Accepted queries are expanded using a locally compiled MeSH vocabulary derived from PubMed. The retrieval pipeline then scores indexed discussions using BM25 lexical matching and dense similarity over stored embeddings. Scores are combined using a reciprocal-rank-style fusion procedure, with a small cross-query boost for documents that rank well across multiple query variants. A PubMed Best Match-style scoring layer incorporates evidence from MeSH heading overlap, field-weighted term matches, phrase matches, and query-term proximity across titles, summaries, clinical concepts, recommendations, claims, and original thread text.

The search module is intentionally separated from the Multi-Agent Summarizer. This separation allows expensive summarization and evidence organization to be performed once per archived discussion, while EIN members can later retrieve relevant prior discussions through fast and explainable search signals.

Discussion coverage: Does the LLM-generated summary accurately cover all important parts of the discussions present in the original thread? • Q2. Key clinical points: Do the LLM-generated Key Clinical Points appropriately provide the overall clinical points mentioned in the Thread? • Q3. Recommendation quality: Are the LLM-generated recommendations reasonable based on the contents of the original thread? • Q4. Thus, adding the third annotator improved the robustness of the analysis on disputed cases but did not reverse the main finding that the V1 systems were preferred over DeepSeek V2 for expert-rated summary quality.

Setup We analyzed 32 evaluator feedback submissions collected while UIowa EIN member users compared the new search tool against the existing EIN search. Each submission included satisfaction and relevance ratings on a five-point scale, together with a comparative preference score: 0 indicated preference for our tool, 50 indicated uncertainty, and 100 indicated preference for the existing tool. We also reviewed written feedback for recurring strengths and limitations. The search Tool's UI is displayed in Figure 2.

Results

Users preferred our tool in 26 of 32 evaluations (81.3%); the remaining 6 evaluations (18.8%) were uncertain, and no evaluator preferred the existing tool. Both satisfaction and relevance ratings were strongly concentrated at the maximum score: 29 of 32 evaluations (90.6%) assigned a rating of 5/5 for each measure. Mean satisfaction was 4.78/5, and mean relevance was 4.81/5.

Evaluators consistently reported that the new search retrieved more clinically relevant threads, including older discussions and posts whose primary topic was not present in the thread title. They also praised its handling of abbreviations, terminology variants, and misspellings, including variants of C. difficile and misspellings of "recurrent." Direct search was generally described as focused and fast, whereas hybrid search added breadth and clinical context but was occasionally slower and overly broad.

The principal failure case was the query "ulnar wrist pain," for which generic terms such as "pain" and "syndrome" appeared to outweigh clinically important concepts such as "ulnar nerve" and "nerve entrapment". Based on the general feedback, the existing search tool performed better in cases where the input search query matched the headings of the search results. While this functionality was not implemented, we plan to integrate this mechanism into our UI and search systems in the future.

Lessons Learned and Future Direction

Our findings indicate the value of developing clinical AI systems in close collaboration with domain experts. For EIN-RECAP, this collaboration helped us identify differences between what automated evaluations inflate and what clinicians consider useful when reviewing expert discussions.

Our most important lesson was that human expert supervision should be part of the design and evaluation of systems that summarize open-ended clinical conversations. These discussions contain differing opinions, practical experience, and unresolved questions whose importance cannot be reliably captured by automated scores alone. For instance, DeepSeek V2 performed strongly under traditional metrics and LLM-based evaluation, but expert reviewers identified repetitive recommendations, occasional inaccuracies, and overconfident statements. Our findings showed that adding agents and refinement stages does not necessarily produce summaries that better serve clinical readers.

Expert feedback also directly informed our subsequent design. It motivated an ensemble that combined information from the candidate summaries while preserving distinct clinical points.

Future work will continue to address needs identified through collaboration with EIN members, including the following: • Improving PubMed retrieval through expert relevance feedback and checking whether cited evidence supports the associated claims.

• Expanding expert evaluation to a broader range of discussions and separating the effects of ensemble aggregation from those of a larger underlying model.

• Developing a feedback process through which members can flag omissions, misleading recommendations, and irrelevant evidence to guide system updates.

• There are some clear directions of improving the search tool by integrating a date filter functionality as well as improving the hybrid search to adaptively expand the search radius for terms. Additionally, based on the feedback received from the users during the beta version launch of the tool, there are some User Interface fixes that need to be implemented.

• Based on the feedback that was received during the usage of the search tool, future work will aim to ask for userspecified 'knobs' to control the size of the summary to be displayed in the search results.

Conclusion

In this work, we present EIN-RECAP, an AI-assisted platform deployed in beta for summarizing and searching infectious-disease expert conversations. Developed in collaboration with EIN experts, the system connects structured summaries with historical discussions and published evidence to help members access knowledge accumulated across the network. Our evaluation showed that stronger automated scores did not consistently correspond to higher expert-rated summary quality, highlighting the importance of expert involvement in system design and evaluation. Expert feedback motivated an ensemble that improved discussion coverage and key clinical points on a targeted evaluation set, while identifying retrieval relevance as a continuing challenge. In conclusion, this work demonstrates how collaboration with domain experts can guide the development of AI tools for accessing clinical community knowledge and motivates further evaluation during routine use.

Detailed Description of Version V2 of the Multi-Agent Summarizer Perspective-Specific Generation Agents:

The Generation stage constructs multiple candidate interpretations of the same discussion. Instead of repeated application of an identical summarization prompt, we employ three agents with complementary objectives: 1. Factual Generation Agent: This agent focuses on the primary clinical question, patient characteristics, diagnostic findings, treatments, outcomes, published studies, and standard management practices. It emphasizes information supported by the original conversation and retrieved scientific literature.

2. Counterfactual Generation Agent:

This agent examines alternative diagnoses, competing explanations, contraindications, adverse effects, treatment failures, and assumptions that may not be fully supported by the thread. Its purpose is to recover clinically relevant minority viewpoints that may be omitted when only the dominant interpretation is summarized.

3. Community Generation Agent:

This agent tries to gather evidence from the corresponding replies and discussions made by EIN members in the current thread. It focuses on implementation challenges, institutional differences, antimicrobial stewardship, follow-up practices, and areas of agreement or disagreement. These observations may be useful even when they are not directly supported by formal studies. Before generating a candidate summary, each Generation Agent proposes a bounded set of search queries based on the clinical conditions, pathogens, treatments, diagnostic procedures, and uncertainties identified in the thread. These queries are passed to a deterministic retrieval module.

Retreival Module:

The retrieval module searches PubMed and approved journal sources, applies the predefined journal and publication-date restrictions, and returns the eligible articles to the Generation Agents. For a thread originating on date D i , only articles published on or before D i -1 year are considered. Articles must also satisfy the configured source and similarity requirements. When the available publication metadata is insufficient to establish temporal eligibility, the article is conservatively excluded.

The module also retrieves semantically related prior EIN threads. Only discussions that precede the current thread and satisfy the configured similarity threshold are retained, thereby preventing information from future conversations from influencing the summary of an earlier thread. Finally, each agent produces a candidate containing a thread summary, key clinical concepts, supported and uncertain claims, and clinical recommendations. This perspective-specific design is intended to increase content coverage.

Reflection Agent:

The Reflection Agent independently reviews every generated candidate. It evaluates whether the candidate accurately represents the source conversation, whether its factual claims are supported, whether clinically important information has been omitted, and whether uncertainty is communicated appropriately. The agent distinguishes among three categories of claims: 1. Evidence-supported claims: Claims supported by the text retrieved from the retrieved similar PubMed articles.

2. Community-supported claims:

Claims grounded in the current discussion or in an eligible earlier EIN thread. 3. Uncertain claims: Claims that cannot be verified, are supported only indirectly, are disputed within the conversation, or depend on missing patient information.

After model-based reflection, citation identifiers are validated deterministically. If a generated citation does not correspond to a retrieved article or eligible historical thread, the associated claim is moved to the uncertainty category. The Reflection Agent also evaluates each candidate according to clinical completeness, evidence grounding, safety, usefulness, and treatment of uncertainty.

Proximity Agent:

The Proximity Agent measures semantic similarity among the current thread, earlier discussions, candidate summaries, and generated recommendations. At the thread level, it identifies earlier discussions that address related clinical questions. At the recommendation level, it detects recommendations that describe similar clinical actions. This results in improved grounding and diversity of the information. The Proximity Agent supplies this information to the Ranking Agent so that closely related recommendations can be compared directly, and the final list is not dominated by paraphrases of the same action.

Ranking Agent:

The Ranking Agent evaluates recommendations generated by all three Generation Agents. Recommendations are compared pairwise according to their relevance to the clinical question, faithfulness to the discussion, supporting evidence, clinical safety, feasibility, novelty, and usefulness. The pairwise outcomes are aggregated using an Elo-style rating procedure (Elo 1978). For two recommendations with ratings ρ a and ρ b , the expected preference for recommendation r a is P (r a ≻ r b ) = 1 1+10 (ρ b -ρa )/400 . After each comparison, the ratings are updated according to the observed preference. The final recommendation list is ordered using the resulting Elo scores.

Evolution Agent:

The Evolution Agent refines highly ranked recommendations using feedback from the Reflection, Proximity, and Ranking Agents. It may merge redundant recommendations, clarify the intended patient population, preserve clinically important qualifications, simplify difficult-to-implement statements, or revise recommendations judged to be overly broad or insuf-ficiently grounded.

The agent is restricted to information contained in the original discussion and retrieved evidence. Its purpose is to improve the clarity, specificity, and feasibility of existing recommendations rather than introduce new clinical advice.

Meta-Review Agent:

The Meta-review Agent integrates the outputs of the preceding agents into the final structured summary. It receives the original conversation, perspective-specific candidates, reflection critiques, validated claims, retrieved articles, related historical discussions, recommendation rankings, and evolved recommendations.

The candidate with the strongest reflection score provides the primary thread summary, clinical concepts, validated claims, and limitations. However, the final recommendation section is constructed from highly ranked recommendations pooled across all Generation Agents. This allows a useful recommendation to contribute to the final output even when the complete candidate from which it originated is not selected.

DeepSeek V2 also had more incorrect or hallucinated comments (8) than DeepSeek V1 (4) or OLMo V1 (5), and was the only model marked for overconfident or dogmatic phrasing. These qualitative patterns align with the normalized score analysis, suggesting that DeepSeek V2's lower expert preference was driven less by broad topical failure and more by repetitive recommendations, occasional overreach, and clinically important inaccuracies.

Figure 6: The V2 summarization pipeline. Three generation agents process the same thread in parallel under different objectives, namely factual, counterfactual, and community practice, so that clinically relevant minority interpretations survive into the candidate set. The Reflection Agent reviews each candidate for faithfulness, completeness, and treatment of uncertainty, while the Proximity Agent supplies related prior threads and detects near-duplicate recommendations. Recommendations pooled across all three generation agents are ordered by the Ranking Agent using pairwise Elo comparison and refined by the Evolution Agent. The Meta-review Agent then combines the candidate with the strongest reflection score, which supplies the summary prose and clinical concepts, with the ranked recommendation list, so a recommendation can reach the output even when the candidate it came from was not selected. Citation validation remains deterministic, as in V1.
