To fix Exact P-Values

What we found

Show details

Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 3 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Cortical surface area (cSA) is associated with intelligence and cognition, yet structural MRI studies of neurodegeneration in Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD) have emphasized quantification of cortical thickness (cTH) and volume, with very limited evaluation of cSA. We examined whether cSA of AD-relevant brain regions provide diagnostic, prognostic, and biological information beyond cTH and volume across 5,430 participants (3,124 cognitively unimpaired; 1,634 mild cognitive impairment [MCI]; 672 ADRD) from three aging and dementia cohorts: the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center, Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative, and Carotids and Minds study. In cross-sectional analyses, cSA and cTH showed weak correlation, and lower cSA was independently associated with greater odds of MCI and ADRD after adjustment for cTH and covariates. Participants with both low cSA and low cTH had greater risk of MCI and ADRD compared to low cSA, cTH, or volume alone. In longitudinal models, baseline cSA predicted incident ADRD independent from cTH, and combined cSA/cTH models outperformed cSA, cTH, or volume alone for predicting incident ADRD over a mean follow-up of 4.3 years.

Complementing these findings, proteomics analyses revealed distinct signatures associated with cSA compared to cTH. Overall, these findings support cSA as a clinically informative and biologically distinct dimension of AD-related cortical vulnerability.

Introduction

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is characterized by neurodegeneration and a progressive decline in cognitive function, with structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) playing a central role in quantifying disease-relevant brain atrophy. 1 MRI-derived cortical thickness (cTH) and volume are widely used markers of neurodegeneration in AD and related dementias (ADRD), 2 providing non-invasive measures of cortical atrophy. Cortical thinning within AD-vulnerable regions commonly referred to as the AD-Signature 3 has been shown to distinguish prodromal AD from cognitively unimpaired controls, identify individuals with AD-like biomarker profiles, and predict risk of ADRD-related cognitive decline. [4] [5] [6] These findings have established cTH as a robust MRI marker of ADRD-related neurodegeneration and have reinforced a broader tendency to conceptualize cortical atrophy primarily through thinning.

Cortical surface area (cSA) has received comparatively less attention in aging and dementia research, despite cortical volume being a composite measure of both cTH and cSA, two metrics reflecting distinct dimensions of cortical morphology. cSA has been linked to cognition and intelligence across the lifespan [7] [8] [9] and has been shown in some populations to have stronger influence on cortical volume than cTH. 10, 11 Developmental models link cortical surface expansion to progenitor proliferation and tangential expansion of cortical columns, whereas cTH reflects radial growth and layer formation within the cortical sheet. 12, 13 Twin and genetic studies of adolescent participants further demonstrate that cSA and cTH are highly heritable but genetically distinct. 10, 14, 15 Together, these findings indicate that volume combines separable sources of biological variation, and evaluating these dimensions separately may provide a more precise framework for interpreting cortical morphometry in brain aging than volume or cTH alone. In the context of ADRD, we hypothesized that cSA . may reflect a clinically relevant dimension of cortical vulnerability that is not captured by thinning.

Although previous studies suggest that surface-based morphometrics may capture information not fully represented by cTH, [16] [17] [18] [19] the evidence remains limited and inconsistent.

A previous cross-sectional study showed that cSA and cTH differ across cognitively unimpaired controls (CU), mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and AD groups but was limited by sample size and did not test whether cSA adds independent diagnostic or prognostic value beyond cTH or volume. 16 Longitudinal studies reported distinct cSA, cTH, and volume atrophy patterns in early AD 17 and across diagnostic groups 18, 19 but were limited to short follow-up intervals, small sample size, whole-brain regional assessment, and did not determine whether cSA predicts clinical diagnosis or incident ADRD independent of other established morphometric measures. Furthermore, it has not been established whether cSA is independent of cTH, whether combined cSA/cTH phenotypes stratify ADRD risk, or whether cSA adds diagnostic or prognostic value beyond cTH or volume. Beyond this, whether cSA and cTH are associated with distinct molecular/cellular pathways remains understudied in neurodegeneration. Such evidence could help determine whether these measures reflect distinct biological pathways in aging and neurodegeneration.

To address these gaps, we examined cSA, cTH, and volume of AD-relevant brain regions using 5,430 participants drawn from three cohorts: [20] [21] [22] [23] [24] the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center (NACC), Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI), and

University of Arizona Carotids and Minds study (CAM). The AD-Signature and Default

Mode Network (DMN) regions were selected as they have been related to early cortical changes associated with ADRD. 3, 25, 26 Through cross-sectional and longitudinal evaluation, we assessed whether cSA provides clinically relevant prognostic and diagnostic information independent of cTH, and whether their combined evaluation provides clinically meaningful . information beyond cTH or volume alone. We further assessed whether cSA and cTH were associated with overlapping or distinct proteomic signatures in ADNI, providing molecular and cellular context for their associations with ADRD-related outcomes. This multi-cohort study aims to determine whether cSA represents a distinct and clinically informative dimension of cortical vulnerability across the ADRD spectrum. The original goal of ADNI was to test whether serial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), positron emission tomography (PET), other biological markers, and clinical and neuropsychological assessment can be combined to measure the progression of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and early Alzheimer's disease (AD). The National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center (NACC) was established in 1999 by the National Institute on Aging (NIA) and serves as the centralized data repository for the ADRCs. NACC and ADNI were used as large multisite aging and AD cohorts to evaluate cross-sectional diagnostic associations and progression to incident AD. 20, 21 CAM was included as a single-centre, vascular-enriched cohort to evaluate demographic and vascular disease associations with . morphometry in participants enriched for extracranial carotid atherosclerotic disease (ECAD) and vascular risk. [22] [23] [24] For NACC and ADNI, participants >50 years with at least one available structural MRI were included (Supplemental Fig. 1). Exclusions included neurological or psychiatric disease/disorder other than MCI or ADRD, serious or unstable medical illness, and missing demographic or diagnostic data. In NACC, MRI visits were required to be within 180 days of clinical diagnosis. Baseline cognitive status was categorized as cognitively unimpaired (CU), mild cognitive impairment (MCI), or ADRD according to cohort-specific diagnostic procedures including NACC Uniform Data Set procedures 27, 28 and ADNI diagnostic criteria. 21 In NACC, impaired-not-MCI and MCI groups were combined, and the ADRD group included both AD-dementia and vascular dementia. Longitudinal analyses were restricted to ADNI participants who were CU or MCI at baseline with at least one follow-up MRI assessment (Supplemental Fig. 2). In ADNI, status of hyperlipidaemia, smoking history, and diabetes were extracted from medical history text fields. In-depth ADNI protocols can be found at https://adni.loni.usc.edu/help-faqs/adni-documentation/.

Materials and methods

Participants

The CAM study recruited participants aged 50-85 through cardiology and vascular surgery clinics across Tucson, AZ for an ongoing study of vascular disease association with dementia risk. [22] [23] [24] Inclusion criteria include ECAD and/or at least two vascular risk factors (hypertension, hyperlipidaemia, history of smoking, or diabetes). ECAD was defined as having a percent carotid artery stenosis greater than or equal to 50%, determined using the North America Symptomatic Carotid Endarterectomy Trial criteria and time-of-flight carotid MRI as described in a previous study. 22 Study exclusion criteria included a clinical diagnosis of ADRD or other neurological disorders, stroke within six months, major medical illness, or major depression. Participants with large cortical infarcts were further excluded from this study (Supplemental Fig. 3). CAM does not include clinical/cognitive staging. All . participants in the current study provided written informed consent in accordance with institutional review board guidelines.

MRI Data Acquisition & Processing

Structural T1-weighted MRI data were obtained according to cohort-specific protocols. ADNI MRI was acquired across ADNI phases using standardized protocols at 1.5T and 3T; 29 both original and pre-processed images (including gradient distortion and intensity inhomogeneity correction) were utilized. 30 Both 1.5T and 3T MRI from ADNI were included to increase sample size. NACC MRI was acquired at participating Alzheimer's Disease Research Centers; 31 imaging used from the NACC cohort was acquired at 3T and not preprocessed. CAM imaging was acquired on a single 3T scanner (Magnetom Skyra, Siemens Healthineers) using a previously described protocol. 32 Cortical reconstruction and morphometric estimation were performed using FreeSurfer v7 using the standard pipeline with a single T1-weighted image as input. 33 NACC morphometrics were derived from T1-weighted images by NACC using FreeSurfer v7.2, ADNI morphometrics by the Banner Alzheimer's Institute (Phoenix, AZ) research group using FreeSurfer v7, and CAM morphometrics locally using FreeSurfer v7.3. Primary measures included cSA, cTH and volume. cSA and volume were computed as bilateral region sums, and cTH as the bilateral mean.

The primary region of interest was an AD-Signature cortical composite, defined using FreeSurfer Desikan-Killiany atlas regions implicated in AD neurodegeneration (Figure 1A): entorhinal cortex, inferior temporal cortex, middle temporal cortex, temporal pole, inferior parietal cortex, superior parietal cortex, praecuneus and posterior cingulate cortex. 3, 34 Temporal pole was unavailable in NACC and omitted from that cohort's composite.

Secondary analyses using a Default Mode Network (DMN) composite were also performed given established vulnerability in ADRD. 25, 26 cSA and volume were adjusted for total intracranial volume (TIV) within each cohort using residualization with CU participants serving as reference. cTH was not TIV-adjusted.

Outlier detection was performed per cohort and metric: Whole brain cSA or cTH values exceeding ±3 standard deviations from the cohort mean were flagged, and regional morphometric values were reviewed to identify biologically plausible variation, data entry errors, or processing artifacts. Participants with implausible or missing morphometric values were excluded.

CSF Proteomics

We obtained SomaScan 7k panel (v4.1) 35 data for 7008 proteins which were simultaneously assayed in CSF from 694 ADNI participants with available cSA and cTH measurements at their baseline MRI. The SomaScan assay utilizes modified single-stranded DNA aptamers to bind protein targets with high sensitivity and specificity, which are quantified by DNA microarray. 36, 37 Proteins are measured in relative fluorescent units (RFU). Initial data normalization was performed by Somalogic, 35 while assay and quality-control procedures were performed by the Cruchaga lab at WashU as previously described. 35

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were conducted independently in NACC, ADNI and CAM. Correlations between cSA and cTH were assessed within each cohort using Spearman correlation.

Demographic associations with cSA and cTH were evaluated using multivariable linear regression models. Association of cSA and cTH with MCI and ADRD were evaluated using cross-sectional multivariable logistic regression comparing CU vs. MCI and ADRD as outcomes. All models were adjusted for age, sex, years of education, race, ethnicity, and apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 carrier status; ADNI models additionally included magnetic field strength and image pre-processing status. β estimates for all regression models were standardized. In models estimating risk associated with lower morphometrics, measures were multiplied by -1 so that odds/hazard ratios greater than 1 reflected higher odds/hazard per 1standard deviation lower morphometric value. Secondary models were performed using the DMN as a regional composite, and ADNI sensitivity analyses were performed restricting the sample to participants whose morphometrics were derived from 3T, un-processed imaging.

To evaluate cross-sectional cSA/cTH phenotypes, NACC and ADNI cohort-specific lower-quartile thresholds were used to classify participants as normal cSA/normal cTH, normal cSA/low cTH, low cSA/normal cTH, and low cSA/low cTH, with "low" defining values below the threshold, and "normal" defining values above the lower-quartile threshold. This was performed instead of using the highest quartile to define "normal" in order to maximize sample size. Similar was done to delineate high/low volume. Multivariable logistic regressions were used to evaluate the association of morphometric phenotypes with MCI and ADRD compared to CU using normal cSA/normal cTH as the reference factor. Additional models were assessed exchanging the reference factor to assess differences between phenotype groups. To assess cross-sectional discrimination across phenotypes, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses were performed for CU vs. ADRD.

Associations of baseline cSA and cTH to incident ADRD were assessed using Cox proportional hazards models among ADNI participants who were CU or MCI at baseline. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using scaled Schoenfeld residuals for each model. Time-to-event was defined as time from baseline MRI to first observed ADRD diagnosis. Participants who did not progress to ADRD were censored at the last available MRI follow-up visit date. All Cox models included age, sex, education, race, ethnicity, APOE ε4 carrier status, magnetic field strength and imaging preprocessing status as covariates.

Nested Cox models were used to assess whether cSA provided incremental prognostic information beyond cTH and whether decomposed morphometry outperformed cortical volume. Likelihood ratio tests compared nested models including covariates alone (age, sex, education, race, ethnicity, APOE ε4 carriage, magnet strength, image preprocessing status), covariates plus cSA, covariates plus cTH, covariates plus volume, covariates plus cSA and cTH, and covariates plus cSA, cTH, and volume. Model performance was summarized using Akaike information criterion (AIC) and Harrel C-index.

Optimized cSA, cTH, and volume cutoffs were derived within the longitudinal ADNI cohort using ROC curve analyses among participants who were CU/MCI at baseline. For each measure, ROC curves were used to evaluate the discrimination of incident ADRD.

Optimal thresholds were defined using Youden's J statistic. Participants were classified as having normal/low cSA, cTH, and volume, based on the threshold maximizing Youden's J statistic. Binary cSA/cTH classifications were combined into four phenotypes: normal cSA/normal cTH, normal cSA/low cTH, low cSA/normal cTH, and low cSA/low cTH which were used in subsequent survival analyses. Kaplan-Meier curves were generated for normal/low cSA, normal/low cTH, normal/low volume, and combined cSA/cTH phenotype groups, with differences assessed using log-rank tests. Statistical significance was defined as P<.05 in all analyses. Testing was performed in R version 4.4.3 using the following packages: survival, survminer. 38, 39 ADNI CSF proteomics data were analysed using multiple linear regression models adjusted for age, sex, race, ethnicity, and MRI magnet strength to identify differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) associated with cSA and cTH. DEPs were visualized using volcano plots and protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks. PPI networks were constructed using STRING (12.0). PPIs had a minimum confidence of >0.4 (cSA) and >0.7 (cTH).

Overrepresentation analysis (ORA) was performed with DEPs as input and 7008 SomaScan proteins as background to identify pathways associated with cSA and cTH using ShinyGO (0.85.1). 40 The Reactome and Gene Ontology (GO) gene sets were queried. 37 Pathways containing 10-500 proteins were considered significantly enriched. All P values were corrected for multiple comparisons using Benjamini Hochberg False Discovery Method (FDR, P <0.05).

Results

Participants 2,993 NACC, 2,255 ADNI, and 182 CAM participants were included in the study (Supplemental Table 1). Participants were older adults with mean ages of 71±8 years in NACC, 73±7 in ADNI, and 72±7 in CAM. Female sex was 63% in NACC, and 47% in both ADNI and CAM. APOE ε4 allele was present in 38% of NACC, 46% of ADNI, and 27% of CAM with available genotype data. Vascular risk factors (hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidaemia and smoking) had greater prevalence in CAM, consistent with its vascularenriched study design. NACC included a larger proportion of CU participants (CU=2,094; MCI=605; ADRD=294), while ADNI had a larger proportion of MCI and ADRD (CU=848; MCI=1,029; ADRD=378) (Supplemental Table 1). The CAM study did not involve clinical/cognitive staging.

Associations of cSA and cTH with Demographic and Genetic Factors

In CU participants, AD-Signature cSA and cTH (Figure 1A-B) showed associations with demographic and genetic factors across cohorts (Figure 1C-E). Older age was consistently associated with significantly lower cSA and cTH while education, ethnicity and APOE ε4 did not show any significant associations. Non-White racial groups were associated with lower cSA and cTH in both NACC and ADNI. Male sex was associated with greater cSA in ADNI.

1.5T magnetic field strength and pre-processed imaging (including gradient distortion and intensity inhomogeneity correction) were associated with lower cSA in ADNI (Supplemental Fig. 4). Field strength and image preprocessing status were consistent across NACC and CAM and was thus not assessed. Full demographic and neuroimaging covariate associations are shown in Supplemental Figure 1. Similar demographic findings were observed in the DMN-associated brain regions and for the ADNI sensitivity analysis restricted to 3T, unprocessed imaging (Supplemental Fig. 5 6).

The correlations between cSA and cTH were near zero in the full cohorts of NACC (r=0.018, P<0.001), ADNI (r=-0.041, P=0.054) and CAM (r=-0.024, P=0.75) (Figure 1F-H).

Similar was observed in the DMN (Supplemental Fig. 7). Among CU, MCI and ADRD participants, APOE ε4 allele presence was associated with lower cTH and volume in NACC and ADNI, and lower cSA only in ADNI (Supplemental Fig. 8).

Analyses in CU participants showed no consistent association between common vascular risk factors and cSA across NACC, ADNI, and CAM (Supplemental Fig. 9). Hypertension was weakly associated with lower cTH in NACC and ADNI (β=-0.12 and β=-0.14, respectively; both P<0.05). In CAM, carotid artery disease was associated with lower cSA (β=-0.52; P<0.01) but not cTH; carotid disease was not evaluated in NACC and ADNI. None of the vascular risk factors showed significant independent association with cSA or cTH in CAM.

Associations of cSA and cTH with Cognitive Status

Mean cSA and cTH differed significantly across cognitive status (CU, MCI, ADRD) in NACC and ADNI (all P<0.001; Figure 2A-B). In multivariable logistic regression models, lower cSA was associated with higher odds ratio (OR) of MCI and ADRD independent of cTH and other covariates (age, sex, education years, race, ethnicity, APOE ε4 presence, and magnetic field strength and image preprocessing status in ADNI) in both NACC (MCI: OR, 1.53; ADRD: OR, 2.49; P<0.001) and ADNI (MCI: OR,1.48; ADRD: OR, 2.14; P<0.001) (Figure 2C-D). Lower cTH was also independently associated with higher odds of MCI and ADRD in NACC (MCI: OR, 1.67; ADRD: OR, 6.18; P<0.001) and ADNI (MCI: OR, 2.28; ADRD: OR, 9.25; P<0.001). Full model results are provided in Supplemental Fig. 10. Similar associations were observed for DMN morphometry, and associations remained significant in the ADNI sensitivity analysis restricted to 3T unprocessed imaging (Supplemental Figure 11 -13).

Associations of Morphometric Phenotypes with Cognitive Status -Normal vs. Low cSA and cTH

Cross-sectional combined cSA/cTH phenotypes were defined by lower-quartile thresholds in NACC and ADNI (Supplemental Fig. 14). In both cohorts, ADRD participants made up a majority of the low cSA/low cTH phenotype (53% for both). Phenotype distributions within the DMN brain regions and ADNI sensitivity sub-cohort also followed this pattern (Supplemental Fig. 15 16). In adjusted logistic regression models, low cSA/low cTH, low cSA/normal cTH and normal cSA/low cTH were associated with higher odds of MCI and ADRD relative to the normal cSA/normal cTH group (Figure 3A-B). Low cSA/low cTH showed significantly greater odds than either isolated low cSA or low cTH, with the strongest odds of clinical impairment across all phenotypes in NACC (MCI: OR, 4.47; ADRD: OR, 86.58; P<0.001) and ADNI (MCI: OR, 10.28; ADRD: OR, 83.13; P<0.001).

Low volume also showed significantly greater odds of MCI and ADRD relative to normal volume in NACC and ADNI, but odds ratios were numerically less than the low cSA/low cTH phenotype (Figure 3A-B). In area under curve (AUC) analyses, cSA/cTH phenotypes demonstrated strong discrimination of ADRD from CU in NACC and ADNI, where low cSA/low cTH showed the greatest classification performance and exceeded low volume alone (Figure 3C-D). Full phenotype model results, DMN analyses, and ADNI sensitivity analyses are provided in Supplemental Figures 17 18 19 20 21.

Association of cSA and cTH with Longitudinal ADRD Progression

Longitudinal analyses included 1,558 ADNI participants who were CU or MCI at baseline (mean age: 73±7 years, 46% female, 42% APOE ε4 carriers, 42% CU); mean follow-up span of MRI observations from baseline was 4.3±3.1 years with a maximum follow-up span of 15.8 years (Supplemental Table 2). 336 participants had a conversion to ADRD over their follow-up span. NACC was not evaluated longitudinally as sample size was smaller for CU/MCI participants having at least one follow-up MRI visit (n=754) and mean follow-up span was lower (1.7±0.8 years, max=4.2 years) with only 44 ADRD conversion events. In ADNI, lower baseline cSA and cTH were independently and significantly associated with greater risk of progression to incident ADRD (Supplemental Fig. 22). In a secondary analysis restricted to participants who were CU at baseline, lower baseline cSA and cTH were also independently and significantly associated with greater risk of progression to incident MCI (Supplemental Fig. 22).

In nested Cox proportional hazards models predicting incident ADRD, addition of cSA significantly improved the model fit beyond demographic, genetic, and imaging covariates, including age, sex, education, race, ethnicity, APOE ε4 allele presence, field strength, and image pre-processing status (likelihood ratio test [LRT] χ²=25.41; P<0.001; Supplemental Fig. 23). cSA remained informative after accounting for cTH in the model: adding cSA to the cTH-adjusted model significantly improved model fit (LRT χ²=18.46; P<0.001). Compared to the volume model, the cTH and cSA model had a lower Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) (ΔAIC=59.94) and greater Harrel C-index (0.81 vs. 0.78; Supplemental Fig. 23).

Models including cortical morphology measures of both cSA and cTH outperformed models based on cortical volume as the sole morphometric predictor. Adding cSA and cTH to a volume-adjusted model significantly improved fit (LRT χ²=64.24; P<0.001), whereas adding volume to the cSA and cTH model did not result in substantial improvement (LRT χ²=2.30; P=0.13, Supplemental Fig. 23). Consistent with this, discrimination was essentially unchanged after adding volume to the cSA + cTH model (ΔAIC=0.30; Supplemental Fig. 23).

Youden's J-derived cSA, cTH and volume cutoffs for phenotype delineation and associated cohort distributions are provided in Supplemental Fig. 24-25. Kaplan-Meier analyses of baseline CU/MCI participants demonstrated significant differences in ADRD-free survival by cSA, cTH and combined cSA/cTH phenotype (all log-rank P<0.001; Figure 4A-C). Participants with low cSA or low cTH had lower ADRD-free survival compared with those in the normal groups (Figure 4A-B). When cSA and cTH were modelled jointly, the low cSA/low cTH phenotype showed the steepest decline in ADRD-free survival, whereas the normal cSA/normal cTH group showed the highest ADRD-free survival probability (Figure 4C). The low cSA/low cTH and low volume phenotypes were both associated with significantly poorer ADRD-free survival (both log-rank P<0.001), with low cSA/low cTH showing a steeper decline in survival probability (Figure 4D, Supplemental Figure 26).

In multivariable Cox proportional hazard models, baseline cSA/cTH phenotype was independently associated with greater risk of incident ADRD after adjustment for demographic, genetic, and imaging covariates (Figure 4E). Compared to the normal cSA/normal cTH group, normal cSA/low cTH was associated with a 4.11-fold higher hazard of incident ADRD, low cSA/normal cTH with a 2.02-fold higher hazard, and low cSA/low cTH with a 7.62-fold higher hazard (all P<.01). Direct comparison of low cSA/low cTH, excluding intermediate phenotypes from the model, showed a significantly higher hazard of incident ADRD than normal cSA/normal cTH (hazard ratio [HR], 8.42; P<0.001), where low volume was also associated with increased ADRD risk, although with a numerically smaller effect size (HR, 6.70; P<0.001) (Figure 4F). Full models in AD-Signature and DMN are shown in Supplemental Figures 27 28.

CSF Proteomic Signatures of cSA and cTH

CSF proteomics was available for 694 ADNI participants (mean age 74±7, 43% female, 49% APOE ε4 carriers, 20% ADRD); the mean interval between CSF collection and baseline MRI was 89±281 days (Supplemental Table 3). Serum proteomics were only available in a smaller subset and were thus not assessed in this study. 67 Differentially Expressed Proteins (DEPs) were identified for cSA while 169 DEPs were identified for cTH, only 12 of which were shared (Supplemental Fig. 29). Gene Ontology Biological Processes (GO-BP) enrichment analysis revealed distinct biological pathways associated with cSA and cTH (Figure 5, Supplemental Fig. 29). Down-regulated proteins associated with semaphorin receptor signalling, ephrin receptor signalling, MET regulation, and Wnt signalling were associated with lower cSA (Figure 5, Supplemental Fig. 30, Supplemental Data), while these pathways were not significant for cTH. Down-regulated proteins associated with calcineurin signalling, RAB geranylgeranylation, 14-3-3 adaptor protein-IGF pathway interactions and synaptic MAP/Erk/Akt pathway interactions were associated with reduced cTH (Figure 5). Functional pathways of synapse assembly and axonogenesis were shared between cSA and cTH (Figure 5). A full summary of proteins and pathways associated with cSA and cTH is provided in Supplemental Data. Unaltered protein-protein interaction networks are shown in Supplemental Figure 30.

Discussion

The majority of ADRD structural neuroimaging studies to date have focused on cTH and volume, reflecting strong historical evidence that ADRD is associated with cortical thinning and regional atrophy. 3, 5, [41] [42] [43] Although cortical volume is a composite measure of cSA and cTH, few studies have evaluated cSA as an independent factor of cortical vulnerability. In this multi-cohort study across vascular-enriched and memory clinic cohorts, we demonstrated that cSA in the AD-Signature and DMN regions provide clinically relevant information not captured by cTH or volume alone. Lower cSA was independently associated with MCI and ADRD cross-sectionally and predicted incident ADRD over a mean follow-up of 4.3 years. Moreover, individuals with both low cSA and low cTH showed the greatest clinical vulnerability i.e., the highest risk for MCI and ADRD, supporting the value of decomposing cortical volume into its component dimensions. Together, the results of this study suggest that cortical vulnerability in ADRD is better represented when evaluating cSA in addition to cTH.

Multiple aspects of our data support conceptualization and evaluation of cSA and cTH as highly independent morphometric features that may reflect distinct biological vulnerabilities. cSA and cTH were only weakly correlated in all three cohorts, and both metrics showed independent associations with MCI, ADRD, and incident ADRD. The proteomics analyses also support this concept through demonstration of distinct cellular and molecular pathways in cSA and cTH. Although cSA has not been as well studied in the aging and dementia fields, evaluating cSA as an independent feature of brain morphometry is in keeping with developmental biology studies that have shown cSA and cTH are highly heritable traits with distinct genetic pathways. 10, 14, 15 Moreover, these findings are consistent with evolutionary and developmental studies that have shown greater cSA is associated with higher intelligence and cognitive ability in humans [7] [8] [9] and with mammalian studies suggesting that evolutionary expansion of cSA supports greater cognitive capacity across species. 44, 45 Together, these findings underscore that cSA, like cTH, represents a fundamental and unique dimension of cortical architecture.

From a clinical perspective, combined cSA/cTH phenotyping may provide a practical framework for morphometric risk stratification. Cortical volume and cTH are commonly used for prognostication of dementia risk, however our data suggest that evaluating cSA and cTH together improves identification of individuals at greater risk for clinical progression.

Participants with low cSA and low cTH had the poorest ADRD-free survival and the highest risk of incident ADRD than either morphometric dimension alone. One possible interpretation is that low cSA may reflect a more developmentally constrained or premorbid dimension of cortical vulnerability, where low cTH may more closely reflect active neurodegenerative tissue loss. Under this framework, the low cSA/low cTH phenotype may represent a subgroup with more extensive or multifactorial cortical damage, helping explain its stronger association with ADRD progression than either metric alone.

The added value of cSA/cTH phenotyping may reflect, in part, the limitations of cortical volume as a composite measure. Because volume reflects both dimensions, similar volumetric values can arise from different cSA/cTH profiles that may not carry the same biological or clinical meaning; collapsing these into a single aggregate volume measure may mask this separable morphometric information. Consistent with this interpretation, models incorporating both cSA and cTH demonstrated better prognostic performance than models using cTH or volume alone and adding volume to a cSA+cTH model did not significantly improve model fit. Mathematically, the combined measure may also disproportionally reflect whichever dimension accounts for more variance, and it has been suggested that more interindividual variation in cortical volume is attributed to cSA rather than cTH. 10, 11 Furthermore, a composite measure makes it difficult to distinguish additive versus combined interactive effects that may hinder causal inference if cSA and cTH have different associations with the outcome of interest. As such, decomposing volume into cSA and cTH may therefore improve not only prognostic performance, but also biological interpretability.

Risk factor analyses further suggest that cSA and cTH may not be equally sensitive to all sources of dementia risk. Across cohorts, older age was consistently associated with lower cSA and cTH, consistent with prior work showing age-related reductions in both cortical dimensions. 46, 47 APOE ε4 allele presence was not significantly associated with either morphometric dimension in CU participants, but full cohort (with MCI and ADRD participants) analyses showed an association with lower cTH, consistent with previous studies linking APOE ε4 to cortical thinning. 48, 49 However, we found no consistent association between APOE ε4 allele presence and cSA. General vascular risk factors showed inconsistent associations with cortical morphometry, suggesting that broad vascular risk burden may not map uniformly onto cSA or cTH. Hypertension was associated with lower cTH in NACC and ADNI, in agreement with prior reports linking vascular risk to cortical thinning, [50] [51] [52] [53] [54] but this was not observed in CAM. Notably, carotid disease in CAM participants was associated with significantly lower cSA, but not cTH, highlighting a potentially distinct pattern related to cerebrovascular disease. These distinctions are interesting because they indicate hypertension, APOE ε4 allele presence, age, and carotid stenosis may influence cortical structure through different mechanisms or timescales, and these differences highlight the benefit of assessing both cSA and cTH and the need for further research into cSA/cTH interactions. Because ADNI and NACC do not evaluate large vessel cerebrovascular disease, the extent to which carotid stenosis or other measures of vascular burden contribute to cortical morphometry in these cohorts remains unknown and will require further investigation.

The proteomics analyses provide added biological insight into the morphometric and clinical findings. Proteomics findings were broadly in agreement with published findings regarding pathways relevant in cTH deficits, 55 the majority of which were not associated with cSA. Shared pathways significant in both cSA and cTH included axonogenesis and synapse assembly, pathways well-established in ADRD-related neurodegeneration. [56] [57] [58] [59] cSA had several distinct molecular/cellular pathway associations that may help explain why it contributed information beyond cTH and volume. One such pathway associated with decreased cSA is reduced ephrin/semaphorin signalling, which has established relevance in mutual repulsion of Cajal-Retzius (CR) cells, promoting final dispersion of CR cells over the cortical surface during neurodevelopment. 60 Defects in migration of CR cells, controlled by Eph/Ephrin and Sema/Plexin pathways, can impair cortical aerealization. 61 Relevant to ephrin/semaphorin signalling, preclinical studies have shown that amyloid beta oligomers can directly trigger degradation of EphB2 leading to reduced synaptic plasticity, 62 while loss of PLXNA4/PLXND1 is associated with memory impairment 63 and blood-brain barrier injury, 64 linking reduced ephrin/semaphorin signalling to pathways of early neurodegeneration. We also found an association between decreased cSA and reduced Wnt signalling, an important pathway for cellular proliferation; loss of Wnt signalling has been associated with synaptic loss and amyloidogenic processing of amyloid precursor protein in preclinical studies of AD. 65 These results further support our findings that cSA and cTH play independent roles in cortical vulnerability, reflecting distinct biological pathways that may differentially contribute to ADRD-related neurodegeneration.

Several methodological features relevant to prior gaps in the literature add to the rigor of this study. First, the use of a large sample drawn from three separate cohorts increases generalizability and reduces concern that the findings reflect cohort or acquisition/processingspecific biases. However, not every analysis could be performed with each dataset due to cohort-specific limitations in variable of interest availability. Second, cSA and cTH were examined within a priori AD-Signature and DMN brain region composites rather than through unconstrained regional testing across the whole cortex reducing multiple-comparison burden and grounding the analysis in regions with established ADRD vulnerability. Third, longitudinal analyses with up to 15-year follow-up provide evidence that baseline cSA relates to future clinical progression. Finally, CSF proteomic analyses offer biological context for the distinct associations seen in cSA and cTH.

This study also has several limitations. 1) The observational design precludes causal inference, and lower cSA/cTH may reflect preexisting vulnerability or other accumulated disease-related differences. 2) Although models were adjusted for key demographic, genetic, and imaging covariates, residual confounding of comorbidities and other cohort-specific characteristics remains possible. 3) Morphometric estimates rely on FreeSurfer segmentation accuracy, which may be dependent on participant age and post-processing protocols 32 . 4) Normal/low phenotypes are useful for visualization and risk stratification but are cohortderived and should thus be interpreted as pragmatic summaries rather than fixed biological . thresholds. 5) Our longitudinal analyses show that baseline cSA and cTH predict future clinical progression, but do not assess cSA or cTH over time. Future studies could determine whether cSA is primarily a vulnerability marker, dynamic disease marker, or both. 6) Analyses did not separately evaluate progression from CU to MCI and MCI to ADRD or stratify these transitions by evidence of amyloid pathology; therefore, incremental prognostic value of cSA beyond cortical volume, APOE ε4, and baseline cognition within biomarkerdefined clinical subgroups remains uncertain. Future studies incorporating longitudinal morphometric measurements, stage-and biomarker-specific analyses, and independent replication are needed to address these limitations and further clarify the biological and prognostic significance of cSA.

In conclusion, these findings support cSA as a clinically relevant dimension of cortical morphology. Future studies should consider decomposing cortical volume into cSA and cTH to better characterize distinct and complementary patterns of cortical vulnerability.

Incorporating cSA into ADRD neuroimaging frameworks may improve risk stratification, clarify heterogeneous pathways towards dementia, and provide a stronger morphometric bridge to studies of neurodegeneration.
