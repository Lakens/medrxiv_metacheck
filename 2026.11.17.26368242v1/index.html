Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Predicting clinical trial outcomes requires interpreting biological and clinical evidence in the context of a specific intervention, population and study design. Here we test whether a general-purpose large language model (LLM) can predict trial outcomes from publicly available evidence. We introduce TrialDossier, which assembles standardized evidence dossiers, and TrialOutlook, which uses these dossiers with GPT-5 to estimate the probability that a trial meets its prespecified primary endpoint. Among 694 ongoing trials with results expected within six months of cohort assembly, 69 outcomes first disclosed after forecast recording had been adjudicated by 20 October 2026. TrialOutlook achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.819 (95% confidence interval, 0.708-0.914) and an average precision of 0.860. TrialOutlook correctly identified 27 of 29 failures (93.1%) and incorrectly classified 17 of 40 successes as failures. We further recorded forecasts for 5,809 ongoing trials associated with US-listed companies and established a public benchmark linking archived evidence and fixed forecasts to adjudicated outcomes as they become available.

Introduction

Clinical development requires decisions about investigational treatments based on incomplete biological and clinical evidence. [1] [2] [3] We ask whether large language models (LLMs) can assemble and interpret publicly available biomedical evidence to predict individual trial outcomes before their public disclosure. Such prediction requires relating an intervention's biological rationale and prior clinical findings to the trial's population, comparator, endpoints and other design features. Reliable forecasts could help clinical development teams assess programme risk, identify where further evidence is needed and allocate resources. 4, 5 Human genetic support for drug targets is associated with clinical development success, [6] [7] [8] and models integrating molecular, disease and trial information have predicted historical outcomes [9] [10] [11] and prospectively forecast progression from phase 2 to phase 3. 12 More recent systems based on LLMs have generated forecasts from trial protocols and biomedical evidence, 13, 14 while initiatives including CT Open 15 and ClinicoBench 16 provide frameworks for evaluating predictions as new outcomes emerge. These advances motivate a closer examination of how reliably LLM can forecast outcomes using the evidence available for ongoing trials, particularly when that evidence is incomplete, indirect or conflicting.

For an individual trial, relevant evidence is distributed across registries, publications, regulatory records and sponsor disclosures. 17, 18 A forecasting system must identify which findings apply to the intervention and clinical setting, reconcile differences in reporting and record where evidence is unavailable. Prospective evaluation requires forecasts recorded before public disclosure of outcomes. 19, 20 Performance estimates also depend on which trials enter the cohort and which yield adjudicable outcomes during follow-up. Trials with early readouts in a cohort selected for near-term results may not represent the broader population of ongoing trials. The practical relevance of forecasts also depends on the balance between missed failures and successful trials incorrectly predicted to fail.

Here we develop TrialDossier and TrialOutlook to assemble publicly available biomedical evidence and predict clinical trial outcomes. TrialDossier uses a trial identifier to compile a standardized dossier covering intervention biology, prior clinical findings, trial design, safety and operational characteristics. We assess the accuracy and coverage of dossier fields through manual review and comparisons with reference annotations, including those in ChEMBL 21 . TrialOutlook uses these dossiers with a general-purpose LLM to estimate the probability that a trial meets its prespecified primary endpoint. Among 69 eligible adjudicated readouts from an initial cohort of 694 trials, forecasts recorded before outcome disclosure discriminated between successes and failures. TrialOutlook identified 27 of the 29 failures while incorrectly classifying 17 of 40 successful trials as failures. To extend prospective evaluation beyond trials selected for near-term readouts, we recorded forecasts for a broader cohort of 5,809 ongoing trials conducted or sponsored by US-listed companies. We will report prospective forecast performance through the TrialOutlook website (https://trialoutlook.com/) that links evidence dossiers and recorded forecasts to adjudicated outcomes as results accumulate.

Results

Development and evaluation of an AI system for clinical trial outcome prediction

We first developed TrialDossier, a two-stage AI agent that uses a ClinicalTrials.gov identifier (NCT ID) to assemble a standardized evidence dossier for clinical trial outcome prediction (Fig. 1a; Methods). In stage 1, TrialDossier retrieves and extracts evidence from ClinicalTrials.gov, 17 PubMed, 22 Open Targets, 23 openFDA, 24 prespecified web sources and company disclosures, organizing it into 28 fields. Stage 2 standardizes or condenses eight fields and retains the remaining 20 without semantic rewriting. The resulting dossier covers intervention biology, prior clinical findings, trial design, safety and operational characteristics. Information that cannot be retrieved is explicitly recorded as missing.

TrialOutlook, our clinical trial outcome prediction system, uses these dossiers to estimate the probability that an individual trial meets its prespecified primary endpoint through a reasoning expert (Fig. 1b; Methods). Specifically, the reasoning expert uses GPT-5, 25 a general-purpose LLM, prompted to update a phase specific base rate using the dossier evidence (Methods).

We also evaluate an embedding expert as an exploratory alternative. The embedding expert uses four text inputs, including the reasoning expert's assessments of direct human evidence and of target and mechanism validation, functional summaries of target genes, and disease descriptions. A text embedding model 26 converts these inputs into numerical vectors, and then TabPFN-3, a foundation model for tabular data, 27, 28 predicts clinical trial outcomes from these representations using labelled historical trials as reference examples (Methods).

We used four cohorts for model development, retrospective testing, and prospective evaluation (Fig. 1c; Methods). Outcome classification followed prespecified criteria appropriate to the source data. The development cohort comprised 1,950 trials with known outcomes (975 classified as successful and 975 as unsuccessful), including 1,800 fitting trials and 150 validation trials. Following model selection, the 1,800 fitting trials served as the reference set for TabPFN-3. To assess performance on trials excluded from model development, we used a separate retrospective test cohort of 143 trials with readouts first disclosed between June 2025 and March 2026, including 81 classified as successful and 62 as unsuccessful.

For an initial prospective assessment, we assembled 694 ongoing trials on 5 July 2026 with expected readouts within the following six months. Trials were identified through BioMedTracker, filings with the US Securities and Exchange Commission and company announcements; evidence was collected between 5 and 9 July 2026. Prospective comparisons included only adjudicated outcomes disclosed after the forecasts had been recorded (Methods). Early follow-up raised concern that successful trials could be overrepresented among the available readouts. To extend evaluation beyond trials selected for anticipated readouts, we assembled a broader prospective cohort of 5,809 ongoing trials in October 2026, comprising all eligible ongoing trials identified across US-listed companies at cohort assembly. We recorded forecasts before outcome disclosure and continue to evaluate performance as results become available.

Accuracy, coverage and efficiency of TrialDossier

We compared TrialDossier with manual curation in a simple random sample of 20 trials from the prospective benchmark cohort of 5,809 trials (Methods). A PhD student in biostatistics, blinded to TrialDossier's outputs, completed the same dossier schema using web searches, including Google Search and Gemini summaries. We restricted our comparison to the molecular target, target gene and mechanism of action (MOA), which require biological interpretation and restricted our comparisons to the prospective benchmark cohort to reduce the concern of knowledge leakage in LLM (Methods). For the 20 randomly selected trials, the human curator's molecular target annotations were judged correct in 18 of 20 trials (90%), target gene annotations in 16 of 20 (80%) and MOA annotations in all 20 trials (100%). TrialDossier's annotations were judged correct for all three fields in all 20 trials (100%) (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). Furthermore, manual curation required a mean of 30.5 min per trial, corresponding to an estimated 353 h for all 694 trials. Using the July 2026 US average hourly earnings for private nonfarm employees ($37.62) 29 as a wage benchmark, we estimated labour costs of $19.12 per trial. In comparison, TrialDossier's LLM token cost was approximately $0.80 per trial (Fig. 2a).

To assess disease annotations across a larger sample, we compared the Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms 30 assigned by TrialDossier with those in ClinicalTrials.gov. The comparison was restricted to the 308 trials for which the registry assigned a single MeSH term to the registered conditions in the initial prospective cohort. MeSH identifiers matched exactly in 260 trials (84.4%) (Supplementary Table 3). We manually reviewed the 48 discordant pairs to assess how well each term represented the registered conditions (Fig. 2b; Supplementary Table 4). In 24 pairs, one term represented a broader category and the other a more specific condition within that category. The more specific term was judged more appropriate in each case, favoring TrialDossier in 14 and ClinicalTrials.gov in 10. The remaining 24 pairs differed in the conditions or disease attributes represented, such as histology versus anatomic site, or involved errors in term or identifier assignment. Review favored TrialDossier in 12 cases and ClinicalTrials.gov in four; both annotations were acceptable in the remaining eight.

We further assessed the coverage and concordance of drug annotations by comparing TrialDossier with ChEMBL 21 . In a random sample of 200 trials from the prospective benchmark cohort, all 262 drug records named a drug intervention, representing 214 unique drugs. TrialDossier provided MOA descriptions for all 262 records (100%) and target gene annotations for 258 (98.5%). ChEMBL provided the corresponding annotations for 146 (55.7%) and 141 (53.8%) records, respectively (Fig. 2c; Supplementary Table 5).

Among 131 trial-drug pairs representing 92 unique drugs that were matched to ChEMBL by exact preferred name and had a target annotation in TrialDossier and a curated mechanism in ChEMBL, target gene annotations agreed directly in 107 pairs (81.7%) (Supplementary Table 6). Discordances were assessed using Claude Opus 5.5 and then reviewed manually against prescribing information and primary literature (Supplementary Table 7). No contradictory mechanisms were identified. Of the 24 discordant pairs, 3 reflected alternative names for the same target, and 20 reflected differences in annotation granularity or scope rather than conflicting mechanisms. These included annotations of entire protein families or complexes instead of specific drug-binding members, and differences in whether targets of antibody-drug conjugate payloads were included. For the remaining pair (tebentafusp), TrialDossier additionally annotated CD3 (CD3E), which is bound by the drug's anti-CD3 domain on T cells (Fig. 2d; Supplementary Note 1).

We classified trial readouts using prespecified criteria applied to public disclosures (Methods). Success required evidence that the prespecified primary endpoint had been met, either through an explicit statement in the outcome disclosure or, when no such statement was available, by comparing the reported results with the primary endpoint registered on ClinicalTrials.gov. Failure required evidence of a missed primary endpoint or trial discontinuation without reported attainment of the primary endpoint. Outcomes remained unresolved when the evidence supported neither classification. We also assessed disclosure framing [31] [32] [33] using a prespecified codebook, with framing judged by whether the dominant message of the disclosure (title, subtitle and conclusion) was favorable (Methods). Among 58 trials classified as unsuccessful with readouts between June 2025 and March 2026, the corresponding disclosures were classified as positively framed in 8 cases (13.8%), negatively framed in 48 (82.8%), and neutral in two (3.4%) (Fig. 2e; Supplementary Table 8). The positively framed disclosures emphasized secondary endpoints (3 trials), reframed a missed primary endpoint (3), or emphasized subgroup results (1) or response rates or biomarkers (1). For example, the sponsor of a phase 2b trial of zelicapavir in high risk adults with respiratory syncytial virus infection (NCT05568706) described the findings as "positive topline results" in the press release headline and highlighted a subgroup benefit, while disclosing the absence of a treatment effect on the primary endpoint only in the body of the release (Supplementary Note 2). These findings highlight the importance of adjudicating trial outcomes using prespecified criteria and explicit evidence.

Development and retrospective evaluation of TrialOutlook

The reasoning expert used GPT-5 to estimate trial success probabilities from the TrialDossier dossier using a fixed prompt. To assess the contribution of the additional curated evidence, we compared the same GPT-5 model supplied with either the full dossier or a predefined trial profile from ClinicalTrials.gov in an additionally curated 150 trials from validation cohort (75 successes and 75 failures). Both conditions used the same outcome definition, prompt, and inference settings (Methods). Providing the full dossier increased ROC AUC by 0.034 (95% CI, 0.006-0.069) and AP (average precision) by 0.028 (95% CI, -0.001 to 0.059) relative to registry information alone (Fig. 3b; Supplementary Table 9).

To extend the use of GPT-5 beyond direct probability estimation, we took its output assessments as inputs to an embedding expert. We developed this expert using the 1,800 fitting trials of the development cohort (Methods). The embedding expert encodes four text inputs, including the GPT-5 reasoning expert's assessments of direct human evidence and of target and mechanism validation, functional summaries of target genes retrieved through MyGene.info, 34 and disease descriptions associated with MeSH terms. This design was adapted from PRIORITI, which combines embeddings of biological rationales generated by an LLM with embeddings of gene summaries and disease definitions to forecast the success of target-indication pairs; 35 here it is applied to individual trials. We compared five candidate classifiers using nested cross-validation grouped by primary investigational drug. 36 TabPFN-3 achieved the highest area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC AUC) (0.838) in the final cross-validation comparison across the 1,800 fitting trials and was selected for subsequent analyses (Fig. 3a; Supplementary Table 10). After classifier selection, the 1,800 fitting trials served as labeled reference examples for TabPFN-3.

We evaluated both experts retrospectively in 143 trials, comprising 81 successful and 62 unsuccessful trials, with results first disclosed between June 2025 and March 2026. These disclosures occurred after GPT-5's reported knowledge cutoff of September 30, 2024.

We assessed discrimination using ROC AUC 37 and the prioritization of successful trials using AP, with success as the positive class. The reasoning and embedding experts achieved ROC AUCs of 0.797 (95% CI, 0.718-0.876) and 0.737 (95% CI, 0.651-0.816), respectively, with corresponding AP values of 0.786 (95% CI, 0.684-0.892) and 0.776 (95% CI, 0.678-0.872) (Fig. 3c,d; Supplementary Tables 11 and 12).

We assessed the accuracy of the individual experts' probability estimates using the Brier score and summarized calibration using expected calibration error (ECE). 38, 39 Brier scores were 0.189 (95% CI, 0.164-0.214) for the reasoning expert and 0.211 (95% CI, 0.176-0.249) for the embedding expert. Corresponding ECE values were 0.134 (95% CI, 0.104-0.213) and 0.107 (95% CI, 0.079-0.197), respectively (Supplementary Table 11). Although the embedding expert incorporated the reasoning expert's assessments alongside gene and disease information, it achieved a lower ROC AUC than the reasoning expert. We therefore focused mainly on the reasoning expert in the prospective evaluation.

Prospective evaluation of clinical trial forecasts

The initial prospective cohort comprised 694 ongoing trials identified through BioMedTracker, filings with the US Securities and Exchange Commission, and company announcements, with readouts expected within six months of cohort assembly. Forecasts from the reasoning expert were recorded on 29 July 2026. Only outcomes first disclosed after this date were included in the prospective evaluation, ensuring that all forecasts preceded their corresponding outcome disclosures (Fig. 4a). By 20 October 2026, results had been reported for 90 trials after the cutoff. We manually adjudicated these trials against prespecified outcome criteria, classifying 40 as successful and 29 as unsuccessful, while 21 remained unresolved (Supplementary Table 13).

Across the 69 adjudicated trials, the reasoning expert achieved a ROC AUC of 0.819 (95% CI, 0.708-0.914) and an AP of 0.860 (95% CI, 0.747-0.955) (Fig. 4b). The embedding expert achieved a ROC AUC of 0.813 (95% CI, 0.694-0.907) and an AP of 0.855 (95% CI, 0.741-0.938) (Supplementary Fig. 2; Supplementary Table 14). In an exploratory analysis, the reasoning expert had ROC AUC point estimates of 0.896 in phase 3 and 0.741 in other phases (Fig. 4b).

At the prespecified success probability threshold of 0.5, the reasoning expert correctly identified 27 of 29 failures (93.1%), while it incorrectly classified 17 successful trials as failures (Fig. 4c). Success predictions from the reasoning expert were correct in 23 of 25 cases (92.0%), although these captured only 57.5% of the 40 observed successes. With registry information alone, the same GPT-5 model and prompt achieved a ROC AUC of 0.796 (95% CI, 0.675-0.897), a difference of 0.023 (95% CI, -0.023 to 0.075) from the full dossier, but correctly classified 42 of 69 trials (60.9%) compared with 50 of 69 (72.5%) with the dossier (difference, 11.6%; 95% CI, 2.9-20.3; Supplementary Fig. 1).

We examined share price responses to outcome disclosures and whether they differed between trials whose outcomes the reasoning expert had predicted correctly or incorrectly. 40, 41 Of the 69 adjudicated prospective trials, 50 contributed eligible announcements (22 unsuccessful and 28 successful), after excluding trials with no listed equity exposure (n = 6), suspended trading (n = 1), trial status updated by the company without a clear news announcement (n = 2), multiple eligible trials disclosed in one release (n = 4), or other eligible announcements by the same company on the same event day (n = 6) (Supplementary Table 15). For each announcement, we estimated cumulative abnormal returns (CAR) over the first three trading sessions able to reflect the disclosure, using an XBI adjusted single factor model fitted to pre-announcement data. Event dates corresponded to the first disclosure supporting the adjudicated outcome (Methods). Negative CARs followed 21 of the 22 unsuccessful and 13 of the 28 successful trial announcements (Fig. 4d; Supplementary Table 16).

The reasoning expert correctly predicted failure for 21 of 22 unsuccessful trials with eligible announcements (median CAR, -20.2%), and the single unsuccessful trial predicted to succeed (NCT07108153) was followed by a CAR of -56.9%. Among successful trials, median CAR was -0.3% for the 15 correctly predicted to succeed by the reasoning expert and 3.0% for the 13 incorrectly predicted to fail (Fig. 4e). One illustrative example was MAXPIRe (NCT06132256), a phase 2 trial evaluating the CSF-1R antibody axatilimab in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. The reasoning expert assigned a success probability of 0.22. Although acknowledging axatilimab's clinical activity in chronic graft-versus-host disease, the expert judged its relevance to pulmonary fibrosis to be limited, citing the historically high failure rate of phase 2 trials in this indication and the variability of the 26-week home spirometry endpoint (Supplementary Note 3). The trial subsequently failed to meet its primary endpoint, leading the sponsor to discontinue development in this indication. The failure announcement was associated with a CAR of -15.7%. As a sensitivity analysis, we repeated the analysis using all 62 of the 69 adjudicated prospective trials for which a CAR could be estimated, and the results were similar (Supplementary Note 4).

To evaluate performance beyond trials selected for anticipated readouts, we assembled a prospective cohort of 5,809 eligible ongoing trials across US-listed companies in October 2026, including trials without an announced readout date, and recorded forecasts before outcome disclosure (Methods). Of these trials, 1000 were phase 1, 2055 were phase 2 and 1457 were phase 3; the remaining 1297 comprised 730 phase 1/2, 168 phase 2/3, 65 early phase 1 and 334 phase 4 trials. For comparison, Chen et al. 16 reported primary endpoint attainment rates in a retrospective cohort of trials initiated in 2021 or later were 48%, 51% and 69% for phase 1, phase 2 and phase 3 trials, respectively (Fig. 4f). To assess whether AI agents could predict clinical trial outcomes, we evaluated Claude Code with Claude Opus 5.5 as an additional reasoning expert using the same prompts and evidence dossiers as the GPT-5 reasoning expert. We also evaluated Claude Code with Claude Opus 5.5 and Codex with GPT-6.1 Sol as zero-shot forecasters on the same prospective cohort (Methods; Fig. 4f). At the 0.5 threshold, the GPT-5 reasoning expert predicted success for 52.4% of phase 1-3 trials, close to the historical rate of 53% for phases 1-3 combined, whereas the Claude Opus 5.5 reasoning expert predicted success for 68.5% and the zero-shot forecasts of Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6.1 Sol for 71.5% and 77.5%, respectively (Fig. 4f). This live prospective benchmark that compares clinical trial forecasts as outcomes become available is at https://trialoutlook.com/. The benchmark preserves evidence dossiers and forecasts recorded before outcome disclosure, linking them to manually adjudicated outcomes and supporting sources. Original forecasts remain fixed as outcomes accumulate. Monthly updates compare our methods and external forecasts submitted before disclosure on shared eligible trials, summarizing discrimination, prioritization, classification errors, and, for probability forecasts, accuracy and calibration. Furthermore, SHA-256 hashes of the four forecast files are provided in this manuscript to enable verification of their integrity and detection of any subsequent modifications (Methods).

Discussion

Our prospective evaluation shows that a general-purpose LLM supplied with publicly available biomedical evidence can discriminate subsequent clinical trial successes from failures. TrialDossier and TrialOutlook link forecasts recorded before outcome disclosure to standardized evidence dossiers and subsequently adjudicated outcomes, allowing both predictions and their supporting evidence to be examined. Differences in prediction targets, trial populations and information access limit direct comparisons with earlier evaluations of LLM forecasting. [13] [14] [15] [16] TrialDossier provides a standardized record of the evidence supplied to the forecaster, allowing input quality to be assessed alongside predictive performance. The full dossier improved discrimination relative to registry information alone in the historical validation cohort. A further comparison using archived inputs from the prospective cohort also showed higher classification accuracy at the prespecified threshold, although the estimated AUC gain remained uncertain.

TrialOutlook estimates the probability that a clinical trial meets its prespecified primary endpoint, an outcome that can be adjudicated at readout when sufficient results are available. Phase transition and regulatory approval concern broader development programmes that also depend on commercial priorities, development strategy and regulatory requirements. 1, 2 Thus, estimating phase transition probabilities or the probability of technical and regulatory success (PTRS) 10 would require distinct prediction targets and evaluation. Also, TrialOutlook does not quantify the magnitude or clinical importance of treatment benefit, or its balance against harms. We leave these important topics to future research.

Interpreting these forecasts requires consistent outcome adjudication. Some sponsor disclosures framed unsuccessful trials positively, [31] [32] [33] underscoring the need to apply prespecified success criteria to the reported results. Our adjudication rules also classified terminated trials as unsuccessful, although termination does not necessarily establish endpoint failure. Excluding the three terminated trials left 66 trials in the initial prospective benchmark, including 26 failures. ROC AUC and AP were 0.836 and 0.879, respectively, compared with 0.819 and 0.860 in the full 69 trial evaluation. The reasoning expert identified 25 of 26 failures after exclusion, compared with 27 of 29 in the full cohort. These results indicate that the observed discrimination was not driven by the inclusion of the three terminated trials.

The classification errors illustrate why discrimination must be interpreted alongside the consequences of individual predictions. At the prespecified success probability threshold of 0.5, the reasoning expert identified 27 of the 29 observed failures but also classified 17 of 40 successful trials as failures. In other words, 27 of the 44 failure predictions were correct (61.4%). A threshold suitable for identifying trials for additional scientific review may be inappropriate for decisions about continuing a development program. Using these probability estimates requires assessment of calibration and decision thresholds that reflect the consequences of missed failures and false warnings in the intended setting. 42, 43 In the exploratory market analysis, correctly predicted failures were accompanied by substantial negative abnormal returns, whereas successful trials elicited more variable and often modest responses, 41 with no clear separation by forecast correctness. This pattern suggests that identifying likely clinical failures may have greater relevance to anticipating adverse market reactions. However, market responses also reflect investor expectations, commercial implications, and concurrent news. These observations characterize the market consequences of trial readouts but do not establish whether the forecasts provide information beyond investor expectations or could generate investment returns, which we leave to future research.

The initial prospective benchmark was limited to 69 adjudicable outcomes from trials selected for readouts expected within six months. A further 21 disclosures made after the forecasts remained unresolved. Disclosure timing and completeness may differ between successful and unsuccessful trials, affecting which outcomes contribute to performance estimates. Concern that available readouts might overrepresent successful trials motivated the broader cohort of 5,809 ongoing trials, which includes trials without an anticipated readout date. We expect this broader cohort will provide a less biased evaluation of whether general-purpose LLMs can predict clinical trial outcomes. We will update our prospective results with new readouts during revision.

As LLMs are updated, published trial outcomes may enter their training data, making performance on historical trials difficult to interpret when prior exposure to the results cannot be excluded. 15, 20, 44 A dossier assembled before disclosure does not by itself resolve this problem if it is supplied to a newer model after the outcome is public. The benchmark therefore preserves both the evidence supplied and the forecast recorded before disclosure. This record allows the original predictions to be assessed regardless of what later models learn, while the dossiers and adjudicated outcomes can support further model development.

To maintain prospective evaluation as models evolve, we will repeat evidence collection annually for all benchmark trials, retain each dossier version and previous forecast, and generate new forecasts only for trials whose outcomes remain undisclosed. Comparing a fixed model version across successive dossiers will assess changes associated with updated evidence. Comparing model versions using the same dossier will assess changes associated with model updates, including changes in their underlying knowledge. All forecasts being compared will be recorded for the same trials before outcome disclosure, with matched access to external sources. Performance will then be evaluated as outcomes are adjudicated, with monthly updates to the public benchmark enabling continued assessment by the research community. 15, 20 In summary, our results demonstrate prospective discrimination of trial outcomes using public biomedical evidence. The benchmark will test whether this performance generalizes and improves as models and evidence are updated. Studies with clinical development teams could assess whether access to these forecasts improves assessment of programme risk and decisions about further evidence collection.

Methods

TrialDossier: evidence construction

TrialDossier used a two-stage workflow to assemble and standardize evidence for individual clinical trials. For the development, validation and retrospective test cohorts, it used Claude Sonnet 4.6 for retrieval, cleaning, and extraction in stage 1 and GPT-5.1 for standardization in stage 2; for the initial prospective cohort, it used Claude Sonnet 4.6 at both stages; for the expanded prospective benchmark, it used Claude Opus 5.5 at both stages.

Stage 1 accepted a ClinicalTrials.gov identifier (NCT ID) and produced a record containing 28 fields (Supplementary Table 17). The registry module queried the ClinicalTrials.gov v2 REST API 17 and assembled the trial identifier, title, conditions, interventions, locations, principal investigator and affiliation, sponsor, recruitment status, phase, primary and secondary outcome measures, posted results and serious adverse events, allocation, masking, brief summary, eligibility criteria, study dates, investigator trial history, and protocol amendment history. Within this module, the LLM cleaned and deduplicated the intervention list and selected the core drug.

Downstream retrieval modules ran in parallel. The literature module queried PubMed Entrez Utilities 22 for prior phase 1, phase 2, and preclinical reports of the core intervention. The regulatory module queried the openFDA REST API 24 for approved indications, contraindications, warnings, adverse reactions, boxed warnings, and approval status. The investigator module summarized the principal investigator's registered trial history. The disease module assigned a disease subcategory from the registered conditions and mapped it to a Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) term. 30 The sponsor module used web search to characterize sponsor type, primary focus, and founding team expertise. The structure module retrieved the drug structure from PubChem, 45 and a separate web search identified the molecular target.

Target resolution proceeded sequentially. A mechanism of action (MOA) resolver identified the intervention's pharmacological action and candidate targets. A gene resolver mapped targets to human gene symbols approved by the HUGO Gene Nomenclature Committee (HGNC). 46 The genetics module then queried the Open Targets Platform GraphQL API 23 for targetdisease associations, tractability, known drugs, safety, expression, interactions, and dependency evidence.

Stage 2 converted the retrieved record into a standardized dossier (Supplementary Table 17). Twenty fields were copied without semantic rewriting; eight were condensed or standardized and returned in fixed JSON schemas.

The genetic evidence field retained Open Targets information relevant to the trial indication. The endpoint profile removed repetition from outcome measures, endpoint families, and assessment windows. The eligibility profile organized criteria into four categories (population, inclusion, allowed, and exclusion) with entries specifying category, criterion, and value. It retained thresholds relevant to study design, including age, body mass index, performance status, and recent event windows, while removing consent, contraception, and routine laboratory boilerplate. Approved indication text was reduced to disease names, biomarker or subgroup qualifiers, and age limits.

Adverse reactions and boxed warnings were derived together from openFDA safety text. Boxed risks were represented by short labels; adverse reactions were grouped as common, serious but not boxed, or leading to discontinuation or death. Related events were combined into categories without repeating terms across lists. Investigator history was shortened by selecting up to eight titles, according to relevance to the trial disease, from up to 24 prior registered trials. Founding team expertise was summarized for up to four people, with normalized roles and short descriptions of relevant experience; finance, legal, and commercial roles were excluded. This was the only stage 2 field permitted to use additional web searches. All other fields were restricted to evidence retrieved in stage 1. Unavailable information was retained as missing rather than inferred or imputed.

Development cohort

Development candidates were assembled from two historical pools. The earlier development pool comprised 2,104 records from a manually annotated 2020-2024 subset of the CTO benchmark 47 and 657 records from BioMedTracker readout events and terminated ClinicalTrials.gov trials. Trials in the CTO subset retained their original outcome labels. BioMedTracker events reporting positive topline results were classified as successes, whereas trials listed as terminated in ClinicalTrials.gov were classified as failures. Excluding a previous holdout of 150 trials left 2,611 candidates.

The second pool was assembled from ClinicalTrials.gov records of completed trials with a completion date in January 2020 or later, an industry sponsor, a publicly listed parent company, and a specified phase (11,852 trials), together with 3,584 terminated trials. Completed trials were labeled using Claude Opus 5 in Claude Code. Subsequent trials were identified programmatically, and every success label was verified through web search. Terminated trials were labeled as failures on the basis of registry status. We excluded trials without a clear outcome signal, trials in the retrospective test cohort, trials whose success labels could not be confirmed on repeat verification, trials enrolling healthy volunteers, and records without an intervention. This yielded 4,222 trials: 1,515 successes and 2,707 failures.

From the pooled candidates, the 900 successes and 900 failures with the most recent completion dates formed the fitting set of 1,800 trials. A separate validation set of 150 trials, comprising 75 successes and 75 failures, was sampled at random from the remaining candidates completed during 2020-2023 after excluding the retrospective test. The validation set contained 19, 19, 19, and 18 successes and 23, 15, 14, and 23 failures completed in 2020, 2021, 2022, and 2023, respectively. Together, the fitting and validation sets comprised 1,950 trials. Classifier comparison used the 1,800 fitting trials; the 150 validation trials were used for the comparison of the reasoning expert with and without the full evidence dossier.

Retrospective test cohort

The retrospective test cohort comprised 143 trials with results first disclosed between June 2025 and March 2026: 81 successes and 62 failures. Candidate readout events were obtained from BioMedTracker. Events were adjudicated using the outcome rules described below.

Initial prospective cohort

The initial prospective cohort was assembled from trials expected to report results within six months. The BioMedTracker calendar supplied 1,000 upcoming topline result events. Of these, 686 entries were mapped to ongoing ClinicalTrials.gov records, representing 525 unique trials; 496 trials were retained after dossier construction and exclusions. Searches of recent filings from more than 1,000 US listed sponsors identified 349 unique trials expected to report results during 2026. Of these, 210 were not already present in the BioMedTracker derived set and were added. Trials were excluded if they were not ongoing at assembly or could not be mapped to a unique registry record. Matching NCT IDs against the development and retrospective test cohorts identified nine and three overlapping trials, respectively, which were removed, leaving 694 trials.

Expanded prospective benchmark cohort

We queried the ClinicalTrials.gov v2 API for trials with an ongoing status: recruiting, not yet recruiting, active and not recruiting, or enrolling by invitation. The query returned 121,961 records. We retained trials whose lead sponsor or collaborator was a company listed on the New York Stock Exchange, Nasdaq, NYSE American, or Cboe, including listings through American depositary receipts and subsidiaries mapped to their listed parent (10,607 trials). Restriction to interventional studies left 8,686 trials; requiring a specified phase, including early phase 1, left 7,418. Excluding trials enrolling healthy volunteers left 6,855.

We checked by web search the 1,736 trials whose primary completion date had passed or that had posted results. Removing 872 trials with a disclosed final result or an interim stop left 5,983. We then excluded 86 trials whose dossiers listed no intervention, 20 trials present in the development cohort, 25 in the retrospective test cohort, and 43 from the initial prospective cohort whose readouts had been disclosed before forecast recording. The final benchmark contained 5,809 trials.

Manual curation and cost benchmark

A PhD student in biostatistics completed the same dossier schema for a simple random sample of 20 trials from the expanded benchmark of 5,809 trials. The curator received only the NCT IDs, was masked to TrialDossier outputs, and could consult ClinicalTrials.gov, the open web, and publicly accessible databases, including Google Search and Gemini summaries. Curation time was recorded for each trial. TrialDossier annotations were taken from the corresponding benchmark dossiers. Registry derived variables were not compared because registry records can change after posting and the website and API do not present identical content. The annotation comparison was restricted to molecular targets, target genes, and MOA.

A molecular target annotation was considered correct if it identified the primary pharmacological target of the core intervention. Target gene annotations were assessed against HGNC human gene symbols; protein aliases were not accepted as gene symbol matches. For interventions without a human gene target, a not applicable value, a blank value, or a non-gene entry was accepted. An applicable annotation was considered incorrect if any listed entry was wrong or no target was supplied. Incorrect entries included the drug itself, the gene encoding a protein drug or a symbol not approved by HGNC. For bispecific antibodies and T cell engagers, both bound antigens were accepted. For gene therapies and genetically modified cell products, the transgene and its product were accepted. An MOA annotation was correct if it identified the therapeutic target or modality without factual error and incomplete if it provided only a general description identifying neither.

Manual effort for the initial cohort was estimated by multiplying the mean curation time in the 20 trial sample by 694. Labor cost was calculated from elapsed hours using the July 2026 US average hourly earnings for private nonfarm employees of $37.62. 29 Automated expenditure was expressed as LLM token cost per trial and extrapolated to the same cohort.

MeSH audit

MeSH assignments were compared with the ClinicalTrials.gov MeSH module in the 308 trials of the initial prospective cohort for which the registry assigned a single term to the registered conditions. Assignments were concordant when their MeSH identifiers matched exactly. One author reviewed discordant pairs against the registered conditions and eligibility criteria and classified each pair as favoring TrialDossier, favoring ClinicalTrials.gov, or supporting both terms. A random sample of approximately 10% of concordant pairs was reviewed using the same procedure to identify clinically inappropriate assignments despite matching identifiers (Supplementary Tables 3 and 4).

Drug mechanism and target gene audit

The drug annotation audit used a random sample of 200 trials from the expanded prospective benchmark. Trial identifiers were sorted in ascending order, and two consecutive samples of 100 were drawn without replacement using one random number generator with seed 20261028. Each core intervention in a sampled trial constituted one drug record. ChEMBL release 37 annotations were retrieved through its web services, including molecular targets and target genes. 21 Coverage was calculated separately for MOA and target gene annotations as the proportion of eligible drug records with a valid annotation. ChEMBL matching for coverage used preferred names, synonyms, or validated searches. Direct concordance analysis was restricted to records matched by exact preferred name that had a target annotation in TrialDossier and a curated mechanism in ChEMBL (Supplementary Tables 5 6 7).

Gene annotations were split on delimiters and represented as HGNC symbols. Records were concordant if their normalized target names matched or, when names differed, their gene sets were identical; the latter were recorded as nomenclature or alias differences. Partial overlap, including subset relationships, was flagged as a potential difference in annotation scope. Records for which neither target names nor associated genes could be mapped reliably received the highest review priority. Claude Opus 5.5 reviewed flagged records using both sources' annotations, mechanism descriptions, and associated evidence. It assigned a predefined category with an evidencebased justification: alias or standard name differences; family or class enumeration; antibodydrug conjugate payload representation; perspective or granularity differences; modality representation; a documented target absent from ChEMBL; an absent or unknown ChEMBL mechanism despite supporting evidence; pharmacological direction or terminology differences; or genuine biological disagreement. The first five categories and pharmacological direction or terminology differences were treated as biologically reconcilable. Missing ChEMBL annotations were recorded as gaps, and genuine biological disagreement remained unresolved.

An author subsequently checked every model adjudicated record against the cited prescribing information or primary literature. Manual decisions superseded model classifications, and the final classification and rationale were retained. Annotation conventions affecting multiple records were recorded as systematic differences rather than independent errors. A random sample of approximately 10% of automatically concordant records underwent the same manual review.

Sponsor disclosure framing

We obtained BioMedTracker events dated June 2025 to March 2026, of which 58 were labeled unsuccessful.

Disclosure framing was classified as positive, negative, or neutral or unclear based on the dominant message conveyed by the title, subtitle, and conclusion concerning the target trial, drug, and indication. Positive framing emphasized favorable efficacy or clinical findings; negative framing emphasized endpoint failure, lack of efficacy, safety concerns, or development discontinuation; and neutral or unclear framing included process updates, disclosures without outcome claims, or mixed messages. For positively framed disclosures, we additionally recorded whether primary endpoint attainment was explicitly reported and the basis for favorable framing, including secondary endpoints, subgroup findings, or favorable interpretations of missed primary endpoints. All classifications were subsequently manually reviewed by the authors against the source disclosures.

Trial outcome adjudication

Trials were classified as successful or unsuccessful according to whether the reported results satisfied the prespecified criteria for the primary endpoints. Explicit statements of primary endpoint attainment or failure in trial readouts were used when consistent with the trial's prespecified design. When such statements were absent or ambiguous, the authors compared the reported results with the primary endpoints documented in ClinicalTrials.gov. Trials terminated before completion were classified as unsuccessful, unless attainment of the prespecified primary endpoint had been reported.

For trials with multiple primary endpoints, adjudication followed the prespecified criteria for overall trial success. Trials requiring all co-primary endpoints to be met were classified as unsuccessful if any required endpoint failed, whereas trials permitting success on a subset of primary endpoints were evaluated according to their prespecified multiplicity-adjusted testing strategy. Trials were classified as unresolved when the available results or prespecified decision criteria were insufficient to determine whether the primary endpoints had been met, or when conflicting information could not be resolved.

Reasoning expert

The reasoning expert used GPT-5 through Azure OpenAI Service. The model received the dossier fields listed in Supplementary Table 17. The GPT-5 reasoning expert had no web search or other external retrieval access during inference; its reported knowledge cutoff was September 30, 2024.

The model estimated the probability that the trial would meet its prespecified primary endpoint. Preprocessing recorded the trial phase and assigned clinical and therapeutic characteristics using predefined rules. Evidence was organized into eight domains covering clinical, preclinical, mechanistic, trial design, class precedent, population, sponsor, and evidence quality considerations.

Fixed prompts instructed the model to update a base rate using the supplied evidence (Supplementary Note 5). The base rate was fixed at 48% for phase 1 trials, whereas for other phases the model estimated a base rate interval from its own knowledge. Strong direct clinical evidence or disqualifying findings warranted larger probability updates, while indirect or uncertain evidence received less weight.

The model returned success probability intervals, supporting and opposing evidence, uncertainties, and a rationale. Each run's forecast was the midpoint of its success probability interval. Four runs were initiated per trial, and their successful outputs were averaged to obtain the reasoning expert's forecast.

On the expanded prospective benchmark, Claude Opus 5.5 was also run as a reasoning expert in Claude Code at extra-high thinking effort, with the prompts of the GPT-5 reasoning expert (Supplementary Note 5), the same dossier fields and no tool or web access.

Contribution of curated evidence

The reasoning expert received either the full dossier or registry information from the 150 validation trials. Both used the same GPT-5 model, prompt, and inference settings. The registry condition supplied ten fields: NCT ID, phase, core intervention, disease, endpoint profile, randomization, blinding, eligibility profile, sponsor, and sponsor type. Endpoint and eligibility profiles retained their stage 2 condensed representations. Each condition was run four times per trial, and run probabilities were averaged. Paired differences in the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC AUC) and average precision (AP) were estimated from 2,000 bootstrap resamples stratified by outcome, using seed 2, with 95% percentile confidence intervals (Supplementary Table 9). In the 69 adjudicated prospective trials, the registry condition was evaluated post hoc. Registry forecasts were generated after the outcomes had been adjudicated, from the same ten fields of the dossiers used for the prospective forecasts, with four runs per trial, and were compared with the reasoning expert's prospective forecasts. Differences in ROC AUC and in the proportion of trials correctly classified at the prespecified threshold of 0.5 were estimated with the same stratified bootstrap.

Embedding expert

Each trial was represented by a vector of 1,792 dimensions. The first 768 dimensions encoded the reasoning expert's assessments. In each of the four reasoning runs, the assessment of direct human evidence and the assessment of target and mechanism validation were joined into one text, which was embedded with Gemini embedding 001 at 768 dimensions using the classification task type. Each run vector was normalized to unit L2 norm, the vectors of runs with assessment text were averaged, and the mean was normalized again; a trial without assessment text in any run received a zero vector.

A further 512 dimensions encoded functional summaries retrieved through MyGene.info for HGNC symbols parsed from the target gene field. Another 512 dimensions encoded the scope note of the trial's MeSH term, retrieved from the National Library of Medicine MeSH service. The MeSH term was mapped from the dossier's disease field using the procedure of the TrialDossier disease module. If no term was available, the scope note was sought by disease name. If no scope note was available, the MeSH term or, failing that, the disease name was embedded. A gene symbol replaced its functional summary when MyGene.info returned no summary. A single space was embedded when no target gene or disease text was available. Target and disease embeddings were generated once per trial with Gemini embedding 001 at 512 dimensions and the classification task type, then normalized to unit L2 norm.

TabPFN-3 27, 28 classified the resulting vectors using the v3 classifier checkpoint in tabpfn 8.0.8. Settings were defaults except that limits on reference set size and the number of features were disabled with ignore_pretraining_limits. The embedding expert was applied to the retrospective test cohort and the initial prospective cohort, with random seed 42 for both. At inference, TabPFN-3 conditioned on labeled reference examples without gradient updates to the model parameters. The same 1,800 fitting trials were used as the reference set for every cohort.

Classifier comparison

Five classifier families were compared using the same features in the 1,800 fitting trials: TabPFN-3, random forest, 48 RealMLP (pytabkit 1.7.3), 49 logistic regression, and TabDPT (tabdpt 1.2.0). 50 Trials were grouped by primary investigational drug, defined as the first normalized noncomparator entry in the core intervention field. This produced 1,315 groups; ten trials without a drug name each formed a separate group. The outer cross-validation loop used five folds stratified by outcome and grouped by drug, with seed 42. No primary drug appeared in both the fitting and evaluation portions of an outer split.

Within each outer fitting split, a grouped inner loop with three folds selected hyperparameters by ROC AUC for random forest and logistic regression. Random forests used 500 trees, maximum features of either the square root of the feature count or 0.1 of the features, and minimum leaf sizes of 1 or 5. Logistic regression used standardized inputs and C values of 0.001, 0.01, 0.1, or 1. TabPFN-3, TabDPT, and RealMLP used default settings. Classifier performance was summarized by the mean and standard deviation of ROC AUC across the five outer folds; TabPFN-3 had the highest mean and was selected (Supplementary Table 10).

Prospective evaluation

For each of the 694 initial prospective trials, Claude Opus 5.5 in Claude Code identified public announcements of primary or topline results or trial discontinuation. An author manually checked every provisional outcome against the linked source and finalized labels using the criteria for recent readouts.

By October 20, 2026, 155 trials had reported results or discontinuation and 539 had no final result. We excluded 65 trials with disclosures before 29 July 2026. Of the remaining 90, nine lacked sufficient evidence for either outcome, seven reported an endpoint that did not match the registered primary endpoint, and five reported interim data only. These 21 trials remained unresolved. The evaluation therefore included 69 adjudicated trials: 40 successes and 29 failures (Supplementary Table 13).

Share price responses to disclosures

The announcement analysis began with the 69 adjudicated prospective trials. For each, we identified the listed company with the greatest exposure to the outcome and verified the date and time of the first public disclosure supporting the label. Event day 0 was the first trading session able to reflect the disclosure: the announcement date for releases before the open or during regular trading, and the next trading day for releases after the close or on a nontrading day. We excluded six trials without listed equity exposure, one with suspended trading, two whose trial status was updated by the company without a clear news announcement, four whose releases reported multiple eligible trials, and six with another eligible announcement by the same company on the same event day. The analysis contained 50 announcements: 22 unsuccessful and 28 successful trials (Supplementary Table 15). Abnormal returns were estimated using a single factor market model with the SPDR S&P Biotech exchange traded fund (XBI) as the index. Ordinary least squares was fitted to daily simple returns calculated from adjusted closing prices over trading days -130 through -11 relative to day 0. Cumulative abnormal returns (CARs) were obtained by summing the differences between observed and model predicted daily returns over days 0-2 for the primary analysis and days 0-1 and 0-5 for alternative windows. For listings outside the United States, local closing prices were paired with the XBI close on the same calendar date. Announcements accompanied by other material company news during the event window, including financial results, financing, or results from other trials, were flagged; the analyses were repeated after excluding them. CARs were compared between trials whose outcome the reasoning expert had predicted correctly or incorrectly at the 0.5 threshold, separately for successful and unsuccessful trials. Differences in median CAR (correctly minus incorrectly predicted) were summarized with 95% percentile bootstrap intervals from 2,000 resamples drawn with replacement within each group. In a sensitivity analysis, the comparison was repeated in all 62 adjudicated prospective trials for which a CAR could be estimated, including the announcements excluded above (Supplementary Note 4).

Zero-shot forecasts

For each trial of the expanded prospective benchmark, GPT 6.1 Sol was run in the Codex command line interface at medium thinking effort, in a separate session per trial, with the NCT ID as the only input. The fixed prompt (Supplementary Note 6) allowed live web search. The model returned an integer success probability from 0 to 100 with up to three reasons. Claude Opus 5.5 was run in Claude Code at medium thinking effort under the same conditions, with the prompt given in Supplementary Note 6, and returned the same output.

Statistical analysis

Success was the positive class. Discrimination was summarized by ROC AUC, calculated from the Mann-Whitney statistic with half credit for tied scores, and by AP. Probability accuracy was assessed with the Brier score and calibration with expected calibration error calculated using ten bins of equal width. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value used the 0.5 cutoff. With success as the positive class, specificity denotes the proportion of unsuccessful trials classified as failures.

Ninety-five percent confidence intervals for ROC AUC, AP, the Brier score, and expected calibration error were percentile bootstrap intervals from 2,000 resamples of trials with replacement, using seed 42. Resamples containing only one outcome class were discarded. Intervals for single proportions were two sided Clopper-Pearson exact intervals. The comparison of evidence conditions used its separately specified stratified bootstrap with seed 2. No adjustment was made for multiple comparisons.

Exploratory phase analyses compared trials registered as phase 3 with those registered as phase 1, phase 1/2, phase 2, or phase 2/3. Phase specific outcome proportions retained the registry phase categories. Analyses used Python 3.12.4 with NumPy 1.26.4, SciPy 1.13.1, scikit-learn 1.4.2, pandas 2.2.2, statsmodels 0.14.2, and matplotlib 3.8.4. [51] [52] [53] [54] [55] [56]
