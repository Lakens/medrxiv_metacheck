Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Human preimplantation embryos express the SARS-CoV-2 entry factors ACE2, TMPRSS2 and BSG (CD147) on trophectoderm (TE) cells, suggesting susceptibility to infection. Likewise, SARS-CoV-2-pseudotyped reporter viruses induced cytopathic effects in blastocysts, while the zona pellucida (ZP) appeared to confer partial protection. The impact of authentic SARS-CoV-2 infection on embryonic lineage specification and developmental competence has not been established. Here, we exposed cryopreserved human embryos, donated under ethics approval and informed consent, to authentic SARS-CoV-2 (B.1.617.2, Delta variant). While embryos with an intact ZP remained uninfected, laser-assisted hatching rendered 19/21 (90.5%) of embryos susceptible to infection, as confirmed by detection of viral dsRNA. Moreover, SARS-CoV-2 infection had a pronounced effect on blastocyst development with a possibly fatal outcome. Compared to mock-treated embryos (n = 47), infected blastocysts (n = 48) showed a significantly reduced surface area (p = 0.0003), reduced total cell number (p = 0.0139), and reduced NANOG + epiblast cell numbers (p = 0.0054) 24 hours post-exposure, while the number of GATA4 + primitive endoderm cells remained unaffected (p = 0.5255). Intriguingly, despite viral replication being largely confined to the trophectoderm (TE), this selective epiblast reduction suggests a disruption of TE-derived paracrine signaling required for epiblast specification. Finally, prolonged viral replication resulted in embryonic lysis and death. Digital transcriptomic profiling (mock, n = 29 embryos; SARS-CoV-2, n = 28 embryos; each in n = 5 minipools) revealed upregulation of innate antiviral and inflammatory genes, including IFIH1, IRF7, NOD2, and TNFRSF1A, as well as the recently identified SARS-CoV-2 entry factor TMEM106B, alongside altered developmental signatures concerning genome activation, pluripotency, and lineage-specific programs. These findings show that ZP disruption makes human blastocysts susceptible to productive SARS-CoV-2 infection, with adverse consequences for lineage allocation and direct implications for biosafety and virological counselling in medically assisted reproduction practice.

INTRODUCTION

When SARS-CoV-2 appeared as a global health emergency in early 2020, its rapid spread posed an unprecedented challenge to healthcare systems worldwide, causing excess morbidity and mortality and disrupting medical care across virtually all specialties. Among the populations of particular concern were pregnant women and those undergoing medically assisted reproduction (MAR): clinicians and patients facing infertility treatment confronted an immediate and unanswered question: was it safe to continue 1 ?

As clinical experience accumulated, evidence suggested that MAR technologies could generally be performed safely following recovery from SARS-CoV-2 infection, with clinical pregnancy, miscarriage, and live birth rates largely comparable to those of uninfected patients 2.

However, several studies reported reductions in embryo quality with molecular profiles of developmental delay, and an increase in miscarriage rate following recent infection, but the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood [2] [3] [4] . Moreover, clinical outcome studies cannot determine whether these observations result from direct viral effects on the embryo or indirect maternal and endometrial factors, underscoring the need for experimental models that specifically address embryonic susceptibility to infection.

Beyond the preimplantation period, SARS-CoV-2 infection during pregnancy has been associated with an increased risk of adverse maternal and fetal outcomes, including preterm birth, fetal growth restriction, placental pathology, and stillbirth 5 . The severity of these complications differed between SARS-CoV-2 variants. Epidemiological studies consistently identified the Delta (B.1.617.2) variant of concern as being associated with the highest risk of severe maternal disease and adverse perinatal outcomes compared with earlier and subsequent variants 6, 7 . Consequently, the Delta variant represents a clinically relevant model for investigating the effects of SARS-CoV-2 on early human development.

While these complications have been extensively investigated during established pregnancy, little is known about the effects of viral exposure before implantation, a question particularly relevant to MAR, where human gametes and embryos are manipulated outside the maternal environment and may be exposed to infectious agents during laboratory procedures. Although embryos are cultured under highly controlled conditions 8 , procedures such as oocyte retrieval, oocyte denudation (enzymatic softening and mechanical stripping of the cumulus and corona cells from an egg), embryo culture, assisted hatching, trophectoderm (TE) biopsy, and cryopreservation can transiently compromise their natural barriers.

Human embryonic development begins with fertilization, when a sperm cell fuses with an oocyte to form a totipotent zygote (day 1 post-fertilization, dpf) 9 . The embryo subsequently undergoes a series of cleavage divisions while enclosed within the zona pellucida (ZP), an extracellular glycoprotein matrix secreted during folliculogenesis 10 , reaching the compacted morula stage by approximately 4 dpf 11 . Around 5 dpf, cavitation gives rise to the blastocyst, composed of an outer TE and an inner cell mass (ICM) 12 . The ICM subsequently segregates into the NANOG-positive epiblast, which gives rise to the fetus, and the GATA4-positive primitive endoderm (hypoblast), which contributes to extraembryonic tissues 12, 13 . Throughout this period, the embryo remains enclosed within the ZP, which mediates speciesspecific sperm binding and prevents polyspermy, while protecting the conceptus from mechanical stress and enzymatic degradation during its transit through the fallopian tube 14, 15.

Owing to its semi-permeable structure, the ZP also restricts the passage of large macromolecules and pathogens, including several viruses such as Adenovirus and SARS-CoV-2 16, 17 . As the blastocyst expands, mechanical pressure combined with enzymatic degradation by TE-secreted and uterine proteases causes the ZP to thin and rupture, a process termed hatching, typically occurring at 5-6 dpf 18, 19 . Hatching precedes implantation. It enables direct contact between the TE and the endometrial epithelium, thereby facilitating blastocyst apposition, adhesion, and subsequent invasion into the endometrium. However, hatching also marks the loss of the embryo's protective shell, exposing the conceptus directly to the uterine environment for the first time, a vulnerability that becomes particularly relevant when considering exposure to infectious agents.

A molecular basis for embryo susceptibility was established when the entry receptors angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2), Basigin (BSG/CD147), and transmembrane serine protease 2 (TMPRSS2), the principal host factors mediating SARS-CoV-2 cell entry, were detected on TE cells of human blastocysts, suggesting that preimplantation embryos possess the machinery required for viral infection 20, 21 . However, viral RNA, measured by RT-PCR, has not been detected in follicular fluid, cumulus cells, or endometrial tissue of SARS-CoV-2positive women 22 . Likewise, RT-PCR has detected viral RNA only very infrequently in the semen of infected males (pooled prevalence 1.76%) 23 . This suggests that the periconceptional environment may limit natural viral exposure at the gamete stage, although it does not exclude exposure, particularly in the laboratory during MAR.

A previous study showed that human blastocysts can be infected in vitro with SARS-CoV-2 spike-pseudotyped reporter virions; the resulting transduced embryos exhibited impaired developmental progression, and the study suggested that an intact ZP may protect against viral entry 17 . These findings provided the first evidence that preimplantation embryos might be susceptible to SARS-CoV-2 under experimental conditions.

While the above study modeled direct viral exposure in vitro, subsequent studies examined embryos derived from mothers with acute infection and found evidence of impaired . development in more detail. Maternal infection prolonged the appearance of pronuclei, altered the expression of embryonic genome activation-related genes, and delayed the timing of the two-, three-, four-, and five-cell stages as well as blastulation. It was also associated with altered energy metabolism, enhanced glycolysis, and abnormal methylation/demethylation patterns in preimplantation embryos 4, 24.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethical statement

Human cryopreserved embryos were used following approval by the local ethical committee of UZ Leuven and UZ Brussel (BUN1432020000258) and the Belgian Federal Committee for research on human embryos (ADV_085bis_UZBrussel). Surplus cryopreserved embryos were donated to research, after informed written consent and 5 years of cryopreservation, by patients who had completed their fertility treatment at Brussels IVF (UZ Brussel) and no longer needed them.

Culture of human embryos

Vitrified embryos were warmed and cultured at the research lab of Brussels IVF as previously described 25, 26 . Briefly, 3 dpf (8-12 cell stage) embryos and 5 dpf (blastocyst stage) embryos, the latter derived from preimplantation genetic testing (PGT) cycles and therefore already possessing a ZP opened using LAH, were warmed using the Vit Kit-Thaw kit (90137-SO, Irvine Scientific) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The 3 dpf embryos were cultured in equilibrated 25 μl droplets of blastocyst medium (83060010, ORIGIO Sequential Blast, with phenol red) overlaid with Ovoil (10174, Vitrolife) to prevent evaporation, at 37 °C, 5% O2, and 6% CO2 in a G185 flatbed incubator (K-Systems, CooperSurgical) for 24 h until 4 dpf before transport to the BSL-3 facility at the Rega Institute (KU Leuven). The 5 dpf embryos were allowed a 2-hour recovery period after warming before further transport. Only embryos that resumed development after cryopreservation were used in subsequent experiments; embryos showing signs of degeneration (including cytoplasmic vacuolization, cellular fragmentation, or cell lysis) in more than one cell were discarded. No clinical information on the donors, including maternal conditions or the indication for PGT, was available, and these factors could therefore not be accounted for in the analysis.

Following culture or recovery, embryos were transferred into microvials (5000-1020, Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing pre-equilibrated blastocyst medium and placed in a G95 Portable Incubator (K-Systems, CooperSurgical) at 37 °C for transport to the BSL-3 facility. There, embryos were immediately recovered from the microvials into fresh, pre-equilibrated 25 μl droplets under the same culture conditions. The 4 dpf embryos were cultured for a further 24 h until reaching the 5 dpf blastocyst stage before assignment to experimental groups; the 5 dpf embryos were randomly assigned to experimental groups directly following transport and reequilibration. Embryos from the same experiment were warmed, cultured, and infected on the same day under identical conditions, so that each infected group had a matched mock group, and were subsequently allocated to downstream analysis by immunofluorescence or nCounter-based transcriptomic profiling; for the latter, embryos were transferred into 20 μl RLT lysis buffer (Qiagen) and snap-frozen.

Opening/removal of zona pellucida

For the 3 dpf embryo groups, the ZP was opened by either laser-assisted hatching (LAH) or removed by chemical treatment with acid Tyrode's solution (TS, pH 2.5 ± 0.3; T1788, Sigma-Aldrich).

LAH was performed as previously described 27 . Briefly, embryos were immobilized with the ZP positioned in the focal plane, and a series of consecutive laser pulses (80% intensity, millisecond-range pulse duration) was applied to a region distant from the blastomeres to create an opening of approximately 20 µm. Care was taken to avoid direct laser exposure of embryonic cells, after which the embryos were washed and returned to culture. Chemical ZP removal was performed in 50 μl microdroplets of acid Tyrode's solution or HEPES (4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid; H6147, Sigma-Aldrich) medium supplemented with human serum albumin, under an Ivesta 3 Greenough stereomicroscope with integrated camera (Leica). Embryos were first rinsed in HEPES medium by gentle pipetting, then transferred to an acid droplet, where the outer zona layer was dissolved by gentle pipetting under continuous observation. Once the outer layer was nearly fully dissolved, pipetting was stopped immediately to avoid damaging the embryo. Embryos were then washed three times in HEPES medium and transferred to a new culture dish.

SARS-CoV-2 viral infection

All work involving live virus was conducted in the high-containment BSL-3 facilities of the KU Leuven Rega Institute under license SBB219.2024/1599, in accordance with institutional guidelines. SARS-CoV-2 Delta strain (B.1.617.2; EPI_ISL_2425097) virus stock was propagated in Vero E6 cells, and the median tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) was determined by end-point titration using A549-Dual hACE2-TMPRSS2 cells (Invivogen, Cat. No. a549d-cov2r), as previously described 28.

For the infection assay, blastocyst-stage embryos were placed in equilibrated 25 μl droplets of blastocyst medium mixed either with Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, 10564011, Gibco) supplemented with fetal bovine serum (2%, A5256701, Gibco) as a control, or with virus at concentrations of 3x10 3 , 3x10 4 , and 3x10 5 TCID50/ml. Embryos were cultured at 37 °C, 5% O2, and 6% CO2 for 24 h, until 6 dpf.

Immunofluorescence

Embryos were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (047392.9L, Thermo Scientific Chemicals) in 1X PBS (10010023, Gibco) for 10 min at room temperature (RT) to cross-link proteins and preserve cellular integrity. After fixation, embryos were washed in 2% BSA (A2153, Sigma-Aldrich) in 1X PBS for 5 min, then permeabilized in 0.1% Triton™ X-100 (X100, Sigma-Aldrich) in 1X PBS for 20 min at RT with gentle side-to-side agitation on a reciprocal shaker at 100 rpm.

To reduce non-specific antibody binding, embryos were transferred to a blocking buffer of 10% FBS and 2% BSA in 1X PBS and incubated for 1 h at RT. Embryos were then incubated overnight at 4 °C in primary antibody (Table 1) diluted in 10% FBS-2% BSA in 1X PBS.

Following overnight incubation, embryos were washed in 2% BSA-1X PBS (3 × 10 min) and incubated for 1 h in secondary antibody (Table 1) diluted in 10% FBS-2% BSA in 1X PBS. Embryos were imaged using a confocal microscope (LSM800 equipped with Airyscan, ZEISS) at 40X magnification. Z-stack scans were acquired for whole-embryo visualization and 3D reconstruction. Images were analyzed in FIJI 29 . Each channel was manually adjusted for brightness and contrast, with the same settings applied across all images of a given stain within each experiment. The number of cells belonging to each embryonic lineage (NANOG for epiblast, GATA4 for primitive endoderm) was assessed manually; a nucleus was considered positive if its signal was visibly above background.

Embryo size measurement

Embryo size was estimated from images taken at [0, 18, and 24 hpi] (Leica S9i brightfield images). In Fiji, the vertical and horizontal diameters of each embryo were measured, and the surface (S) was estimated as S = vertical diameter x horizontal diameter [mm 2 ].

Digital transcriptomics (nCounter)

Digital transcriptomics (nCounter platform, Nanostring Ltd.) was performed as previously described for other viral pathologies and their vaccine outcomes [30] [31] [32] . nCounter counts transcripts directly by hybridization to target-specific probes, without reverse transcription or amplification, which makes it suitable for very low RNA inputs. In brief, embryo lysates were hybridized to unique capture/reporter pairs (50 bp each) targeting 770 transcripts (730 immune transcripts and 40 internal controls present in the "Human Myeloid Innate Immunity" Panel V2, as well as virological transcripts (SARS-CoV-2 transcripts and ACE2/TMPRSS2 receptors), 6 positive and 8 negative control probes (all from NanoString). Results were sequentially corrected for background (negative control probes), technical variation (positive control probes), and RNA content (housekeeping genes).

A single blastocyst does not contain enough RNA to be reliably detected on this platform, so 5-6 embryos were pooled. Pool sizes were kept as equal as possible between mock and infected groups. Phenotypic readouts were assessed per embryo, whereas transcriptomic data represent pool-level measurements.

Counts were corrected sequentially for background (negative control probes), technical variation (positive control probes), and RNA content (housekeeping genes). nCounter is a targeted method: the panel focuses on innate immunity and was not designed for embryonic development, so genes relevant to early development may be missing, and novel transcripts cannot be identified. The transcriptomic findings are therefore considered exploratory.

nCounter gene-expression and module analysis

Each nCounter sample corresponded to a pool of five to six embryos from a single group and experiment (see Digital transcriptomics). Housekeeping gene-normalized counts were log2transformed after addition of a pseudocount of 1, and genes with zero variance across samples were excluded from statistical analyses. Differential expression between SARS-CoV-2-treated and matched mock-treated pools from the same experiment was analyzed using limma, with experiment and treatment included in the linear model to account for the paired design.

Empirical Bayes moderation was applied using robust variance estimation and mean-variance trend modelling. The SARS-CoV-2-versus-mock contrast was used to calculate log2 fold changes, moderated t-statistics, and nominal p values, and p values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Differentially expressed genes were defined by an absolute log2 fold change of at least 1 (a two-fold change in expression) and a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05.

Principal component analysis was performed on log2-transformed expression values of all non-constant genes, without variance scaling. For visualization, expression values were standardized separately for each gene across samples and displayed as gene-wise z-scores.

Predefined transcriptional modules were obtained from a previously published study 12 . For each module, only genes that were both included in the published module definition and represented in the nCounter panel were retained for analysis (Tables 2 and 3). Module scores were calculated as the mean z-score of all non-constant genes assigned to that module, and modules with fewer than two informative genes were excluded. Module scores were compared between matched SARS-CoV-2-and mock-treated pools using two-sided paired t tests, followed by Benjamini-Hochberg correction across modules. All analyses were performed in R using a custom script.

RESULTS

Laser-assisted hatching establishes a robust model for SARS-CoV-2 infection of human blastocysts

To establish a reproducible model for SARS-CoV-2 infection of human preimplantation embryos, we first compared two approaches to compromise the ZP: laser-assisted hatching (LAH) to create an opening in the ZP and treatment with Tyrode's acid solution (TS) to dissolve the ZP (Figure 1A). Since it was previously shown that intact ZP might restrict efficient viral entry, ZP breaching is a prerequisite for establishing a reliable infection model 17.

Donated 3 dpf embryos were warmed at IVF Brussel and assigned to one of three groups. In the LAH group, partial ZP ablation was performed on the day of warming after embryo recovery.

In the untreated group, the ZP was left intact throughout to assess susceptibility to infection without ZP manipulation. In the TS group, the ZP likewise remained intact at this stage. All three groups were cultured to 4 dpf and transported to the BSL-3 facility, where TS-designated embryos underwent gentle chemical ZP removal following a 2-hour recovery period. Culture then continued to 5 dpf, when embryos were randomly assigned to SARS-CoV-2 or mock infection. Untreated, ZP-intact embryos were challenged with 3x10⁵ TCID50/ml of the SARS-CoV-2 (Delta) variant, whereas LAH-and TS-treated embryos were infected with 3x10⁴ TCID50/ml. All embryos were fixed 24 hours post-infection (hpi) for immunofluorescence analysis.

LAH consistently generated embryos susceptible to productive infection. Double-stranded RNA (dsRNA), detected by J2 immunostaining as a marker of active viral replication 33 , was present in 19 of 21 LAH-treated embryos (90.5%), compared with only 6 of 27 TS-treated embryos (22.2%; Fisher's exact test, p < 0.0001) at the viral dose used for quantification (3x10 3 TCID50/ml) (Figure 1B). In contrast, embryos with a completely intact ZP remained dsRNA-negative despite exposure to a ten-fold higher viral inoculum (3x10 5 TCID50/ml), demonstrating that an intact ZP efficiently prevents SARS-CoV-2 infection. Consistent with this, partially hatched embryos showed dsRNA signal restricted to the exposed TE cells with no signal detected in the region still covered by ZP, indicating that the ZP acts as a local physical barrier to infection rather than conferring protection at the whole-embryo level (Supplementary Figure 1A).

Interestingly, one embryo with an apparently intact ZP displayed a dsRNA-positive signal, localized to a small, discrete spot rather than distributed across the embryo, with signal confined to the cytoplasm of approximately two TE cells (Supplementary Figure 1B). As this embryo originated from intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI), the infection may have occurred through the microscopic opening created during sperm injection.

TS-treated embryos also exhibited reduced tolerance to viral exposure. At 3x10 4 TCID50/ml, the dose used for LAH embryos, 9 of 13 TS-treated embryos degenerated following infection (Supplementary Figure 1C). To preserve embryo viability, the viral inoculum for the TS group was therefore reduced to 3x10 3 TCID50/ml. The 22.2% infection rate and all subsequent analyses of TS-treated embryos are based on this lower viral dose.

Representative immunofluorescence images illustrate the marked difference in infection efficiency between intact and removed ZP (Figure 1C). dsRNA signal was distributed throughout the TE in the LAH and TS groups, with localized patches of stronger signal.

Together, these findings demonstrate that efficient disruption of the ZP is essential for productive SARS-CoV-2 infection of human blastocysts. Compared with TS treatment, LAH provided substantially higher infection efficiency while maintaining embryo viability, establishing it as the preferred method for generating a robust and reproducible embryo infection model.

SARS-CoV-2 infection reduces blastocyst growth, selectively affecting the epiblast lineage

Following establishment of the infection model, we investigated the effect of SARS-CoV-2 infection on blastocyst development using donated 5 dpf blastocysts that had previously undergone TE biopsy for preimplantation genetic testing (PGT) (Figure 2A). As these embryos already had a laser-created opening in the ZP, no additional manipulation was required before infection. After warming, embryos were transported to the BSL-3 facility, allowed to recover for 2 h, and either mock-infected or infected with the SARS-CoV-2 B.1.617.2 (Delta) variant (3x10 4 TCID50/ml). At 24 hpi, embryos were fixed for immunofluorescence analysis or processed for digital transcriptomic profiling using the NanoString nCounter platform. Representative immunofluorescence images of mock-and SARS-CoV-2-infected blastocysts are shown in Figure 2B. SARS-CoV-2 infection induces innate antiviral and inflammatory transcriptional responses in human blastocysts

To characterize the molecular response of human blastocysts to SARS-CoV-2 infection, digital transcriptomic profiling was performed using a NanoString nCounter platform. A total of 57 embryos (29 mock and 28 SARS-CoV-2-infected) originating from five independent experimental cohorts collected as minipools (n = 5) of similarly treated embryos were analyzed using a targeted panel comprising more than 750 genes associated with immune responses and viral infection 32, 34, 35.

Hierarchical clustering based on the SARS-CoV-2 panel genes indicative of the presence of viral genomic (orf1ab) and antigenomic (orf1ab_rev) as well as subgenomic RNAs (sgRNAs: nucleocapsid, orf7a, envelope, orf3a, and spike) revealed clear expression of viral transcripts in infected embryos from experimental cohorts 1, 2, and 5, whereas embryos from cohorts 3 and 4 showed little or no detectable viral gene expression (Figure 3A). While detection of both antigenomic and sgRNA provides unequivocal evidence for successful infection and active viral replication, this pattern indicates variability in infection efficiency between individual cohorts despite identical experimental conditions during inoculation.

To investigate global transcriptional changes due to SARS-CoV-2 exposure, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using all genes included in the NanoString panel.

Most mock-and SARS-CoV-2-infected embryos showed substantial overlap, reflecting the overall similarity of transcriptomes. However, infected embryos from experimental cohorts 1 and 5 separated from the remaining samples along the principal components, consistent with their higher abundance of viral transcripts observed in the hierarchical clustering analysis (Figure 3B). Together, these findings indicate that the embryos with abundant viral transcript expression displayed the most distinct transcriptional profiles, whereas infected embryos with little or no detectable viral RNA clustered more closely with mock-treated controls. Differential expression analysis identified a distinct host transcriptional response to SARS-CoV-2 infection (Figure 3C). Genes involved in viral RNA sensing and innate antiviral signaling were upregulated in infected embryos, including IFIH1 (encoding MDA5, a cytosolic sensor of viral double-stranded RNA), IRF7 (a key transcriptional regulator of type I interferon responses), and USP18 (an interferon-stimulated gene involved in regulation of interferon signaling) [36] [37] [38] .

NOD2, an intracellular pattern-recognition receptor involved in NF-κB activation 39 , was similarly upregulated. TMEM106B, an ACE2-independent SARS-CoV-2 entry factor 40 , was also increased following infection. Infection further induced genes associated with inflammatory signaling, including TNFRSF1A, TNFRSF1B, and TNFAIP6. In addition, several genes linked to developmental processes were differentially expressed, including FGF18 and ERVW-1, which encodes syncytin-1, a mediator of trophoblast fusion and differentiation 41.

To examine the response of individual genes, the normalized expression of selected genes was compared between matched mock and SARS-CoV-2-infected pools (Figure 3D, E).

Infection was associated with higher expression of NOD2 (p = 0.0312), TNFRSF1A (p = 0.0101), FGF18 (p = 0.0451), and ERVW-1 (p = 0.0064). The antiviral genes IFIH1 and IRF7 showed a trend toward increased expression that did not reach significance in this paired comparison (p = 0.1384 and p = 0.1739, respectively).

Together, these findings suggest that SARS-CoV-2 infection is associated with an innate antiviral and inflammatory transcriptional response in human blastocysts, accompanied by trends toward altered expression of genes involved in early embryonic development. The response appeared to be stronger in pools with higher viral transcript abundance than in pools with few viral transcripts, although this was not formally tested.

Infected embryos show trends toward altered developmental transcriptional programs

To investigate whether SARS-CoV-2 infection influences transcriptional programs associated with early human embryonic development, embryo pool transcriptomes were scored against eight previously defined developmental gene modules representing distinct stages and lineages of preimplantation development, including embryonic genome activation, pluripotency, epigenetic regulation, primitive endoderm, and TE identities (Figure 4A) 12.

Hierarchical clustering of developmental module genes revealed a pattern similar to that observed for the SARS-CoV-2 viral gene panel (Figure 3A). Infected pools from experimental cohorts 1, 2, and 5, which exhibited the highest viral transcript abundance, also showed increased expression across multiple developmental modules compared with their matched mock controls, whereas infected embryos from cohorts 3 and 4 clustered more closely with mock-treated embryos (Figure 4A).

Module scores were subsequently compared between matched mock-and SARS-CoV-2infected pools from each experimental cohort (Figure 4B). Although none of the eight developmental modules reached statistical significance following correction for multiple testing (all FDR > 0.05), most modules, including those associated with embryonic genome activation (ZSCAN4 and DUXA), pluripotency (POU5F1B), epigenetic regulation (DNMT3L and IFI16), and TE identity (GATA2 and NR2F2), showed higher scores in infected embryos across the majority of experimental cohorts. In contrast, the primitive endoderm module (GATA4) showed no consistent difference (FDR = 0.822). Across several developmental programs, module scores were consistently higher in infected pools, most clearly in the cohorts with the highest viral transcript abundance. None of these differences reached statistical significance after correction for multiple testing, and because transcriptomes were measured in pools of embryos from five matched experiments, the results reflect average responses and should be considered exploratory. Nevertheless, they raise the possibility that SARS-CoV-2 infection is accompanied by shifts in embryonic transcriptional state, and they provide effect-size estimates for designing larger studies. Single-cell RNA sequencing of individual embryos, together with a panel that covers developmental genes, would show which cells and lineages are affected and whether the response is uniform across embryos, and functional analysis of embryo development after infection would test whether these shifts have consequences. While LAH consistently resulted in efficient viral infection, TS treatment frequently left residual zona material surrounding the embryo, substantially reducing infection efficiency. This observation is consistent with the bilayered structure of the human ZP, in which the outer layer is more readily dissolved while the compact inner layer is considerably more resistant to acidic digestion 42, 43 . Beyond explaining the lower infection rate observed after Tyrode treatment, this offers a practical methodological consideration for future studies of pathogen interactions with human embryos and for investigating differences in the physiological function of individual ZP layers.

Notably, all but one embryo with an intact ZP remained protected. The exception showed viral replication despite apparently preserved zona integrity, but had previously undergone ICSI, suggesting that even submicroscopic discontinuities as created by microneedle puncture during microinjection may suffice to permit viral access, raising the possibility that any experimental procedure compromising ZP integrity may influence susceptibility.

Following ZP disruption, SARS-CoV-2 infection impaired blastocyst development, as evidenced by reduced embryo expansion, decreased total cell number, and a selective reduction in NANOG-positive epiblast cells, while GATA4-positive primitive endoderm cell numbers remained unchanged. The overall reduction in embryo growth is consistent with a generalized developmental impairment, whereas the disproportionate loss of epiblast cells suggests that this lineage may be particularly vulnerable to the downstream consequences of infection. Because viral replication was predominantly observed within the TE cell lineage, these developmental alterations are unlikely to result solely from direct infection of the embryonic lineages themselves but rather from a broader perturbation of the overall developmental program.

A plausible explanation is that SARS-CoV-2 infection disrupts the paracrine communication between the TE and the ICM. Beyond its structural role in blastocyst formation, the TE functions as an important signaling niche for the ICM by secreting factors such as IL-6 that support normal blastocyst development and lineage progression 44 . In addition, TE-derived cells possess a functional type I interferon system capable of protecting embryonic stem cells through paracrine signaling, whereas embryonic stem cells themselves exhibit limited capacity to produce antiviral interferons 45 . Disruption of this supportive relationship by viral infection therefore represents a possible mechanistic link by which TE infection could selectively impair epiblast development without requiring direct viral replication within the epiblast. For example, infection of pre-and peri-implantation embryos with teratogenic Zika virus was largely restricted to the TE yet was sufficient to impair subsequent embryonic and fetal development 46 , supporting the concept that dysfunction of this extraembryonic lineage can influence embryonic development even in the absence of direct infection of the epiblast. This evidence is indirect, as it comes from other models and was not designed to test TE-epiblast signaling, and this mechanism was not tested in the present study; it should therefore be seen as a hypothesis.

Alternative explanations should also be considered. The epiblast may be intrinsically more sensitive than the primitive endoderm to inflammatory stress induced by viral infection.

Similarly, the observed phenotype may reflect a generalized developmental delay that disproportionately affects the rapidly expanding epiblast population. The present study cannot distinguish between these possibilities. Future research combining lineage-specific viral detection with single-cell transcriptomics and TE-ICM co-culture systems will be important to determine whether disrupted paracrine signaling represents a direct mechanistic link between TE infection and impaired epiblast development.

Transcriptomic profiling further revealed that SARS-CoV-2 infection induces an intrinsic antiviral response in pools of preimplantation embryos. Increased expression of IFIH1, IRF7, USP18, NOD2, and TNF-associated signaling pathways indicates that embryonic cells can detect viral infection and activate innate immune programs before implantation, consistent with previous evidence that human preimplantation embryos express functional pattern-recognition receptors and respond to pathogen-associated stimuli 47 . The increased expression of TMEM106B, recently proposed as an ACE2-independent host factor for SARS-CoV-2 entry 48, 49 , raises the possibility that alternative viral entry mechanisms may contribute to embryonic susceptibility, warranting further investigation.

The extent of transcriptional responses varied largely between individual embryo cohorts, with two cohorts showing stronger induction of antiviral as well as interference with developmental gene modules than the others. Obviously, the observed variability may reflect variation in infection efficiency. However, more likely, differences in embryo quality, such as donor-specific characteristics and an experimentally more difficult-to-control timing of developmental stages in the already heterogeneous pools, can be expected to have a greater impact. Limitations of the study

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, our study evaluated a single SARS-CoV-2 variant, Delta (B.1.617.2), and viral entry or replication characteristics may differ between variants. However, the latter variant may serve as a representative prototype, given that the Delta variant, which became the dominant global strain by mid-to-late 2021 and drove a surge in infections, exhibited a particularly strong impact on reproductive health 6 . Moreover, the direct and timed exposure of ZP-disrupted embryos to a defined viral inoculum does not recapitulate the complex conditions of natural conception. The in vitro system further lacks maternal immune, endocrine, and uterine interactions that shape early pregnancy. Either contribution will need to be studied in future experiments, for which we herewith provide a base dataset. In addition, infection was confirmed by dsRNA staining, which indicates viral replication but does not quantify the level of infection per embryo. Second, digital transcriptomics (nCounter) is a targeted method. The panel used here focuses on innate immunity and was not designed for early embryonic or pregnancy development, so genes relevant to these processes may have been missed, and novel transcripts or pathways cannot be identified. Its sensitivity, of digital transcriptomics, though possibly equally, if not more, sensitive than other methodologies such as RNA sequencing 60 is also limited. In combination with the low cell number of human preimplantation blastocysts at 6 dpf (~200) 61 and resulting low RNA yields, this required pooling of individual embryos. While use of minipools may have helped average experimental errors between biological replicates, a striking inter-cohort variability remains. Likewise, the limited number of pools available for transcriptomic analysis resulted in reduced statistical power for exploratory pathway and developmental module analyses; therefore, these findings require validation in larger cohorts. Nevertheless, disruption of certain transcriptional patterns linked to an established pseudotime of early development suggests a risk of loss of lineage commitment and stage-specific vulnerabilities 12 . However, pinpointing such specific vulnerabilities should be feasible from transcriptional data sets as presented herein. Third, the embryos were surplus embryos donated to research, and no clinical background information on the donors, such as maternal conditions or the indication for PGT, was available, and the sex of the embryos was unknown. These factors could therefore not be taken into account. Random allocation of embryos to matched mock and infected groups within each experiment was used so that such variability would be distributed across both groups. Finally, although our data support a model in which TE infection indirectly affects epiblast development, the underlying mechanisms remain to be established. Targeted disruption of developmental programs, such as by viral infection in the current study, may help elucidate such natural dependencies. The in vitro system also cannot capture maternal factors or assess longer-term consequences after implantation, which will require further investigation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our findings identify the human ZP as the principal barrier preventing SARS-CoV-2 infection of preimplantation embryos. Once this barrier is disrupted, authentic SARS-CoV-2 readily infects blastocysts, impairs embryonic growth, selectively affects epiblast development, and induces a cell-intrinsic antiviral response. While direct embryonic infection during natural conception is likely uncommon, these findings provide mechanistic insight into early embryonic susceptibility to viral infection and underscore the importance of maintaining appropriate biosafety considerations during MAR procedures.

Only five experiments were analyzed, and embryos had to be pooled because a single embryo does not yield enough RNA for detection, so individual embryos could not be studied; our conclusions are therefore preliminary. The variability we observed between experiments may partly reflect the natural biological variability of human embryos, which should be kept in mind when interpreting responses to viral infection.While our experimental model demonstrates that human blastocysts can support SARS-CoV-2 infection as soon as the ZP barrier is compromised, the relevance of this scenario to natural conception requires careful consideration. Additional physiological barriers can be expected to limit infection of the embryo during natural conception. The intact ZP physically isolates the preimplantation embryo during transit through the reproductive tract, and productive SARS-CoV-2 infection of the female reproductive tract itself appears uncommon. Indeed, viral mRNA was undetectable in endometrial biopsies from women with active COVID-19 despite ACE2 expression in this tissue50 . Although SARS-CoV-2 RNA has been sporadically detected in semen and reproductive tissues, studies in acute COVID-19 have generally failed to demonstrate consistent seminal shedding51 . Detection of viral RNA in semen from some individuals with Long COVID has raised the possibility of persistent reproductive tract reservoirs52 . However, whether this represents infectious virus with implications for fertility and transmission remains unresolved.Embryonic infection in vivo would additionally require a tightly synchronized sequence of events, including maternal infection during the narrow preimplantation window and sufficient viral dissemination to the uterine environment before implantation. Direct embryonic infection during natural conception is therefore likely rare; adverse reproductive outcomes associated with maternal COVID-19 more plausibly arise through indirect mechanisms, including systemic inflammation, cytokine dysregulation, altered endometrial receptivity, or placental dysfunction after implantation[53] [54] [55] . Maternal immune activation may represent an additional mechanism by which infection can influence early development. Although a controlled inflammatory response is an integral part of implantation and early pregnancy, excessive or dysregulated production of inflammatory mediators can disrupt the cellular and molecular processes required for normal embryonic development56, 57 . Thus, even in the absence of direct embryonic infection, maternal infection-associated inflammation may create an adverse developmental environment during critical windows of early pregnancy.The implications may differ in MAR settings, where procedures such as LAH or ICSI intentionally breach the ZP. Laboratory transmission of SARS-CoV-2 is considered extremely unlikely under modern biosafety protocols58, 59 , but our findings show that embryos become susceptible once this physical barrier is compromised. The viral inoculum used here was chosen to establish a reliable experimental infection and does not reflect typical clinical exposure levels; the consequences of lower-dose exposure, and whether limited viral replication could influence implantation, lineage specification, or later fetal development, remain open questions. Importantly, these findings do not suggest that routine IVF procedures pose a substantial risk of SARS-CoV-2 transmission, or viral infections in general. Nevertheless, they highlight the importance of understanding how specific micromanipulation procedures may alter the physical barriers that normally protect the embryo. Several experiments could validate our main conclusions. First, repeating the single-cell RNA sequencing approach used to define the developmental modules 12 on infected and mock embryos would show which cells and lineages respond, and whether the response is uniform across embryos. Second, following individual embryos, with measurement of their development, lineage composition, and gene expression, would link infection directly to fate, which pooling did not allow. Third, larger cohorts, other viral variants, and quantitative measurement of infection in each embryo would test whether the response depends on the amount of virus.
