Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. 1. Introduction

Type B aortic dissection (TBAD) is a life-threatening cardiovascular condition routinely evaluated and monitored using computed tomography angiography (CTA), which represents the clinical standard imaging modality for diagnostic assessment ( [9]). TBAD is characterised by an intimal tear that allows blood to enter the aortic wall, creating a false lumen (FL) alongside the true lumen (TL) ( [10]). In uncomplicated cases, management is predominantly conservative and relies on lifelong imaging surveillance ( [1]), whereas thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) or the STABILISE technique are indicated in the presence of complications such as malperfusion or impending rupture, and may also be considered in selected high-risk patients [20, 8]. Current clinical decision-making and evaluation of aortic remodeling are largely based on the extent of the dissection, false lumen patency, total aortic diameter, and dimensional changes in both lumina ( [19]). Although these metrics are clinically established, they provide only a partial representation of the disease, failing to capture the full spatial complexity of aortic remodeling and offering limited predictive value for late aortic events. The conventional definition of "complete remodeling" is dichotomous and based on complete false lumen thrombosis or disappearance, whereas "partial" or "positive remodeling" encompasses a wide spectrum of heterogeneous morphological changes ( [10]). To better characterise these intermediate conditions, increasing attention has been directed toward volumetric and geometric descriptors of aortic dissection.

Several studies have demonstrated the potential of volumetric analysis. Sobocinski et al. quantified true and false lumen volumes to compare surgical strategies ( [25]), while subsequent works showed that volumetric parameters can predict long-term aortic events ( [5, 13]). In particular, the perfusion dissection index (PDI), defined as the ratio between perfused FL volume and total perfused aortic volume, has been suggested as a robust biomarker of aortic remodeling and a predictor of reintervention ( [11]). Despite this growing evidence, the clinical adoption of volumetric analysis remains limited by the need for accurate segmentation of aortic lumen compartments, a process that is time-consuming when performed manually, typically requiring 60-75 minutes per scan. This limitation is further exacerbated in postoperative scenarios, where stent-induced artifacts and altered vascular geometry complicate image interpretation and delay quantitative assessment ( [24]). Deep learning has enabled substantial progress in automated image processing ( [23]), and especially in aortic segmentation, with approaches achieving high accuracy in delineating TL and FL on preoperative CTA datasets ( [3, 7, 14, 18, 28]). These methods, based on convolutional neural networks and more recently, transformer-based architectures, enable fast and accurate morphological analysis. However, segmentation accuracy alone does not necessarily guarantee reliable extraction of clinically relevant volumetric biomarkers. Overall, a significant translational gap persists. Most existing methods treat TBAD analysis primarily as a conventional segmentation problem, optimizing voxel-wise overlap metrics on curated datasets. However, longitudinal clinical surveillance requires substantially more than accurate contour delineation. Robust deployment in real-world settings demands anatomical consistency across heterogeneous domains, preservation of volumetric biomarker fidelity, and resilience to postoperative alterations such as stent-induced beam-hardening artifacts. These challenges remain largely unaddressed by current monolithic segmentation pipelines. As a result, no unified framework enables robust, clinically deployable analysis of TBAD across both native and post-TEVAR settings. In this work, we propose AORTA-SCAN, a hierarchical and domain-adaptive framework for volumetric surveillance of primary and residual TBAD. Unlike conventional end-to-end segmentation pipelines, the proposed approach explicitly decouples anatomical localization from pathological refinement, enabling robust segmentation of the TL and FL together with volumetric analysis across heterogeneous CTA domains, including post-TEVAR anatomy. The main contributions of this study are threefold. First, we introduce a hierarchical segmentation strategy that separates global anatomical localization of the aorta from fine-grained lumen analysis through a probabilistic soft-prior, improving robustness and computational efficiency. Second, we implement a domain adaptation scheme based on transfer learning and partial-label learning to mitigate distribution shifts between curated datasets and heterogeneous real-world clinical data. Third, we extend the framework to postoperative scenarios by incorporating a metal-aware input representation and explicitly modeling the stent as a topological anatomical constraint, enabling reliable partitioning of intra-stent and extra-stent volumes despite severe beam-hardening artifacts. Importantly, the proposed framework is designed not only to optimize voxel-wise segmentation accuracy but also to preserve the stability and reproducibility of clinically relevant volumetric biomarkers across heterogeneous clinical domains. By integrating these components into a unified pipeline, AORTA-SCAN enables automated extraction of volumetric descriptors, including TL and FL volumes and the PDI, directly from routine CTA scans.

2. Related Works

Early work by Cao et al. demonstrated the feasibility of fully automated TBAD segmentation in CTA using convolutional neural networks ( [3]), while subsequent multi-stage frameworks have further improved segmentation performance and enabled volumetric analysis ( [7]). More recently, UNet-based architectures have achieved high accuracy in segmenting aortic dissection components; however, these studies have generally been limited by relatively small datasets and constrained generalizability ( [14]). Integrated deep learning pipelines have also been described to jointly perform aortic segmentation, dissection detection, TL/FL segmentation, and Stanford classification, achieving high accuracy across multiple tasks ( [28]). While these approaches demonstrate the potential of end-to-end automation for comprehensive analysis, they remain primarily focused on classification and segmentation performance, without addressing longitudinal volumetric quantification and biomarker extraction. In parallel, deep-learning-based segmentation methods have enabled automated quantification of aortic diameter and dissection components, including TL, circulating FL, and thrombus ( [12]). Notably, local FL volumes measured near the maximal aortic diameter have been shown to outperform global volumetric measures and diameter-based metrics in predicting adverse aortic remodeling, emphasizing the importance of spatially resolved volumetric descriptors. More broadly, artificial intelligence-assisted approaches have demonstrated strong performance in the automated detection of acute aortic syndromes (AAS) across large multicenter datasets, supporting their potential role in rapid clinical triage ( [6]). Complementary strategies have also explored the automatic detection of anatomical landmarks for longitudinal assessment. In particular, deep reinforcement learning (DRL) approaches have demonstrated high accuracy in localizing aortic landmarks in TBAD ( [2]). However, these methods are inherently limited to sparse geometric representations and do not capture the full volumetric distribution of luminal compartments required for comprehensive morphological and volumetric characterization. Collectively, these findings highlight the need for robust frameworks capable of integrating accurate segmentation, volumetric biomarker extraction, and postoperative robustness across heterogeneous clinical domains.

3. Methods

>Methods

3.1. Study Design and Data Sources

The framework was developed through four sequential stages using five arterial-phase CTA datasets with complementary anatomical coverage and annotation characteristics (Table 1, Figure 1). This staged design was adopted to separate coarse anatomical localization from fine luminal segmentation and to progressively adapt the model from curated public datasets to heterogeneous real-world clinical data. Such a strategy was particularly important because the number of locally annotated clinical CTA scans was limited, especially in the postoperative setting, making standalone training on the ASST Lecco cohort insufficiently robust. In SEG1, the AortaSeg24 ( [16]) dataset was used to train a binary aortic localization model based on a U-Net-like architecture capable of identifying the thoraco-abdominal aortic tract and defining a subject-specific region of interest for downstream analysis. In SEG2, the ImageTBAD dataset ( [26]) was used to train an initial TL/FL segmentation model on the localized aortic subvolume. A native lumen refinement stage was subsequently performed in SEG3. First, the model was refined using the Graz dataset ( [22]), which provided broader thoracoabdominal anatomical coverage than ImageTBAD. Second, preoperative domain adaptation was performed by continuing refinement using the Graz dataset together with the ASST Lecco preoperative cohort. This combined refinement strategy preserved the broader anatomical coverage available in the Graz annotations, including luminal extensions into branch vessels, while simultaneously adapting the model to the heterogeneous imaging characteristics of the ASST Lecco cohort. The final postoperative stage, SEG4, extended the framework to stented anatomy using the ASST Lecco postoperative cohort, composed of 21 cases. Due to inadequate contrast enhancement for arterial lumen delineation and volumetric surveillance, one venousphase examination was excluded. The final SEG4 dataset thus comprised 20 arterial-phase CTA scans, establishing arterial-phase acquisition as a mandatory baseline requirement for the reliable execution of the AORTA-SCAN framework. Among these, 9 patients had paired preoperative CTA scans available for longitudinal analysis. SEG2, together with its refinement stages SEG3 and SEG4, was implemented using a custom attention-enhanced residual 3D U-Net backbone described in Section 3.4. ASST Lecco data, provided in pseudo-anonymized form, were acquired under the Ethical committed approval entitled STABILISE REGISTRY n. 529, 2019 (Regione Lombardia ASST Lecco). The postoperative ASST Lecco dataset is publicly available at https://drive.google.com/drive/folders/1pGjqeXYNm_SI0jprhsuw0Wre97ARkgrR?usp=sharing.

3.2. Domain Adaptation Strategy

The integration of heterogeneous arterial-phase CTA datasets required a unified annotation and domain adaptation strategy to ensure morphological consistency across native and stented domains. In the AortaSeg24 dataset, the original multi-class annotations were consolidated into a single aortic mask to support robust anatomical anchoring during coarse localization. In the ImageTBAD dataset, false-lumen thrombus annotations, when present, were merged into the FL class to preserve volumetric consistency during TL/FL quantification. The ImageTBAD and Graz datasets provided voxel-wise annotations of TL, FL, and background, which were used as reference standards for supervised training and refinement. In contrast, the local ASST Lecco annotations were generated within a clinical workflow and were restricted to the aortic channel, without the extended branch-vessel coverage available in the Graz annotations. To mitigate inter-observer variability and improve adaptation to heterogeneous clinical annotations, a transfer learning and partial-label learning strategy was adopted. Lecco annotations were therefore treated as partial labels during refinement, allowing the model to adapt to the local imaging domain while avoiding penalization of anatomically present but unlabeled regions. For postoperative scans, explicit stent annotations were unavailable. Therefore, metallic regions were identified using an anatomically constrained threshold-based strategy. The coarse aortic mask was first used to restrict the candidate search region, after which metallic voxels were identified using a high-intensity threshold (> 600 HU), exploiting the characteristic attenuation of endovascular devices in CTA. Despite blooming artifacts and partial-volume effects, this approach provided a reproducible anatomical reference for stent localization and postoperative lumen partitioning across heterogeneous postoperative datasets.

3.3. Data Preprocessing

To ensure consistency across datasets with heterogeneous acquisition protocols and annotation characteristics, all CTA volumes underwent a standardized preprocessing pipeline implemented using the MONAI framework ( [4]). Volumes were resampled to an isotropic resolution of 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 mm using bilinear interpolation and reoriented to a standard Right-Anterior-Superior (RAS) coordinate system. Preprocessing strategies were adapted according to the specific requirements of the hierarchical framework and the imaging domain. For the initial coarse aortic localization stage, image intensities were clipped to a broad vascular range (-200 to +600 HU) to enhance contrast between the aorta and surrounding soft tissues. For finegrained lumen segmentation, a domain-specific multi-windowing approach was implemented to maximize feature extraction. In preoperative scans, a dual-channel input representation was adopted, consisting of a vascular intensity channel, clipped to -200 to +1500 HU and normalized using 0.5%-99.5% percentile scaling with adaptive histogram equalization, together with a probabilistic Gaussian soft-prior derived from the coarse aortic segmentation. In postoperative scans, this representation was extended to a triplechannel input by including a metal-sensitive channel. This third channel was obtained using high-intensity thresholding (+600 to +2000 HU), enabling explicit localization of metallic structures and mitigating beam-hardening artifacts. Furthermore, given the limited number of postoperative cases and the sparse spatial distribution of metallic stent voxels, the additional metal-sensitive channel was introduced to improve representation of metallic structures during training. All input channels were subsequently normalized using instance-wise Z-score normalization to ensure consistent feature scaling across different CT scanners.

3.4. Hierarchical Geometry-Aware Framework 3.4.1. Model Architecture

The AORTA-SCAN framework was trained as a hierarchical multi-stage segmentation process designed to address the main challenges of TBAD imaging, including anatomical localization, heterogeneous luminal morphology, and postoperative metallic artifacts (Fig. 2). In the first stage, a coarse anatomical localization module based on a U-Net-like architecture was trained to identify the thoraco-abdominal aortic tract and define a subjectspecific region of interest. The coarse target mask was defined as the union of all available aortic anatomical labels and dissection-related structures, providing a robust anatomical envelope for downstream localization. By isolating the aorta from surrounding anatomical structures, this stage reduced background variability and constrained the downstream analysis to the relevant vascular anatomy. 3.4.2. Training Strategy and Implementation Details

To improve generalization and reduce overfitting, on-the-fly 3D data augmentation was applied during training. This included random extraction of 128 3 voxel patches, spatial transformations (axis flipping and 90 • rotations, both with p = 0.4), and intensity augmentations including random contrast adjustment (γ ∈ [0.5, 1.5]), Gaussian noise (σ = 0.1), and Gaussian smoothing (σ ∈ [0.5, 1.0]). Intensity-based augmentations were applied exclusively to image channels to preserve the structural integrity of the softprior input. To address class imbalance between lumen structures, stent voxels, and background, training was performed using a patch-based sampling strategy with a fixed input size of 128 × 128 × 128 voxels. Patches were extracted using MONAI label-guided cropping, which increased the probability of sampling regions containing annotated foreground classes rather than uniformly sampling the full CTA volume. For native scans, foreground sampling targeted TL and FL voxels, whereas for postoperative scans, it additionally targeted stent voxels to compensate for the sparse spatial distribution of metallic structures. This strategy increased the representation of clinically relevant structures during training while preserving sufficient background context within each patch. The patch-based balancing strategy followed principles previously adopted for multi-task encoder-decoder segmentation ( [21]). The AORTA-SCAN framework was implemented using the PyTorch library and the MONAI framework ( [4]). All model training procedures were conducted on a workstation equipped with a single NVIDIA H100 PCIe GPU (80 GB VRAM). We used the AdamW optimizer with an initial learning rate of 1 × 10 -4 and a weight decay of 1 × 10 -5 . The learning rate was adjusted using a cosine annealing schedule. Training was performed with a batch size of 4 and continued until early stopping based on the validation macro Dice score; therefore, the number of epochs varied across folds and training stages, with the maximum number of epochs set to 250. The choice of objective function was tailored to each stage of the hierarchical pipeline: Generalized Dice Loss was employed for the initial training and fine-segmentation stages to address class imbalance. For domain adaptation and refinement phases involving heterogeneous clinical annotations, Partial Label Dice-Cross Entropy Loss was adopted. This masked objective computes gradients exclusively for classes explicitly present in the reference standard, preventing the penalization of unannotated structures and enabling the model to learn effectively from partially labeled clinical datasets. Final model selection within each fold was determined by the highest validation macro Dice score.

3.4.3. Inference Strategy

During inference, the framework operated sequentially according to the hierarchical architecture described above. First, the coarse localization model (SEG1) identified the thoraco-abdominal aortic tract and generated a binary aortic mask. This mask was used to define a subject-specific region of interest and to compute the Gaussian anatomical soft-prior propagated to the downstream segmentation stages. The localized subvolume, together with the anatomical soft-prior, was subsequently processed by the lumen segmentation network. In native CTA scans, the inference pipeline used the dualchannel configuration to generate TL and FL predictions. In postoperative CTA scans, the additional metal-sensitive channel was included to support stent-aware segmentation and postoperative lumen partitioning. The final output consisted of voxel-wise TL, FL, and, when applicable, stent segmentations, which were subsequently used for volumetric and geometric biomarker extraction.

3.6. Evaluation Metrics

Model performance was evaluated using a multi-level validation strategy assessing both voxel-wise segmentation accuracy and the clinical reliability of derived volumetric biomarkers. Segmentation performance was quantified using the Dice coefficient, Intersection over Union (IoU), sensitivity, precision, and specificity, computed at the patient level. Clinical agreement was assessed through linear regression and Bland-Altman analysis of true lumen volume (V T L ), false lumen volume (V F L ), and Perfusion Dissection Index (PDI). In addition, the Relative Volumetric Error (RVE) was calculated to quantify proportional discrepancies between predicted and reference volumes. Robustness was evaluated across heterogeneous datasets with different acquisition protocols and annotation schemes, including public benchmarks and real-world clinical cohorts. For partially annotated clinical datasets, partial-label learning was adopted during training, while masked evaluation metrics were used to restrict performance assessment to explicitly annotated regions. Validation was performed using a 5-fold cross-validation scheme with strict patient-level separation (80% training, 20% testing per fold) to prevent data leakage. Final results are reported as the mean across folds. Continuous variables were reported as means ± standard deviation. Statistical comparisons between AORTA-SCAN and the baseline model were performed using the paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Differences were considered statistically significant for p < 0.05.

4. Experimental Results

>Experimental Results >Preoperative cohorts

4.1.1. Segmentation Results

The segmentation framework was evaluated against a conventional nnU-Net ( [17]) baseline operating without hierarchical localization or anatomical soft-prior guidance (Table 3). The resulting out-of-fold predictions from the five-fold cross-validation achieved strong voxel-level segmentation performance for both luminal compartments. The Dice score improved from 0.754 to 0.816 for the TL (p = 3.01 × 10 -5 ) and from 0.707 to 0.837 for the FL (p = 5.26 × 10 -8 ). Similar performance increases were observed for IoU, precision, and recall across both classes. For the TL, precision and recall increased from 0.810 and 0.732 in the baseline to 0.853 and 0.796, respectively. The largest improvement was observed in the FL, where precision increased from 0.722 to 0.819 and recall from 0.720 to 0.875. Overall, these results indicate that the hierarchical refinement strategy improved both boundary reliability and lumen completeness relative to the baseline. In particular, the marked gain in FL recall suggests a more complete recovery of the dissected lumen, while the simultaneous increase in precision indicates that this improvement was not achieved at the expense of excessive false-positive segmentation. Generalizability on the D4a ASST Lecco clinical cohort (preoperative) was further assessed using masked metrics to account for heterogeneous clinical annotations. The model achieved a masked Dice score of 0.786 for the TL and 0.723 for the FL, with a global masked Dice score of 0.874. Qualitative comparison between predicted segmentations and specialist ground truths confirmed the framework's ability to resolve the complex topology of the intimal flap even in the presence of variable contrast opacification. Beyond segmentation accuracy, the fully automated pipeline processed a complete case in less than 1 minute on a standard PC workstation (Intel i7-12850HX 16-core CPU, 32 GB RAM, 8 GB GPU), corresponding to an approximately 85% reduction in analysis time compared with manual expert segmentation.

Linear regression analysis (Figure 4, top) demonstrated strong agreement between predicted and reference volumetric measurements. The FL exhibited excellent consistency (Pearson r = 0.92), while the TL showed a moderate-to-strong correlation (r = 0.79). Residual variability was primarily localized to anatomically complex distal regions, where severe luminal compression, variable contrast opacification, and slit-like morphologies made delineation intrinsically challenging and likely contributed to the remaining discrepancies in TL quantification. The Bland-Altman analysis (Figure 4, bottom) confirmed the absence of substantial systematic bias, with mean differences centered near zero across volumetric scales. Specifically, on the clinical cohort (D4a), the framework maintained stable agreement, with mean RVE values of -17.43% for the TL and -12.6% for the FL. Among the evaluated biomarkers, the PDI demonstrated the highest clinical stability. AORTA-SCAN achieved a mean absolute PDI error of 0.039, improving upon the 0.087 obtained with the baseline 3D U-Net (p = 0.025). Furthermore, the negligible mean PDI bias of -0.0017 relative to expert references confirmed the framework's robustness in preserving volumetric relationships between luminal compartments. By providing stable volumetric measurements that are robust to heterogeneous clinical annotations, the proposed framework established a reliable quantitative baseline for longitudinal TBAD surveillance. Regarding the preoperative ASST Lecco data, the average of the zonal relative PDI biases across the thoracic, visceral, and aorto-iliac regions remained below 17%. Region-specific analysis showed lower variability in the thoracic and visceral regions, while the aorto-iliac segment showed the highest discrepancy (Table 4).

4.2.1. Segmentation Results

Performance on the stented postoperative cohort (N = 20) demonstrated the resilience of the triple-input architecture. By isolating metallic components via the high-intensity channel (> 600 HU), the framework successfully segmented the stent as a distinct class, achieving a Dice score of 0.723. While this score reflects the inherent sparsity of the metallic mesh, the framework maintained high accuracy for the vascular compartments, achieving a TL Dice score of 0.900 and a FL Dice score of 0.751. This multi-class approach enabled the automated partitioning of the intra-stent compartment, where the TL resides, and the extra-stent space constituted by the residual FL. This helped mitigate beam-hardening artifacts that typically obscure the intimal flap in standard architectures. The clinical reliability of this partitioning was confirmed by a strong Pearson correlation of r = 0.96 between predicted and expert TL volumes, supporting the accuracy of the model despite metallic blooming (Fig. 5).

4.2.2. Clinical Agreement and Volumetric Reliability

The PDI was highly stable in the postoperative setting, with a global mean absolute error of 0.0134. In addition to the global volumetric assessment, a spatially resolved zonal PDI analysis was performed by dividing the aortic volume into three centerline-ordered anatomical regions: thoracic, visceral, and aorto-iliac, as performed by [11]. Across the postoperative cohort, global PDI showed strong agreement between automated predictions and expert references, with a mean ground truth PDI of 0.1177 and a mean predicted PDI of 0.1188, corresponding to a relative PDI bias of 11.39% and a mean absolute error of 0.0134 (Table 5). Zonal analysis showed similarly low errors in the thoracic and visceral regions, with mean absolute PDI errors of 0.0076 and 0.0078, respectively. The aorto-iliac region showed higher variability, with a mean absolute PDI error of 0.0485, consistent with the greater anatomical complexity of distal residual dissection and postoperative remodeling. Despite this regional variability, the global PDI remained stable, supporting its robustness as a volumetric biomarker while suggesting that zonal PDI may provide complementary information for identifying localized discrepancies in FL burden. In the subset of patients with paired preoperative and postoperative CTA scans (n = 9), predicted PDI values were compared longitudinally to quantify volumetric remodeling after treatment. The change in PDI was defined as ∆PDI = PDI preop -PDI postop , so that positive values indicate a postoperative reduction in relative FL burden. Global PDI decreased in all paired cases, with ∆PDI ranging from 0.231 to 0.606 (Table 6). Regionally, the largest reductions were generally observed in the thoracic segment, consistent with the expected effect of proximal endovascular exclusion. In contrast, the aorto-iliac region showed more heterogeneous remodeling, including one case with increased postoperative regional PDI. The framework automatically identified the longitudinal extent of the aorta and extracted equivalent diameter (D eq ) profiles from the global aortic mask, defined as the union of TL and FL segmentations (Table 7). Equivalent diameters were computed on axial cross-sections orthogonal to the extracted aortic centerline, while slices with cross-sectional areas below 50 mm 2 were excluded to avoid unstable measurements caused by small disconnected components and segmentation noise. The maximum ground-truth D eq was 39.83 ± 6.20 mm, compared with 41.49 ± 6.90 mm obtained with AORTA-SCAN, corresponding to a mean signed error of 1.66±3.71 mm and a mean absolute error of 2.56±3.15 mm. More spatially robust longitudinal descriptors demonstrated substantially stronger agreement. In particular, the 95 th percentile D eq exhibited a mean absolute error of only 1.05 ± 1.67 mm, while the mean D eq showed the lowest variability, with a mean absolute error of 0.72 ± 0.66 mm. These findings suggest that percentile-based and longitudinal diameter descriptors are less sensitive to isolated local segmentation irregularities than single-slice maximal measurements (Table 7).

5. Discussion

>Discussion

5.1. Main findings

The proposed hierarchical framework achieved robust and clinically reliable performance across both native and postoperative TBAD datasets. In the preoperative setting, the pipeline consistently improved TL and FL segmentation accuracy on both the Graz and ASST Lecco cohorts relative to the baseline architecture, while the postoperative stent-aware configuration preserved high segmentation performance despite severe metallic artifacts and altered vascular anatomy (see Table 3). Volumetric biomarker analysis demonstrated strong agreement with references computed on original surfaces, with the global PDI error remaining below 15% in the Graz cohort and below 12% in both the preoperative and postoperative ASST Lecco cohorts (see Tables 4 and 5). Importantly, the limited global PDI bias confirmed the framework's ability to preserve the relative volumetric relationship between the true and false lumina, supporting reliable regional and whole-aorta volumetric surveillance. Longitudinal analysis further demonstrated consistent postoperative reduction of the global PDI across paired examinations, reflecting favorable remodeling after treatment, while regional analysis revealed heterogeneous distal remodeling patterns that may provide complementary prognostic information (see Table 6). In addition, equivalent diameter analysis demonstrated good agreement with expert measurements, particularly for longitudinal and percentile-based descriptors, which showed lower variability and greater robustness than single-slice maximal diameter estimation (see Table 7). The complete automated workflow, including segmentation and volumetric analysis, was completed in less than 1 minute on a standard PC-based workstation equipped with an 8 GB GPU.

5.2. Hierarchical Strategy and Robustness

The adoption of a hierarchical approach was motivated by the clinical need to decouple global anatomical localization from fine-grained pathological analysis. By leveraging an initial model (SEG1) trained on the entire aorta, the framework isolated the aortic volume from surrounding anatomical structures, such as the spine or kidneys, thus avoiding interference with the following TL and FL segmentation performed by the second-stage models SEG3 (pre-op) and SEG4 (postop). This spatial gating reduced the com-putational burden, allowing the model to process the region of interest at high resolution without the aggressive downsampling often required by fullvolume approaches. The constrained search space enabled the second-stage model to focus its representational capacity on the subtle textural differences between the intimal flap and the two lumina. Such a strategy was particularly relevant in TBAD, where the false lumen may extend over long vascular segments and exhibit heterogeneous contrast enhancement, compression, or partial thrombosis.

5.3. Technical and Clinical Significance of Stent Segmentation

The ability to explicitly account for stented anatomy distinguishes the framework from conventional segmentation approaches that are not specifically designed to handle postoperative CTA. The explicit modeling of the stent is clinically relevant because it provides a topological reference for distinguishing the intra-stent compartment from the residual extra-stent space. In postoperative CTA, metallic blooming and beam-hardening can obscure the intimal flap and reduce the reliability of conventional intensity-based segmentation. By incorporating a metal-sensitive representation and treating the stent as an anatomical class, the framework constrains the interpretation of postoperative anatomy and supports more consistent lumen partitioning. This design is particularly relevant for residual TBAD surveillance after TEVAR, where the clinical question is not only whether the aorta can be segmented, but whether the residual FL can be reliably separated from the treated TL compartment. In this context, stent-aware segmentation provides an anatomical constraint that may improve the reproducibility of postoperative volumetric biomarkers and reduce the risk of overestimating luminal volumes in regions affected by metallic artifacts.

5.4. Volumetric Biomarkers and Longitudinal Surveillance

The relationship between voxel-wise overlap and volumetric reliability is clinically important. In TBAD, small local segmentation discrepancies may have a limited impact on global volumetric biomarkers, whereas errors in compressed or distal luminal regions can disproportionately affect overlap metrics. For this reason, biomarker agreement should be interpreted alongside traditional segmentation metrics. The PDI is particularly suited to this role because it summarizes the relative contribution of the FL to the total perfused aortic volume and therefore reflects luminal compression and residual disease burden. The zonal formulation of PDI further extends this concept by enabling spatially resolved assessment of remodeling. A global biomarker can summarize overall disease burden, but it may obscure regional differences between the thoracic, visceral, and aorto-iliac segments. This distinction is especially relevant after TEVAR, where proximal exclusion may promote thoracic remodeling while distal residual dissection can persist through re-entry flow or incomplete FL thrombosis. Zonal PDI may therefore provide complementary information for identifying persistent regional FL burden during follow-up. The higher volumetric and PDI variability observed within the aorto-iliac segment (see Tables 4 and 5) compared to the thoracic and visceral regions can be attributed to distinct anatomical and imaging challenges. Specifically, the tapering lumen diameter and the complex tortuosity near the iliac bifurcation inherently increase boundary ambiguity for the segmentation network. Furthermore, the frequent presence of dense peripheral calcifications in this district often may induce blooming artifacts ( [15]), which can artificially alter Hounsfield Unit thresholds and obscure the fine separation between the true and false lumens. The automated extraction of equivalent diameter profiles also supports integration with current clinical practice, which remains largely diameter-driven. However, single-slice maximum diameter measurements can be sensitive to local segmentation irregularities, aortic tortuosity, and slice selection. Longitudinal or percentile-based diameter descriptors may provide a more stable representation of vessel caliber along the aortic axis. Taken together, these considerations support the use of automated volumetric and geometric biomarkers as complementary tools rather than replacements for established diameter-based surveillance.

5.5. Comparison with Literature

Previous deep learning studies have demonstrated high segmentation accuracy for TBAD analysis on curated CTA datasets. In ( [3]), the authors evaluated a serial multi-task CNN on a single-center cohort of 276 preoperative TBAD CTA scans, reporting Dice scores up to 0.93 for TL segmentation and 0.91 for FL segmentation. A multi-stage framework was validated with anatomical straightening on a multicenter dataset of 120 TBAD patients, achieving Dice scores of 0.95 and 0.89 for TL and FL segmentation, respectively ( [7]). More recently, a fully automated nnU-Net-based pipeline was developed for AD identification, segmentation, and Stanford classification on 386 CTA scans, reporting Dice scores of 0.968 for TL and 0.971 for FL segmentation ( [28]). In the postoperative setting, ( [27]) evaluated a global-local fusion framework on a multicenter cohort of 133 patients with 306 post-TEVAR follow-up CTA scans, achieving Dice scores of 0.962 for TL and 0.921 for FL segmentation. Although these studies reported slightly higher voxel-wise overlap metrics, they were generally evaluated on highly curated datasets with homogeneous annotations and limited domain variability. In contrast, AORTA-SCAN was specifically designed to operate across heterogeneous real-world clinical domains, including external preoperative cohorts with partial annotations and postoperative CTA scans affected by severe metallic artifacts and altered vascular anatomy. Therefore, the slightly lower segmentation overlap observed in our experiments likely reflects the substantially greater anatomical and clinical variability of the evaluated cohorts. Importantly, despite this increased complexity, the framework maintained stable volumetric biomarker agreement, with the global PDI error remaining below 15% in the Graz cohort and the global PDI bias remaining below 12% in both the preoperative and postoperative ASST Lecco cohorts, supporting the clinical robustness and translational applicability of the proposed approach. Beyond segmentation, several recent studies emphasized the clinical relevance of volumetric biomarkers for TBAD surveillance. (author?) [11] demonstrated on a clinical STABILISE cohort of 42 patients that the perfusion dissection index (PDI) can predict postoperative aortic reintervention, while in ( [12]) the authors showed in patients with residual aortic dissection that false-lumen volumetric descriptors may outperform diameterbased measurements in predicting adverse remodeling. Similarly, in ( [19]) it was reported on a cohort of 40 post-TEVAR TBAD patients that volumetric measurements more accurately characterize postoperative aortic remodeling compared with conventional diameter analysis alone. However, these studies relied on manual or semi-manual volumetric quantification workflows, which remain difficult to integrate into routine large-scale clinical surveillance. In contrast, AORTA-SCAN provides a fully automated end-to-end framework capable of directly extracting volumetric and geometric biomarkers, including PDI and equivalent diameter profiles, from routine CTA scans in both native and postoperative settings, while maintaining clinically stable agreement with expert-derived measurements.

5.6. Limitations and Future Directions

This study has limitations that should guide future development. A primary constraint remains the limited availability of high-fidelity annotated data, particularly for postoperative stented cohorts. From a technical perspective, postoperative segmentation in the aortic arch remains challenging, likely reflecting a labeling bias in the training data, where clinical annotations primarily focused on the descending thoracic aorta. Further developments will also refine the spatial analysis of postoperative remodeling by standardizing thoracic, visceral, and aorto-iliac zonal definitions and validating zonal PDI trends in longitudinal follow-up studies. More broadly, AORTA-SCAN establishes the basis for automated longitudinal imaging surveillance following TBAD treatment. By enabling reproducible extraction of volumetric biomarkers across serial CTA examinations, the framework may support standardized follow-up workflows and facilitate earlier identification of adverse remodeling patterns during postoperative monitoring.

6. Conclusions

In this work, we developed and validated AORTA-SCAN, a hierarchical deep learning framework for the fully automated characterization of native and postoperative Type B aortic dissection (TBAD). By decoupling global anatomical localization from fine-grained pathological refinement, the framework enables consistent three-dimensional segmentation of the true and false lumina across heterogeneous CTA domains. The integration of stent-aware modeling further supports postoperative surveillance by explicitly accounting for endovascular devices and reducing the impact of metallic artifacts on lumen partitioning. Beyond segmentation, AORTA-SCAN enables automated extraction of clinically relevant volumetric and geometric biomarkers, including TL and FL volumes, PDI, and equivalent diameter descriptors. By reducing the manual burden of volumetric assessment, AORTA-SCAN may support more scalable integration of three-dimensional biomarkers into routine TBAD follow-up.
