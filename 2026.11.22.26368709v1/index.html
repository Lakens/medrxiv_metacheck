How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Full text

Abstract

Responsive feeding -recognising and answering a child's hunger and satiety cues without coercion -is a World Health Organization-recommended component of complementary feeding, yet it is rarely addressed or measured in Kenya's community health system, particularly in arid and semi-arid counties.

Objective. To determine whether a Community Health Promoter (CHP)-led responsive feeding intervention improves responsive feeding practices among caregivers of children aged 0-59 months in West Pokot County, Kenya, compared with usual community health services, and to evaluate its implementation.

Methods. This is a pragmatic, two-arm, parallel-group cluster randomized controlled trial with an embedded RE-AIM mixed-methods process evaluation. Eighteen Community Health Units in two sub-counties will be randomized 1:1, stratified by sub-county, and approximately 340 caregiver-child dyads enrolled. The intervention comprises five-day competency-based CHP training, monthly household counselling and coaching visits over three months, mother-tomother group sessions, pictorial job aids, SMS reminders and supportive supervision with fidelity assessment. The comparator is usual care. The primary outcome is the Responsive Feeding Practices Index (RFPI, 0-100), combining observed mealtime and caregiver-reported items, at three months after intervention initiation. Secondary outcomes include caregiver knowledge, attitudes and self-efficacy, minimum dietary diversity, meal frequency and ageappropriate feeding. The primary analysis is a baseline-adjusted mixed-effects linear regression with a random intercept for Community Health Unit, by intention to treat. Reach, adoption, fidelity and maintenance, with key informant interviews and focus group discussions, will be integrated by RE-AIM domain.

The trial is approved by the Masinde Muliro University of Science and Technology Institutional Scientific and Ethics Review Committee (MMUST/ISERC/314/2026) and licensed by the National Commission for Science, Technology and Innovation (NACOSTI/P/26/4195285). Findings will be shared through publication, county feedback meetings and a policy brief.

Introduction

Responsive feeding describes the reciprocal exchange in which a caregiver perceives a child's hunger and satiety signals, responds promptly and contingently, encourages without coercion, and supports developmentally appropriate autonomy at mealtimes. It is one of the behavioural pathways through which the Nurturing Care Framework links caregiving to child growth, selfregulation and cognitive, emotional and social development [1]. The 2023 World Health Organization (WHO) guideline for complementary feeding of infants and young children aged 6-23 months issued a strong recommendation that children in this age band be responsively fed, noting that delivery of the recommendation depends on the capacity of health workers to counsel caregivers and families [2]. Responsive feeding is therefore a guideline-level intervention target with defined delivery requirements.

Systematic review and meta-analysis of parenting interventions delivered in the first three years of life shows improvements in parenting practices, parent-child interaction and child development across low-and middle-income settings [3], and harmonisation work has clarified how responsive feeding recommendations should be integrated into dietary guidance for infants and young children [4]. A mixed-methods study among low-income caregivers of toddlers found responsive feeding practices to be modifiable and associated with better diet quality [5].

Nutrition education and behaviour change communication trials in Ethiopia, including cluster randomized designs delivered through community structures, have improved complementary feeding knowledge and practice [6, 7]. However, most trials measure knowledge, dietary diversity or growth, and few prospectively specify a responsive feeding behavioural outcome as the primary endpoint.

In sub-Saharan Africa, where the pooled prevalence of stunting among children aged 6-59 months remains high [8], caregiver feeding style is shaped by workload, household food insecurity and prevailing norms. Observational work in Ethiopia has documented substantial use of pressuring and forced feeding, patterned by caregiver and child characteristics [9].

Comparable findings have been reported in Kenya: a case study in Teso South found responsive feeding difficult to enact under everyday constraints [10], and work in low-income Nairobi settlements documented feeding behaviours that diverge from responsive principles [11].

Kenya delivers community-level promotive and preventive services through Community

Health Units (CHUs), each served by a team of Community Health Promoters (CHPs) supervised by Community Health Assistants, under the Primary Health Care Act 2023 and the Kenya Community Health Policy 2020-2030 [12, 13]. National survey data continue to show suboptimal infant and young child feeding, with a minority of children aged 6-23 months achieving a minimum acceptable diet [14]. Community platforms have delivered measurable nutrition gains in Kenya: the Baby Friendly Community Initiative improved exclusive breastfeeding [15] and complementary feeding practices [16] in cluster-randomized and quasiexperimental evaluations. Policy review nonetheless indicates that the responsive caregiving domain of the Nurturing Care Framework is the least operationalised in Kenyan community health practice [17]. Responsive feeding counselling is not currently a defined, supervised, competency-assessed CHP task.

West Pokot County is a predominantly arid and semi-arid land (ASAL) county with dispersed pastoral settlement, seasonal food insecurity and variable road access; the county has recorded substantial caseloads of severe and moderate acute malnutrition [18, 19]. Local work has identified cultural determinants of malnutrition among children aged 6-59 months in the county [20], and evidence from comparable pastoral ecological zones shows a distinct undernutrition profile relative to agricultural settings [21, 22]. These features make West Pokot both a high-need setting and a demanding test of whether a counselling-based intervention can be delivered with adequate reach and fidelity through an existing community platform.

Three gaps remain. First, no randomized evaluation has tested a structured, competency-based, CHP-delivered responsive feeding package integrated into routine Kenyan community health services. Second, responsive feeding is rarely measured as a directly observed behavioural outcome in trials conducted in ASAL settings; measurement typically relies on caregiver report alone. Third, where community nutrition interventions have been evaluated, implementation determinants are seldom captured prospectively alongside effectiveness, so it is not known whether an efficacious package can be delivered at acceptable reach, adoption and fidelity by CHPs carrying existing workloads. Findings from other paediatric populations cannot be extrapolated directly to pastoral ASAL communities, where feeding norms, food availability and household mobility differ [21, 22]. This trial therefore adopts an effectiveness-implementation hybrid orientation [23], in which a randomized effectiveness evaluation is the primary aim and a prospectively specified, RE-AIM-guided mixed-methods process evaluation runs concurrently [24, 25]. Its objective is to determine whether a CHP-led responsive feeding intervention improves responsive feeding practices, measured by the Responsive Feeding Practices Index (RFPI), among caregivers of children aged 0-59 months in West Pokot County, compared with usual community health services, and to characterise the reach, adoption, implementation fidelity and early maintenance of the package within the county community health system.

Objectives and hypotheses

Primary effectiveness objective

To determine the effectiveness of a CHP-led responsive feeding intervention, compared with usual community health services, in improving caregiver responsive feeding practices measured by the RFPI (0-100) at three months after intervention initiation.

Secondary effectiveness objectives

To determine the effect of the intervention on caregiver responsive feeding knowledge, attitudes and self-efficacy; on minimum dietary diversity among children aged 6-23 months; on age-appropriate meal frequency; on age-appropriate feeding practices among children aged 24-59 months; and on caregiver-reported experience of counselling.

Descriptive objective

To characterise baseline responsive feeding practices, and their distribution by child age module, caregiver characteristics and household food security, among caregivers of children aged 0-59 months in the participating CHUs.

Implementation objective

To evaluate implementation of the intervention using the RE-AIM framework, specifically Reach, Adoption, Implementation (fidelity and dose) and early Maintenance.

Qualitative and contextual objective

To explore barriers to and facilitators of intervention uptake, delivery and sustainability from the perspectives of caregivers, CHPs, supervisors and county health managers.

Hypotheses

Primary hypothesis. Caregiver-child dyads in CHUs allocated to the CHP-led responsive feeding intervention will have higher endline RFPI scores, adjusted for baseline RFPI, than dyads in CHUs allocated to usual care.

Secondary hypotheses.

Relative to usual care, the intervention will increase caregiver responsive feeding knowledge, attitude and self-efficacy scores; increase the proportion of children aged 6-23 months meeting minimum dietary diversity; and increase the proportion of children receiving age-appropriate meal frequency and age-appropriate feeding practices.

Secondary hypotheses are supportive and are not used to establish the primary conclusion of the trial.

Materials and methods

Study design and framework

This is a pragmatic, two-arm, parallel-group cluster randomized controlled trial with an embedded mixed-methods effectiveness-implementation (process) evaluation guided by the RE-AIM framework [24, 25]. The trial is designed within a superiority framework. The unit of randomization is the CHU; the unit of enrolment, follow-up and analysis is the caregiver-child dyad. The same dyads enrolled at baseline are followed prospectively to the endline assessment. The protocol is reported in accordance with SPIRIT 2025 [26] and the SPIRIT-Children and Adolescents (SPIRIT-C) 2026 extension [27]; the intervention is described following TIDieR principles [28]. Completed SPIRIT 2025 and SPIRIT-C 2026 checklists are provided as S1 File and S2 File. A pragmatic orientation was adopted because the research question concerns whether the package works when delivered by the CHPs who already serve these communities, under existing supervision arrangements and workloads, rather than under idealised conditions. The Nurturing Care Framework provides the content framework, defining responsive caregiving and nutrition as the domains targeted [1]. Orem's Self-Care Deficit Nursing Theory and Pender's Health Promotion Model, which underpin the parent doctoral work, articulate the presumed mechanism of change: caregivers experience knowledge, skill and confidence deficits in responsive feeding that CHPs address through counselling and coaching, and adoption of the resulting behaviours is conditioned by perceived benefits, perceived barriers and self-efficacy. RE-AIM is used only as the implementation evaluation framework.

Trial setting

The trial will be conducted in West Pokot County, north-western Kenya. The county covers approximately 11,075 km² and is administratively divided into six sub-counties (West Pokot, North Pokot, Kacheliba, Pokot Central, Pokot South and Kipkomo), 30 wards and 184 locations, served by 252 CHUs. All sub-counties are classified as arid or semi-arid except Pokot South and part of West Pokot sub-county, which are largely highland. Approximately 80% of the county population resides in ASAL sub-counties, and children under 14 years constitute about half the population [19]. Integrated Food Security Phase Classification analyses have documented substantial burdens of severe and moderate acute malnutrition in the county [18].

The features of this setting that bear directly on the trial are dispersed pastoral settlement, long and seasonally variable travel distances to health facilities, seasonal fluctuation in food availability and household composition, and reliance on the CHP cadre for most householdlevel promotive contact. These conditions constrain contact frequency, make household visiting the only feasible delivery modality for many families, and create a risk of differential loss to follow-up related to mobility. They also make the findings relevant to other Kenyan and East African ASAL settings.

Two geographically non-adjacent sub-counties, Kacheliba and Kipkomo, were purposively selected as the trial area on the basis of CHU functionality, accessibility for supervision and monitoring throughout the intervention period, and physical separation sufficient to limit crossarm contact. Sub-county is a stratification and contextual variable only; sub-counties are not randomized and do not define study arms.

Cluster eligibility

CHUs are eligible for randomization if they are officially gazetted and functional within the county community health information system; have an established CHP team with an assigned Community Health Assistant or equivalent supervisor; have not received a structured responsive feeding or infant and young child feeding counselling programme from any implementing partner in the preceding 12 months; are accessible for monthly supervisory visits throughout the intervention period; and are not immediately geographically adjacent to another CHU already selected for the trial. CHUs are excluded if a partner-implemented nutrition behaviour change programme is scheduled to start in the CHU during the trial period, or if the CHU is subject to insecurity or population displacement that would prevent scheduled household contact. Eighteen eligible CHUs, nine per sub-county, will form the trial sampling frame; where more than nine CHUs are eligible in a sub-county, the nine will be selected to maximise geographical separation, and the selection will be documented before randomization.

Participant eligibility

Inclusion criteria (caregiver-child dyad). (i) Mother or primary caregiver aged 18 years or older who is principally responsible for the index child's daily feeding; (ii) index child aged 0-59 months; (iii) resident within the selected CHU catchment for at least three months preceding enrolment; (iv) intention to remain resident within the CHU catchment for the duration of follow-up; (v) written informed consent, including consent for observation of a mealtime.

Exclusion criteria. (i) Index child with a condition that fundamentally alters the feeding interaction such that observation of ordinary responsive feeding is not meaningful or not appropriate, including orofacial clefts, severe neurodevelopmental impairment affecting oral intake, and current enteral or tube feeding; (ii) index child currently in inpatient therapeutic care for complicated severe acute malnutrition at screening, since feeding is then clinically directed (such children are referred and may be screened after discharge if follow-up remains feasible); (iii) caregiver unable to provide informed consent. Where more than one eligible child is present in a household, one index child will be selected at random by the electronic data capture form, and only that dyad will be enrolled. No exclusion is applied on the basis of child nutritional status, parity, marital status, socioeconomic position or household composition.

Adolescent caregivers. Caregivers younger than 18 years will not be enrolled, because their inclusion would require a separate parental permission and assent framework and additional safeguarding provisions. Adolescent caregivers identified during household listing continue to receive routine community health services, including routine CHP counselling in intervention CHUs, but do not contribute trial data; this limits generalisability to adolescent mothers.

Child assent.

All index children are aged 0-59 months, so assent is not developmentally applicable and will not be sought. The consenting caregiver gives permission for the child's participation, including mealtime observation. Child-level safeguards during observation are specified under Harms and safeguarding.

Community Health Promoter eligibility

CHPs are eligible to deliver the intervention if they are formally registered as CHPs serving a CHU allocated to the intervention arm, are active in the county community health information system, and consent to training, supervision and fidelity assessment. All CHPs serving the nine intervention CHUs will be invited; the CHU establishment in this county is approximately eight to ten CHPs per unit. CHPs in control CHUs continue their routine duties and will be offered the training package after completion of the final trial assessment.

Recruitment and timing relative to randomization

Recruitment is sequenced so that neither cluster selection nor individual enrolment can be influenced by knowledge of allocation. The following order will be followed and documented: 1.

Identification and confirmation of the 18 eligible CHUs against the cluster eligibility criteria, and documentation of the final cluster list.

2. Community entry and sensitisation meetings with sub-county health management teams, CHU committees and community leaders, conducted identically in all 18 CHUs and without reference to arm.

3. Household listing within each CHU to enumerate households containing a resident child aged 0-59 months. 4. Systematic random selection of eligible dyads from the CHU listing, using a random start and a sampling interval derived from the listing size, until the target of approximately 19 dyads per CHU is reached.

5. Individual informed consent.

6. Baseline assessment, including the observed mealtime module where feasible. 7. Disclosure of cluster allocation to the county team, CHPs and field teams, only after baseline data collection has been completed and the baseline dataset for all 18 CHUs has been locked. 8. CHP training in intervention CHUs, followed by initiation of the intervention.

The allocation sequence is generated in advance but concealed until baseline lock (step 7).

Enrolment will be tracked against target by CHU, and retention will be supported through collection of two contact telephone numbers, recording of household landmarks, CHP-assisted tracing, appointment reminders through the CHP, and flexible scheduling of endline visits around seasonal movement.

Randomization and sequence generation

The 18 eligible CHUs will be arranged into two strata corresponding to the two sub-counties, with nine CHUs per stratum. Stratified restricted randomization will be used to achieve nine intervention and nine usual-care CHUs overall. Because each stratum contains an odd number of clusters, the independent statistician will first select at random (probability 0.5) the stratum that receives five intervention CHUs; the other stratum receives four. Within each stratum, the CHU codes will then be placed in a computer-generated random order and the fixed number of intervention CHUs assigned, so that every combination of intervention CHUs within a stratum is equally likely; the remaining CHUs form the usual-care arm. Stratification by sub-county balances the arms on contextual differences in market access, agro-ecology and health service configuration.

The sequence will be generated by an independent statistician who has no role in cluster selection, participant recruitment, intervention delivery, outcome assessment or analysis, using a reproducible pseudo-random number generator in R with a seed recorded before the run. The CHUs in each stratum will be listed in an order fixed before sequence generation and identified to the statistician by anonymised cluster codes only. The sequence, seed and code will be retained by the statistician in a password-protected file and released to the trial team only at the allocation disclosure step.

Allocation concealment and implementation

The allocation sequence will remain inaccessible to the principal investigator, recruiters, enumerators and CHPs until all baseline procedures across all 18 CHUs are complete and the baseline dataset is locked. Cluster allocation will then be implemented by the independent statistician, who will transmit the arm assignment for each cluster code to the trial coordinator; the coordinator will communicate assignments to the county health management team and to CHU supervisors. No member of the field team will hold the sequence at any time. The date and time of allocation disclosure will be recorded. Because whole clusters are allocated, there is no participant-level allocation step.

Masking

Masking of CHPs and of caregivers in intervention CHUs is not possible: the intervention consists of counselling and coaching delivered face to face by an identifiable provider. The following masking will be implemented. Endline outcome assessors will be enumerators who were not involved in intervention delivery or supervision, will be assigned to CHUs on a rotating basis where logistics permit, and will not be informed of cluster allocation; caregivers will be asked at the start of each endline visit not to discuss any counselling they may have received until the assessment is complete. The primary analysis will be conducted by the trial statistician, who is separate from the independent statistician who generates and releases the sequence, with arms coded as A and B; the primary analysis will be finalised before arm labels are revealed. The data manager responsible for database lock will be masked to arm labels.

Qualitative analysts cannot be masked, because interview content reveals exposure.

Residual risks of observer and ascertainment bias are addressed by using a structured, behaviourally anchored observation instrument with standardised scoring rules, by conducting observer standardisation and inter-rater reliability exercises before and during data collection, by supervisor back-checks on at least 10% of households, and by prespecifying the analysis 340 model. Success of assessor masking will be recorded at the end of each endline visit by asking 341 the assessor to state which arm they believe the cluster was in, and this will be reported.

Materials.

Pictorial counselling cards and a flipbook covering cue recognition, non-coercive feeding, responsive positioning and pacing, age-appropriate texture and diversity, and feeding during illness; a household counselling register; a fidelity checklist; a supervisory checklist; and a referral directory. Caregivers receive a take-home pictorial reminder card. SMS reminders reinforcing key practices and signalling upcoming visits are sent to caregivers who provide a functioning telephone number and consent to receive them.

Procedures, mode and location of delivery.

Delivery is face to face, in the caregiver's home for individual counselling, and at an agreed community venue for group sessions. Each household visit follows the assess-analyse-act counselling sequence: the CHP observes or discusses a recent feeding episode, identifies one or two specific practices to change, demonstrates or role-models the practice with the caregiver, negotiates a feasible action, and reviews progress at the next visit. Group sessions use practical demonstration of meal preparation and responsive mealtime routines with peer problem-solving.

Schedule, intensity and duration.

The active intervention period is three months. Each enrolled dyad receives monthly household counselling and coaching visits during this period (a minimum of three visits, each lasting approximately 45-60 minutes), together with monthly mother-to-mother group sessions at CHU level and SMS reminders. Supportive supervision visits with fidelity assessment occur monthly. The active period is followed by a maintenance observation period to six months after initiation, during which no study-delivered intervention is provided and continuation of responsive feeding counselling as routine CHU practice is observed.

Tailoring and adaptation.

Counselling content is tailored to the index child's age module (breastfeeding responsiveness for children aged 0-5 months; complementary feeding responsiveness for children aged 6-59 months), to the specific practices identified as needing change in that household, and to household food availability. Counselling is delivered in the language preferred by the caregiver. Adaptations made during delivery will be documented prospectively on the fidelity checklist and reported.

Adherence, fidelity and modification.

Delivery adherence is monitored through CHP contact registers, monthly supervisory re-observation of at least 10% of counselling contacts, and fidelity checklist scoring (see Intervention fidelity). Where a CHP falls below the fidelity threshold, corrective coaching is delivered and the CHP is re-assessed at the next supervisory visit. There are no participant-level dose-escalation rules.

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying the intervention for an individual participant.

Counselling will be paused or discontinued for a dyad if the caregiver withdraws consent; if the index child is admitted for inpatient care, in which case counselling resumes after discharge if the family remains resident; if the caregiver or household requests a pause; or if a safeguarding or safety concern arises. Discontinuation of counselling does not remove the dyad from follow-up or from the intention-to-treat analysis, and outcome data will continue to be sought unless the caregiver withdraws consent for further data collection.

Concomitant care.

All routine county health services, including facility-based nutrition services, integrated management of acute malnutrition, immunisation, growth monitoring and existing health education, continue unchanged and are permitted in both arms. No care is withheld. Prohibited in control CHUs during the trial is the study-delivered responsive feeding package itself, including the training, job aids and SMS component.

Comparator

The comparator is usual community health service delivery. In control CHUs, CHPs continue their existing scope of work as defined under the Kenya Community Health Policy [13]: routine household visits, general health education, growth monitoring and promotion where scheduled, screening and referral for acute malnutrition using mid-upper arm circumference, immunisation defaulter tracing, and general maternal, newborn and child health messaging including generic infant and young child feeding advice. Control CHUs also retain access to all facility-based services and to any county or partner services available county-wide. What control CHUs do not receive during the trial is the structured five-day responsive feeding competency training, the responsive feeding counselling protocol and job aids, the scheduled monthly responsive feeding coaching visits, the group sessions, the SMS component and the responsive feeding fidelity supervision. Comparator activity is not standardised or intensified for trial purposes, consistent with the pragmatic design, and the actual content and frequency of routine contacts in control CHUs will be documented so that the comparator can be described empirically. On completion of the final trial assessment, CHPs in control CHUs will be offered the same five-day training and job aids, as agreed with the county.

Contamination prevention and monitoring

Contamination is addressed prospectively through design and monitoring. The two participating sub-counties are geographically separated; immediately adjacent CHUs are not both enrolled; CHPs are instructed in writing not to deliver responsive feeding counselling, job aids or demonstrations outside their assigned CHU, and this instruction is repeated at each supervisory visit; job aids and flipbooks are numbered, issued to named CHPs and accounted for at each supervisory visit; and group sessions are held within CHU boundaries with attendance registers. Movement of CHPs between CHUs, whether through transfer, reassignment or informal assistance, will be recorded. At endline, all caregivers in both arms will be asked the same set of exposure questions covering whether they received responsive feeding counselling, from whom, how often, and whether they received job aids or SMS messages. Cross-cluster contact will also be explored in the qualitative strand. Contamination is reported descriptively by arm and examined in a prespecified sensitivity analysis; the primary analysis remains intention-to-treat by allocated cluster.

Intervention fidelity

Fidelity is defined prospectively as the percentage of required counselling content correctly delivered during an observed contact, scored against a checklist covering the required modules for the child's age band: cue recognition, non-coercive feeding, positive prompting and pacing, autonomy and positioning, age-appropriate diversity and frequency, feeding during illness, use of job aids, negotiation of a specific action, and documentation. Each element is scored as delivered, partially delivered or not delivered, and the contact-level fidelity score is the percentage of the maximum attainable score. A contact-level score of at least 80% is the prespecified programmatic benchmark for high fidelity. Fidelity is assessed by supervisors through direct re-observation of at least 10% of household contacts each month and through review of counselling registers for all contacts. Dose delivered (number and duration of contacts per dyad), content coverage (proportion of required modules delivered across the intervention period), participant receipt (caregiver-reported recall of counselling content at endline) and documented adaptations are recorded separately from the fidelity score. Fidelity data are used descriptively, for corrective coaching, and in prespecified secondary and sensitivity analyses; they are not used to redefine the primary analysis population.

472 Construction and status of the instrument. The RFPI is a study-constructed composite that 473 has not yet been externally validated. Its observed mealtime module is adapted from published 474 structured observation approaches to caregiver feeding behaviour, and its caregiver-reported 475 module adapts items from existing responsive feeding and feeding practice questionnaires. The composite scoring, item weighting, age modules and missing-data rules are specific to this study and are prespecified here and in the instrument appendix. Age modules. Module BF applies to children aged 0-5 months and assesses breastfeeding responsiveness through five observed items and five caregiver-reported items. Module CF applies to children aged 6-59 months and assesses complementary feeding responsiveness through nine observed items and nine caregiver-reported items, with scoring anchors interpreted against age-appropriate expectations for self-feeding, texture and autonomy.

Exactly one module is administered per dyad, determined by the child's age in completed months at the assessment.

Scoring.

Observed items are scored 0 (not demonstrated), 1 (inconsistent or partial) or 2 (consistent). Caregiver-reported items use a five-point frequency scale mapped to 0, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5 and 2.0; negatively worded items are reverse-scored before mapping. For Module CF, the observed sum has a maximum of 18 and the reported sum a maximum of 18; RFPI = ((observed sum + reported sum) / 36) × 100. For Module BF, each sum has a maximum of 10; RFPI = ((observed sum + reported sum) / 20) × 100. Domain sub-scores are computed as the percentage of the maximum attainable within the domain. All items carry equal weight. Because both modules are expressed on a common 0-100 metric, scores from the two modules are analysed together, with child age module included as a covariate and as a prespecified exploratory subgroup factor.

Missing components.

For Module CF, a combined RFPI requires at least seven of nine observed items and at least seven of nine reported items; for Module BF, at least four of five in each set. Where the threshold is met, the sub-score is prorated over the completed items.

Where an observed mealtime is not feasible, a reported-only score is computed as (reported sum / maximum reported) × 100, the record is flagged, and the reason is documented. Reportedonly records are included in the primary analysis with an indicator variable for observation status, and a prespecified sensitivity analysis is restricted to dyads with a complete observed module.

Measurement properties to be assessed. During pretesting (see Pretesting and instrument piloting), internal consistency will be estimated using Cronbach's alpha and McDonald's omega for each module and reported as internal consistency estimates, not as evidence of validity. Inter-rater reliability of the observed module will be assessed through paired independent observation of at least 10% of pretest and main-study observations, reported as percentage agreement and as intraclass correlation coefficients or weighted kappa as appropriate. Structural properties will be examined through inter-item and item-total correlations and exploratory factor analysis of baseline data; because the trial is not powered as a psychometric study, these analyses are exploratory. Sensitivity to change will be examined descriptively in the control arm between baseline and endline. Content relevance was reviewed by the supervisory team during instrument development and will be re-examined with county nutrition staff before fieldwork.

Observation procedure

Where feasible, one naturally occurring mealtime or snack episode is observed in the home for 10-15 minutes for Module CF, and one breastfeeding episode for Module BF. Caregivers are informed at consent that an ordinary mealtime may be observed and are asked to feed the child as they normally would; no advance coaching or instruction is given, and the observation is scheduled by agreement with the caregiver. The observer remains unobtrusive, does not intervene during scoring except where the child's safety requires it, records behaviours in real time on the electronic form, and notes start and end times. Observations occur in a location chosen by the caregiver that affords adequate privacy. Enumerators are standardised through video-based scoring drills to a prespecified agreement threshold during training, with repeat drills at mid-point; paired observations for inter-rater reliability are conducted on at least 10% of assessments. If the child becomes distressed, the caregiver wishes to stop, or a safety concern arises, the observation is paused or abandoned and the reason recorded; the dyad then contributes a reported-only score.

Secondary outcomes

Caregiver responsive feeding knowledge is measured as a percentage score (0-100) on a structured test covering hunger and satiety signs, age-appropriate feeding, responsive practices and feeding during illness. Caregiver attitudes and self-efficacy are measured using 10-12 five-point Likert items summarised into subscale scores. Minimum dietary diversity is defined per the WHO/UNICEF 2021 indicator guidance as consumption of foods from at least five of eight defined food groups in the previous day, and applies only to children aged 6-23 months [30].

Minimum meal frequency is applied per the same guidance to children aged 6-23 months according to age and breastfeeding status [30]. For children aged 24-59 months, who fall outside the WHO infant and young child feeding indicator age range, a study-specific ageappropriate feeding practice measure retaining the WHO food-group taxonomy will be used and reported separately as a study-specific adapted measure, not as a WHO indicator. Caregiver experience of counselling is measured at endline using a short 5-7 item respectful counselling scale. Household food insecurity is measured using the Household Food Insecurity Access Scale and is treated as a covariate and subgroup factor rather than an outcome [31].

Caregiver knowledge, attitudes and self-efficacy are caregiver behavioural and cognitive outcomes and are reported as such; only the dietary diversity, meal frequency and ageappropriate feeding measures are child feeding outcomes.

Implementation outcomes

Implementation outcomes are organised by RE-AIM domain with prespecified numerators, denominators, data sources, measurement frequency and programmatic benchmarks (Table 3).

Reach is the proportion of enrolled eligible dyads in intervention CHUs receiving at least one responsive feeding counselling contact, with contact intensity as the mean number of contacts per enrolled dyad. Adoption is the proportion of trained CHPs delivering at least one responsive feeding counselling session, with session volume as the median number of sessions per CHP.

Implementation comprises the mean contact-level fidelity score, the proportion of required modules delivered, session completion and documentation completeness. Maintenance is assessed at six months after initiation and comprises the proportion of CHPs and supervisors reporting continued responsive feeding counselling, the proportion of intervention CHUs in which responsive feeding counselling appears in the routine CHU workplan, and reported resource requirements. Effectiveness within RE-AIM is represented by the trial's primary and secondary effectiveness outcomes. The benchmarks (at least 60% reach, at least 85% CHP adoption, at least 80% mean fidelity and a median of at least six responsive feeding counselling sessions per CHP over the three-575 month active period) are prespecified programmatic benchmarks agreed for this trial and its county context. They are not externally validated thresholds, and results will be reported against the observed distributions as well as against the benchmarks.

Process evaluation and qualitative component Purpose and timing. The qualitative strand explains how and why observed implementation and effectiveness patterns arose, and identifies determinants of sustainability. Data collection occurs at two points: at the end of the active intervention period (month 3), and at the maintenance assessment (month 6).

Sampling. Purposive sampling with maximum variation will be used. Intervention CHUs will be ranked by a composite implementation performance score derived from reach, adoption and fidelity, and higher-and lower-performing CHUs will both be sampled. Caregivers will be sampled to include those with high and low intervention exposure. CHPs will be sampled to include those with higher and lower fidelity scores. Supervisors and managers will be sampled by role.

Participants and volume. Approximately 12-16 key informant interviews will be conducted with county and sub-county health and nutrition managers, CHU in-charges, Community Health Assistants and supervisors, and where relevant partner organisation representatives.

Approximately six to eight focus group discussions will be conducted, comprising caregiver groups of 8-10 participants each and CHP groups of 6-10 participants each. These figures are planning estimates; the stopping rule is information power, judged against the specificity of the sample, the strength of dialogue and the analytic aim [32].

Procedures. Semi-structured guides organised around Adoption, Implementation and Maintenance, and around barriers and facilitators, will be used. Interviews and discussions will be conducted in the language preferred by participants by trained qualitative researchers who did not deliver the intervention, in a private location, lasting approximately 45-90 minutes.

Separate written informed consent, including explicit consent for audio recording, will be obtained. Recordings will be transcribed verbatim, translated into English where required, and back-checked against the recording for a sample of transcripts. Transcripts will be de-identified before analysis, with a separate secure linkage file.

Analysis.

A hybrid deductive-inductive thematic analysis will follow an established six-phase approach [33]. An initial codebook will be developed from the RE-AIM domains and the interview guides and expanded with inductive codes arising from the data. Two analysts will independently code the first three transcripts to refine the codebook; at least 20% of all transcripts will be double-coded thereafter. Coding discrepancies will be resolved by discussion, with a third team member adjudicating where consensus is not reached. An audit trail of codebook versions, analytic memos and team decisions will be maintained, and the team will record reflexive notes on the influence of professional and institutional positioning on interpretation. Data management and coding will use NVivo or comparable software.

Mixed-methods integration.

The design is convergent: quantitative and qualitative strands are collected in the same period and integrated at the interpretation and reporting stages, with integration also built in at design level through shared RE-AIM constructs [34]. Integration will be operationalised through joint displays organised by RE-AIM domain, in which quantitative implementation indicators for each CHU are placed alongside corresponding qualitative themes. Convergence, complementarity and discordance will be examined explicitly, and discordant findings will be reported and interpreted. Qualitative findings will also be used to interpret heterogeneity in effectiveness across clusters.

Sample size

The trial is powered on the primary outcome, the continuous RFPI (0-100). Because no prior RFPI data exist, a baseline mean of 55 and a standard deviation (SD) of 15 are planning assumptions; the pretest will provide a preliminary estimate of variability, but the sample size will not be revised. A between-arm difference of 6 points, equivalent to 0.4 SD, is treated as the minimum programmatically meaningful improvement; it is consistent with the pooled effects of early parenting interventions on parent-child interaction (standardised mean difference 0.39) and parenting practices (0.33) [3].

With a two-sided alpha of 0.05 and 80% power, the individually randomized requirement is n₀ = 2σ²(z₁₋α/₂ + z₁₋β)² / δ² = 2 × 225 × (1.96 + 0.84)² /36 = 98.1, that is 99 dyads per arm.

Approximately 19 dyads will be enrolled per CHU; allowing 10% loss to follow-up leaves about 17 analysable dyads per cluster. With an assumed intra-cluster correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.03, within the range reported for behavioural and health-related outcomes in community-based studies [35], the design effect is 1 + (17 -1) ×0.03 = 1.48, giving 99 ×1.48 = 147 analysable dyads per arm. Enrolling approximately 170 dyads per arm (340 in total across 18 CHUs, nine per arm) provides about 153 analysable dyads per arm after 10% attrition.

Because the number of clusters is fixed and small, power was also computed from a non-central t distribution with 2k -2 = 16 degrees of freedom [36, 37]. For an unadjusted comparison of endline means, power is 0.77. The prespecified primary analysis is baseline-adjusted (ANCOVA); assuming a baseline-endline correlation of 0.5, residual variance is reduced by the factor 1 -0.5² = 0.75 [38], and power is 0.88 (0.86 if loss to follow-up reaches 15%). The cluster-level formula with a t-correction [36] requires eight clusters per arm under ANCOVA (9.5 unadjusted); the planned nine per arm is therefore adequate for the primary baselineadjusted analysis. A correlation of 0.5 is a conservative assumption for repeated measurement of the same caregiver behaviour three months apart; the observed correlation and ICC will be reported, and no post hoc re-powering will be undertaken.

Data collection

Data are collected at three levels. Individual (dyad) level: socio-demographic characteristics, caregiver knowledge, attitudes and self-efficacy, RFPI observed and reported modules, infant and young child feeding indicators from a 24-hour recall, child illness in the preceding two weeks, household food insecurity, distance and access, and, at endline, exposure questions and the counselling experience scale. CHP level: training attendance and competency assessment results, contact registers, session records, fidelity checklist scores and supervisory notes.

Cluster level: CHU characteristics, CHP establishment and staffing changes, group session registers, supervisory visit records, workplan review at maintenance, and documented contamination events.

Baseline and endline surveys use the same instruments and procedures in both arms.

Enumerators and research assistants will receive a three-day training covering research ethics and consent, the study instruments, tablet-based data capture, interviewing technique, observation scoring drills and referral procedures, followed by supervised field practice and an inter-rater reliability exercise.

Electronic data capture

Data are captured electronically using KoboToolbox forms on mobile devices, with skip logic, required fields, range and logical consistency checks, constrained response options, automatic timestamping of form start and end, and daily encrypted synchronisation to a secure server.

Access is role-based, with distinct permissions for enumerators, supervisors, the data manager and the statistician. Devices are password-protected and forms are not retained locally after successful synchronisation. Pretesting and instrument piloting

The study instruments, including the RFPI observed and reported modules, will be pretested in Tiaty West Sub-County, Baringo County, in December 2026. This is an instrument pretest and observer standardisation exercise, not a pilot randomized trial, and no randomization, allocation or intervention delivery occurs there. Tiaty West was selected because it is an ASAL sub-county with predominantly pastoral livelihoods and a malnutrition burden comparable to the trial area, and because it lies outside West Pokot County and therefore outside the sampling frame. Pretest participants are not enrolled in the trial and pretest data will not be included in any trial analysis.

Quality assurance

Quality assurance procedures comprise standardised enumerator training with a certification threshold; observer standardisation drills and repeat inter-rater reliability exercises; daily field supervision and end-of-day team debriefs; supervisor back-checks by re-visit or telephone verification in at least 10% of households; automated completeness and consistency reports generated weekly; review of records flagged for extreme values, high rates of not-applicable responses or implausibly short administration times; documented correction procedures with all changes captured in the audit trail; and monthly data quality reports to the trial coordinator.

Each cluster receives a unique CHU identifier and each dyad a unique participant identifier constructed from the cluster code and a serial number. Primary analysis

The primary analysis is a mixed-effects linear regression of endline RFPI with a random intercept for CHU, a fixed effect for allocated arm, adjustment for baseline RFPI, and adjustment for the prespecified covariates listed below. This analysis-of-covariance formulation is more efficient than analysis of change scores when baseline and endline are measured on the same dyads and avoids regression-to-the-mean artefacts. The treatment effect is reported as the adjusted mean difference in RFPI with a 95% confidence interval and a twosided p-value at alpha 0.05, together with a standardised effect size and the estimated ICC with a confidence interval. Denominator degrees of freedom will be based on the number of clusters using the Kenward-Roger approximation. Model assumptions will be examined through inspection of residuals at both levels; if the residual distribution is materially non-normal, a cluster-level bootstrap or a rank-based alternative will be used and reported.

Prespecified covariates

The following covariates are prespecified and are included irrespective of their statistical significance: baseline RFPI; randomization stratum (sub-county); child age module (0-5 versus 6-59 months); child age in months; child sex; caregiver education; and household food insecurity. Covariates are not selected using univariable p-value screening. The covariate set is deliberately limited because the trial has 18 clusters; no cluster-level covariate other than the stratification factor is included in the primary model.

Secondary outcome analyses

Continuous secondary outcomes (knowledge, attitude, self-efficacy and counselling experience scores) are analysed using mixed-effects linear regression with a random intercept for CHU and the same covariate set, with adjustment for the corresponding baseline value where measured. Binary secondary outcomes (minimum dietary diversity among children aged 6-23 months; minimum meal frequency) are analysed using mixed-effects log-binomial regression to obtain risk ratios; where the model fails to converge, modified Poisson regression with robust cluster-level variance estimation will be used [39]. Risk ratios rather than odds ratios are reported because these outcomes are common. Ordinal outcomes, where they arise from Likertbased items analysed at item level, are analysed using cumulative link mixed models with a random intercept for CHU; the proportional odds assumption will be assessed, and a partial proportional odds or robust alternative used where it is violated. Implementation outcomes are summarised descriptively with cluster-level and CHP-level denominators and 95% confidence intervals; they are not subjected to between-arm hypothesis testing.

Multiplicity

One primary outcome and one primary hypothesis are specified, and no adjustment for multiplicity is applied to it. Secondary outcomes are interpreted as a set, primarily through effect sizes and confidence intervals; the number of comparisons will be stated and secondary findings described as supportive. Subgroup analyses are labelled exploratory. No interim efficacy analysis is planned.

Missing data

The extent, pattern and stated reasons for missing data will be tabulated by arm, distinguishing item-level missingness, missing observed modules and complete loss to follow-up. Baseline characteristics of dyads retained and lost will be compared descriptively. Dyads lost to followup will not be replaced; the cohort is fixed at baseline. The primary model, being likelihoodbased, provides valid inference under a missing-at-random assumption conditional on the covariates and the observed baseline value. Where endline outcome data are missing for more than 10% of dyads, multiple imputation by chained equations will be used as a supportive analysis, with the imputation model including arm, baseline RFPI, all analysis covariates and cluster membership, so that it respects the clustered structure and is congenial with the analysis model. Departures from missing-at-random will be examined through a tipping-point analysis applying successively less favourable assumptions to missing intervention-arm outcomes.

Missing values of prespecified baseline covariates will be handled by single imputation with a missing-indicator, an approach that is valid for baseline covariates in randomized trials [40], so that every prespecified covariate is retained in the primary model.

Sensitivity and subgroup analyses

Prespecified sensitivity analyses are: (i) restriction to dyads with a complete observed RFPI module; (ii) alternative RFPI scoring, using domain-mean scoring in place of item-sum scoring; (iii) exclusion of control dyads reporting substantive responsive feeding counselling exposure; (iv) weighting for cluster-size imbalance, and a cluster-level (two-stage) analysis of cluster mean scores; (v) exclusion of dyads with major protocol deviations; (vi) a per-protocol analysis defined by fidelity and dose as above; and (vii) alternative missing-data assumptions as described.

None of these replaces the primary intention-to-treat analysis; all are reported alongside it.

Prespecified exploratory subgroup analyses examine effect modification by child age module (0-5 versus 6-59 months), household food insecurity status, randomization stratum and baseline RFPI tertile, using treatment-by-subgroup interaction tests. Subgroup findings will be interpreted as hypothesis-generating; the trial is not powered for interaction. Analyses will be conducted in R or Stata. The full statistical analysis plan will be finalised and dated before the endline database lock and will be made available in the trial registry record.

Harms and safeguarding

This is a low-risk behavioural intervention delivered within routine community health services, and no physical or pharmacological risk is anticipated. Foreseeable harms are nonetheless specified. Participant burden arises from survey administration (approximately 45-60 minutes) and mealtime observation; discomfort or self-consciousness may arise during observation; social discomfort may arise if counselling is perceived as criticism of a caregiver's feeding; confidentiality risks arise from household-level data collection in small communities; and inappropriate advice could be given by a CHP whose competence has drifted, which the competency assessment, fidelity monitoring and corrective coaching are designed to prevent.

Counselling could also unintentionally increase caregiver anxiety about a child's intake; counselling protocols therefore emphasise non-blaming, strengths-based framing, and CHPs are trained not to set intake targets.

Harms will be assessed through spontaneous report to CHPs, supervisors or enumerators, and systematically through a standard end-of-visit enquiry at every household assessment. All reports are recorded on a standardised form, reviewed by the principal investigator within 72 hours, and classified by seriousness and relatedness to trial participation. Serious events, defined as any event resulting in hospitalisation, significant disability or death of a participant or index child, and any safeguarding concern, are reported to the principal investigator within 24 hours and to the Masinde Muliro University of Science and Technology Institutional Scientific and Ethics Review Committee (MMUST-ISERC) within 72 hours of notification, and to the county health authority. A cumulative harms log is maintained and will be reported by arm.

Referral. Any index child identified during any study contact with a mid-upper arm circumference or other indicator meeting county thresholds for moderate or severe acute malnutrition, with nutritional oedema, with a persistent feeding difficulty, or with danger signs of serious illness, is referred immediately to the nearest health facility using the county referral protocol, in both arms; referral is never delayed for study procedures. Referrals are documented and followed up by the CHP, and referral completion is recorded.

Child safeguarding. SPIRIT-C safeguards are applied [27]. Observation is limited to a single naturally occurring feeding episode of 10-15 minutes. Feeding is not interrupted or manipulated for study purposes. If the child becomes distressed, the observation is paused or ended. The caregiver may stop the observation at any time without giving a reason and without any effect on services received. Observation takes place in a private setting chosen by the caregiver. No photographs or video recordings of children are taken. Children are never left alone with a study team member or CHP. Any suspicion of child harm or neglect is escalated to the principal investigator and referred to the county child protection authority in line with Kenyan law, and this is stated in the consent process. Study staff and CHPs receive safeguarding orientation as part of training, and CHPs are trained not to label ordinary refusal or fussiness as a disorder. Participation does not alter the child's daily routine: assessments take place at home at times chosen by the caregiver, and the only child-facing procedure is observation of one ordinary feeding episode. Families will be thanked individually at the endline visit and recognised collectively at the community feedback meetings; no payment is made for participation. No assessment of outcomes or harms is planned after the month-6 maintenance assessment; children continue to receive routine county health and nutrition services, including referral, after the trial.

Discussion

This trial addresses a specific gap: responsive feeding has become a guideline-level recommendation [2] without a corresponding evidence base on how it can be delivered through the frontline cadres that reach households in low-resource, dispersed settings. By randomizing CHUs rather than individuals or sub-counties, the design tests the package as it would be deployed -through an intact community health team, under existing supervision, within an existing workplan -while preserving the internal validity of a randomized comparison.

Conclusion

This trial will generate evidence on whether a structured, competency-based responsive feeding package delivered by Community Health Promoters improves caregiver responsive feeding practices in an arid and semi-arid Kenyan setting, and on whether that package can be delivered with acceptable reach, adoption and fidelity within routine community health services. By combining a randomized effectiveness comparison with a prospectively specified implementation evaluation, the trial is intended to inform decisions about integrating responsive feeding counselling into the Kenyan community health platform and about its relevance to comparable pastoral and ASAL contexts.

Measurement combines direct observation of feeding interaction with caregiver report, which addresses the susceptibility of self-reported feeding style to social desirability. Because reach, adoption, fidelity and maintenance are prespecified with defined numerators and denominators, the trial will also produce the information a county health management team would need to decide whether to adopt the package [23] [24] [25].

Delivering counselling through CHPs with existing caseloads, without supplementing staffing, means the estimated effect is an effect under realistic conditions and is likely to be smaller than an efficacy estimate obtained under enhanced supervision. Domains.

Items load onto four conceptual domains: (i) Cue Responsiveness -recognition of and prompt, appropriate response to hunger and satiety cues; (ii) Positive Prompts and Pacing -warm encouragement, modelling and assistance, with pacing matched to the child's tempo; (iii) Autonomy and Structure -age-appropriate self-feeding opportunity, predictable routine and supportive positioning; (iv) Non-Coercion and Distraction Management -absence of pressure, force, threats or bribes, and management of competing distractions.
