Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Background: During the 2014 Ebola outbreak in Sierra Leone, widespread infections among healthcare workers at Kenema Government Hospital severely disrupted clinical operations. Whether these infections primarily arose from repeated patient exposures or from transmission among colleagues remained unclear, particularly as patient admissions exceeded the hospital's capacity for safe isolation and care.

Methods: We revisited the outbreak using expanded viral surveillance and classical genomic analysis, Bayesian transmission inference with juniper, and firsthand epidemiologic accounts from hospital staff. We analyzed 279 Ebola virus genomes from Kenema District, including 35 from infected healthcare workers, and 514 genomes in the broader transmission reconstruction, explicitly accounting for unsampled cases.

Results: The 35 healthcare worker genomes comprised 28 haplotypes, including five shared by multiple cases. In the reconstructed transmission network, 22 candidate healthcare worker-to-healthcare worker links exceeded a posterior probability threshold of 5%, representing 6.1-fold enrichment over expectation. Healthcare workers accounted for 33.2% of expected onward transmissions at their peak in the two-week window beginning June 23, 2014. Firsthand anecdotal accounts situated inferred relationships within specific wards, clinical procedures, and working relationships of KGH, including care of infected colleagues and infections among laboratory, pharmacy, and other personnel beyond routine bedside care.

Conclusions: Ebola transmission involved interconnected groups of healthcare workers across multiple hospital departments during a period of severe resource constraints and expanding patient demand. These findings underscore the importance of protecting and surveilling healthcare workers as an interconnected workforce during emerging outbreaks.

Introduction

Healthcare workers (HCWs) are among the populations at greatest risk during outbreaks of viral hemorrhagic fever [1] [2] [3] . They care for highly infectious patients, often under conditions of resource scarcity and intense operational strain, and have experienced disproportionate infection and mortality during outbreaks of Ebola virus disease (EVD), Marburg virus disease, and Lassa fever [3] [4] [5] [6] [7] [8] [9] [10] . These infections not only cost lives but can erode the workforce needed to sustain an outbreak response 4 . Protecting HCWs is therefore essential both for individual safety and for maintaining the functioning of the health system.

The 2013-2016 West African EVD epidemic, the largest EVD outbreak recorded to date, exposed the consequences of healthcare-associated transmission at unprecedented scale 11 . As Ebola spread across Guinea, Sierra Leone, and Liberia, healthcare facilities became major sites of exposure, and frontline workers faced repeated risk through patient care and within health systems under rapidly escalating pressure 4, 6, [11] [12] [13] . Kenema Government Hospital (KGH) was at the center of Sierra Leone's early response 6, 14 . For decades, KGH had served as a regional referral center for Lassa fever, with specialized clinical and laboratory capacity for viral hemorrhagic fever diagnosis and care 6, 14, 15 . After EVD was confirmed in Guinea, the KGH Lassa fever team rapidly established Ebola diagnostic capacity, confirmed Sierra Leone's first Ebola case, and converted the Lassa fever ward into the country's first dedicated Ebola treatment unit as cases increased 6, 12, 15, 16 . KGH's deep expertise with viral hemorrhagic fever could not compensate for the lack of protective infrastructure needed for an outbreak of this scale. During the early months of the epidemic, shortages of personal protective equipment (PPE), clean water, chlorine, reliable electricity, trained personnel, and isolation capacity constrained infection prevention and control 1, 6, 15 . Clinical and laboratory staff at times cared for large numbers of highly infectious patients with limited resources, while infections among healthcare workers further reduced staffing and intensified pressure on the response 4, 6.

Although HCW infections became a defining feature of the 2014-2016 West African EVD epidemic, it remained unclear how those infections arose and spread. Infection prevention appropriately focuses on exposure from infected patients, but healthcare workers are also part of a highly interconnected workforce, interacting across wards, procedures, handoffs, shared spaces, and care of sick colleagues. 6 At KGH, where the workforce was heavily affected, a central unresolved question was whether infections primarily reflected separate exposures to infected patients or whether Ebola had also begun to propagate among healthcare workers themselves.

Reconstructing these transmission pathways has historically been difficult. Outbreaks often yield too few sampled cases, incomplete epidemiologic information, or insufficient genomic coverage to resolve transmission pathways. Even when dense sequencing is available, viral genomes can change too slowly relative to the pace of transmission to distinguish closely related infections: during the West African Ebola epidemic, successive cases could carry identical or nearly identical consensus genomes, leaving both the direction of transmission and the possibility of unsampled intermediates unresolved 12, 17, 18 . More recently, transmission-reconstruction approaches that jointly model phylogeny, transmission, and unsampled cases have made it possible to extract substantially more information from densely sampled outbreak datasets 19, 20.

Here, we revisit the KGH outbreak amongst HCWs using expanded viral genomic sequencing, transmission inference with juniper, epidemiologic investigation, and firsthand accounts from KGH staff. We sought to assess the evidence for transmission among healthcare workers, examine plausible pathways across wards, clinical events, and working relationships, and understand how these interactions may have heightened the vulnerability of the hospital workforce.

Results

Classical genomic analysis identifies a closely related HCW cluster but cannot resolve transmission

To reconstruct transmission among healthcare workers at KGH, we expanded existing genomic surveillance to capture substantially more of the infected healthcare workforce. Earlier sequencing efforts established that the Sierra Leone epidemic descended from a single introduction across the Guinean border and documented sustained local transmission within Kenema District, but were not designed to examine transmission within the KGH healthcare workforce 12, 18 . We therefore generated 63 additional Ebola virus genomes from KGH patients and staff, including 21 previously unsequenced healthcare workers. Together with our earlier sequencing efforts, this yielded a dataset comprising 279 of 591 (47%) confirmed EVD cases reported in Kenema District during the May-November 2014 period and 35 of 79 infected KGH healthcare workers during that period (44%) 6.

Expanded genomic sampling revealed that healthcare worker infections were concentrated in several closely related clusters within the broader Kenema outbreak. Using IQ-TREE, we inferred a maximumlikelihood phylogeny from the Kenema District consensus genomes, with branch lengths representing substitutions per site (Figure 1A) 21 . HCW sequences were concentrated within a limited region of the phylogeny constructed from cases sequenced at KGH, many of which originating in Kenema District, while other HCW sequences were interspersed among non-Kenema District cases. Because KGH also cared for patients referred from other districts, this phylogeny does not represent all patients treated at KGH. HCW infections were genomically concentrated but did not form a single monophyletic clade. Genomic comparison showed that many HCW infections were separated by very little sequence variation. The 35 sequences collapsed into 28 distinct haplotypes, with most neighboring haplotypes separated by only one or two substitutions and five haplotypes shared by more than one case (Figure 1C). The network was consistent with an infinite-sites model, with each of the 55 variable consensus sites (Figure 1B) represented by exactly one substitution across the network (Figure 1C), requiring no recurrent mutations or reversions. Several epidemiologically proximate HCWs carried identical consensus genomes, while others differed by only one or two substitutions.

The evolutionary rate of Ebola virus helps explain why classical genomic analysis has limited resolution among closely spaced transmission events. Published estimates from the West African epidemic place the evolutionary rate at approximately 1.2 × 10⁻³ substitutions per site per year (95% CrI: 1.13-1.27 × 10⁻³), corresponding to roughly one consensus substitution across the 18.9-kb genome every 16 days 22, 23 . With mean serial intervals of approximately 14-15 days, successive infections could carry identical consensus genomes, while differences of one or two substitutions could not distinguish direct transmission from chains involving unsampled intermediates 24 . Classical genomic analysis thus established a closely related and clustered set of HCW infections at KGH, but could not determine the direction or probability of transmission among them.

Epidemiologic accounts situate inferred HCW transmission within the hospital

Our genomic analyses identified potential transmission relationships among healthcare workers but could not establish where exposures occurred or what clinical circumstances connected individual cases. We therefore integrated the genomic reconstruction with epidemiologic records and firsthand accounts from KGH staff, including one of the authors (M.M.), who worked at KGH during the outbreak. These accounts helped contextualize inferred links through specific wards, clinical procedures, and working relationships within the hospital (Figure 3).

The earliest reconstructed HCW infections linked a hospital outreach driver with a nurse who cared for him. HCW.3, a driver for the KGH Lassa fever outreach team, had recently traveled to Koindu, where Ebola had circulated before its recognition in Kenema. The driver's viral sequence was identical at the consensus level to that of an unidentified patient. HCW.3 was admitted to the Annex Ward on June 18, 2014, when consistent use of appropriate PPE had not yet been established in the ward. HCW.9, a nurse who cared for HCW.3, developed illness eleven days later. The two HCWs' viral sequences were also identical at the consensus level. Together, the genomic similarity, documented care relationship, and timing of illness supported a transmission link between HCW.3 and HCW.9, although the sequence data alone could not establish direction.

As Ebola spread among KGH staff, caring for infected colleagues under severe resource constraints created additional opportunities for HCW-to-HCW exposure. When HCW.9, who was pregnant, deteriorated clinically, nurses HCW.11 and HCW.19 attempted a stillbirth delivery, a procedure routinely performed in Lassa fever care. Both nurses developed symptoms in the following weeks and subsequently tested positive for Ebola. Their viral consensus sequences were identical, and juniper inferred a potential transmission link between them. Their involvement in HCW.9's care represented a plausible shared exposure, although the available evidence could not distinguish direct transmission between the nurses from infections acquired through a common source.

A further cluster subsequently emerged in the Lassa Ward, involving three nurses and a physician (Figure 3). HCW.12, HCW.15, and HCW.16 tested positive for Ebola on July 14, 2014, followed six days later by HCW.20, a physician on the ward. HCW.12, HCW.16, and HCW.20 shared identical viral consensus sequences, while HCW.15 differed by two consensus SNPs (Figure 1B-C). The juniper reconstruction identified multiple potential transmission links among these four HCWs (Figure 2A), consistent with the closely connected relationships depicted in Figure 3. Their shared clinical setting, closely spaced diagnoses, and genomic relationships support a cluster of epidemiologically linked infections within the Lassa Ward, although the precise sources and direction of transmission remain uncertain.

Transmission relationships also extended beyond HCWs providing routine bedside care with direct patient contact. HCW.18, a pharmacy dispenser, carried a viral consensus sequence differing by one SNP from that of HCW.3, the driver involved in the early Annex Ward exposure (Figure 1B-C). The juniper reconstruction identified a potential transmission linkage between HCW.18 and HCW.14, a Lassa Ward nurse, whose sequences differed by four consensus SNPs (Figures 2A, 3). HCW.14 and HCW.25, another Lassa Ward nurse, shared identical viral consensus sequences and were also linked in the transmission reconstruction (Figures 1B-C, 2A, 3). These relationships suggested connections between clinical staff and personnel working elsewhere in the hospital, although the precise exposure settings and transmission pathways remained uncertain. Other infected HCWs included HCW.24, a member of the hospital burial team, whose duties involved handling the bodies of deceased patients and presented distinct occupational exposure risks (Figure 3).

As Ebola patient admissions overwhelmed the Lassa Ward, KGH progressively converted its remaining wards into designated Ebola treatment areas, with routine hospital services for other illnesses effectively coming to a halt by late-August. Infections among HCWs continued amid this hospital-wide reorganization, and inferred transmission relationships involved staff across the broader hospital wards. The juniper reconstruction linked HCW.17, a General Ward nurse, with HCW.29, a laboratory technician, whose viral sequences differed by one consensus SNP, and HCW.31, another laboratory technician, with HCW.35, a General Ward nurse, whose sequences differed by two SNPs (Figures 1B-C, 2A, 3). The broader network also identified potential links involving HCW.30, a security officer, and HCW.13 and HCW.23, a Lassa Ward nurse and General Ward nursing aide, respectively (Figure 2A). Other HCW infections, including HCW.28, HCW.32, HCW.33, and HCW.36, occurred later in the outbreak but were not connected by specific inferred pairwise links (Figures 1A, 3). These findings suggest that infections among HCWs extended across multiple occupational groups and continued beyond the initial Lassa Ward cluster, although the available evidence could not determine whether individual infections arose through occupational exposure, contact outside the hospital, or separate introductions into the workforce.

Together, the genomic and epidemiologic evidence supports transmission among healthcare workers across multiple hospital settings. Where sequence data were unavailable, we inferred links by combining epidemiologic accounts with the genomic comparisons among sequenced cases in the proposed chain; these links carry greater uncertainty. Even with its limitations the combined reconstruction supports a coherent pattern: an early exposure near the hospital's periphery, movement through the Annex and General Wards, further amplification during care of infected colleagues and within the Lassa Ward, and additional infections among staff outside direct bedside care before a later, distinct HCW cluster emerged.

Genomic epidemiology can reveal patterns of viral spread, but understanding how transmission unfolds within a health system also requires examining the human relationships and circumstances behind those patterns. At KGH, expanded genomic sampling identified closely related HCW clusters, but classical genomic analyses could not resolve individual transmission pathways. We used juniper transmission inference to estimate posterior probability for alternative transmission links while explicitly accounting for unsampled cases. Epidemiologic records and firsthand accounts then situated inferred links within specific wards, clinical events, and working relationships, including care of infected colleagues under severe resource constraints. By integrating these approaches, we could better understand the circumstances and vulnerabilities that may have enabled transmission within the healthcare workforce, while retaining uncertainty about individual sources of infection. KGH illustrates the danger of an outbreak outpacing the resources needed to provide safe care. Despite longstanding experience with viral hemorrhagic fever, KGH could not compensate for shortages of protective supplies, personnel, and isolation capacity as Ebola admissions increased. 6, 15 HCW infections peaked before or during the expansion of external support, although the timing alone cannot establish the effects of individual interventions (Figure 4). Preparedness plans should therefore equip frontline hospitals with protective supplies in advance and provide for rapid expansion of staffing and isolation capacity at the first signs of an outbreak.

Outbreak response must not only protect healthcare workers during patient care but also prevent infection from spreading among colleagues. Staff with repeated patient exposures continue to interact through handoffs, shared workspaces, meals, transportation, and care of sick colleagues. Without early detection and adequate protection, one infection can spread among colleagues and weaken the very team needed to care for patients. Healthcare workers should therefore be a priority surveillance population, with rapid access to testing, active investigation of clusters among clinical and support staff, and infection-control practices that explicitly consider transmission between colleagues.

Although we identified broader patterns of transmission within the healthcare workforce, our study has several limitations, particularly in resolving individual transmission links. We analyzed genomes from approximately 47% of confirmed EVD cases in Kenema District and 44% of infected HCWs, leaving unsampled intermediates plausible for many links. Uneven sequencing coverage also means that the inferred HCW contribution to onward transmission reflects the reconstructed network rather than the epidemic as a whole. Although juniper models unsampled cases, its transmission assignments remain probabilistic and depend on the model's assumptions 19 . Close genomic relationships alone cannot establish direct transmission or exclude shared patient exposures and community acquisition. 25 We also could not account systematically for social or personal interactions among healthcare workers outside the hospital, and some transmission attributed to workplace relationships may instead have occurred through contact between colleagues in the community or while caring for one another outside formal clinical settings. The staff recollections we used in the study may be incomplete or inaccurate, even when case records support the reported timing. We relied on those accounts to help interpret the inferred links, not to confirm them independently.

The 2026 Bundibugyo virus outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo underscores both the continuing vulnerability of HCWs and the importance of genomic epidemiology in revealing transmission risks. HCWs were among the earliest recognized fatalities, and no vaccine or specific treatment is currently approved for Bundibugyo virus disease 26, 27 . Continued genomic epidemiologic investigations, integrated with local epidemiologic knowledge and firsthand accounts, may reveal other vulnerabilities that would otherwise remain hidden. Methods

Patient sample collection

Research involving human subjects at the Kenema Government Hospital was approved by the Sierra Leone Ethics and Scientific Review Committee, Tulane University and Harvard University Institutional Review Boards (IRBs) in accordance with National Institutes of Health guidelines. All patients enrolled through 25 May 2014 provided informed consent and were enrolled as having suspected Lassa fever prior to the first diagnosis of an Ebola case in Sierra Leone. Because of the Ebola outbreak, a waiver of written consent was granted for testing samples obtained from patients who enrolled after 25 May. Scientists wishing to obtain a key linking the current case designations of deidentified patients to previously published deidentified patient identifiers should contact the authors.

To gain a deeper understanding of transmission we first identified additional samples stored at the KGH biobank for viral sequencing. From June through November 2014, 591 cases of EVD originated in Kenema District, including 79 (15%) from HCWs. We identified 14 HCW sequences previously sequenced and generated 22 new sequences from known HCWs for a total of 36 sequences, representing approximately half of infected HCWs in Kenema District 12, 18 . We also generated 42 new Ebola sequences from the general population of Kenema District collected around the same time as the HCWs. Sequencing of these samples occurred at the Kenema Government Hospital's sequencing core and the Broad Institute. Combined with sequences obtained in our earlier studies this brings the number of sequences from Kenema District to 280, 47% of the total EVD cases reported during this period.

Sample Processing

Serum/plasma from whole blood was prepared according to the method described in (Matranga et al., 2014) and, used to extract Ebola RNA using QIAamp Viral RNA Minikit (Qiagen) according to manufacturer's instructions 28 . Briefly, 140 µl of serum/plasma were inactivated by AVL and ethanol treatment. Purification of extracted RNA was completed by washing the spin column twice using antiviral wash buffers (AW1& AW2). Purified RNA was then eluted in 60 µl of elution buffer in a 1.5ml eppendorf tube and kept in a -20˚C freezer while in processing and in a -80˚C freezer for a longer period during shipments.

We utilized Sybr Green RNA-to-Ct 1-Step qRT-PCR assay (Life Technologies) for absolute quantification of Ebola virus genome in real-time. We used Kulesh primer set (Forward and Reverse) to amplify region within the NP gene according to protocol previously described in (Drosten et al., 2002) on Roche LightCycler 96 with some modification 29 . Briefly, kulesh primers were used at a loading concentration of 10 µM and a reaction volume of 10 µl containing 3µl of RNA and 7µl of master mix was used and, each sample was run in triplicate.

Viral Sequencing

Towards constructing sequencing libraries, samples were enriched by both 18s depletion and turbo DNase treatment and, cDNA was synthesized in batches as previously described (Adiconis et al., 2013 was referenced in Matranga et al., 2014 for 18s depletion (26)) 28, 30 . Sequencing libraries were constructed using Illumina Nextera library construction kits according to the methods of Matranga et al. 28 . Complete sequencing libraries were cleaned up using Ampure DNA SPRI and, quantified by both KAPA qPCR and BioAnalyzer using the manufacturer's instructions. Pooled libraries were paired-end sequenced at a loading concentration of 12 pM for 101 cycles using Illumina V2 kit on Illumina MiSeq platform.

Genomic Analysis

Consensus genomes were aligned to the Ebola virus reference KJ660346.2, following the parameters from Gire et al.. Alignments were screened for clustered same-direction transitions before analysis, as ADARmediated editing can introduce runs of T-to-C substitutions within a short genomic interval that do not represent independently accumulated mutations as we previously reported in Park et al. 18 . Genomic sites were masked where 5 or more same-direction T-to-C transitions occurred within a consecutive 200bp window. One such cluster met this criterion: eight T-to-C substitutions between positions 5515 and 5560 in HCW.11 and HCW.19. These sites were masked in all downstream analyses, including phylogenetic reconstruction, haplotype network construction, and transmission inference.

Maximum-likelihood phylogenies were inferred in IQ-TREE, with the best-fitting nucleotide substitution model selected using ModelFinder and branch lengths estimated in substitutions per site 21 . Trees were visualized in FigTree v1.4.4, with patient-only clades collapsed for display 31 . Pairwise SNP distances among the 35 HCW consensus genomes were computed directly from the masked multiple-sequence alignment, excluding sites with ambiguous calls in either sequence. The haplotype network was constructed from the 35 HCW consensus genomes: sequences with identical genotypes across all variable sites were collapsed into single haplotypes. Each edge is annotated with the specific genomic positions differentiating the haplotypes it connects. Because the network is a minimum spanning tree, a single topology is shown where several equally parsimonious connections may exist; alternative resolutions are not displayed.

Analyses and figures were generated in Python v.3.12.4.

Transmission Reconstruction Analysis

Transmission histories were reconstructed using juniper, a Bayesian framework that jointly infers the phylogeny and the transmission network from consensus genome sequences, sampling times, and case metadata, while explicitly modeling unsampled cases 19.

The model was fit to 521 Ebola virus consensus genomes with associated collection dates. 7 genomes were excluded due to a set sample cutoff date of Nov 16, 2014, resulting in a total of 514 genomes included in the juniper analysis. Priors were specified as a multivariate normal distribution on the log-transformed parameter vector (log λT, log ψ, log troot, log μ), with mean (2.904165, 0.60158, 0, -6.9077551) and identity covariance matrix, giving independent log-normal priors with unit standard deviation on the log scale. This corresponds to prior medians of 18.25 year -1 for the transmission rate λT, 1.8 year -1 for the sampling rate ψ, 1 year for the root time troot, and 1.0 x 10 -3 substitutions per site per year for the substitution rate μ, each with a 95% prior interval spanning roughly a seven-fold range above and below the median (for example, 1.41 x 10 -4 to 7.10 x 10 -3 for μ). Inference was run for 10,000 MCMC iterations, with the first 20% discarded as burn-in. Convergence was assessed by effective sample size (ESS), which exceeded 200 for all reported parameters (outbreak start date, ESS = 980; substitution rate, ESS = 262; time-dependent reproductive number, minimum ESS = 599).

Posterior estimates were summarized as medians with 95% credible intervals. The substitution rate was estimated at 1.16 x 10 -3 substitutions per site per year (95% CrI 1.05 x 10 -3 -1.28 x 10 -3 ; Figure 2C) and the outbreak start date at 17 February 2014 (95% CrI 14 January-05 March 2014; Figure 2B). The timedependent reproductive number, R = λ/(γ + ψ), is reported as the posterior median with 95% credible interval (Figure 2D).

Pairwise transmission probabilities were extracted as the posterior frequency with which each ordered pair appeared as a direct transmission link across sampled trees. The network in Figure 2A displays edges with posterior probability ≥ 0.05, with edge width proportional to probability; links inferred between two healthcare workers are highlighted separately. To produce Figure 2E, each inferred transmission event was assigned to the two-week window containing the infector's infection date and classified by the infector's status (healthcare worker, close contact, or unknown), and the resulting offspring means were averaged across posterior trees, including transmissions to unsampled cases.

Anecdotal contact tracing

Kenema Government Hospital housed Sierra Leone's national Lassa fever laboratory and was, throughout the epidemic, the only facility in the country with established infrastructure for viral hemorrhagic fever diagnostics, surveillance, and clinical care 15 . The KGH viral hemorrhagic fever team operated on behalf of and in collaboration with the Sierra Leone Ministry of Health and comprised clinical, laboratory, and outreach staff, several of whom are authors on this study.

Contextual information on ward assignments, clinical procedures, and working relationships among KGH staff was obtained from firsthand accounts provided by team members who worked at KGH throughout the 2014 outbreak, including M.M., who led the KGH laboratory; L.K., who led the KGH outreach team; A.G., director of the viral hemorrhagic fever laboratory; and other hospital staff. Reported details were corroborated where possible against laboratory case logs and dates of symptom onset or confirmed diagnosis held at KGH. Details that could not be independently verified against these sources are identified as such.

Accounts were used to situate transmission relationships already inferred from the genomic data rather than to generate candidate links independently. For each pair of cases connected in the reconstruction, we asked whether the two individuals had occupied a common ward, participated in a shared clinical procedure, or maintained a professional or personal relationship that would have likely brought them into contact or exposure during the relevant interval. Where sequence data were unavailable for an individual named in an account, that case was positioned in the reconstruction by triangulating the reported epidemiologic contacts against the SNP distances separating the nearest sequenced cases on either side. Links inferred in this way carry greater uncertainty than those supported directly by sequence comparison and are indicated in Figure 3.

Case identifiers (HCW.1-HCW.36) were assigned to de-identify individual healthcare workers, and the mapping between identifiers and genomic sequence labels is held in a restricted key available to qualified researchers on request. Roles are reported at the level of occupational category (nurse, physician, laboratory technician, driver, pharmacy dispenser, burial team member) rather than by individual post.
