In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). Abstract

Objective: Oscillations in field potentials are associated with a large spectrum of brain function. These oscillations are altered by deep brain stimulation (DBS), but the mechanistic understanding of interactions between brain oscillations and DBS is incomplete. In people living with Parkinson's disease, DBS can alter pre-existing gamma oscillations or induce gamma oscillations, with a frequency at half the stimulation frequency in both cases. However, these half-harmonic responses, vary between patients. The objective of this work is to provide a dynamical-systems explanation for the heterogeneity in response to DBS. Approach: A Wilson-Cowan model of neuronal populations was used to provide a broad theoretical framework that could explain the heterogeneity of observed responses. Main result: In the absence of stimulation and noise, the model exhibited either damped oscillations or self-sustained oscillations, depending on parameter values. Off-stimulation behaviour determined observed stimulation response. When oscillations in the gamma range were strongly-damped off-stimulation, the only observed stimulation response was a driven oscillation at the stimulation frequency. When oscillations were weakly-damped off-stimulation, stimulation resulted in either harmonic or half-harmonic responses. Half-harmonic responses only occurred for a band of frequencies around half the stimulation frequency and only for sufficiently large amplitude stimulation. When self-sustained oscillations were present off-stimulation, the self-sustained oscillations were entrained by the stimulation frequency, and harmonic, halfharmonic and many other subharmonic responses emerged. Ramping stimulation amplitude up and down revealed hysteresis with the onset and offset of half-harmonic responses appearing at different amplitude thresholds. Addition of noise blurred distinctions between different states but the underlying dynamical structure was preserved.

Significance: This framework reproduces the heterogeneity of DBS responses seen in patients and proposes subharmonic phenomena which have not previously been reported. The modelled hysteresis highlights potential challenges for adaptive control systems which rely on a one-to-one relationship between stimulation settings and stimulation outcome.

1 Introduction

Deep brain stimulation (DBS) is an invasive neurostimulation technique used in the treatment of neurological conditions where pharmacological treatments prove to be ineffective.

Predominantly used for the treatment of Parkinson's Disease (PD), said to be the fastest growing neurological condition [13], the number of DBS devices implanted each year is increasing [39].

The approach to the delivery of DBS treatment has hardly changed since its inception in the 1980s [24, 23, 19].. The default method continues to rely on delivery of electrical pulses at a stimulation setting (pulse width, waveform, amplitude and frequency) chosen by clinicians and typically fixed at the same value throughout the day and night [51, 25, 29]. The location of the pulse delivery depends on the neurological condition, with subthalamic nucleus (STN) or globus pallidus (GPi) the most common locations used in the treatment of PD [54].

State-of-the-art DBS devices have considerable capabilities to simultaneously deliver stimulation and monitor local field potentials (LFP) at the point of stimulation. This dual capability has led to adaptive DBS systems which adjust stimulation settings in real time in response to live measurements of the LFP [27, 33]. To better exploit the full capabilities of these devices a fuller understanding of the mechanisms behind DBS is needed.

According to a widely accepted concept, the effects of DBS may be mediated by altering oscillations in neural networks [10]. For example, in Parkinson's, DBS suppresses beta oscillations, which are associated with bradykinesia and rigidity [7, 2]. In addition, DBS can induce or interact with oscillations in the gamma band. Spontaneous gamma oscillations have a frequency between 60-90Hz. Gamma oscillations in response to DBS are characterised by narrowband activity with a width of approximately 5-10 Hz. These are referred to as finely tuned gamma (FTG) oscillations. They occur at half the stimulation frequency i.e. one neuronal oscillation for every two DBS pulses [28, 46, 32], and have been observed in recordings from the points of stimulation (STN or GPi), as well as from electrocorticograph (ECoG) and electroencephalogram (EEG) recordings [41, 22, 52]. These half-harmonic re-3 sponses are not seen in all patients and were initially thought to be a device-induced artefact but are now understood as a physiological response of the brain [40, 30].

In some patients, even in the absence of stimulation, narrowband gamma activity is observed in response to dopaminergic medication [52, 30]. This dopaminergic gamma-band response is usually referred to as spontaneous finely tuned gamma (sFTG). DBS can shift the frequency of the sFTG oscillation so that the peak frequency of response is at precisely half the stimulation frequency, i.e. a half-harmonic response. Thus, responses in the gamma band are heterogeneous between patients: half-harmonic FTG responses are found in some patients, but not others. In some patients, these half-harmonic responses occur as a result of shifting sFTG oscillations, but not in all cases [30]. The brain's rhythmic nature has been extensively studied and described [9], with references to it as a neuronal oscillator dating back to the 1980s [44]. Physiologically-based mathematical and computational models, to account for the oscillatory phenomena related to DBS, have been proposed [26], but as yet there is no consensus as to the key populations / networks involved. For example, Rubin & Terman considered the interaction of subthalamic nuclei (STN), the internal and external segments of the globus pallidus (GPi and GPe respectively) and thalamocortical (TC) cells [37]. Their framework was then extended to include multi-site STN stimulation in [17]. More recently Ahmadipour et al. considered interactions between oscillations in the striatum network and the globus pallidus pars externa, and the interaction with the STN [1]. Despite substantial progress there remains a disconnect between current physiological models and practical guidance for engineers designing control systems and clinicians delivering therapy to patient.

An alternative approach is to consider phenomenological models which focus on general principles which are agnostic to the details of the physiology [31, 43]. For example, the alteration of sFTG rhythms by DBS has been described using a Wilson-Cowan modelling framework [41, 14], with one excitatory and one inhibitory population of neurons. In [41], the stimulation pulse was modelled as a forcing term acting on the inhibitory neurons. Model parameters were found by fitting to off-stimulation patient data. Predictions were then made 4 as to the effect of stimulation, successfully demonstrating that stimulation could result in the observed half-harmonic responses. The modelling made the highly non-intuitive prediction, subsequently observed in patients, that increasing stimulation amplitude does not necessarily result in a stronger half-harmonic response but can instead lead to the disappearance of the response. The half-harmonic response was linked to the classic Arnold-tongue entrainment framework in which the frequency and amplitude of the response are dependent on the stimulation frequency and amplitude [2]. In the DBS field, the half-harmonic response highlighted in the phenomenological model of Sermon et al. [41, 14] remains a rare example of a model prediction which subsequently was observed in recordings.

Here, our aim is to investigate whether a single modelling framework can provide a mechanistic account of the occurrence, or otherwise, of the different responses to DBS in the gamma band. We start by illustrating that the modelling framework needs to be nonlinear for halfharmonic responses to be observed in response to simulation. This illustration translates a standard result from linear systems theory to the DBS setting. We then turn to the (nonlinear) Wilson-Cowan mathematical formulation and carry out a dynamical-systems analysis highlighting that the model can exhibit either damped or self-sustained oscillations in the absence of stimulation, depending on parameters. We systematically explore the response of the model to stimulation and find that the Wilson-Cowan model provides a candidate mechanism explaining the observed heterogeneity of responses to stimulation, see Fig. 1.

We highlight that multiple subharmonic responses, not only the half-harmonic, should occur and identify the presence of hysteresis. This extends the previous formulation, which referred to this phenomenon in terms of Arnold tongues only. We end with a short discussion of the interpretation and significance of our results. 6

2.2 Mathematical methods for studying the behaviour of the Wilson-Cowan model in the absence of stimulation

In the absence of stimulation (and noise), the dynamical behaviour of equations (1) was considered by carrying out a bifurcation analysis. In this approach, the number and stability of steady-state (equilibrium) solutions of the equations are considered, where steady-states correspond to solutions such that the firing rates of excitatory and inhibitory neuronal populations do not change with time (dE/dt =0 = dI/dt). Generically, steady-state solutions are either stable or unstable. Stable steady-state solutions are locally attracting, meaning that when solutions are slightly perturbed, they return to the stable steady-state solution.

Unstable steady-state solutions are locally repelling, meaning any small perturbation results in solutions diverging from the unstable steady-state.

We tracked the position of the steady-state solutions as a function of the parameters and considered how their number and stability changed. We identified transition points (bifurcations) where solutions either changed stability or solutions were gained or lost. Of particular interest were points where steady-state solutions lost stability by undergoing a Hopf bifurcation and a periodic solution was created. These periodic solutions (i.e. self-sustained oscillatory solutions) arising at the Hopf bifurcation were also tracked as a function of the parameters.

Understanding the bifurcation structure facilitated an understanding of the overall dynamical behaviour of the model and robustness of the modelling framework. We note that it is possible to find analytical conditions for the steady-state solutions and analytical conditions defining when Hopf bifurcations occur, see the Supplementary Materials Section 1. However, 9 since these conditions are implicit coupled nonlinear equations that are not readily solvable by hand, we turned to numerical bifurcation methods and used the Matcont package for Matlab [12]. Matcont uses numerical continuation methods to find, classify and follow equilibria and periodic solutions of a dynamical system as a function of the parameters and also locates and tracks bifurcation points.

2.3 Simulation techniques

In addition to carrying out a numerical bifurcation analysis of the system in the absence of stimulation, we carried out simulations of equations (1), to systematically investigate model behaviour with stimulation-on, simulate DBS neuromodulation experiments, and predict the effect of new stimulation regimes. For deterministic simulations (ζ = 0) calculations were carried out by integrating equations (1) using Matlab's built-in ODE-45 solver. For stochastic simulations (ζ > 0), we used the Euler-Maruyama method, with a time step of 10 -5 s. All numerical integrations were carried out in Matlab [47, 48].

Three different general physiological parameter regimes were considered corresponding to the existence of (i) strongly-damped oscillations, (ii) weakly-damped oscillations, and (iii) self-sustained oscillations. All baseline physiological parameter values are listed in the Supplementary Materials Section 3.

For each setting, the response to the first 500 stimulation pulses was discarded to remove transient behaviour. Following that, the system was integrated for a further 250 stimulation pulses.

To calculate the oscillator phase θ N the solutions were first centred at the temporal means of the excitatory (( Ẽ) and inhibitory ( Ĩ) populations (E(t) → E(t) -Ẽ, I(t) → I(t) -Ĩ. The translated solutions in the (E, I) plane were then represented using polar coordinates (r, θ).

When the rotation number was rational (i.e., it could be written as ρ = p/q, where p and q are integers), every q periods of the forcing results in p oscillations of the system. Thus, the first harmonic has a rotation number ρ = 1, whereas a half-harmonic response has ρ = 1/2.

Spectrograms and power spectral densities (PSDs) of the resulting time series were computed using Hamming windows (of 1 s) with no overlap or PSD rescaling.

In some regions of the stimulation frequency-amplitude space, hysteresis was observed. A characteristic of hysteresis is the co-existence of multiple stable solutions to the system.

Which solution is observed depends on the initial conditions, with each stable solution having a 'basin of attraction'. Basins of attraction were constructed by integrating equations (1) over a domain of initial conditions. For each set of initial conditions, the rotation number was computed after 1000 stimulation pulses.

2.4 Sensitivity analysis

Robustness of our findings in the case of the deterministic system was examined by performing a sensitivity analysis in which we varied the coupling strength parameters ω EE,EI,IE , 11 background input parameters η E,I and the time scale constants τ E,I . Parameters were varied one at a time by ±5% from their baseline values.

For each parameter set, we located the relevant equilibrium and calculated the eigenvalues of the local Jacobian. The eigenvalues enabled us to evaluate which side of the Hopf stability boundary the parameters were and, in the damped case, the strength of the damping.

For each parameter set, the model was simulated for varying frequency and amplitude of stimulation as described in section 2.3. In the resulting frequency-amplitude bifurcation set we visually assessed the presence or absence of subharmonic response regions. We then quantitatively focused on the assessment of the half-harmonic tongue. For each frequencyamplitude point, solutions were classified as belonging to the half-harmonic point if the rotation number satisfied |ρ -0.5| < 0.02. Regions of complex dynamics, as discussed later, were excluded from the comparison.

For each identified half-harmonic tongue, we estimated the area and the location of the tongue tip in frequency and amplitude dimension. For the case of self-sustained oscillations, we checked for the existence of hysteresis by integrating the system for two initial conditions.

Area calculations were expressed relative to the corresponding baseline case. Results were plotted against the perturbation magnitude, with -5%,0%, and 5% representing the decrease, baseline and increase of the parameters, respectively.

Perturbations in parameters around the strongly-damped case, yielded no qualitative change to the responses in the frequency-amplitude stimulation plane. Oscillations remained stronglydamped and the only responses to stimulation were harmonic to the drive (ρ = 1).

4 Discussion

FTG rhythms have been observed in multiple clinical studies. In most patients FTG rhythms have been shown to have prokinetic effects, but there is some evidence they are associated with dyskinesia, particularly when no sFTG is found off-stimulation [30, 18]. In addition, FTG has been proposed as a putative biomarker for adaptive DBS systems [36]. Whether to increase FTG rhythms to enhance prokinetic effects, suppress FTG rhythms to reduce dyskinesia or use FTG rhythms as a biomarker, there is a need for theoretical frameworks to aid understanding.

4.1 Broad theoretical framework and relation to finely tuned gamma (FTG) rhythms

Here, we have set the previously-discussed entrainment of gamma oscillations in PD in a broader theoretical framework.

Instead of referring to Arnold tongues as the explanation for all half-harmonic enhancement of gamma oscillations, here we argue that patients who exhibit no sFTG off-stimulation and no half-harmonic response on stimulation are consistent with physiological parameter regimes in which there are only strongly-damped gamma oscillations.

Patients who exhibit no sFTG off-stimulation and half-harmonic responses on stimulation agree with physiological parameter regimes in which there are weakly-damped gamma oscil-29 lations. This challenges the previous assumption that all patients exhibiting a half-harmonic response to the stimulation must also exhibit sFTG, even if the oscillation is too small to be detected in the LFP measurements [30, 42, 32].

Patients who exhibit sFTG off-stimulation which are entrained to give a half-harmonic response on stimulation can be modelled by physiological parameter regimes in which there are self-sustained gamma oscillations.

4.2 Consequences of the theoretical framework

This broad theoretical framework highlights three generic consequences. First, in the absence of self-sustained oscillations (no-sFTG), the model predicts that there is no self-sustained rhythm to entrain, and the system is more accurately described as a driven system. Driven systems typically respond at frequencies which are harmonic to the stimulation (drive). To respond at a half-harmonic the system needs to be nonlinear, and the stimulation has to have a sufficiently large amplitude. Stimulation amplitude has to be sufficiently large because, generically, even in nonlinear systems, in the neighbourhood of equilibrium solutions in phase space the dynamics are linear. Consequently, stimulation needs to be sufficiently large to perturb the system out of this local linear neighbourhood.

Second, the mathematical formulation suggests that in the presence of self-sustained oscillations (sFTG), oscillations can be entrained at frequencies which are harmonic or at multiple different subharmonics to the stimulation. We note that our results highlight that the three simulated half-harmonic tongue-like regions shown in [41] are not Arnold tongues in the conventional sense, since for the parameter values used there were no self-sustained oscillation to entrain. Within our wider theoretical framework, these tongue-like regions are a driven nonlinear response.

We have not found any published reports of subharmonics other than the half-harmonic, but we are aware that observations of other subharmonic responses may have been underreported in the past. In Fig. S11, we show example patient data for the case of a ρ = 1/4 subharmonic 30 in which an endogenous rhythm of approximately 50 Hz is entrained to one quarter of the stimulation frequency for some stimulation amplitudes. These data were collected by some of the co-authors and analysed using the approaches specified in [8].

Third, characteristic of nonlinear systems, we hypothesise that there may be regions in stimulation parameter space where there is co-existence of solutions i.e. the same stimulation parameters lead to different responses depending on the initial conditions (i.e. past history).

Consequently, changes in stimulation may result with hysteresis in responses. Hysteresis in biological and physiological phenomena has been observed and modelled in other systems, including stimulation of ventricular cells [55] or entrainment of circadian rhythms by multiple zeitgebers [35]. Such path-dependent responses pose a challenge for adaptive stimulation algorithms since a simple feedback-control system will have difficulty tracking and controlling responses that do not vary continuously and monotonically with control parameters.

Hysteresis has not yet been reported, to our knowledge, in the case of neuromodulation.

4.3 Robustness of the theoretical framework

The results we have present are based on a minimal physiological model and general dynamical systems theory which indicate that response to forcing (stimulation) should differ substantially between damped systems and those with self-sustained oscillations [20, 15].

We provided a sketch proof to show that low-dimensional linear driven systems with impulsive forcing cannot exhibit subharmonic response. Although we only considered impulsive stimulation and low-dimensional systems, the general principles behind dynamical systems argue that sufficiently close to stable equilibria, behaviour is dominated by the slowest decaying modes and may be approximated by a local linear system. Since half-harmonic responses are observed, the implications are that, first, not surprisingly, the dynamics of the brain are nonlinear. Second, for half-harmonic responses to occur when oscillations are damped, the stimulation has to be sufficiently strong for nonlinearity to have an impact.

The Wilson-Cowan model includes seven physiological parameters. We only considered vary-31 ing three of them (ω EE , ω EI , ω IE ). As other parameters are varied, the number of equilibria and the regions of oscillatory behaviour will change. Indeed, in the Supplementary Materials we demonstrate that up to five equilibria can occur depending on the parameter values. However, the central theme that response to stimulation will depend on whether oscillations are strongly-damped, weakly-damped or self-sustained relies on generic properties of dynamical systems which are agnostic to the details.

In a noise-free model, the transition between damped and self-sustained oscillations occurs at Hopf bifurcations at well-defined parameter values. But in the presence of noise, more representative of a physiological system, the distinction is blurred.

In our simulations, following previous work [41], only additive Gaussian noise was used as a minimal representation of background fluctuations and measurement error. Given that our central results arise from the underlying nature of the deterministic system and the presence (or absence) of self-sustained oscillations we anticipate that a moderate-coloured noise might change the shape or incline of the enhancement tongues but will not fundamentally alter our conclusions.

Sensitivity analysis revealed that the behaviour of the tongues changed in a manner that was consistent with the broader theoretical framework. Hysteresis was consistently found when self-sustained oscillations were present off-stimulation.

We note that the general dynamical framework we set out is agnostic to brain region and is not specific to gamma rhythms. In patients with Parkinson's Disease, half-harmonic responses have been reported from recordings in the cortex, STN and GPi. Recent work has revealed half-harmonic responses in the gamma band with stimulation of the ventral intermediate nucleus in patients with Multiple Sclerosis [38]. Also consistent with our framework is harmonic entrainment of sFTG by applying stimulation in the gamma band, at the frequency of recorded sFTG [49].

4.4 Other theories

An alternative explanation for the appearance of half-harmonic responses is a priming-like mechanism. In the context of DBS, priming has been investigated in the form of delivering paired pulses and observing that the response to the second pulse differed from the first one [4, 6]. While this could be a basis for the gamma responses, we are not aware of any reports showing that paired pulses directly result in half-harmonic oscillations in continuous DBS

4.5 Limitations

Our results rely on broad principles of driven nonlinear dynamical systems and a minimal physiological model. However, the phenomenological nature of the model is a limitation in that it is not possible to make direct comparison between model parameters and physiology, and stimulation amplitudes are necessarily in arbitrary units. The presented form of the model was initially developed in [41], with parameters fitted and model predictions verified in patients with both STN and GPi implanted DBS. In all these cases, recordings were taken using ECoG arrays, suggesting that the model captures general features and phenomena relating to DBS across different stimulation and recording locations. However, this does not imply that stimulation at STN and GPi have an equivalent physiological outcome. In fact, response to stimulation differs depending on the target location on micro-, meso-, and macro-scales [34]. STN and GPi engage different upstream and downstream patterns, with different levels and balances of excitatory and inhibitory inputs, which could influence the parameters of the model. However, sufficiently robust and large-scale studies with structured changes to stimulation regimes at different targets are currently lacking. Limiting our ability to accurately comment and hypothesise about the link between parameter choices and stimulation and recording location. Given the physiological complexity and the lack 33 of consensus on the mechanism by which DBS acts, we are some way from developing a spatially-resolved model which includes all relevant networks and stimulation effects. It is in this context that our minimal model is of value as it captures broad features and highlights classes of phenomena which should be expected to occur.

In addition, we have only focussed on oscillations in the gamma band. The interplay between other prominent rhythms observed in PD, in particular, the bursts of beta rhythms and their suppression, may prove crucial in fully understanding the mechanisms behind DBS.

The mechanisms described by this framework do not capture DBS-induced FTG, observed in patients off-medication. In [52], STN stimulation in the absence of levodopa induced gamma oscillations but found no consistent response of these oscillations to changes in stimulation frequency. Furthermore, oscillations induced in that way persisted after the stimulation was terminated, further arguing against a direct entrainment mechanism. This strongly suggests that the structure of the network and the properties of the model are strongly affected by the medication state. The two mechanisms should not be seen as mutually exclusiveour framework highlights that the presence of weakly-damped or self-sustained oscillations to drive or entrain the rhythm to a stimulation frequency-dependent response. Capturing DBS-induced FTG together with its long transient after stimulation is switched off, is likely to require the networks dynamics to be described at a different, slower timescale. This is not included in the current framework.

Finally, even in this minimal model we found regions of complex dynamics at high stimulation amplitudes, which we have not fully investigated. This includes period-doubling and chaotic dynamics characteristic of other forced systems [16, 21], although in the presence of noise these more complex dynamics may not be observable.

4.6 Final words and further extensions

We have assumed that physiological parameter values are fixed. In reality, the balance between excitation and inhibition is likely to change depending on activity, sleep/wake state, 34 or ultradian and circadian oscillations [45, 56, 50]. As physiological parameters vary, as shown in Fig. 3, damped oscillations may change to self-sustained oscillations or viceversa. In addition, damped oscillations may change from being weakly damped (near Hopf bifurcations) or strongly damped. Hence making parameter values medication-dependent and / or brain state (sleep/wake) dependent and / or time-of-day dependent may explain how gamma band responses change over time.

Overall, our results show that the Wilson-Cowan framework offers a possible dynamical systems explanation of the considerable heterogeneity in responses of oscillations in the gamma range to DBS and highlights that subharmonic responses, not only harmonic and halfharmonic responses, are a predicted feature of entrained systems. Our modelling framework could guide further experimental studies to better understand the complex brain-stimulus interactions to improve adaptive DBS.
