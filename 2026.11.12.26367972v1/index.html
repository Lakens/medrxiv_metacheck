Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Abstract

Background: Mental health problems among young people impose a burden extending beyond healthcare, including impaired health-related quality of life and high societal costs from disrupted education, lost productivity, and informal care. Youth-friendly interventions may reduce this burden by providing early support and preventing escalation. However, their broader societal economic impact remains insufficiently explored. Introduction

Mental disorders commonly emerge during adolescence and early adulthood and contribute substantially to the adverse impacts of disease, with economic costs extending well beyond the healthcare sector (1) (2) (3). Despite high levels of need, young people's access to timely and appropriate mental health care can be limited (4, 5). In many countries, young people often face uncertainty when seeking access to services. This uncertainty is compounded by limited access to support in primary care, lengthy referral processes, long waiting lists, the need to consult multiple individuals before treatment initiation, and concerns about privacy and confidentiality.

Moreover, multiple barriers may prevent young people from accessing care or from remaining engaged with it (6).

Youth-friendly, low-threshold interventions delivered outside of the formal healthcare system may reduce adverse societal impacts by providing early support and reducing the risk of escalation (7). The wave of innovation in youth mental health care is spreading globally, with at least 13 countries adopting an integrated youth primary care model adapted to local cultural, health financing patterns, and workforce contexts (4). YOUTHreach aims to evaluate the clinical and cost-effectiveness of three existing innovative interventions in seven European countries: Germany, Ireland, the United Kingdom, the Netherlands, Italy, Spain and Estonia, as well as in Australia (8). These three complementary youth mental health interventions are YEAH (Youth mEntal heAltH support centres), youth-friendly Integrated Youth Mental Health Services (IYMHS) offering free and easily accessible support, generally without waiting lists or appointments (9); SELFIE (Self-Esteem Low-intensity Friendly Intervention Ecological), a transdiagnostic blended ecological momentary intervention providing personalised, real-time support to young people with low self-esteem who have experienced childhood adversity (10); and MOST (Moderated Online Social Therapy), a hybrid digital platform integrating evidencebased psychological treatment with professional and peer support within a moderated online community (11, 12). Across the three evaluations, care as usual (CAU) will comprise the health, mental health, social, and other support services ordinarily available to participants within each country and study site. Participants in the control group will continue to access these services but will not receive the intervention under evaluation. As care pathways differ across countries, the type and intensity of CAU will be documented using a context-specific Resource Use Measurement (RUM) instrument, developed for this study through a multistakeholder involvement process and incorporated into the economic evaluations (8).

SELFIE and MOST will be evaluated in a randomised controlled trial, while YEAH will be evaluated using a quasi-experimental design, reflecting its real-world, open-access implementation. The base-case economic analyses will adopt a societal perspective, while scenario analyses will examine the healthcare perspective. The societal perspective accounts for healthcare costs (e.g., hospitalisations, GP visits, medications, rehabilitation, formal caregiving, and intervention costs), costs to young people and their families (e.g., travel expenses, out-of-pocket payments, time spent receiving care, and informal caregiving time), productivity costs including absenteeism, presenteeism, and unpaid work loss, as well as costs outside the healthcare system (e.g., social care, education, and support services) (13, 14). A healthcare perspective considers only costs and outcomes occurring within the healthcare system or within the scope of the relevant healthcare budget holder, typically including healthcare resource use and direct medical costs (13).

Emerging economic evidence on youth-friendly mental health interventions is promising but remains heterogeneous. In Australia, a national evaluation of the headspace programme estimated an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio of AUD $44,722 per quality-adjusted life year (QALY) gained over 18 months, suggesting that the programme represented cost-effective care.

However, results varied substantially across service locations and modelling assumptions (15).

In the Netherlands, the @ease walk-in IYMHS have been shown to serve a population with a considerable illness burden, with mean health-related quality of life of 0.64 compared with 0.91-0.96 in the general Dutch youth population, and average costs of €579 per person in the three months preceding their first visit, equivalent to €2,318 per person annually (16). From a societal perspective, the Dutch SELFIE intervention had an estimated incremental cost per QALY gained of approximately €54,000. At the same time, the healthcare-perspective analysis yielded an incremental cost of €15,671 per QALY and a 77% probability of cost-effectiveness at a willingness-to-pay threshold for health care providers of € 50,000 per QALY gained (17).

In Australia, an economic evaluation of Horyzons, an earlier moderated online social therapy intervention underlying MOST, found significantly lower costs than treatment as usual from both healthcare and societal perspectives and was dominant in the cost-effectiveness analysis based on social functioning, although the cost-utility analysis showed fewer QALYs and the differences in outcomes were not statistically significant (18). Together, these studies provide promising but mixed evidence on the economic value of youth mental health interventions and underline the need for harmonised evaluations across interventions and countries. Economic evaluations conducted alongside clinical studies estimate resource use, costs, outcomes, and cost-effectiveness over an observed follow-up period using individual-level data and generally require fewer assumptions than model-based analyses (19, 20). However, trialbased economic evaluations may not capture consequences beyond the study horizon, which is often just one year or less (21). This is particularly relevant in youth mental health, as the effects of untreated or inadequately treated mental health problems may persist well beyond the study period (22). YOUTHreach will combine trial-based and model-based economic evaluations within a harmonised methodological framework. Three 12-month trial-based economic evaluations will assess YEAH, SELFIE, and MOST separately against CAU. YEAH will be evaluated using propensity score-adjusted quasi-experimental data, whereas SELFIE and MOST will be evaluated using randomised controlled trial data. These analyses will be complemented by three model-based economic evaluations projecting the costs and outcomes of the same intervention-comparator pairs over 2-, 3-, 5-, and 10-year time horizons.

For each intervention, individual-level resource-use and outcome data from all participating countries will be pooled. One participating country will be selected as the reference country for the pooled base-case valuation framework, based on prespecified criteria including participant numbers, data completeness and quality, availability of national unit costs and outcome value sets, and national health technology assessment guidance. The selected country's costing methods, outcome valuation methods, analytical perspectives, and other relevant methodological recommendations will be applied consistently in the pooled base-case analyses.

The methodological assumptions will be examined in scenario and sensitivity analyses.

Many economic evaluation methods and resource-use tools were developed mainly for adult populations and may not fully capture the broader costs and outcomes relevant to some younger people. This is particularly important in youth mental health, where impacts may extend beyond healthcare to education, social care, family support, and other sectors (23, 24). To address this gap, YOUTHreach developed a context-specific RUM instrument through a multi-stakeholder process involving young people, health economists, policymakers, psychologists and service professionals. Their input ensured the instrument reflects young people's and service providers' experiences and captures relevant resource use within and outside the healthcare system, including education and social support services. The development process was informed by established health-economic methods, including principles developed by the Institute for Medical Technology Assessment (iMTA) Medical Consumption Questionnaire (iMCQ) and . Productivity Cost Questionnaire (iPCQ) (25, 26), to support valid and consistent cost estimation across the YOUTHreach economic evaluations.

This study aims to establish a harmonised methodological framework for evaluating the costeffectiveness of YEAH, SELFIE, and MOST compared with CAU among young people aged 12-25 years. Twelve-month cost-utility and cost-effectiveness analyses will be conducted using individual-level cost and outcome data, complemented by model-based analyses projecting costs and outcomes over 2-, 3-, 5-, and 10-year time horizons. The base-case analyses will be conducted from a societal perspective, with healthcare-perspective analyses conducted as scenarios. The following research questions will be addressed.

RQ1: How will the 12-month cost-effectiveness of YEAH compared with CAU be estimated using propensity score-weighted quasi-experimental data?

RQ2: How will the 12-month cost-effectiveness of SELFIE and MOST compared with CAU be estimated using randomised controlled trial data? RQ3: How will the long-term cost-effectiveness of YEAH, SELFIE, and MOST be projected over 2-, 3-, 5-, and 10-year time horizons using the Markov model?

In addition, the study describes the multistakeholder development of a context-specific RUM instrument that captures relevant societal costs and provides translated versions for all participating countries to support its use in future studies.

Methods

Study design and setting

Figure 1 summarises the designs of the three multicentre, multicountry studies. YEAH is a prospective quasi-experimental cohort study with non-equivalent groups. The intervention group consists of young people presenting to a participating YEAH IYMHS for the first time.

However, previous or concurrent use of counselling or mental health services is permitted.

Control participants are recruited separately from the corresponding catchment areas via local mental health services, schools, student and educational counselling services, digital outreach such as targeted online advertisements and social media, and population registers. To enhance recruitment efficiency, data from 336 participants assigned to the control conditions of the SELFIE and MOST trials will also be included in the YEAH control group. Propensity score weighting will be applied to adjust for measured baseline differences between the YEAH and control groups (8, 9). SELFIE and MOST are multicentre, multicountry, parallel-group randomised controlled trials in which eligible participants are individually randomised 1:1 to receive either the respective intervention plus CAU or CAU alone. Outcomes are assessed at baseline and at 3, 6, and 12 months of follow-up. In SELFIE, allocation is determined by a concealed randomisation sequence generated by an independent trial statistician and stratified by clinical investigation site. Outcome assessors and analysts remain blinded to allocation. In MOST, computergenerated block randomisation is conducted separately at each operational site and stratified by baseline psychological distress severity and gender. (12, 27). Note: Participants in the intervention groups receive the respective YOUTHreach intervention in addition to any care as usual available to them; participants in the control groups receive care as usual alone. Therefore, the formal comparisons are YEAH plus care as usual versus care as usual alone, SELFIE plus care as usual versus care as usual alone, and MOST plus care as usual versus care as usual alone.

Study Status and Timeline

Eligibility criteria are assessed during the screening procedure before enrolment, and eligible participants complete baseline assessments before entering the study conditions (6, 8, 17).

Eligible participants receive study information and provide informed consent before participating. For minors, parental or guardian consent is obtained where required, in accordance with country-specific ethical and legal requirements (8). Data collection

Participants complete electronic questionnaires at baseline and follow-up time points using secure data collection systems. Participation is voluntary, and participants may skip questions or withdraw without consequences for the support they receive. Questionnaire data are linked over time using anonymised or pseudonymised identifiers. Personal identifying information is stored separately from analytical datasets. Data are stored securely and accessed only by authorised researchers in accordance with GDPR, national regulations and institutional data management policies. Further details are described in the published YOUTHreach protocol (8).

Young people who access participating services or digital platforms receive support from trained staff or moderators, depending on the intervention. During the initial contact, staff members explore the reasons for seeking support and provide guidance or appropriate support options. Clinical professionals are available within the services or on call to provide additional support or referrals if required.

In addition to the RUM and outcomes, the first part of the questionnaire collects demographic information (e.g., age, gender, occupation, living situation, country of birth, level of education, parental mental health).

Identification, Measurement, and Valuation of the Costs and Outcomes

A pooled economic evaluation will be conducted for each intervention using individual-level resource-use and outcome data from all participating countries. To maintain consistency in valuing costs and outcomes, one participating country will be selected as the reference country for the pooled base-case valuation framework before the economic analyses. Selection will be based on prespecified criteria, including participant numbers, completeness of follow-up, availability and quality of resource-use and outcome data, availability of validated outcome measures and national value sets, feasibility of assigning national unit costs, and the existence of national costing and health technology assessment methodological guidance. The selected reference country's costing framework, outcome valuation methods, analytical perspectives, and other relevant methodological recommendations will be applied uniformly in the pooled base-case analyses.

Cost estimation will follow a three-step microcosting framework consisting of the identification, measurement, and valuation of costs (29). Relevant cost categories have been identified by the HTA team and through expert consultations with the Health Technology Assessment Advisory Group (HAG) and Young Advisory Group (YAG) of the YOUTHreach consortium. These categories include healthcare costs (e.g., primary care, specialist visits, diagnostic procedures, hospitalisations, medications, and rehabilitation services), costs in other sectors (e.g., educational services), non-healthcare costs (e.g., informal care), and productivity costs, including absenteeism and presenteeism related to paid work, as well as productivity losses associated with unpaid work.

The resource-use volumes, including formal and informal care utilisation, will be collected through self-report using the context-specific Resource Use Measurement (RUM) instrument, applying recall periods appropriate to each resource category. Unit costs will be assigned to the measured resource-use volumes using reference prices from the selected reference country (14, 30, 31). Medication costs will be estimated using participant-level prescription costs, where available (32). Productivity losses will be valued in accordance with the selected reference country's pharmacoeconomic guidelines, using either the friction cost approach or the human capital approach, depending on national recommendations (29, 33).

Health outcomes will be measured using the EQ-5D-5L and CORE-10. EQ-5D-5L responses will be converted into utility values using the value set of the selected reference country, where available, and used to estimate quality-adjusted life-years. CORE-10 scores will also be analysed as a secondary clinical outcome in the cost-effectiveness analyses (34, 35). For the model-based economic evaluation, future costs and outcomes will be discounted in accordance with the national health economic guidelines of the selected reference country. Resource use measurement

The RUM instrument was developed through a multi-step, stakeholder-informed process to comprehensively measure resource use and associated impacts among individuals aged 12-25, as shown in Figure 2. Initially, a methodological review identified established approaches for measuring healthcare and broader societal resource use. This review underscored the need for a youth-specific instrument that captures resource use and impacts relevant to this age group.

Consequently, an initial draft of the RUM instrument was created.

The draft instrument was subsequently reviewed with project partners from participating countries. These consultations identified relevant healthcare services, mental health care pathways, educational support, and other service structures within each context, while ensuring comparability across countries. Additional expert consultations were held with members of the project's HAG, who evaluated the proposed resource-use categories, terminology, recall periods, and the instrument's coverage of costs from both healthcare and societal perspectives.

The YAG subsequently reviewed the draft instrument. Their feedback addressed the clarity, accessibility, and relevance of the questions, as well as the comprehensibility of terminology describing healthcare services, educational support, and other forms of assistance for young people.

After these consultations, the English-language questionnaire was translated into the relevant study languages, including Italian, Estonian, Spanish, German, and Dutch. The HTA team and participating country partners reviewed the translated versions to ensure linguistic accuracy, contextual appropriateness, and consistency with national healthcare and service terminology.

The instrument was subsequently pilot-tested by project partners and youth representatives. The team incorporated feedback from the translation, review, and pilot-testing stages during a final refinement phase, resulting in a context-specific RUM instrument for implementation across the YOUTHreach studies.

The instrument records contacts with healthcare professionals across primary and specialist care, including general practitioners, practice nurses, hospital-based specialists such as paediatricians, psychiatrists, psychologists, and neurologists, and community-based mental health professionals such as counsellors and psychotherapists. It also documents interactions with occupational therapists, social workers, school psychologists, and other relevant professionals.

The RUM also captures use of acute and inpatient services, including emergency department visits, ambulance transport, hospital admissions, psychiatric inpatient care, day treatment, and diagnostic visits in hospitals or mental health facilities. Additionally, it collects information on medication and supplement use, including antidepressants, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder medications, antipsychotics, sleep medications, and supplements.

To capture broader societal costs, the instrument includes questions about educational and workplace support services, informal care provided by friends or family, and productivity losses from school or work absenteeism and presenteeism.

Intervention Costs

Intervention costs will be estimated using intervention-specific resource-use data collected reference-country base-case analysis, depending on the availability and quality of relevant information. Given this complexity, YEAH intervention cost estimates will be interpreted with caution and reported transparently, along with the assumptions made.

SELFIE intervention costs will include the platform cost associated with the TherapyDesigner system; clinician time for six intervention contacts per participant (three face-to-face sessions and three email contacts) delivered by trained mental health professionals; and costs related to the smartphone-based application. Intervention costs will include relevant one-off and participation costs, such as training and implementation, consistent with the previous SELFIE economic evaluation (17).

Intervention costs for MOST will be estimated at the individual level using a micro costing approach. The costs associated with clinician support will vary according to the assigned support intensity level, ranging from asynchronous weekly therapeutic messages and ondemand support (self-guided level) to synchronous moderator contact by phone or video of up to 20-40 minutes per week (coaching and blended teletherapy levels), consistent with previous economic evaluation of the MOST-based Horyzons intervention (18, 28). Costs will also include moderator time, peer support, vocational support (where applicable), training costs, and platform infrastructure and maintenance.

Outcome measurement

The EQ-5D-5L, for which permission was obtained from EuroQol (36), and the Clinical Outcomes in Routine Evaluation-10 (CORE-10) are included to assess generic and mental health-specific quality of life and psychological distress, respectively.

The EQ-5D-5L is a standardised, generic instrument for measuring health-related quality of life developed by the EuroQol Group (37). It consists of five dimensions: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain or discomfort, and anxiety or depression, each rated on five levels of severity ranging from no problems to extreme problems (38). The dedicated "anxiety/depression" dimension among its five makes the instrument a valid and widely used measure of HRQoL in mental health (39). Responses are converted into utility scores using country-specific value sets, where scores range from less than zero, representing health states considered worse than death, to one, representing full health (40). In the pooled base-case analysis, EQ-5D-5L health states will be converted into utility values using the value set of the selected reference country. Where sufficient data, time, and methodological guidance are available, country-specific EQ-5D-5L value sets may also be applied to participants from the respective countries. The approach to utility valuation will be finalised and prespecified before the economic analyses.

The CORE-10 is a brief 10-item self-report measure assessing psychological distress over the previous week. Items evaluate symptoms and functioning, including anxiety, depression, trauma, relationships, and risk, and are rated from 0 ("not at all") to 4 ("most or all of the time").

Total scores range from 0 to 40, with higher scores indicating greater psychological distress; a score of 11 or above indicates clinically significant distress. The CORE-10 has good internal consistency (α=0.90), strong convergent validity with the CORE-OM, and demonstrated psychometric performance among older adolescents and young adults attending community mental health services (35, 41). Due to its brevity and applicability across settings, it was selected as the harmonised secondary clinical effectiveness outcome for the cost-effectiveness analyses of YEAH, SELFIE, and MOST, and is collected at baseline and at 3-, 6-, and 12-month follow-up.

Trial-based economic evaluation

The trial-based economic evaluation will use individual-level data on costs and outcomes (20) collected over a 12-month study period. The primary outcome of the economic evaluation will be a cost-utility analysis, in which outcomes will be expressed as quality-adjusted life-years derived from the EQ-5D-5L. There will also be a secondary cost-effectiveness analysis, in which outcomes will be expressed as changes in psychological distress measured using the CORE-10. The comparator is CAU. The base-case analyses will adopt a societal perspective, while scenario analyses will examine the healthcare perspective.

These analyses will cover a 12-month follow-up period (t3) and will incorporate outcome data collected at baseline, 3 months (t1), 6 months (t2), and 12 months (t3). For YEAH, given the quasi-experimental design, propensity score weighting will be applied within the multilevel framework to adjust for systematic baseline differences between the intervention and control groups arising from non-random group formation. Model-based economic evaluation and return on investment tool

In addition to the trial-based analyses, a model-based economic evaluation will be conducted to estimate the long-term costs and benefits of scaling up access to the interventions if shown to be effective. Again, the comparator for each intervention in the model is CAU. The model-based economic evaluation will use a probabilistic Markov decision analytic model to extrapolate impacts on resource use and outcomes beyond the observed 12-month trial followup period. The model will be populated using individual-level data from the trial-based economic evaluation, published literature, and expert input from the HAG and YAG through several online meetings. Trial evidence may not highlight key factors that influence implementation in different contexts. One way to capture this information is through a theory of change process, which explains how an intervention is expected to achieve its impact (46, 47). A theory of change approach in these meetings will be used to identify and document factors around engagement and pathways of care, key outcomes and impacts, and barriers and facilitators to successful implementation. This will be an iterative process with opportunities for feedback and revision as the theory of change analysis develops. The effectiveness evaluation will inform the final model time horizons; costs and outcomes are anticipated to be projected over 2, 3-, 5-, and 10-year time horizons.

Transition probabilities between health states will be estimated from observed individual-level state transitions from baseline to the three-, six-, and twelve-month follow-up time points in the trial, separately for the intervention and control arms of each trial. Health state utility values will be derived by mapping EQ-5D-5L scores to CORE-10 severity categories using observed data from trial participants. Intervention costs will be carried forward from the trial-based microcosting as described in the intervention costs section. The persistence of treatment effects beyond the trial follow-up period represents a key structural assumption. In the base case, treatment effects will be assumed to wane linearly over two years after the end of the intervention, then return to natural-history transition probabilities thereafter. The persistence assumption will be varied in scenario analyses, including full persistence of effects and immediate return to natural history at trial end.

Internal validation will be conducted by comparing model-predicted outcomes over the 12month trial period with observed outcomes to verify that the model accurately reproduces the trial data. External validity will be assessed by comparing model predictions with published epidemiological data on the natural history of psychological distress in young people. We will consult with the HAG to obtain their feedback on model structure and preliminary results.

Model development will be informed by an iterative consultation and feedback process with relevant clinical, methodological and stakeholder experts, while model validation and reporting will follow ISPOR-SMDM good research practices for modelling studies (48).

A return on investment (ROI) modelling tool that compares the cost of investment in the three interventions with the monetary value of costs averted, and any improvement in quality of life, will also be created. Policymakers and other stakeholders can use this tool to identify the longterm return on investment of the interventions (49). End users will be able to vary some of the model assumptions; for instance, this may include changes to the rates of uptake and continued engagement with interventions, implementation costs, discount rates, and the persistence of effects on quality of life and mental health. This also means that parameters can be varied in the ROI analysis to account for country-specific factors, such as differences in cost-per-QALY thresholds.

Statistical Analysis

In line with the effectiveness studies, the economic analyses will follow the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle, including all participants in the groups to which they were originally assigned.

Missing data will be handled using multiple imputation under the missing-at-random (MAR) assumption (50, 51). The final imputation approach, including the variables in the imputation model, the number of imputations, and any eligibility requirements for inclusion in the analysis, will be prespecified in the statistical analysis plan, informed by the observed data structure and relevant methodological guidance. Any deviation from the full ITT population will be reported and justified transparently. Estimates derived from multiply imputed datasets will be combined using Rubin's rules (52, 53). For YEAH, given the higher expected attrition rate of approximately 30 per cent at six months, the imputation model will additionally include the recruitment channel as an auxiliary variable to account for systematic differences in dropout across control-group sources.

The model-based and trial-based economic evaluations will present the expected incremental cost per QALY and per clinical outcome (CORE-10) of each intervention compared to CAU.

QALYs will be calculated using the area-under-the-curve (AUC) approach based on EQ-5D-5L utility values measured at baseline and at 3, 6 and 12 months. Baseline utility differences will be accounted for where appropriate (54).

For the trial-based economic evaluations, uncertainty in the incremental cost-effectiveness ratios will be characterised using non-parametric bootstrapping with 5,000 replications. The resampling approach will reflect the design and relevant hierarchical structure of each intervention study. Results will be presented on the cost-effectiveness plane and summarised using cost-effectiveness acceptability curves. For model-based economic evaluation, to explore the robustness of the results, deterministic and probabilistic sensitivity analyses will be performed (55). The model will be developed in a flexible structure that allows the incorporation of country-specific parameters where appropriate. The country-specific parameter differences are highlighted in Appendix Table 1.

Sensitivity, Subgroup and Scenario Analysis

A series of sensitivity analyses will be conducted to assess the robustness of the base-case incremental cost-effectiveness results (19, 56). The primary analysis will use unit costs from the selected reference country, identified using the prespecified selection criteria described earlier. First, all unit costs will be varied by 10% and 20% above and below their base-case values to assess the impact of uncertainty in unit cost estimates on the incremental costeffectiveness ratio. Where sufficient information is available, additional sensitivity analyses will examine the influence of alternative assumptions regarding intervention-related resource use and cost valuation across YEAH, SELFIE, and MOST. Second, a complete-case analysis will be conducted, including only participants with observed cost and outcome data at all four time points (53), alongside the primary analysis using imputed data, which uses all available data under the MAR assumption (51). Third, if the reference country value sets are used as the primary analysis, they will be re-estimated using an alternative EQ-5D-5L value set to assess sensitivity to the choice of utility tariff. Fourth, results will be re-estimated from a healthcare payer perspective, excluding productivity losses, informal care costs, and patient and family out-of-pocket costs, to assess how results change depending on whose costs are counted (57, 58). For YEAH specifically, a sensitivity analysis will exclude the n=336 borrowed controls from SELFIE and MOST to assess whether the base-case incremental cost-effectiveness estimate is driven by including participants recruited under different eligibility criteria.

Subgroup cost-effectiveness analyses will be conducted for predefined vulnerable groups identified in the YEAH and MOST protocols and applied consistently across all three trials including children of parents with a history of mental health problems, migrants and refugees, care-experienced young people, including care leavers, young people with disabilities, and young people identifying as LGBTQ+. These subgroups reflect the YOUTHreach consortium's equity focus (8). Subgroup analyses will use the same multilevel mixed-model framework as the primary analysis, with an additional interaction term between condition and subgroup membership. Given the exploratory nature of subgroup analyses and the reduced sample sizes within each subgroup, results will be interpreted with caution and presented with appropriate uncertainty intervals. These analyses are pre-specified but hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Scenario analyses for the model-based economic evaluation are planned across four broad dimensions, in line with ISPOR-SMDM modelling good research practices (59). They may be refined as the model is developed. These are anticipated to include: alternative time horizons (e.g., 2, 3, 5, and 10 years) alongside the base-case, to examine how the incremental costeffectiveness estimate evolves over different planning horizons; re-estimation from a healthcare payer perspective, excluding productivity losses, informal care costs, and patient and family out-of-pocket costs (57, 60); alternative costing assumptions, discount rates applied to future costs and effects; and alternative structural assumptions regarding the persistence of treatment effects, including full persistence beyond the trial period and immediate return to natural history transition probabilities at trial end, in place of the base-case linear waning assumption.

Discussion

This protocol describes a harmonised approach for conducting trial-based and model-based economic evaluations across a multicountry project. By integrating evidence generated across sites, the study aims to produce policy-relevant cost-effectiveness estimates for three youth mental health interventions that are both methodologically consistent and informative for assessing the potential transferability of findings across different healthcare systems. As part of this work, we also provide a context-specific RUM tool, applicable beyond this study, that captures costs extending beyond the healthcare sector. The use of EQ-5D-5L and CORE-10 as harmonised utility and effectiveness denominators across all three trials is a deliberate, methodologically justified choice, enabling consistent comparisons of cost-utility and cost-effectiveness. Where appropriate, multilevel mixed-effects regression models will be used to account for the hierarchical structure of participants nested within sites and countries. The final model specification will reflect the design and data structure of each intervention study (45, 61).

Strengths

The multicountry design itself presents both an opportunity and a challenge: it strengthens external validity by capturing heterogeneity in clinical practice, unit costs, and health system structures, but introduces methodological complexity from differences in data availability, costing methods, outcome valuation, and willingness-to-pay thresholds across countries. This protocol addresses these challenges through a standardised data collection instrument, predefined analytical guidelines, and country-specific adaptations where necessary.

Nonetheless, residual heterogeneity may persist and should be interpreted carefully when comparing base-case and country-specific sensitivity analysis results.

Limitations

Integrating decision-analytic modelling within the model-based economic evaluation also requires several assumptions, both structural and parametric. These include assumptions about long-term effectiveness and cost trajectories. Although sensitivity analyses will address this uncertainty, the validity of the model-based projections ultimately depends on the quality and completeness of available data. In multicountry settings, variations in epidemiological and cost data may further amplify uncertainty. Transparent reporting and validation of the model structures are therefore essential to support credibility and reproducibility (60). Several methodological limitations should be acknowledged. First, the quasi-experimental design of YEAH represents the most significant analytical challenge in this evaluation. Because young people presenting to IYMHS cannot ethically be randomised to a waitlist or no-treatment control, the YEAH control group is recruited separately, through multiple channels, from the same catchment areas, resulting in systematic differences in help-seeking behaviour, prior service use, and baseline severity that propensity score weighting cannot fully address (42) (43) (44).

The reliance on self-reported resource use data introduces potential recall bias, particularly given the three-month retrospective recall period used in the RUM. This is a known limitation of trial-based economic evaluation and is common to all studies using self-report resource use measurement (29). The use of a stakeholder-developed, youth-friendly instrument with clear and accessible language for each service type should mitigate but not eliminate this limitation.

A further limitation of multicountry economic evaluation is the absence of fully harmonised methodological guidance across national healthcare systems. Participating countries differ in their recommended analytical perspectives, discount rates, outcome valuation methods, willingness-to-pay thresholds, and unit-cost sources, as summarised in Appendix 1. These differences limit the extent to which one set of pooled cost-effectiveness estimates can directly represent every participating country. To maintain internal consistency, individual-level data from all participating countries will be included in pooled analyses, while one country will be selected as the reference country for the pooled base-case costing, outcome valuation, and methodological framework. Uncertainty in unit costs will be examined by varying them by 10% and 20% above and below their base-case values, while alternative EQ-5D-5L outcome value sets, discount rates, and other methodological assumptions will be explored where relevant.

These analyses will assess the robustness and potential transferability of the pooled findings but should not be interpreted as complete country-specific economic evaluations.

Comparison with Existing Evidence

Previous economic evaluations of youth mental health interventions have predominantly been conducted in single countries, most often Australia, with limited transferability to European healthcare systems (17, 18, 28). The headspace model in Australia that is one of the participating IYMHS of YEAH has been the subject of the most extensive evaluation to date, demonstrating high satisfaction rates and improvements in access and functioning, but economic evidence remains limited particularly with respect to long-term cost-effectiveness and societal costs (28). In Europe, evaluations of similar services such as Jigsaw in Ireland, @ease in the Netherlands and soulspace in Germany have provided important descriptive and pre-post data, but none have incorporated formal economic evaluation with a control group or modelled long-term outcomes (6). The YOUTHreach economic evaluation addresses these gaps by providing a controlled, multicountry, multiperspective economic evaluation framework for three distinct youth mental health service models simultaneously.

The previous SELFIE economic evaluation, conducted alongside the Dutch multicentre RCT, provided initial evidence on the cost-effectiveness of SELFIE from both societal and healthcare perspectives (17). The present multicountry economic evaluation extends this evidence to participating countries with greater population diversity and heterogeneity of healthcare systems, providing a broader basis for assessing the potential transferability and policy relevance of the findings across contexts.

Policy Relevance

Mental health problems in young people represent one of the leading causes of disease in highincome countries, and early intervention has been identified as a priority investment for reducing long-term societal costs (2, 4, 7). Decisionmakers require economic evidence that is relevant to their national health and social care contexts, but such evidence remains limited for youth mental health interventions. This evaluation will provide pooled base-case costeffectiveness estimates for YEAH, SELFIE, and MOST using a consistent reference-country valuation framework. Sensitivity and scenario analyses will examine the influence of uncertainty in unit costs, outcome value sets, discount rates, and other key assumptions. The accompanying return-on-investment tool will allow policymakers to vary selected parameters, including intervention uptake, implementation costs, discount rates, and persistence of effects, to explore the potential relevance of the findings to their own settings.

The inclusion of vulnerable subgroups including children of parents with mental health problems, migrants and refugees, care-experienced young people, young people with disabilities and those identifying as LGBTQ+ reflects the equity focus of the YOUTHreach consortium and ensures that cost-effectiveness evidence is available for the populations most at risk of mental health problems (2) and barriers to accessing care (8).
