Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Abstract

Speech abnormalities can often be early or clinically important manifestations of neurologic disease, yet access to clinicians with specialized expertise required for comprehensive motor speech evaluation may limit timely assessment. Brief remote speech assessment could provide a scalable approach for identifying patients who warrant more comprehensive evaluation, but its utility across heterogeneous speech presentations remains unclear. This study evaluated the validity and clinical utility of the Mayo Speech Test (MST), a brief (~5-minute) audio-recorded remote speech assessment, relative to a comprehensive inperson motor speech examination (~1-hour). Specifically, we examined whether MST could support clinically meaningful screening and triage decisions by distinguishing normal from abnormal speech and identifying the presence of a speech disorder.

Method: One hundred participants completed a comprehensive in-person speech evaluation and a remote MST. Participants were excluded when the interval between assessments exceeded 90 days (n = 13) or either rater judged audio quality insufficient for diagnostic interpretation (n = 6), with overlapping exclusions resulting in a final analytic sample of 82 participants. Remote recordings were independently evaluated by two speech-language pathologists (SLPs) who were blinded to participant identity. In-person evaluation classifications served as the clinical reference standard and were grouped as normal (n = 33), dysphonia only (n = 5), or speech disorder (n = 43). One participant could not be classified. Performance was evaluated for two clinically motivated binary decisions: distinguishing normal from abnormal (such as dysphonia or speech disorder; Tier 0) and identifying normal speech or dysphonia only versus speech disorder (Tier 1).

Sensitivity, specificity, predictive values, agreement, and error patterns were examined.

Results: Both remote raters demonstrated similar overall performance despite differing error profiles. For Tier 0 screening, Rater 1 demonstrated 93.6% sensitivity and 71.9% specificity for identifying abnormal voice/speech, whereas Rater 2 demonstrated 78.7% sensitivity and 93.9% specificity. For Tier 1 identification of speech disorder, sensitivity and specificity were 83.3% and 86.5%, respectively, for Rater 1 and 71.4% and 97.4% for Rater 2. Interrater agreement was 79.7% for Tier 0 (κ = 0.60) and 86.1% for Tier 1 (κ = 0.72). Missed speech disorders represented an important source of error, with differences between raters indicating distinct thresholds for assigning abnormality and speech-disorder classifications.

Conclusions: A brief remote speech assessment showed promising performance for broad clinical classification, particularly when used to address screening and triage questions rather than reproduce the diagnostic granularity of a comprehensive examination. Similar overall performance across raters masked meaningful sensitivity-specificity tradeoffs, highlighting the importance of examining error profiles in addition to aggregate accuracy. MST may therefore have clinical utility for identifying patients who warrant comprehensive motor speech evaluation, but should not be considered a substitute for a full diagnostic assessment.

Introduction

Access to specialized speech-language pathology (SLP) services can be limited by clinician availability, geographic distance, mobility limitations, and the time and resources required for comprehensive evaluation (Morton et al., 2022; Rameau et al., 2023). These challenges are particularly relevant for patients with neurologic disease, for whom access to clinicians with specific expertise in motor speech disorders may be limited. Workforce data further underscores this concern. In a 2025 survey of speech-language pathologists working in health care settings, more than half reported that job openings exceeded available job seekers in their geographic area and practice setting, and 44.8% reported funded but unfilled SLP positions at their facilities (American Speech-Language-Hearing Association, 2025). As demand for SLP services continues to increase, scalable approaches that can efficiently identify patients requiring comprehensive specialist evaluation may help extend the reach of limited clinical resources (Rezaii & Estiri, 2026).

Efficient access to motor speech assessment may be especially important because changes in speech can be an early or prominent manifestation of neurologic disease. Motor speech disorders, including dysarthria and apraxia of speech (AOS), occur across a range of acquired and neurodegenerative conditions and may provide clinically meaningful information about underlying neurologic dysfunction (Duffy, 2012). In Parkinson's disease, for example, changes in voice and speech may occur early in the disease course and have been investigated as potential markers of disease presence and progression (Ma et al., 2020). In amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), physiologic changes across speech subsystems may be detectable before overt declines in speech intelligibility (Ball et al., 2002; Green et al., 2013 , Rong et al., 2015).

Speech impairment can also be the presenting or predominant manifestation in neurodegenerative disease, as demonstrated in primary progressive apraxia of speech (Duffy et al., 2021). Collectively, these findings highlight speech as a potentially sensitive behavioral window into neurologic function and underscore the value of timely identification of motor speech abnormalities.

Comprehensive motor speech evaluation and diagnosis, however, requires considerable clinical expertise and typically incorporates a series of speech tasks, perceptual judgments, examination of the speech mechanism, and consideration of the pattern and severity of abnormalities across speech subsystems (Duffy, 2012 , Green et al., 2013). Such evaluations may be difficult to deploy broadly as an initial point of contact, particularly when the primary clinical question is whether a patient demonstrates findings that warrant specialist evaluation. A brief remote assessment capable of reliably distinguishing individuals with and without clinically meaningful speech abnormalities could therefore serve a different but complementary purpose -screening and triage rather than definitive diagnosis.

Telepractice and remote assessment have become increasingly feasible approaches for extending speech-language pathology services beyond traditional in-person settings (Theodoros, 2011; Weidner & Lowman, 2020). Early investigations demonstrated that assessment of dysarthria via telehealth was feasible and that several measures of severity, intelligibility, and perceptual speech characteristics could show acceptable agreement with faceto-face assessment, as well as high inter-and intra-rater reliability (Hill et al., 2006; Hill et al., 2009). Subsequent work has nevertheless highlighted important challenges, including the effects of recording equipment, compression, microphone placement, and internet connectivity on perceptual and acoustic speech measures (Dahl et al., 2021; Sevitz et al., 2021; Tran et al., 2026; Weerathunge et al., 2021).

The existing literature has frequently focused on specific clinical populations, individual acoustic or perceptual measures, or the remote administration of assessment protocols that largely mirror those used during a full comprehensive in-person evaluation (Ma et al., 2025; Migliorelli et al., 2023; Nunes et al., 2024; Theodoros et al., 2003). Considerably less is known about whether a brief, remotely obtained speech sample can provide sufficient information to support clinically useful motor speech screening and triage when compared with a comprehensive in-person evaluation, particularly across a heterogeneous population representing a range of neurologic diagnoses and speech presentations. This distinction is important because the value of a brief remote examination may not depend on its ability to reproduce the diagnostic granularity of a comprehensive motor speech evaluation. Instead, its greatest utility may lie in answering broader clinical questions such as: Does this patient's speech appear abnormal? Is there evidence of a motor speech disorder? Does the patient warrant a more comprehensive evaluation? A remote tool that performs adequately at these levels could facilitate triage and prioritization while reserving more resource-intensive comprehensive examinations for patients most likely to benefit from them.

Mayo Speech Test (MST) is a brief remote speech assessment that can be captured in approximately five minutes and includes speech tasks intended to elicit clinically relevant features of speech production. In contrast, a comprehensive in-person motor speech evaluation may require an hour or longer and provides substantially greater diagnostic detail and information pertinent to clinical management recommendations. The purpose of the present study was, therefore, to evaluate the validity and clinical utility of MST relative to a comprehensive in-person motor speech examination. Rather than evaluating MST solely on its ability to reproduce a specific diagnostic classification, we examined its performance across clinically motivated levels of triage, including its ability to distinguish normal from abnormal voice/speech and to identify the presence of a speech disorder. We additionally examined interrater agreement and patterns of misclassification to characterize how clinician-related differences in decision thresholds may influence the performance of a brief remote assessment.

Methods

Participants

Participants were recruited as part of MST MIND-Mayo Speech Test: MIND (Monitoring Indicators of Neurologic Health and Disease) at Mayo Clinic in Rochester, Minnesota. The study was approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board (IRB# 22-002430). The initial sample consisted of 100 consecutive participants who completed both a comprehensive inperson motor speech evaluation and a brief remote speech assessment using the Mayo Speech Test (MST). The sample was selected to include 50 consecutive participants whose in-person examination was completed by Rater 1 and 50 whose in-person examination was completed by Rater 2. Participants were excluded from the primary analyses if more than 90 days elapsed between the in-person and remote assessments or if either remote rater judged the remote recording as having insufficient audio quality to support clinical judgments. Thirteen participants exceeded the 90-day interval, and six had unusable audio recordings. Because one participant met both exclusion criteria, 18 unique participants were excluded, resulting in a final analytic sample of 82 participants. Of these, 41 had undergone the comprehensive in-person evaluation with Rater 1 and 41 with Rater 2. The median interval between the in-person evaluation and the remote recording was 21 days (IQR 12-34, range 0-82) and did not differ by in-person rater (median 21 days for each). Remote recordings were obtained after the in-person evaluation for 73 participants, on the same day for 1, and before it for 8. The final cohort (Table 1) included 40 male and 42 female participants, ranging in age from 31 to 81 years (mean = 64 years, SD -11.2). Most participants were White (n = 77, 93.9%) and not Hispanic or Latino (n = 76, 92.7%). Comprehensive In-Person Motor Speech Evaluation

Participants underwent a comprehensive in-person motor speech evaluation conducted by one of two certified speech-language pathologists (Rater 1 and Rater 2). The raters have expertise in the assessment of neurologic speech disorders and their clinical practice involves differential diagnosis of acquired and degenerative speech and language disorders in the context of medical workups for both known and unknown etiologies of neurologic symptoms.

Comprehensive evaluations typically lasted approximately one hour and served as the clinical reference standard against which remote MST classifications were compared. The in-person examination routinely included reviewing medical history, oral mechanism examination, and perceptual evaluation (including sustained phonation, alternating and sequential motion rates, passage reading, repetition, and conversational speech). Based on the complete clinical examination, the evaluating SLP determined whether speech was normal or abnormal and, when appropriate, assigned a motor speech diagnosis and associated clinical characteristics, in alignment with the canonical Mayo Clinic classification system (DAB, 1969a,b; DAB,1975; Duffy, 2012).

Mayo Speech Test

MST is a brief remote speech assessment designed to obtain clinically informative speech samples in approximately five minutes (Botha et al., 2026). Participants completed alternating and sequential motion rates, vowel prolongation, counting, word repetition, sentence repetition, passage reading, and picture description. Speech samples were recorded remotely by participants using their preferred electronic device, such a smart phone, computer, or tablet. Of the initial 100 recordings, 94 were considered to have sufficient audio quality to permit annotation of speech features and a diagnostic opinion. Six recordings were excluded because at least one rater judged the audio to be of insufficient quality.

Each MST recording was independently evaluated by two SLPs. Remote ratings were completed with raters blinded to participant identity. Raters completed no calibration exercise before rating and were not informed of the intended clinical use of their ratings (e.g., screening versus diagnostic confirmation), so that ratings would reflect each clinician's usual diagnostic threshold.

Based on the MST recording, raters provided their clinical impression, including whether speech was normal or whether isolated dysphonia and/or a speech disorder was present. When a speech disorder was identified, raters assigned a specific speech diagnosis (i.e., AOS, dysarthria subtype, functional speech disorder, or other). Raters could also indicate when a diagnosis could not be determined from the available recording. For each evaluation, raters reported their confidence in their diagnostic impression as not confident, somewhat confident, or very confident. Remote ratings were completed at a median of 418 days after the in-person evaluation, and for 75 of 82 participants (91%), more than six months had elapsed.

Classification Framework

For analysis, the research team grouped the raters' individual clinical diagnoses into three broader diagnostic categories: (1) normal, (2) dysphonia only, and (3) speech disorder.

Participants were classified as normal when no voice or speech disorder was identified; as dysphonia only when non-specific voice differences were identified in the absence of a speech disorder; and as speech disorder when dysarthria, AOS, functional speech, or other speech disorder was identified, with or without dysphonia. When a rating form contained internally inconsistent selections (e.g., selecting both normal and dysarthria as the diagnosis), these were resolved with the rater before analysis. The same classification procedure was applied to diagnoses obtained from the comprehensive in-person evaluation documentation in the medical record, allowing the remote MST ratings to be compared with the in-person clinical reference standard using a common classification framework.

Within the final analytic cohort, application of this classification framework to the comprehensive in-person clinical reference standard resulted in 33 participants classified as normal, 5 as dysphonia only, and 43 with a speech disorder (dysarthria and/or AOS, n = 37; functional speech disorder, n = 5; speech disruption due to structural change (e.g., partial glossectomy), n = 1). For one participant, a definitive classification could not be determined.

"Unable to determine" responses were treated as not classifiable rather than as a separate diagnostic category and were excluded from analyses requiring a definitive classification. Because MST is substantially shorter than a comprehensive motor speech evaluation, the primary analyses evaluated its utility for screening and triage rather than its ability to reproduce the full diagnostic granularity of the in-person examination. Using the diagnoses from the in-person reference standard, the three-category framework was therefore further collapsed into two clinically motivated binary classification tasks: Tier 0, normal versus abnormal speech, and Tier 1, normal speech or dysphonia only versus a clinically concerning speech disorder. Tier 0 evaluated whether MST allowed the raters to distinguish participants with normal speech from those with any identified abnormality; dysphonia-only and speech disorder classifications were combined into an "abnormal" category. This analysis represented an initial screening decision: Does the speech sample contain evidence of an abnormality that may warrant further clinical evaluation?

Tier 1 evaluated whether MST allowed the raters to identify the presence of a clinically concerning speech disorder. Participants classified as normal or dysphonia only were combined into a "no speech disorder" category and compared with participants with a speech disorder.

This analysis represented the higher-stakes triage decision of primary interest: Does the patient demonstrate evidence of a speech disorder warranting comprehensive motor speech evaluation to determine the specific nature of a speech disorder (e.g., dysarthria versus AOS vs functional speech disorder)? This framework allowed evaluation of MST according to the clinical decisions a brief remote assessment might reasonably support rather than requiring exact reproduction of the diagnostic specificity available from a comprehensive examination.

Statistical Analysis

Analyses evaluated both agreement between the two remote raters and performance of each remote rater relative to the comprehensive in-person clinical reference standard. For the three-category classification (normal, dysphonia only, and speech disorder), interrater agreement was quantified using percent agreement unweighted Cohen's kappa, and linearweighted kappa, treating the categories as ordered (normal < dysphonia only < speech disorder). Binary interrater agreement for the Tier 0 and Tier 1 classifications was quantified using percent agreement, Cohen's kappa, and prevalence-adjusted bias-adjusted kappa (PABAK). Ninety-five percent confidence intervals for agreement statistics were estimated by percentile bootstrap (2,000 resamples of participants).

For comparisons with the clinical reference standard, diagnostic performance was characterized using sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV). Exact (Clopper-Pearson) 95% confidence intervals were computed for sensitivity and specificity. A few cases in which either the reference-standard or corresponding remote classification was "unable to determine" were excluded from analyses requiring a definitive classification (reference standard, n = 1; Rater 1, n = 2; Rater 2, n = 1). Remote 'unable to determine' ratings were not scored as normal or absent, because in clinical deployment such cases would be referred for in-person evaluation rather than treated as negative. Consequently, 79 ratings were available for Rater 1 and 80 for Rater 2 for comparisons with the reference standard, and the participants with a reference-standard speech disorder included in each rater's analysis (n = 42 each) differed by one participant. Interrater analyses were restricted to the 79 participants for whom both remote raters provided classifiable responses.

Error patterns were further characterized for the Tier 1 analysis. A false negative (FN) was defined as a participant classified as diagnosed with a speech disorder during the comprehensive in-person evaluation but classified as having no speech disorder based on MST recordings.

A false positive (FP) was defined as a participant without a speech disorder according to the clinical reference standard labeled with a speech disorder based on MST recordings. FN and FP cases were examined by rater to characterize differences in clinical decision thresholds. Rater confidence was examined in relation to classification accuracy and error type. Distributions of confidence ratings were compared between raters with the Stuart-Maxwell test of marginal homogeneity for paired ratings, and with an exact McNemar test for use of the 'very confident' category. Confidence was compared between correct and incorrect Tier 1 classifications with Fisher's exact test. Interrater Agreement

Interrater analyses included 79 participants for whom both remote raters provided classifiable ratings. For the three-group classification of normal, dysphonia only, and MSD, the two remote raters agreed in 75.9% of cases (unweighted κ = 0.61 , 95% CI 0.47-0.75; linearweighted κ = 0.66, 95% CI 0.52-0.79). Agreement increased when diagnoses were collapsed into the clinically motivated binary classifications. For Tier 0 (normal vs. abnormal), percent agreement was 79.7% (κ = 0.60; PABAK = 0.59). Rater 1 classified 67 of 79 cases (84.8%) consistent with the in-person reference ratings. Rater 1 had 3 false negatives and 9 false positives, relative to the reference standard.

Rater 1 classified 67 of 79 cases (84.8%) concordant with the in-person reference standard. Sensitivity for detecting a speech disorder was 83.3% (95% CI 68.6-93.0), and specificity was 86.5% (71.2-95.5); PPV and NPV were 87.5% and 82.1%, respectively. Of the 42 participants with a reference-standard speech disorder, Rater 1 identified 35 and had seven false negatives; there were five false positives.

Rater 2 classified 66 of 80 cases (82.5%) concordant with the in-person reference standard. Sensitivity for detecting a speech disorder was 71.4% (95% CI 55.4-84.3), whereas specificity was 97.4% (86.2-99.9); PPV and NPV were 96.8% and 75.5%, respectively. Of the 42 participants with a reference-standard speech disorder, Rater 2 identified 30 and had 12 false negatives, with one false positive. As observed for Tier 0, overall Tier 1 accuracy was similar between raters despite differences in the distribution of errors.

Error Patterns and Rater Confidence

Across both raters, Tier 1 errors consisted of 19 false negatives (13 participants, 6 missed by both raters) and 6 false positives (5 participants, 1 incorrectly labeled by both raters). Among the 19 false negatives, 11 (57.9%) were rated with somewhat confident diagnostic confidence and 7 (36.8%) were rated very confident; only 1 (5.3%) was rated not confident. In contrast, none of the six false positives were accompanied by a very confident rating; four (66.7%) were rated somewhat confident and two (33.3%) were rated not confident. Thus, although false positives were not associated with high diagnostic confidence, several false negatives occurred despite the rater reporting high confidence in the remote diagnostic impression.

Missed speech disorders (false negatives) were concentrated in hypokinetic dysarthria.

Of the 9 participants with reference-standard hypokinetic dysarthria, each rater identified 4 (44.4%), compared with 31 of 33 (93.9%; Fisher's exact p = 0.003) and 26 of 33 (78.8%; p = 0.09) participants with other speech disorders for Raters 1 and 2, respectively. The same 5 participants with hypokinetic dysarthria were missed by both raters. Rater 1 classified 3 of these as dysphonia only and 2 as normal, whereas Rater 2 classified all 5 as normal, each with a very confident rating. Other missed diagnoses were AOS (Rater 1, n = 2; Rater 2, n = 3), ataxic dysarthria (n = 1; n = 2), and, for Rater 2 only, flaccid, hyperkinetic, and unilateral UMN dysarthria, functional speech disorder, and structural speech disruption (n = 1 each). Of note, these diagnostic counts overlap, e.g., one participant had hypokinetic dysarthria plus AOS.

Across classifiable remote ratings, Rater 1 characterized 22.8% of ratings as not confident, 49.4% as somewhat confident, and 27.8% as very confident, whereas Rater 2 characterized 3.8% as not confident, 37.5% as somewhat confident, and 58.8% as very confident.

Differences were also apparent among participants with a reference-standard speech disorder: Rater 1 reported being very confident for 6 of 42 (14.3%) cases, compared with 19 of 42 (45.2%) for Rater 2.

Within-and Between-Rater Comparisons

To examine whether prior in-person clinical exposure to a participant influenced remote classification performance, additional analyses compared cases in which the remote MST was rated by the same clinician who conducted the comprehensive in-person examination (withinrater) with those rated by the other clinician (between-rater). For Tier 0, sensitivity for detecting abnormal speech was identical for the within-and between-rater comparisons (85.1% and 87.2), whereas specificity was 78.8% within-rater and 87.5% between-rater. For Tier 1, sensitivity for detecting a speech disorder was 81.0% within-rater and 73.8% between-rater, while specificity was 89.5% and 94.6%, respectively. There were 8 false negatives in the withinrater comparison and 11 in the between-rater comparison. Overall, performance was broadly comparable when remote ratings were compared with evaluations performed by the same versus the other clinician, although sensitivity for detecting speech disorders was slightly lower in the between-rater analysis. An additional finding relevant to clinical implementation was the high proportion of remotely obtained recordings that were clinically interpretable. Of the 100 recordings initially obtained, only six were excluded because at least one rater judged the audio quality insufficient to support a diagnostic opinion, yielding a 94% rate of usable recordings. Three out of the six excluded cases were patients with hypokinetic dysarthria. Because recordings were obtained on participants' own devices without a standardized mouth-to-mic distance, reduced loudness -a distinguishing feature of hypokinetic dysarthria -may be less apparent remotely, which may be relevant to the lower detection of hypokinetic dysarthria observed here. Nevertheless, the high proportion of usable recordings provides preliminary support for the feasibility of brief remote speech assessment, as clinical utility depends on reliably obtaining adequate speech samples outside of traditional in-person settings (Sevitz et al., 2021; Tran et al., 2026). The small number of unusable recordings also underscores the need for timely procedures to identify inadequate samples and obtain repeat recordings when necessary.

An important finding was that similar overall accuracy did not necessarily reflect similar classification behavior between clinicians. For Tier 0, Rater 1 demonstrated higher sensitivity for detecting abnormal voice/speech (93.6%) but lower specificity (71.9%), whereas Rater 2 demonstrated lower sensitivity (78.7%) but higher specificity (93.9%). A similar pattern emerged for Tier 1. Rater 1 identified 81.0% of participants with a speech disorder, with a specificity of 86.5%, whereas Rater 2 identified 71.4% of participants with a speech disorder but demonstrated a specificity of 97.4%. Consequently, Rater 1 produced fewer false negatives but more false positives, whereas Rater 2 produced fewer false positives but more false negatives.

These findings demonstrate that aggregate accuracy alone may provide an incomplete picture of the performance of a brief remote screening assessment. Importantly, raters were not instructed to approach the MST recordings with a specific clinical purpose (e.g., screening/ triage versus diagnostic assessment) and therefore were not directed toward a particular decision threshold. In a triage context, the balance between sensitivity and specificity is particularly important because failing to identify an individual with an MSD may delay referral for comprehensive evaluation, whereas an overcall is more likely to result in additional, perhaps unnecessary comprehensive assessment. Thus, calibration between raters, against an explicitly defined clinical purpose, could help establish shared decision thresholds and improve consistency in future applications of MST.

The pattern of Tier 1 errors further highlights this distinction. Across both raters, 19 remote ratings (of 13 participants; 6 missed by both raters) did not identify a speech disorder that was identified during the comprehensive in-person evaluation; in only six ratings (of 5 participants) was a speech disorder identified remotely that was not present during the clinical reference standard. Thus, missed identification of speech disorders represented the predominant clinically consequential outcome in this study. These false negative cases were concentrated in hypokinetic dysarthria: both raters missed the same five of nine participants with hypokinetic dysarthria, whereas detection of other speech disorders was higher (Rater 1, 93.9%; Rater 2, 78.8%). The brief nature of MST may contribute to these errors because subtle speech abnormalities may not be consistently apparent within a limited speech sample. A comprehensive examination provides substantially more opportunity to elicit and characterize abnormalities across tasks and speech subsystems. Accordingly, a negative finding on a brief remote assessment should not be interpreted as definitive evidence that a speech disorder is absent, particularly when other clinical information raises concerns. An expanded MST, with increased opportunities for connected speech, could be considered in the future with reevaluation of concordance with conclusions from in-person testing.

The confidence findings provide additional insight into the nature of these errors. Most missed speech disorders occurred when raters reported being at least somewhat confident in their diagnostic impression, and seven of the 19 missed speech disorder ratings were accompanied by a rating of very confident. In contrast, none of the six false positives were rated as very confident. Confidence also differed considerably between raters: Rater 2 used the very confident category substantially more often than Rater 1. These findings suggest that confidence may capture aspects of an individual clinician's certainty but should not be assumed to correspond directly to classification accuracy. Importantly, high confidence did not eliminate the possibility of a false-negative. If confidence is incorporated into future triage algorithms or clinical workflows, it may be most useful when interpreted alongside the diagnostic decision and other clinical information rather than as an independent indicator of the likelihood that classification is correct.

Interrater findings similarly support the use of broader classifications for remote screening. Agreement between the two remote raters was 75.9% for the three-group classification but increased to 86.1% when the clinical question was modified to the presence versus absence of a speech disorder. This pattern supports a potential role for MST during triage. Distinguishing among specific diagnostic categories requires a level of perceptual and diagnostic precision that may be difficult to achieve relaibility from a brief remote sample. In contrast, determining whether there is sufficient evidence of a speech disorder to warrant further evaluation represents a broader clinical decision and may be better suited to the amount of information available from MST.

The within-and between-rater analyses also provide useful context for interpreting agreement with a comprehensive evaluation. Tier 0 sensitivity was similar across within-and between-rater comparisons, while Tier 1 sensitivity was slightly higher within-rater than between-rater. Specificity was somewhat higher in the between-rater comparisons for both tiers. Although these analyses cannot completely exclude the possibility of participant recognition, the persistence of similar performance when the remote rater had not conducted the participant's comprehensive evaluation suggests that prior clinical exposure alone is unlikely to account for the observed (dis)agreement.

Finally, our results should be interpreted in the context of what was a deliberately stringent test. The raters completed no calibration exercise and were not told whether their ratings were intended for screening, triage, or diagnostic confirmation. This was intentional, to avoid biasing them toward sensitivity or specificity, but it means each rater applied their own implicit decision threshold, consistent with the distinct sensitivity-specificity profiles observed.

The results should therefore not be taken as a targeted assessment of the performance of MST in any single role, such as screening. Raters also had no control over the speech sample: participants recorded a fixed task set on their own devices, the tasks may not match each clinician's own practice, and raters could not request repetitions or follow up with targeted tasks.

Raters received no clinical context, such as a presenting complaint or referral question, whereas the in-person reference evaluation drew on the full medical record, patient reported history, and clinical examination. MST is also limited to audio, whereas the in-person evaluation incorporates visual and tactile information during the motor speech exam. Together with the interval between assessments and the imperfect reference standard discussed below, these conditions suggest that MST may perform better in a deployed workflow with a defined purpose, calibrated raters, and basic clinical context.

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, although remote MST recordings were restricted to those obtained within 90 days of the comprehensive in-person evaluation, clinical stability during this interval could not be confirmed. Participants may have experienced changes in neurologic status, speech function, or other factors affecting speech between assessments. Additionally, potentially relevant withinday factors were not controlled. For example, medication timing relative to assessment was not available for participants with Parkinson's disease or parkinsonism (n = 6), and fluctuations associated with medication state, fatigue, or the variable presentation of some conditions (e.g., functional speech disorder) could have contributed to real differences between the in-person and remote speech presentations. Thus, some disagreement between assessments may reflect genuine changes or fluctuations in speech rather than differences in the ability of MST to capture motor speech abnormalities.

Second, the same two SLPs who rated the remote MST recordings also participated in the comprehensive in-person evaluations from which the clinical reference standard was derived. While this was by design, and remote ratings were completed without participant identifiers, it is possible that a rater could have recognized a participant from their voice or speech characteristics. This risk was likely reduced by the brief nature of the remote recordings and the time elapsed between the in-person evaluation and the remote rating (more than six months for 91% of participants) Additionally, performance remained generally similar when within-rater and between-rater comparisons were examined, suggesting that prior clinical exposure was unlikely to fully account for the observed agreement between MST and comprehensive evaluation. Nevertheless, future validation by independent raters with no prior clinical contact with participants would provide a more stringent assessment of performance.

Third, the comprehensive in-person evaluation served as the clinical reference standard rather than some sort of objective biological gold standard. Motor speech diagnosis relies in part on expert perceptual judgment, and some degree of clinician variability is therefore expected (Pernon et al., 2022). This consideration is particularly relevant given the rater-specific patterns observed in the present study. Although overall classification accuracy was similar between raters, their sensitivity and specificity differed, particularly for identification of motor speech disorders. These findings suggest that individual clinicians may apply different decision thresholds when interpreting brief remote speech samples and during in-person evaluations.

Similarly, confidence ratings differed substantially between raters and did not consistently protect against misclassification, indicating that clinician confidence should not be interpreted as a uniform proxy for diagnostic accuracy.

Finally, the composition of the sample also warrants consideration. Although the heterogeneous clinical cohort allowed MST to be evaluated across a range of neurologic and abnormal speech presentations, some diagnostic categories had relatively few participants. In particular, the dysphonia-only group was small, limiting conclusions about the ability of MST to distinguish isolated voice abnormalities from speech disorders. Studies with greater representation across neurologic diagnoses, motor speech disorder subtypes, and severity levels will be important for determining whether performance differs systematically across clinical populations. Future work should evaluate MST prospectively in independent clinical samples and determine whether standardized training or decision-support tools, such as explicit classification criteria and standardized scoring guidelines can reduce rater-related variability.

The incorporation of automated speech analysis and artificial intelligence may also provide an opportunity to supplement clinician judgment by identifying speech features that are difficult to reliably detect from brief samples and, ultimately, improve the accuracy and consistency of remote motor speech screening.

Conclusion

The present findings support the clinical utility of MST as a brief, remote evaluation approach to motor speech screening and triage. In approximately five minutes of recorded speech, MST demonstrated meaningful ability to distinguish normal from abnormal speech and to identify individuals with speech disorders, although performance and error patterns varied across raters. These findings suggest that the value of MST lies not in replacing the diagnostic depth of a comprehensive motor speech evaluation, but in helping determine who requires further evaluation and prioritizing those patients for a more comprehensive assessment by a speech-language pathologist.This distinction is particularly important in clinical environments where specialized speech-language pathology services are limited, and comprehensive evaluations may require substantial clinician time. A brief assessment that can be obtained remotely may provide an efficient first step for extending the reach of specialized motor speech expertise, identifying patients who warrant more comprehensive evaluation, and directing limited clinical resources toward those most in need. MST has the potential to support a scalable, tiered model of motor speech assessment in which remote screening facilitates timely access to comprehensive evaluation while preserving specialist resources for the diagnostic and treatment decisions that require them.
