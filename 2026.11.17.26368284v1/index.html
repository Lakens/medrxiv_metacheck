Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Mental health difficulties often begin in adolescence and early adulthood, yet most evidence on green social prescribing, which connects people with nature-based community activities to support health and wellbeing, comes from adult and quantitative research. This qualitative study explored how young people experienced taking part in a community farm programme in Wales and how they felt it influenced their mental health and wellbeing. Twelve young people aged 16 to 25 years took part in individual, semi-structured interviews at Swansea Community Farm in 2026. Data were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis within an interpretivist paradigm.

Four interconnected themes were developed: 'belonging in a supportive community'; 'finding purpose through meaningful participation'; 'personal growth and enhanced wellbeing'; and 'nature as the context for wellbeing'. Participants rarely described nature alone as responsible for feeling better. Instead, the farm brought together people who accepted them, useful work with real responsibility, and time outdoors with animals, and these were experienced together rather than separately. Participants had found the farm informally, and none had been referred through a formal social prescribing pathway. These findings suggest that green social prescribing for young people may work best when it is designed around belonging, meaningful roles and responsibility, with nature as the setting that makes these possible, and that evaluation should capture belonging, confidence and purpose as well as symptom reduction.

They also point to the need for clear, youth-friendly routes into community provision, including self-referral. This was a single-site study, so it cannot show causal effects or represent the experiences of young people who did not engage, but it offers an in-depth account to inform the design and evaluation of provision for young people.

Introduction

Poor mental health among young people is one of the most pressing public health problems globally. Adolescence and early adulthood are important developmental periods for prevention and health promotion [1, 2], and they are also when most mental health difficulties first appear. A global meta-analysis of 192 epidemiological studies estimated that around half of all mental disorders begin before the age of 18 and more than 60% before the age of 25 [3]. These are years of rapid change in education, work, relationships and independence, and it has been argued that adolescence is better understood as lasting into the mid-twenties [2]. Surveys suggest that around one in five children and young people in England has a probable mental health disorder [4], and demand has outpaced the capacity of specialist services, with rising thresholds for access to child and adolescent mental health services [5].

The consequences reach well beyond health services. Young people with mental health difficulties are more likely to be out of education, employment or training later on, a status that is itself associated with poorer mental health [6]. Across the whole population, mental ill health was estimated to cost England around £300 billion in 2022, with most of that borne by people living with mental health difficulties and their families through lost earnings, reduced quality of life and informal care [7]. Difficulties that start early can therefore carry costs for individuals, families, employers and public services for many years. This is one reason why approaches that help young people stay well, and that sit alongside rather than inside stretched clinical services, are attracting growing interest. Social prescribing is one such approach. It connects people with activities and sources of support in their community, often with the help of a link worker, to address the social and practical factors that shape health [8, 9]. In Wales, it sits within national policy, and the national framework recognises several routes into provision, including self-referral, referral from statutory services outside health, and referral from healthcare [10]. This builds on wider commitments to wellbeing and prevention [11] [12] [13] [14]. Green social prescribing (GSP) links people with activities undertaken in natural environments, including community gardening, conservation, care farming and volunteering [8, 9]. The same idea is often called nature-based social prescribing (NBSP) in the literature. We use GSP throughout.

The evidence for GSP is encouraging but uneven. Reviews, mostly of adults, report potential benefits for mental wellbeing [15, 16], anxiety and depression [15, 16], and social connectedness [17, 18], while also describing wide variation in intervention design, populations and outcome measures [15, 19]. Systematic reviews of social prescribing more broadly have repeatedly noted small studies, high risk of bias, a lack of comparison groups and short followup [20]. We also know little about which features of GSP matter. Qualitative studies with adults point to relationships with staff and peers, a sense of belonging, learning new skills and contact with nature itself [21] [22] [23]. These accounts suggest that GSP may work through social and occupational processes as much as through nature exposure, but they were gathered from adults.

Much less is known about young people. Social prescribing has largely been developed and evaluated with adults, and there is limited evidence about whether it is acceptable to young people or what they themselves want from it [24]. Research on nature and the mental health of children and young people suggests potential benefits, but the interventions studied are very diverse [25, 26]. The small number of qualitative studies of young people in GSP-type activities, with young pregnant women [27], care leavers [28] and students in a campus garden [29], report social connection and the restorative qualities of nature as important, but each involves a particular group in a particular setting. Access is a further question. In a recent study of stakeholders in South Wales, one participant asked who was covering young people, given that social prescribers were thought to work mainly with adults [20].

Community and city farms offer a distinctive setting for GSP. These are typically charity-run working farms where people look after animals, grow food and maintain the site alongside others, and in doing so contribute to the running of a place that the local community uses and values. Care-farming research, mostly with adults, suggests benefits for quality of life, depression and anxiety, although the evidence base is small [30], and broader qualitative evidence suggests that social relationships and shared purpose may matter alongside contact with nature [15, 31]. What is missing is an account from young people themselves of what a community farm offers them and which parts of it they find meaningful.

That gap matters for practical reasons. If GSP is going to be offered to young people, commissioners and providers need to know what to build into it, who it suits and why, and how young people will find their way to it. Qualitative research can help with each of these. It can show which features of provision young people value and would need protecting if schemes were scaled up or evaluated, and which outcomes matter to them but may be missed by standard symptom measures [19, 21]. It can also show how young people reach provision in the first place, which bears directly on how referral pathways should be designed.

This study therefore explored young people's experiences of taking part in a community farm programme in Wales and their perceptions of how taking part influenced their mental health and wellbeing.

Materials and methods

Ethics

Study design and methodological approach

A qualitative design employing semi-structured individual interviews was used to explore subjective experiences and meaning. The study was situated within an interpretivist paradigm, which treats knowledge as constructed through people's accounts of their own experience and through the researcher's interpretation of those accounts [32, 33]. The study is reported in line with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) [34], and the completed checklist is provided as S1 Checklist.

Aim and objectives

The aim was to explore young people's experiences of taking part in a community farm programme and their perceptions of how participation influenced their mental health and wellbeing. The objectives were to: (1) understand how young people found their way to the farm; (2) describe their experiences of the farm, including relationships with staff and other participants, the activities, and contact with nature and animals; (3) explore how they felt participation influenced their mental health and wellbeing, including any challenges; and (4) consider what the findings mean for the design of, and access to, green social prescribing for young people.

Setting and participants

Swansea Community Farm is a city farm in South Wales (UK). The farm provides weekly volunteering and community-focused activities including animal care, horticulture, conservation, education and training for those in the local community. Its programmes aim to reduce social isolation and loneliness, address poor mental health and improve wellbeing. Young people take part in weekly sessions choosing animal husbandry or land-based activities such as gardening, woodwork or conservation.

Recruitment and data collection

Participants were purposively sampled. Eligibility criteria were age 16-25 years, current volunteering at the farm, and capacity to provide informed consent. The farm operation manager acted as gatekeeper and informed eligible volunteers about the study during regular farm activities and morning briefings. Interested volunteers arranged participation independently; it was clearly explained that participation was voluntary and that there were no consequences for non-participation.

Interviews took place between June and July 2026, conducted by the first author (AW).

Each participant completed one individual interview after written informed consent was obtained. Most interviews were held in a private room; one took place in an outdoor safari tent when the indoor room was unavailable. The interviews explored motivations for taking part, experiences of the farm, perceived influences on mental health and wellbeing, relationships with staff and other volunteers, experiences of nature and animals, and challenges encountered.

Data analysis

Interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed using Microsoft Word's transcription function, and the transcripts were manually checked (AW) against the original recordings. Transcripts were anonymised. Data were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis (RTA), following Braun and Clarke's approach [35] [36] [37]. The analysis (AW) was inductive and reflexive, with the researcher treated as an active interpreter rather than a neutral coding instrument.

All coding and theme development were undertaken by the first author and discussed with the second author (MB). The process involved repeated reading of transcripts, generation of initial codes, development and review of candidate themes, and refinement and definition of the final themes. Consistent with RTA, inter-rater reliability and coding consensus were not sought. Analytic decisions were documented through reflexive journalling and an audit trail. Recruitment was not based on a predetermined data-saturation criterion. Instead, sample adequacy was considered in relation to the richness and depth of participants' accounts and their relevance to the aim [38, 39].

Reflexivity and trustworthiness

The first author (AW) is a registered Children's and Young People's Nurse and a higher education lecturer with experience of teaching in Further Education. At the time of the study, she was a full-time Master's student in Public Health and health promotion. She has an interest in young people's mental health and wellbeing and had no prior relationship with the participants. Her professional background will have shaped what she noticed and how she interpreted what she heard, and reflexive journalling allowed her to examine her assumptions and their potential influence on interpretation. Credibility was supported through repeated checking of transcripts against recordings, contextual engagement with the setting, and an audit trail.

Some linked their experiences to future aspirations in animal care, agriculture or environmental work.

Accounts were not uniformly positive. One participant valued the friendships made at the farm but did not perceive a meaningful improvement in confidence: "Not really… I've made friends, but confidence? I don't think so." Participants also acknowledged that taking part did not remove difficulties relating to education, employment, mental health or family circumstances.

They more often described it as a source of stability, coping and encouragement than as a cure.

Nature as the context for wellbeing

The natural environment and animals were important parts of participants' experience, but participants rarely described nature exposure alone as responsible for wellbeing. The farm was valued as an outdoor setting that felt different from home, school or college. Participants described fresh air, open space and a sense of being less constrained. They frequently described animals as enjoyable and calming, while animal care combined emotional connection with responsibility and purposeful activity.

Outdoor work also involved physical activity, but participants did not generally frame exercise as the main objective. Digging, lifting, walking, gardening and maintenance were experienced as enjoyable because they formed part of meaningful work. Seasonal rhythms, animal births, planting, harvesting and ongoing projects also gave participants a sense of contributing to something beyond themselves.

Discussion

Mental health difficulties in young people are common, costly and tend to start early, yet we know little about how young people experience GSP or what makes it worth their time. We set out to explore how young people experienced taking part in a community farm programme in Wales and how they felt it influenced their mental health and wellbeing, so that what they value can inform the design and evaluation of GSP for their age group. We developed four interconnected themes: belonging in a supportive community; finding purpose through meaningful participation; personal growth and enhanced wellbeing; and nature as the context for wellbeing.

The main thing we take from these accounts is that the farm did not work through nature alone. What it appeared to do was bring three things together in one place: people who accepted participants and were glad to have them there; useful work that carried real responsibility and could be learned at their own pace; and time outdoors with animals and growing things. Participants rarely separated these. Belonging seemed to make it possible to try unfamiliar tasks. Responsibility and visible results built confidence and a sense of being useful.

These are features of what is often called eudaimonic wellbeing, which is concerned with purpose, growth and flourishing rather than simply feeling good in the moment [40, 41]. The outdoor setting and the animals gave the work its character and, for many, a welcome contrast with home, school or college. In terms of our objectives, participants had found the farm by informal routes (objective 1); they valued staff who treated them as individuals, peers who did not judge them and work that mattered (objective 2); they described gradual gains in mood, confidence and motivation rather than a cure (objective 3); and, taken together, their accounts suggest that GSP for young people may be best designed around belonging, meaningful roles and responsibility, with nature as the setting that makes these possible (objective 4).

Belonging as the foundation

Belonging appeared to provide the foundation for much of what followed. Participants repeatedly emphasised being accepted, valued and supported, and described relationships with staff and peers as central. This closely mirrors what adults at the same farm have said. In a service-user study of the farm's adult programme, participants described staff as welcoming and the farm as a kind of family, and a sense of belonging that in turn gave them purpose [21]. That young people and adults describe such similar processes suggests that these may be core features of the farm's approach rather than something specific to age. They also resonate with the few studies of young people in GSP-type provision: young care leavers in a socially prescribed gardening group described social belonging and a safe space [28], and young pregnant women valued nature-based activities partly for the social support they offered [27].

This also complements the wider GSP literature, which considers both environmental and social processes [15, 16, 31].

Self-determination theory offers one way to read these accounts. It proposes that wellbeing depends on feeling related to others, competent and autonomous [42], and participants' descriptions of acceptance (relatedness) and of mastering tasks and skills (competence) fit that picture. Others have used the same theory to understand adult social prescribing and found that trusting relationships with staff and peers and the chance to develop new skills were central [23]. Social identity may be a further route, since being part of a group with a shared purpose is thought to be one way social prescribing builds connection [22]. Seen through an ecological lens [43], the farm gave young people a setting in which relationships, roles and the physical environment reinforced one another, which may be why participants found them hard to pull apart.

Meaningful participation and what counts as an outcome

Meaningful participation was equally prominent. Participants valued practical work, responsibility, learning and visible contribution, and described these in terms of usefulness, purpose and competence rather than symptom reduction. Similar themes appear in adult accounts of this scheme, where skills and training created 'ripple effects' that reached into life beyond the farm [21]. Ripple effects of nature-based activity beyond the session itself have also been described in a surf-therapy programme for adults with acquired brain injury [44]. They also sit alongside wider evidence that volunteering is associated with lower depression and higher life satisfaction and wellbeing, although that evidence comes mainly from adults [45]. Some participants linked their time at the farm to future aspirations in animal care, agriculture or environmental work, which may matter particularly for young people at a stage when disengagement from education and work is a real risk [6].

This has a direct implication for evaluation. Conventional symptom measures may not capture belonging, confidence, purpose and meaningful contribution, yet these were the outcomes participants talked about [19, 21]. Evaluations of GSP for young people that rely only on symptom scales risk missing what young people value and, equally, what may be driving any change. Measures of belonging, self-efficacy and purpose, developed with young people, would be a sensible addition. Participants were also clear that the farm was not a cure. They described stability, coping and encouragement, and one participant did not notice a change in confidence at all. That fits with GSP as a complement to, rather than a replacement for, clinical care.

Volunteering or green social prescribing?

Because participants were volunteers, a reader may reasonably ask whether this is a study of volunteering rather than GSP. In the findings, we have written about taking part at the farm rather than about volunteering, to keep the focus on the experience rather than the role. But the overlap is real. GSP schemes often take the form of practical gardening, conservation or farm tasks [8, 9], and the farm's volunteering is the vehicle through which its mental health and wellbeing aims are pursued. The features young people valued, such as being needed, having responsibility and contributing to something visible, are also features commonly attributed to volunteering [45]. We therefore see volunteering less as a competing explanation than as one of the active ingredients of this kind of provision. It also raises a design question. A role that asks for responsibility and reliability may suit some young people better than others, and social prescribers in South Wales have described many of their clients as not yet ready for outdoor group activity [20]. Those who most need support may be the least ready to take it up.

Nature as an enabling context

These findings caution against treating 'nature-based' as synonymous with passive exposure to nature. Participants were actively caring for animals, gardening, maintaining the farm and working alongside other people, and nature appeared to function as an enabling context for occupation and relationships rather than as the active ingredient in itself. Adults at the same farm valued nature exposure and connection but placed them within a supportive community [21], and a qualitative study of a campus community garden found that connectedness to nature, growing food and community building were valued together [29]. Staff in child and adolescent mental health services have also suggested that shared outdoor activity can take the pressure off conversation for young people [5]. Participants did not describe it in these terms, but working side by side on shared tasks may have offered a lower-pressure way to build relationships than formal conversation, which would fit the importance they gave to belonging.

Our interpretation is compatible with psychological theories of restorative environments, in which natural settings support recovery from stress and mental fatigue [46, 47], and the calm, fresh air and open space that participants described are in keeping with those ideas. However, greenspace exposure and connection to nature have been linked to health outcomes mainly in observational work [48, 49], and our study did not measure nature connectedness or establish a mechanism of change.

Animal care was particularly notable. Participants described animals as enjoyable, calming and emotionally rewarding, and caring for them brought responsibility and practical competence.

Farm animals have been described as important to many care-farm participants, including young people with behavioural difficulties [50], yet human-animal interaction remains less prominent in the wider GSP evidence base. It may be a distinctive feature of community farms compared with other GSP settings and deserves attention in both design and research.

How young people reached the farm: self-referral and what counts as social prescribing

None of the participants had entered the farm through a formal GSP referral pathway. They had found it through family or friends, internet searches and employment support services. This does not establish that formal referral pathways are ineffective. Rather, it indicates that these young people reached a community asset through informal routes and experienced it as relevant to their wellbeing. It matters because whether self-referral 'counts' as social prescribing is contested. The Welsh national framework recognises self-referral as one route [10], but in a previous stakeholder study in South Wales people disagreed about whether schemes entered without a link worker or health professional should be called social prescribing at all [20]. Our findings add the perspective of young people who arrived this way: they found a place they experienced as supportive, and they did so without being referred.

At the same time, relying on informal discovery carries risks. The routes participants described depend on having family or friends who know about the farm, on being in contact with employment services, or on knowing what to search for. Young people without those connections may never find provision of this kind. The formal route may not currently fill the gap, because providers in South Wales reported receiving fewer referrals into nature-based schemes than they had anticipated [20]. In practice, this points towards offering several routes at once: self-referral that is easy for young people to find and use, referral from employment and education services where young people already are, and link-worker referral for those who need support to take a first step. Whether formal referral changes engagement or outcomes is a question for future research.

Equity and access

Community-based provision can potentially extend support beyond clinical services, but transport, awareness, socioeconomic circumstances and other barriers shape who takes part [51, 52]. Provision is also fragile. Third sector providers in South Wales have described dependence on short-term grant funding [20], and a mixed-methods study of GSP for young pregnant women reported insufficient funding and disappointing experiences with some programmes [27]. Relational continuity with staff appeared important in the adult study of this farm [21], so unstable funding may matter particularly for young people who need time to build trust. We did not recruit to examine inequality and cannot say how accessible the farm is to young people facing the greatest barriers.

Implications for practice, policy and evaluation

Taken together, these findings suggest four things for those designing GSP for young people. First, provision may work best when it is built around belonging, meaningful roles and real responsibility, delivered by staff who treat young people as individuals, with nature as the setting rather than the whole intervention. Second, 'nature-based' should not be read as passive exposure, and schemes that offer little opportunity to contribute or to work alongside others may not offer the same experience. Third, young people need routes in that they can find and use themselves as well as through referral, and these should be visible in the places they already go, including online and through employment support. Fourth, evaluation should include outcomes such as belonging, confidence and purpose, ideally developed with young people, alongside symptom measures.

Limitations

The findings come from a single community farm and twelve participants. The study aimed for depth rather than generalisation, so readers will need to judge how far the accounts apply to other settings with similar features. Reflexive thematic analysis does not rely on a fixed sample size or on saturation; the question is whether the data are rich enough to support the interpretation offered [38, 39], and qualitative studies of comparable scope exist, such as the study of six young care leavers in a community gardening group [28]. Studies at further sites, including rural and larger-scale provision, would clarify which features are transferable to other settings.

Participants were current attendees recruited through the farm manager. We therefore heard from young people who had stayed, who felt comfortable enough to take part, and who may have been inclined to speak well of a place they valued. We did not hear from young people who left, never settled, or could not access the farm, and their experiences may differ.

There was no comparison group, and we did not measure mental health outcomes, so perceived improvements cannot be interpreted as evidence of causal effectiveness.

Participants were not formally referred through a GSP pathway, so the study cannot consider referral processes or link-worker support, and the findings may not apply to young people who arrive by referral, who may differ in needs, readiness and expectations. We have described the farm as a form of GSP because the national framework recognises self-referral [10], but that framing is not universally accepted [20]. The study was also not designed to examine inequality, and we collected no data on socioeconomic circumstances, transport or other barriers. national framework recognises self-referral [10], but that framing is not universally accepted [20]. Transcripts and findings were not returned to participants. In reflexive thematic analysis, themes are the researcher's interpretation of patterns across the dataset rather than a verifiable account of what any one person said, so member checking is not treated as a test of validity [35, 53], although member reflections might have enriched the analysis [53]. Coding was undertaken by one researcher (AW) and discussed with the second author. Reflexive journalling and an audit trail were used to examine how her nursing and teaching background shaped interpretation, but a different analyst might have developed different themes [35, 36].

Future directions

Several questions follow from this study. How do young people discover and access prescribing that is being developed from adult accounts [21], future work could co-develop with young people outcome measures that capture belonging, confidence and purpose.

Conclusions

Young people in this study described community farm participation as supporting their mental health and wellbeing through interconnected experiences of belonging, meaningful participation, personal growth and engagement with nature and animals. Nature was valued as part of a wider social and occupational environment rather than as an isolated therapeutic exposure. For GSP to work for young people, provision may need to prioritise belonging, meaningful roles and responsibility, offer routes into provision that young people can find and use themselves as well as through referral, and be evaluated against outcomes that matter to them. Further research should examine whether these processes are sustained over time, how young people access community nature-based provision, and whether similar processes are present in other settings.
