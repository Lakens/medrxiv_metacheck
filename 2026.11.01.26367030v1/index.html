Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 9 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Introduction

In the revised Alzheimer's Association research and diagnostic framework, "clinical stage 2 -transitional decline" is recognized as a phase of Alzheimer's disease (AD), encompassing subtle cognitive or behavioral changes with minimal impact on daily functioning. 1 Among these manifestations are objectively detectable cognitive changes that do not meet criteria for mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or dementia, a phenotype broadly referred to in the literature as subtle objective cognitive impairment (SOCI). 2 SOCI is identified through standardized neuropsychological assessment and may occur with or without subjective cognitive complaints. 2 However, the specific criteria used to operationalize SOCI vary widely and include performance on individual tests or composite scores, longitudinal intra-individual decline, clinical ratings, and data-driven approaches. 2 Although these definitions converge on the detection of subtle cognitive changes before conventional MCI criteria are met, associations between SOCI and AD biomarkers have varied. [3] [4] [5] [6] This variability may reflect not only differences in SOCI definitions but also differences in the sensitivity of biomarkers and measurement approaches.

Among currently available biomarkers of core AD pathologies, tau positron emission tomography (tau-PET) is most closely associated with cognitive symptoms. 7 In clinically unimpaired (CU) individuals, greater tau-PET uptake has been associated with poorer performance across several cognitive domains. [8] [9] [10] [11] Understanding these early associations has become increasingly relevant as tau-PET is incorporated as an endpoint in preventive trials. 12 In this context, tracer differences in sensitivity could influence the correspondence between tau-PET and clinical outcomes, affecting the interpretation and comparability of biomarker findings across trials. However, it remains unclear whether newer, more sensitive secondgeneration tau-PET tracers, 13 and spatial extent metrics that may better reflect the clinical expression of AD, 14, 15 provide stronger associations with SOCI. Therefore, we compared the FDA-approved [¹ F]Flortaucipir and [¹ F]MK6240 head-to-head regarding their associations with subtle cognitive differences in preclinical AD. Specifically, we aimed to clarify how tau-PET tracer choice influences the characterization of the earliest measurable cognitive manifestations of AD. Secondarily, we investigated whether tau spatial extent provides information beyond conventional SUVR measures in capturing the tau-PET correlates of SOCI.

Methods

Participants

In this cross-sectional study, we assessed individuals diagnosed as CU from the HEAD study (https://head-study.info/), recruited across eight sites in the United States and Canada between November 2022 and March 2025. CU status was defined as not meeting standard diagnostic criteria for MCI or dementia due to AD or another cause, and having a global Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) score of 0. 13, 16, 17 Diagnostic ascertainment was performed by multidisciplinary teams of specialists in dementia, following comprehensive clinical examinations.

Inclusion criteria were age ≥50 years, an available study partner to assess functioning, ability, and willingness to undergo MRI and PET, and fluency in a coordinating-centre-approved language. 13 Exclusions were inability to consent, pregnancy/breastfeeding, and conditions or medications compromising procedural safety or tolerability. 13 No a priori sample-size calculation was performed because all participants meeting the inclusion criteria with complete, quality-controlled data for all study variables were included.

Imaging acquisition and processing

Participants underwent structural MRI, tau-PET imaging with both [¹ F]Flortaucipir and [¹ F]MK6240, and amyloid-β (Aβ) PET imaging (either [¹¹C]PiB, [¹ F]AZD4694, or [¹ F]Florbetaben, depending on site preference).

HEAD imaging protocols have been described previously. 13 Briefly, MRI was acquired on 3T scanners using ADNI-4-aligned protocols, 18 including high-resolution sagittal T1-weighted images obtained with three-dimensional accelerated MPRAGE or IRSPGR sequences. PET acquisition windows after intravenous bolus injection were 40-60 or 50-70 min for [¹¹C]PiB, 90-110 min for [¹ F]florbetaben, 50-70 min for [¹ F]AZD4694, 90-110 min for [¹ F]MK6240, and 80-100 min for [¹ F]flortaucipir. PET images were reconstructed into four 5-min frames using scanner-specific ordered-subset expectation maximization-based algorithms with standardized parameters and no smoothing, with corrections for attenuation, motion, dead time, radioactive decay, scatter, and random events.

Images were processed using a validated PET-MRI pipeline. 13 T1-weighted MRI was nonlinearly registered to the ICBM 2020 Nonlinear Symmetric template using Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs). 19 PET images were linearly coregistered to native-space T1-weighted MRI, then warped into ICBM 2020 stereotaxic space using the concatenated PET-to-T1 and T1-to-template transformations. T1-derived whole-brain masks excluded extracerebral voxels, and scanner-specific Gaussian smoothing yielded a uniform 8-mm full width at half maximum.

PET Image Quantification and Analysis

Tau-PET standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) images were derived using inferior cerebellar gray matter as the reference region. 13 Regional mean SUVR values were computed to quantify tau load. Using the same regions-of-interest (ROIs), we also derived the spatial extent of tauopathy (SEOT), defined as the proportion of voxels exceeding 2.5 standard deviations above the mean of a reference group of 27 CU young individuals (mean [SD] age 22.7 [1.8] years; 59% females) scanned head-to-head with both tau-PET tracers. 20 Tau-PET metrics were extracted from previously described medial temporal lobe (MTL) and neocortical ROIs representing successive phases of AD-related tau propagation (Supplementary Figures 1A-B). 14 The MTL mask included the transentorhinal, entorhinal, amygdala, parahippocampal gyrus, perirhinal, and hippocampus CA1. 14, 21 The neocortical mask consisted of the inferior, lateral, and superior temporal, inferior and superior parietal, supramarginal, insula, anterior and posterior cingulate, precuneus, cuneus, lateral occipital, pericalcarine, inferior and superior frontal, orbitofrontal, rostromedial frontal, paracentral, postcentral, and precentral regions. 14 We additionally examined the entorhinal cortex as an individual ROI because it is commonly considered among the earliest regions affected in tau-PET staging frameworks. 22 To mitigate off-target binding, 23, 24 cortical ROIs were eroded to reduce [¹ F]MK6240 meningeal spill-in, 24 and hippocampal analyses were restricted to CA1 to reduce [¹ F]flortaucipir choroid plexus contamination. 21, 23 Global Aβ SUVR was calculated in a neocortical composite using whole cerebellum as reference and converted to Centiloids (CL). 25 Aβ burden was categorized according to Amyloid Imaging to Prevent Alzheimer's Disease (AMYPAD)-recommended CL ranges as low (<10 CL; CL LOW ), intermediate (10-30 CL; CL INT ), or high (>30 CL; CL HIGH ). 26

SOCI psychometric staging

We classified participants into SOCI psychometric stages by adapting actuarial criteria for objectively defined subtle cognitive decline (obj-SCD) 29, 30 and MCI 31 to the UDSv3 battery. These criteria identify abnormal performance across memory, language, and attention/executive function. The original criteria were adapted using corresponding UDSv3 measures for each domain (Figure 1A): Craft Story delayed recall (paraphrase scoring) and Benson Figure delayed recall for memory; Animal Fluency and MINT for language; and TMT A and B for attention/executive function. Individual test performance was considered abnormal at ≥1 SD below demographically expected values using published UDSv3 norms accounting for age, sex, and education.

Participants were assigned hierarchically to four mutually exclusive SOCI stages based on abnormal scores within and across domains (Figure 1B). Those with two abnormal tests within one domain or one abnormal test in each of three domains met actuarial MCI criteria³¹ and were classified as SOCI-3. They were retained within the SOCI framework because they lacked a clinical MCI diagnosis based on medical assessment and CDR scores, consistent with previous pre-MCI neuropsychological definitions of SOCI. 2 Among remaining participants, those with one abnormal test in each of two domains met adapted obj-SCD criteria and were classified as SOCI-2. 29, 30 One abnormal test defined SOCI-1 (single-test impairment), and no abnormal tests defined SOCI-0 (no impairment).

Thomas et al. extended Edmonds' criteria to include word-list process scores, including intrusion errors and learning slope; 29, 30 these were unavailable in UDSv3 and omitted from SOCI-2 definition. The Functional Activities Questionnaire was excluded from SOCI staging, consistent with more recent operationalizations of actuarial criteria. 2 We also explored a simplified SOCI definition using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA). 32 Consistent with previous research, performance >1 z score below age-, sex-, and education-adjusted expectations from UDSv3 norms was considered subtly abnormal. 33

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted in R version 4.4.1. ROI-based associations between tau-PET SUVR and cognitive performance were evaluated using separate linear regression models for each cognitive domain, tracer, and ROI (MTL and neocortex). Cognitive scores and tau-PET measures were z-standardized using the full study sample. Models were adjusted for age, sex, and education, with additional models further adjusted for CL. In secondary analyses, those models were repeated using tau-PET SEOT in place of SUVR as the main predictor. False discovery rate (FDR) correction was performed using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure separately for each tau-PET metric-cognitive outcome pair, with each family comprising four tests (2 tau-PET tracers × 2 ROIs). Sensitivity analyses were conducted using the entorhinal as the ROI.

Effect-size differences were evaluated using 5,000 paired participant-level bootstrap resamples of 297 participants sampled with replacement. Models used identical resampled participants, outcomes, and covariates; standardized coefficients were recalculated to obtain paired tracer ([¹ F]MK6240 minus [¹ F]flortaucipir) and metric (SEOT minus SUVR) differences.

We further explored SUVR-cognition associations within CL-defined subgroups using linear regressions adjusting only for demographic covariates. FDR correction was applied separately for each tau-PET ROI-cognitive domain pair across the three CL groups and two tau-PET tracers, yielding six tests per correction family.

Voxel-based linear regression analyses were performed in R (RMINC package), with tau-PET as the dependent variable and cognitive measures as the independent variables, adjusting for age, sex, and education. Secondary models further adjusted for CL. Statistical significance was determined using an FDR p<0.05. Analyses were restricted to a mask encompassing all Braak regions defined in the CerebrA atlas (Supplementary Figure 1C). 34 Differences in regional tau-PET measures across SOCI stages were evaluated using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) adjusted for age, sex, and education. Secondary models were additionally adjusted for CL. Estimated marginal means (EMM) were compared using pairwise t-test with Tukey adjustment.

Voxelwise differences in tau-PET across SOCI stages were assessed using linear regression in RMINC, adjusting for the same covariates. SOCI stages were modelled as a four-level categorical variable, with SOCI-0 as the reference and statistical significance defined at FDR p<0.05.

Results

We studied 297 participants with a mean (SD) age of 67.0 (8.4) years, of whom 179 (60.3%) were female (Table 1). Overall, 146 (49.2%) participants were classified as SOCI-0, 94 (31.6%) as SOCI-1, 37 (12.5%) as SOCI-2, and 20 (6.7%) as SOCI-3.

The groups did not differ in age, sex, education, or CL values (all p>0.05). MoCA scores differed across groups (p=0.0002); compared with SOCI-0 participants, scores were lower in the SOCI-2 and SOCI-3 groups, and the SOCI-3 group had lower scores than the SOCI-1 group (Holm-adjusted p<0.05).

ROI-based associations between tau-PET SUVR and cognitive composites

After adjustment for age, sex, and education, greater [¹⁸F]MK6240 SUVR was associated with poorer memory in the MTL (β=-0.15, 95% CI -0.27 to -0.03, p FDR =0.04) and neocortex (β=-0.13, 95% CI -0.25 to -0.02, p FDR =0.04), whereas [¹ F]flortaucipir SUVR was not (Figure 2A-B; Supplementary Table 1). Neither tracer showed significant associations with language, attention, or executive function. Paired bootstrap comparisons showed nominally more negative standardized MTL SUVR coefficients for [¹ F]MK6240 than [¹ F]flortaucipir for memory (p=0.046) and language (p=0.03), with no other nominal differences; none survived FDR correction (Figure 2C). After CL adjustment, [¹ F]MK6240-memory associations attenuated, and no SUVR-cognition associations were significant (Supplementary Table 2).

In sensitivity analyses using the entorhinal as the ROI, greater [¹⁸F]MK6240 SUVR was associated with poorer memory (β=-0.11, 95% CI -0.20 to -0.02, pFDR=0.03), whereas [¹⁸F]flortaucipir SUVR was not; neither tracer showed significant associations with other domains (Supplementary Table 3). CL adjustment attenuated the entorhinal [¹⁸F]MK6240-memory association (β=-0.06, 95% CI -0.16 to 0.04, p=0.22, p FDR =0.45) (Supplementary Table 4).

ROI-based associations between tau-PET SEOT and cognitive composites

After adjustment for age, sex, and education, greater SEOT was associated with poorer memory for MTL [¹⁸F]MK6240 (β=-0.16, 95% CI -0.28 to -0.04, p FDR =0.01), neocortical [¹⁸F]MK6240 (β=-0.18, 95% CI -0.29 to -0.07, p FDR =0.003), MTL [¹⁸F]flortaucipir (β=-0.16, 95% CI -0.28 to -0.04, p FDR =0.01), and neocortical [¹⁸F]flortaucipir (β=-0.19, 95% CI -0.31 to -0.08, p FDR =0.003; Figure 3A-B; Supplementary Table 5).

Within tracers, SEOT coefficients were more negative than SUVR coefficients for [¹ F]MK6240 MTL-attention (p FDR =0.03) and [¹ F]flortaucipir-memory associations in the MTL and neocortex (both p FDR =0.02; Supplementary Figure 2). Differences nominally favoured SEOT for [¹ F]MK6240 MTL-executive function (p=0.049, p FDR =0.20) and neocortex-attention associations (p=0.04, p FDR =0.08); no other differences were significant.

After CL adjustment, the neocortical SEOT-memory association remained significant for [¹⁸F]flortaucipir (β=-0.15, 95% CI -0.27 to -0.04, p FDR =0.03) but was only nominally significant for [¹⁸F]MK6240 (β=-0.13, 95% CI -0.25 to -0.01, p=0.03, p FDR =0.07; Supplementary Table 6). MTL SEOT-executive associations remained nominally significant for both tracers (both p FDR =0.077). No other associations were significant.

Tau-PET Associations With Cognition Emerge at Intermediate and High Amyloid-β Burden

In exploratory CL-stratified analyses, higher MTL SUVR was nominally associated with poorer memory in CL INT for [¹⁸F]flortaucipir (β=-0.38, 95% CI -0.67 to -0.08, p=0.01) and [¹⁸F]MK6240 (β=-0.34, 95% CI -0.64 to -0.04, p=0.03; Figure 3). Neocortical SUVR was nominally associated with poorer executive performance for both tracers in CL INT and poorer memory for [¹ F]MK6240 in CL HIGH . No associations survived FDR correction or were nominally significant in CL LOW.

[¹F]MK6240 and [¹F]Flortaucipir show differential topographic voxelwise associations with cognitive performance

After adjustment for age, sex, and education, voxelwise memory associations were more extensive for [¹ F]MK6240 than [¹ F]flortaucipir, encompassing 17.1% versus 0.7% of Braak-region voxels (Figure 4A-B). [¹ F]MK6240 showed broader neocortical clusters and additional MTL clusters, whereas no MTL clusters were observed for [¹ F]flortaucipir, consistent with the ROI-based findings. Only [¹ F]flortaucipir showed significant attention associations, mainly in a small bilateral occipital cluster (0.3% of voxels). Neither tracer showed significant language or executive associations, consistent with ROI-based SUVR findings.

After Aβ adjustment, only the [¹ F]flortaucipir-attention clusters remained significant (0.3% of voxels); neither tracer showed significant memory, language, or executive associations.

Discussion

This cross-sectional, head-to-head study examined associations of [¹ F]flortaucipir and [¹ F]MK6240 measures with subtle objective cognitive deficits in CU individuals. Both tracers showed modest cognitive associations, but [¹ F]MK6240 demonstrated more spatially extensive tau-SOCI associations and more frequent significant differences in uptake between SOCI stages. SEOT complemented SUVR through additional and stronger cognitive associations and more frequent significant differences between SOCI stages. These findings extend evidence that tau-PET captures subtle cognitive variation early in AD, potentially informing participant characterisation and outcome assessment in preventive trials. 12 Our results align with cross-sectional evidence linking greater tau-PET burden to poorer cognitive performance in CU individuals. Much of this literature has focused on memory, with poorer performance associated with greater MTL tau burden, [8] [9] [10] a pattern also observed in our study. Beyond MTL associations, we found that poorer memory performance was also associated with greater neocortical tau burden, consistent with evidence from across the broader AD continuum. 35 Emerging evidence further links subtle domain-specific deficits to distinct spatial patterns of tau accumulation in preclinical AD. 11, 14 Our nominal association between poorer executive function and greater neocortical SEOT is consistent with links between non-amnestic deficits and broader neocortical tau accumulation in CU individuals. 11, 14 Conversely, voxelwise [¹ F]flortaucipir uptake was associated with attention in a small superficial occipital cluster. Its proximity to the meninges and skull warrants caution because extracerebral spill-in may explain the association rather than cortical tau. 36, 37 Taken together, our findings reinforce the broader tau-PET literature by showing convergent associations between tau-PET uptake and subtle, domain-specific cognitive performance across two tracers, while also revealing potentially tracer-specific association patterns.

Beyond tracer-related differences, tau-cognition associations also varied across the preclinical AD continuum. No significant associations emerged in CL LOW , suggesting that associations at higher Aβ levels may preferentially reflect AD-related tau pathology. Consistent with this interpretation, MTL tau-PET signal in Aβ-CU individuals has been associated with age but not age-adjusted objective cognition.

Tau-cognition associations involved both MTL and neocortical ROIs in CL INT but only neocortical tau in CL HIGH . Although none survived FDR correction, this pattern suggests that regional tau correlates of SOCI may differ by Aβ burden. Neocortical associations in CL INT and CL HIGH may reflect Aβ-associated tau spread beyond the MTL, consistent with longitudinal evidence that antecedent Aβ accumulation predicts subsequent tau accumulation, which relates to cognitive decline in CU individuals. 10 Beyond supporting AD progression models, these findings may inform preventive trials stratifying CU participants by Aβ burden, such as AHEAD 3-45. 12 Although grouping strategies may differ across trials, our results suggest that regional tau-PET correlates of subtle cognitive variation may vary across Aβ-defined populations.

Nevertheless, no subgroup associations survived FDR correction, so these findings remain hypothesis-generating and require validation in larger longitudinal samples.

Although both tracers captured SOCI, only [¹ F]MK6240 showed significant SUVR-based ROI associations with cognitive composites. It also showed broader voxelwise associations with memory, often affected early in typical AD, 39 and more extensive voxelwise and regional uptake differences across SOCI groups. These findings align with [¹ F]MK6240's greater affinity and sensitivity for tau neurofibrillary tangles (NFT) 40 and head-to-head evidence suggesting earlier detection of biologically meaningful tau burden than [¹ F]flortaucipir. 13 Neuropathological reports also indicate limited [¹ F]flortaucipir sensitivity to low-density MTL tau, particularly without advanced Aβ pathology. 41 These characteristics may explain broader [¹ F]MK6240 associations with subtle clinical variation here and with other transitional decline features, including mild behavioral impairment. 42 Despite more frequent significant associations with [¹ F]MK6240, between-tracer ROI effect-size differences did not survive FDR correction. Restricted tau and cognitive ranges in this CU sample may have limited detection of these differences. Broader [¹ F]MK6240 associations therefore do not establish superior clinical performance; implications for individual-level detection, prognosis, and clinical decisions remain uncertain in preclinical AD.

Longitudinal studies must determine whether these cross-sectional differences confer meaningful advantages in detecting and predicting the earliest cognitive manifestations of tau pathology.

Beyond tracer comparisons, we examined whether tau-PET metrics influenced SOCI associations within tracers. Both metrics captured cognitive associations, with SEOT performing at least comparably to SUVR and identifying additional significant associations in selected analyses. Notably, higher neocortical SEOT in participants with mildly impaired MoCA scores accords with evidence that MoCA declines as tau extends into the neocortex, 43 suggesting that SEOT may capture the earliest cognitively detectable phase of this spread. Supporting this interpretation, extent-based measures capture greater interindividual variability at low tau levels and may better reflect early regional tau propagation in AD. 14 Although SEOT advantages were not consistent across cognitive outcomes, selected associations were stronger and no formal comparison favoured SUVR. Adding SEOT may therefore modestly improve sensitivity to early cognitive differences. This aligns with evidence that tau extent and load differ in their associations with downstream cognitive and neurodegenerative changes, providing potentially complementary information across the AD continuum. 15, 44 Selective advantages may reflect the limited tau burden and cognitive variation expected in CU individuals.

Whether this additional sensitivity benefits participant enrichment or targetengagement assessment in preventive trials remains uncertain, although these applications have been proposed. 45 Beyond continuous cognitive measures, we examined tau-PET differences across SOCI stages adapted from validated actuarial obj-SCD and MCI criteria. [29] [30] [31] Tau-PET burden was comparable across SOCI-0, SOCI-1, and SOCI-2 but higher in SOCI-3 across several measures. These findings provide biomarker support for Jak/Bondi actuarial MCI criteria, complementing evidence of improved diagnostic precision, stronger AD biomarker associations, fewer false positives, and better identification of individuals at risk of progression. 46, 47 Importantly, all participants had global CDR=0 and were classified as CU after extensive evaluation by dementia specialists. Greater tau-PET burden in those meeting actuarial MCI criteria therefore suggests that these criteria may detect biologically meaningful cognitive changes before clinically apparent impairment.

By contrast, SOCI-2 participants (Edmonds/Thomas-like actuarial obj-SCD) did not differ in tau-PET burden from SOCI-0 or SOCI-1. This aligns with reports of no cross-sectional plasma phosphorylated tau 181 or Aβ-PET differences between actuarial obj-SCD and non-impaired controls, 3,4 but contrasts with higher Braak III-IV [¹ F]flortaucipir SUVR reported in actuarial obj-SCD versus controls without obj-SCD. 5 That study applied Thomas and colleagues' extension of Edmonds' criteria, 5, 30 using different neuropsychological tests and memory-process indices unavailable in UDSv3. Even within the same domain, tests differ in cognitive demands and regional tau-PET associations; substitutions may therefore affect both classification and the tau-related cognitive changes captured by each framework. 48 Moreover, crosssectional assessment may limit sensitivity to transitional cognitive change, as SOCI definitions incorporating within-person decline have shown tau-PET associations.

Nevertheless, longitudinal measures do not necessarily yield stronger associations with tau pathology. 49 In a neuropathological study of individuals who remained CU near death, Braak NFT stage alone was not associated with antemortem longitudinal trajectories in episodic memory, executive function, or global cognition. 50 Thus, longitudinal ascertainment may therefore improve SOCI construct validity without ensuring direct correspondence with tau burden. These findings highlight the need to compare heterogeneous SOCI definitions 2 for biomarker and prognostic validity.

Extending criteria to broader batteries and validating alternative neuropsychological measures may clarify which psychometric definitions best characterize SOCI within Alzheimer's Association clinical stage 2. 1 Study strengths include a unique, substantial sample of well-characterized CU individuals imaged with two widely used tau-PET tracers. However, without goldstandard neuropathological evaluation, we cannot directly establish which tracer more accurately reflects biologically relevant NFT pathology. The multicenter design reduces single-site dependence, but exclusively North American recruitment warrants replication in more diverse populations and settings to establish generalizability. Another limitation is the cross-sectional operationalization of the SOCI. Because longitudinal data from the HEAD cohort are not yet extensively available, we could not incorporate intra-individual longitudinal cognitive change, as adopted in other work 49 and in the Alzheimer's Association workgroup framework. 1 Moreover, although SOCI criteria were adapted from validated approaches, [29] [30] [31] battery differences prevented exact implementation and may have influenced classification and biomarker associations. Despite these limitations, our findings extend biomarker characterization of a widely used SOCI operationalization.
