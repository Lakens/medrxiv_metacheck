Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Abstract

Background: Workload is increasing in general practice. Large language models (LLMs) are a promising tool that could relieve part of this workload. However, existing studies offer little insight into how professionals and patients want specific LLM applications to be implemented in practice.

Objectives: This study aimed to prioritize a broad range of LLM applications in general practice and identify the benefits, risks, and requirements of each application.

We conducted semi-structured one-on-one interviews with 12 general practitioners (GPs), 7 GP assistants, 6 practice nurses, and 6 patient representatives in the Netherlands between October and December 2025. To guide the interviews, we developed a list of 21 LLM applications derived from literature, existing tools, and GP input. Application were divided into 5 categories: administrative tasks, pre-consultation and triage, clinical decision support, management of (chronic) diseases, and patient interaction and communication. Participants discussed and prioritized each application. Transcripts were analyzed with a combined deductive and inductive approach.

Results: Priorities for LLM applications differed by group: GPs favored clinical decision support, assistants favored triage, and practice nurses and patient representatives favored chronic disease management. The most prioritized applications were summarizing consultations and triage and urgency classification (high priority 18/31; 58%), followed by clarification of medical information around the consultation (14/31; 45%). The qualitative analysis revealed three recurring considerations when applying LLMs for different tasks. First, participants questioned whether an LLM is needed, as simpler or existing tools may suffice. Second, they questioned whether LLMs relieve workload or create extra work. Third, a possible benefit on workload has to explicitly be weighed against risks such as mistakes, reduced personal care and accessibility, and negative effects on professionals.

Conclusions: Professionals and patients see potential for LLM support across many areas of general practice. Introduction

General practice is under increasing pressure [1]. In high-income countries, consultation rates and durations have risen steadily over recent years [2] [3] [4]. As a result, most general practitioners (GPs) experience a high workload; in the Netherlands, this was the case in 78% of practices in 2024 [5]. These high demands are associated with higher rates of emotional exhaustion [6], and negatively impact wellbeing of GPs [7]. Moreover, high workload and low wellbeing among physicians are associated with reduced quality and safety of care, and with poorer patient communication, adherence, and satisfaction [7] [8] [9] [10] [11].

Digital tools have the potential to play an important role in reducing pressure within general practices. Artificial intelligence (AI), especially large language models (LLMs), show promise in addressing common challenges such as administrative burden [12]. Recent studies have explored a wide range of LLM applications in general practice [13, 14]. Three main applications areas emerged: administrative support, clinical decision support, and patient support. Specific examples include automatically generating consultation notes [15], automatic clinical history taking [16], or translating medical information into accessible language for patients [17].

Several studies have investigated attitudes of GPs towards AI and LLMs. Most focus on AI broadly [18] [19] [20] [21], while recent work addresses LLMs specifically [22, 23]. These studies address high-level themes on perceived benefits, risks, and implementation requirements. Possible benefits include reducing administrative work [21, 22] and supporting clinical decisions [19, 20, 23]. Concerns include accountability [18, 19, 22], threats to patientpractitioner relationships [20, 22], privacy [18, 23], accuracy and clinical reliability [18, 19, 21, 22], and deskilling [18, 19, 23]. Implementation requirements include integration into existing electronic health record (EHR) workflows [23], human oversight [22], and adequate clinician training [22]. A small number of studies have examined how future AI or LLM applications are prioritized in general practices [24, 25]. Participants in these studies saw value in both clinical (e.g., diagnostic support) and non-clinical applications (e.g., automated consultation notes) but generally favored non-clinical applications.

However, existing research offers limited guidance for implementing LLM tools in general practice for two reasons. First, most studies address broad, overarching themes rather than attitudes towards specific applications. As outlined above, LLMs applications in general practice span diverse applications [13, 14], each with its own trade-offs. Theme-level findings therefore offer little basis for deciding which tools to implement and how. Second, most studies include only GPs, overlooking other members of the practice team (e.g. GP assistants) as well as patients. Since these stakeholders are likely to be direct or indirect users of LLMs tools, their perspectives are needed for user-informed integration of LLMs into general practice [22].

To address these gaps, we conducted a semi-structured interview study with GPs, assistants, practice nurses, and patients in the Netherlands. Our aim was two-fold. First, we prioritized LLM applications across a broad range of general practice applications. Second, we analyzed the underlying stakeholder preferences and motivations at an application-specific level. These findings can inform future researchers and developers, enabling userdriven integration of LLMs within general practice.

Methods

Study design

This qualitative study examined LLM applications in general practice through semi-structured one-on-one interviews. We adopted a pragmatic, design-oriented approach aimed at generating insights to inform the development of LLM-based tools. This study was embedded in a broader public-private research project on LLM implementation in general practice, involving Erasmus Medical Centre (EMC), Technical University Delft (TU Delft) and Juvoly powered by Tandem Health (Dutch medical AI company).

Study setting

The study was conducted in general practices across the Netherlands between October 2025 and December 2025. In the Netherlands, GPs act as gatekeepers and the first point of contact for patients. They solve the vast majority of health complaints, referring only 6.7% of contacts to secondary care, while accounting for around 5% of national healthcare expenditure [26]. General practice care is mainly delivered by three professionals: GPs (huisarts), the main care providers; practice nurses (praktijkondersteuners), who focus on chronic and mental health care; (GP) assistants (doktersassistenten), the first point of contact for patients, responsible for triage and minor clinical procedures.

Participants and recruitment

This study included two main participant groups. The first group consisted of general practice professionals: GPs, practice nurses and assistants. These would be the primary (professional) users of LLMs applications in Dutch general practices. The second group consisted of patient representatives, reflecting the perspective of patients as both users and recipients of LLM-supported care.

General practice professionals were recruited through the professional networks of the authors and national professional federations. We purposively oversampled GPs because, as the main care providers, they are the only stakeholders involved in all general practice areas. Patient representatives were recruited from regional general practice patient councils and from the Dutch Patient Federation. The latter had completed an AI training course provided by the federation. We selected representatives with either general practice or AI experience to ensure they could meaningfully engage with the hypothetical applications discussed.

Interview materials and procedure

To guide the interview, we developed a list of 21 LLM applications in Dutch general practice. This predefined list allowed us to compare insights across interviews and elicit views on applications participants might not have raised spontaneously. We developed the application list from three sources: the literature on LLMs in general practice and other healthcare specialties, applications already available in practice, and suggestions from early conversations with GPs. We rephrased or merged applications to reduce overlap and ensure a similar level of abstraction. To enhance accessibility, we grouped the 21 applications into five categories spanning different areas of general practice. Finally, one GP reviewed the list for completeness and relevance. The full list is provided in Textbox 1.

The interviews consisted of two parts. In the first part, we discussed all 21 applications. In the second part, participants completed a prioritization exercise. They first selected any number of applications they considered high priority, followed by a top 3 selection from these high-priority applications. A more detailed interview protocol and guide is provided in Supplementary Files 1 and 2.

Data collection

Prior to the interview, the following baseline characteristics were collected: age, gender, years of experience (general practice professionals only), self-reported LLM knowledge, and frequency of LLM use. Interviews were conducted face-to-face or via Microsoft Teams and lasted 45-60 minutes. During the interviews, applications were presented via printed cards (face-to-face) or a Miro board (online). All interviews were audio-recorded and automatically transcribed using Microsoft Teams. Transcripts were subsequently reviewed and manually corrected against the audio recording. During this process, all identifying information was removed from the transcript.

Qualitative data analysis

The first author coded the interviews in MAXQDA (version 24.9.1), combining a deductive and an inductive approach. Deductively, each quote was assigned to the application it was related to. Within each application, quotes were structured into three subcodes: benefits (expected advantages of adoption), risks (potential negative consequences of adoption), and requirements (conditions for adoption). Where participants described multiple distinct functionalities within an application, we additionally double-coded the relevant quotes under a fourth subcode: functionalities, allowing sub-analysis per functionality. Inductively, quotes within each subcode were analyzed using thematic analysis across two coding cycles [27]. In the first cycle, we applied themeing the data [27] to summarize the content of each quote. In the second cycle, we applied pattern coding [27] to group similar codes and identify broader patterns

To ensure coding quality, the last author (RvL) was involved at multiple stages of the analysis as a second coder. First, the coding approach was developed in collaboration with RvL. Following the inductive coding cycles, RvL reviewed and validated the resulting code tree. To assess coding consistency, RvL independently coded one interview using the final code tree. Any discrepancies were discussed until consensus was reached.

Researcher reflexivity

Interviews were conducted by the first author, a PhD candidate with a background in econometrics and no prior relationship with participants. The researcher's training is more technically than clinically oriented, which may have shaped how interviews were conducted and analyzed. To broaden the interpretive perspective, both the interview design and the analysis were reviewed by the wider research team. This team incorporated design engineering, clinical (general practice), and industry backgrounds. Industry partners involved in the project had no role in data collection, analysis, or interpretation.

Ethical considerations

The Human Research Ethics Committee of TU Delft approved this study on September 25, 2025 (application number 5969). Prior to inclusion, all participants provided written informed consent. All interview data were pseudonymized and stored on a secure server accessible only to the research team. Participants received remuneration of €100 (GPs) or €50 (other participants), reflecting differences in typical hourly income.

Application of LLM support

For each application, we summarized the main benefits, risks, and requirements for its adoption identified by participants. Where participants suggested multiple functionalities within an application, we report these separately if their benefits, risks, and requirements differed. A. Administrative tasks A.1. Summarizing consultations

The primary benefits of LLMs for summarizing consultations were workload reduction and more attention for the patient during consultations. Professionals felt that summaries by LLMs were more complete and consistent than manually written notes, improving information transfer among professionals.

However, LLM use could negatively affect workflows. Automated summarization may eliminate the clinical reasoning that occurs during manual note writing. Adopting the new workflow would require verbalizing findings and the physical examination and manually copy-pasting summaries into the EHR when direct integration is unavailable.

Data safety and privacy were also raised as concerns. Patient representatives emphasized that explicit patient permission for recording was required, although explaining the tool to patients could be time-consuming.

Participants also raised concerns about the quality of the summaries. Quality could be negatively affected by inclusion of irrelevant non-consultation information, omission of important non-verbal cues, or hallucinations. To mitigate these risks, participants requested automatic filtering of irrelevant information, support for multiple languages and dialects, and model training on real patient consultations. Human validation was seen as a crucial safeguard for summary quality.

A.2. Summarizing patient records

Benefits of LLM support for summarizing patient records were time reduction for professionals and easier information transfer to third parties such as insurers. Participants, mostly GPs, thought that a better patient overview would lead to more informed medical decisions, improving quality of care. This was seen as particularly helpful for locum or substitute professionals.

However, for regular GPs with their own patient population, summaries were seen as less valuable. Moreover, as current EHRs already provide a patient history through coded episodes, some participants raised doubts about the added value of LLM support. Participants also raised concerns about hallucinations in LLM generated summaries.

Functional requirements were mostly addressed by GPs. They required summaries to be fast and have an adjustable period (e.g., the last x months). The application should retrieve information from multiple sources, including legacy data, medical letters, and external hospital data. GPs also envisioned combining it with speech-to-text software to enable realtime patient information retrieval during consultations.

A.3. Processing medical letters

The primary benefit of LLM support for processing medical letters was time reduction, predominantly raised by GPs. Professionals also anticipated missing fewer follow-up actions from incoming letters. One GP primarily valued this application as groundwork for future digitalization efforts that rely on specialist information, such as patient record summarization (Application A.2). However, several participants questioned the added value of LLMs, as specialist letters often already include a conclusion, and some practices store letters in full regardless.

Participants raised two main concerns: missing important correspondence and reduced engagement with patients due to automation. To mitigate these risks, they suggested an alert system for important letters combined with human validation. Professionals further noted standardization as a practical challenge, as each practice handles correspondence differently. To accommodate this, they requested customizable options such as adjustable summary length.

A.4. Writing referral letters

Participants primarily valued LLMs for making referral letter writing more efficient. LLMs could also ease the overall referral process, for example by helping select the right care provider. However, participants questioned what LLMs would add beyond the digital referral platform already used in the Netherlands. Some would welcome LLM-based extensions of this platform, while others noted that such improvements would not necessarily require LLMs.

Views on quality were mixed. Some professionals expected LLMs to improve referral letter quality, others questioned whether an LLM could accurately capture the referral question.

One practice nurse noted that LLMs cannot capture the patient's subjective context, which is important in some referrals, such as those to mental health care.

To address these concerns, participants outlined several requirements. The GP should retain responsibility for the referral reason, with the LLM handling remaining content. EHR integration was considered essential for retrieving relevant patient information. Generated letters should be reviewed by the professional. Patient representatives proposed an additional patient review step.

A.5. Organizing inbox and correspondence

GPs and assistants, who handle most correspondence, expected time savings as the main benefit. This was particularly relevant for processing overloaded inboxes during busy periods, such as after weekends and holidays. They also anticipated more timely care, as it could prevent delays in handling urgent messages.

However, professionals questioned the added value for three reasons. First, time savings were seen as limited, particularly for smaller practices. Second, all patient information must be processed in the EHR regardless, limiting efficiency gain. Third, existing EHR functionality already supports classification of urgent messages. Professionals raised two additional implementation concerns: the difficulty of standardizing across practices and the risk of urgency misclassification by the model.

As a functional requirement, one GP suggested the automatic scheduling of follow-up tasks arising from incoming correspondence, such as booking lab tests requested in a specialist letter.

A.6. Coding of consultations

Most insights into coding were addressed by GPs, who hold the main responsibility for this application. Within this application, several subtasks emerged. First, LLMs could assist with linking uncoupled consultation notes or episodes, reducing backlog. This was seen as particularly useful when migrating patient data from another practice. Second, LLMs could support coding of incoming medical letters or new consultations, reducing manual labor. Third, LLMs could support re-coding, correcting wrongly coded entries. Fourth, LLMs could group related codes together, reducing redundant or overlapping codes and improving professionals' overview of patient information. Across these subtasks, the primary benefits were improved EHR coding quality and reduced time spent on coding.

However, participants raised several concerns about added value. Many felt that coding is currently not time-consuming, or that existing support is already sufficient. Participants questioned whether algorithmic approaches other than LLMs might be better suited, noting that current LLM coding performance is still lagging. As a result, human validation would remain necessary, potentially limiting the overall efficiency gain.

Some GPs also envisioned extending the current coding methodology, which focuses on diagnosis only. They suggested using an LLM to automatically code the full consultation content, including medical history and physical examination, capturing clinically relevant but currently unstructured information. This could yield richer and more searchable patient data, supporting applications such as summarizing patient records (application A2). These codes could also enable more retrospective and prospective clinical analysis, such as identifying risks for chronic diseases (application D1) However, participants raised concerns about limited transparency and the risk of unintended recording of confidential information.

Participants noted that current EHR functionality would need to be extended to support and use this extended coding system.

B. Pre-consultation and Triage B1. Triage and urgency classification

Participants distinguished two forms of LLM support for triage: one facing the assistant and one facing the patient.

For assistant-facing support, two subtasks emerged, both relying on speech-to-text transcription of the triage call. First, LLMs could reduce administrative workload by automating triage call documentation, similar to summarizing consultations (application A1). Second, LLMs could improve triage quality through real-time conversation support, for example by suggesting triage questions or urgency levels. Several requirements were raised. The application should connect to telephone services and integrate both patient data and national triage guidelines. It should be able to handle unstructured conversations, such as patients presenting multiple complaints, and offering multilingual support. Where needed, assistants should be able to override LLM's recommendations, for instance to adjust urgency level. The main concerns were that this support would not resolve the assistant's shortage and might yield only limited time savings.

Patient-facing support was seen as having a greater impact on work demand. Suggested workflows were a dynamic triage chatbot or questionnaire completed before contacting the practice. Through increased adoption of digital triage, participants envisioned fewer patient contacts, shorter waiting times, and improved accessibility. This could reduce the assistant staffing shortage. However, several strong concerns were raised. First, LLMs lack human intuition and "gut feeling" critical to triage quality. Second, patient resistance could limit adoption. Third, digital tools may reduce accessibility for patients with low digital literacy or socioeconomic status. Fourth, GPs highlighted clinical safety risks, foreseeing accuracy limitations and misjudgment of urgency. For safe implementation, professionals suggested routing difficult or urgent cases to assistants through traditional channels such as the phone.

Another suggestion was limiting the tool to structuring the patient's question without giving advice, leaving completed cases for a GP or assistant to process.

Overall, participants favored a cautious, gradual roll-out rather than rapid adoption. They preferred LLMs to first support assistants before taking on more autonomous triage. They also stressed that any patient-facing tool should follow the triage process assistants already use by phone rather than introduce a new logic.

B2. Self-care advice after triage

Patients can use public self-care services before contacting the GP. Participants wanted more patient requests to be handled through these services to reduce demand in the practice, but were not specific about how LLMs could contribute. Assistants also give selfcare advice by phone after triage, often drawing on the same public sources. An LLM connected to the phone line could generate this advice automatically, tailoring information from public self-care sources to the patient using triage conversation and patient data. Some participants felt this could make giving self-care advice more efficient, improve its quality, and make it more evidence-based and consistent across providers. However, one GP questioned the added value over simply referring to the public self-care sources. Participants warned that the LLM might cite incorrect sources, so it should draw only on high-quality selfcare information. The generated advice should also be validated by a professional before it is sent to the patient.

Several concerns applied to digital self-care in general. Patient representatives noted that limited digital literacy could restrict patient adoption. To improve accessibility, GPs suggested offering multiple communication channels, such as spoken rather than written text. GPs also raised the financial consequences of non-billable self-care pathways and uncertain legal responsibility.

B3. Consultation preparation: patient-focused intake

Participants noted that LLMs could improve patient preparedness by helping patients structure their care question, understand care options, and provide a more complete preconsultation history. This could support patient autonomy and shared decision making. In addition, it could lead to shorter consultations by reducing time spent on medical history. Professionals also foresaw fewer consultations due to earlier redirection of unsuitable care questions and fewer follow-up visits because of missing preparatory information.

However, professionals addressed two potential negative impacts on consultations: the risk of diagnostic tunnel vision and asking patients the same question twice. Automating part of medical history was also seen as potentially reducing job satisfaction. GPs proposed several functional requirements: EHR integration, combining consult preparation data with triage information, and GDPR-compliant data transfer from patient to practice.

Participants also raised several patient-related barriers. Adoption could be limited by resistance to pre-consultation instructions or limited digital literacy. Participants also questioned whether all patients could accurately report their complaints digitally. Offering multiple input formats, such as speech, was suggested to mitigate these risks.

C. Clinical decision support C1. Interpreting test and lab results

LLM support for interpreting test and lab results was valued by GPs for its potential to improve clinical decision-making. It could support them by expanding clinical knowledge and identifying appropriate follow-up actions. GPs also saw potential to improve patients' understanding of diagnostic results through patient-friendly messages. Overall, most participants anticipated a reduction in workload through LLM support.

However, GPs questioned the added value of LLM support, as routine test interpretation does not require specialized AI assistance. Some GPs viewed LLM involvement as a threat to clinical autonomy, potentially further limiting adoption. Additional concerns included overreliance on automated interpretations and ambiguity about legal responsibility.

Professionals highlighted specific risks around quality. Low quality or missing patient data and defensive model behavior could degrade results. Patient-specific nuances and edge cases near cut-off values were seen as particularly hard for LLMs to interpret. Human validation was seen as essential by all participants.

Key input data requirements included clear cut-off values, the clinical indication for the test, patient context, and historical results. Professionals preferred integration of LLM support directly within the EHR inbox, where results are received.

C2. Medication management and prescribing

Professionals valued deprescribing support to improve medication oversight and to reduce unnecessary medication use. They also suggested support for polypharmacy and medication reviews in collaboration with pharmacy. LLMs could reduce the time these require or increase the capacity to carry them out.

Professionals said that prescription support by LLMs could improve overall quality of prescribing by preventing dosing errors and signaling missed treatment opportunities. They also expected time savings. Suggested capabilities included medication conversion and substitution support, interaction alerts, repeat-prescription automation, health insurance reimbursement checks, and medication misuse monitoring (e.g. for benzodiazepines). However, they also questioned the added value of LLMs, as current EHRs already provide built-in prescription assistance.

Participants emphasized that LLM prescription support should be based on up-to-date medical guideline information. Another suggested prerequisite was the availability of an upto-date medication overview. However, participants noted that this is difficult to maintain, as medication information is not always shared consistently between healthcare providers.

One GP was skeptical about an LLM's ability to give accurate medication suggestions using its internal knowledge. Instead, a tool-calling, agent-based system was suggested, in which the LLM uses external structured medicine information to create recommendations.

C3. Support with treatment plan and next steps

LLMs could support treatment planning in several ways. Participants primarily valued more efficient guidelines and protocol searching, and the flagging of blind spots or missed actions. GPs thought this could reduce specialist referrals, as they could resolve more questions themselves. Doctor assistants similarly expected to become more self-sufficient, relying less on the GP. Extending this application, professionals suggested automating follow-up steps, such as ordering labs or enrolling patients in external (self-)care programs.

Several concerns were raised. One GP worried that increased screen use might annoy patients and suggested being transparent about using an LLM support system. One practice nurse stressed that patient-specific context is crucial, making LLM suggested treatment plans hard to standardize. Another practice nurse feared deskilling of new GPs who become overly dependent on the system's clinical support. To guard against this and preserve the human aspect of care, several participants emphasized the importance of clinician override and autonomy.

C4. Support with differential diagnosis

Participants thought LLMs could support critical thinking for differential diagnosis and difficult cases. GPs and assistants expected improved diagnostic accuracy. Professionals also noted that differential diagnosis support could serve as the foundation for other applications, such as support with treatment plan and next steps (task C3).

However, differential support could disrupt the GP workflow. One GP noted that general practice constantly involves decision-making under uncertainty, limiting the value of detailed suggestions. Others feared constant self-doubt, and that reviewing suggestions would add work. To contain this, a doctor assistant suggested limiting the diagnostic scope rather than overloading GPs with possibilities.

Overreliance was another concern, making clinician override and autonomy an important prerequisite. Individual GPs also questioned the added value of LLMs over existing algorithms and raised the variability of LLM output as a concern. For successful implementation, they stressed that both patient information and guidelines should be integrated.

C5. Support during consultations

Professionals valued live consultation support for offering critical-thinking assistance and reducing clinician blind spots. They also thought it could improve history-taking, for example by prompting clinicians to ask about specific alarm symptoms. One GP added that LLMs could suggest or automate standard question formats, such as risk-score frameworks for conditions like gout. Another GP felt it could support professional development and job satisfaction, but doubted colleagues more resistant to change would accept it at all.

The main concern was that it could draw attention away from the patient, although one GP imagined it reshaping consultation style: by reviewing LLM recommendations together with the patient, it could strengthen shared decision-making. This would, of course, require openness with patients about LLM use.

For the system to work well, GPs noted it would need integration of the patient record, differential diagnoses, and treatment guidelines. Overall, participants stressed that clinicians should always be able to override or ignore recommendations. D. Management of (chronic) diseases D1. Identifying risks of (chronic) diseases Professionals felt LLMs could reduce workload in preventive care, for example by automating screening such as drafting patient lists of risk groups. More broadly, it could expand preventive care capacity, currently limited by time constraints. Participants felt it could improve care quality by detecting overlooked patients or enabling more timely care.

The main undesired consequence, raised solely by GPs, was that it could create more work for the practice. One practice nurse stressed that the practice would need more budget to finance additional care. Another GP said the tool should not be too broad but focused on specific care paths. GPs described two contrasting workflows: one in which the tool runs periodically outside the consultation, and another during the consultation. The first GP argued it should not disrupt consultation flow, while the second argued that placing it outside the consultation would lead to preventive care being overlooked.

Some professionals questioned the added value of LLMs over current risk-screening tools or questionnaires. Participants noted this is not inherently an LLM application, and that rulebased algorithms might be better suited. They added that using LLMs here would require very high-quality, granular patient data to enable text-based risk screening.

D2. Patient follow-up and monitoring

Within this application, three main application areas emerged. First, LLMs could support follow-up and action reminders. Participants envisioned a chatbot checking in with patients after consultations. For example, reminding them of follow-up actions or treatment. Patient representatives thought this might improve medication management and adherence. Doctor assistants thought it could increase patients' sense of being cared for. The main risks raised by both professionals and patients concerned patient interaction: low digital literacy, low engagement, and the risk of overburdening could all limit adoption. To mitigate these, participants suggested personalization, patient-friendly design, and the option to opt out. Professionals also proposed a task-scheduling function to reduce missed follow-up actions on their side.

Second, recalling and managing chronic patients was a frequently raised challenge. Professionals felt LLMs could address this by automating the (re)calling process. Many highlighted time savings, alongside reducing patients lost to follow-up. One GP noted, however, that simple digitalization without an LLM would suffice here. A further concern was loss of patient oversight through automation. To mitigate this, professionals suggested an alert system for patients unresponsive to recalls. They added that an online recalling system should be reserved for patients with sufficient digital skills, while other patients should be called directly.

Third, LLMs could support patient self-measurement systems by analyzing and filtering measurements. Patients felt this could make sharing information with the practice easier. Professionals thought it could reduce live consultations for GPs and practice nurses. However, GPs and patients were concerned it would be hard to fit into the current workflow and could increase workload. They also thought patient adoption might be low. For implementation, data should flow automatically into the EHR, possibly via wearables. To manage volume, participants proposed that the system should flag only measurements requiring attention. One GP noted that self-monitoring should only be available to stable patients, and that personal contact should be preserved.

D3. Support for chronic care

Because chronic care is heavily protocolized, LLMs could enable more digital care by automating (part of) a consultation. Participants felt that this could reduce overall workload and free up consultation time for relational care. However, professionals feared this time saving might be redirected to other tasks rather than personal contact, leaving patients with increasingly digital care. They were concerned about a weakened patient-provider connection and the loss of personal non-medical information. They also noted that digital chronic care might lower patients' motivation for treatment and lifestyle adherence. Moreover, limited digital literacy among chronic patients could limit adoption of digital care pathways.

Relying on speech-to-text technology, LLMs could support professionals, especially practicenurses, during or after chronic care consultations. Related to consultation summarization (application A1), professionals suggested automating the recording of standard measurements required by chronic care protocols. Similar to support during consultations (application C5), LLMs could offer care suggestions based on chronic care guidelines, reducing search time and improving guideline adherence. One GP noted that patient information should be incorporated to allow personalized recommendations. However, practice nurses raised two concerns. First, individual patient complexity would limit such automated support. Second, protocols are already visible in the EHR, reducing the added value of an LLM.

E. Patient interaction and communication E1. Clarification of medical information around consultation

Both professionals and patients felt LLMs could make medical information more understandable for patients. Mentioned information sources included specialist letters, patient files, and consultations. Patient-friendly consultation summaries were suggested most often. Overall, they felt this support could improve the patient-provider relationship and reduce workload through fewer follow-up calls and visits. GPs also envisioned LLM support for answering e-consultation messages, saving time while improving answer quality. Beyond patient support, assistants mentioned direct benefits for themselves, for example in translating complex specialist letters into more understandable language.

Professionals particularly valued support for vulnerable groups such as low-literate patients, the elderly, non-native speakers, or those hesitant to ask questions during consultations. At the same time, they worried that limited digital skills could hinder adoption among these patient groups. To mitigate this, professionals and patients suggested multiple information formats (e.g. speech) and delivery channels (e.g. text message, email, patient portal). They also valued multilingual support. GPs were divided on responsibility for the tool: some felt it should lie with the GP, others with the patient. If the GP is responsible, one noted that patient-friendly summaries would need GP review, doubling the validation burden alongside the professional consultation summary. Under a patient-led approach, another GP worried that two parallel patient files could emerge (one for the patient, one for the GP), doubling information sources.

E2. Providing medical explanation and education

Participants discussed using LLMs to provide medical information through a general health chatbot interface. They felt this could improve patient preparation, self-care, and selfmanagement before consultations. Participants emphasized it could raise the overall quality of medical information patients encounter, by offering an alternative to unvalidated online sources. One GP valued that LLMs could provide reliable information in real time, as patients often want immediate answers at any time of day or week. Professionals also thought LLMs could support patients after consultations by providing general health information, for example about their diagnosis. This was seen as especially useful for onboarding new chronic patients. Overall, professionals envisioned this support saving time through reduced patient contact.

Participants raised several patient-related concerns. Adoption might be limited by low digital literacy, doubts about credibility, preference for human explanation, or mismatched language level. As requirements, patient representatives suggested adjustable language level, references to sources, high-quality information inputs, multiple formats (e.g. speech or visual), and multilingual capabilities.

Views on organizing the tool in practice were mixed. Some professionals preferred a central initiative outside GP workflow, while others wanted to retain control themselves. Some GPs were concerned about unclear ownership. They feared that promoting a public tool would lead to patients interpreting its answers as direct advice from them, or that an external tool might use their practice label without their oversight.

E3. Improving professional patient communication

Participants thought LLMs could improve professional-patient communication before, during, and after consultations. This support could be generic, or patient-specific by drawing on a live consultation transcript. Before consultations, participants noted LLMs could help prepare personalized communication strategies and support with difficult conversations. During consultations, the most frequently raised use case was support with motivational interviewing. After consultations, participants valued LLM-supported feedback, for example by analyzing difficult conversations or questioning style. They appreciated that such feedback was available on demand and preferred it over self-review or peer-review because it felt less confrontational. Participants noted this feedback could add value in educational settings for junior staff, as well as for senior staff through recurrent performance evaluation.

However, participants were uncertain whether LLMs could adequately assess communication quality. They felt LLMs could not detect subtle cues or could overlook situational context. Participants also anticipated reluctance among professionals to adopt an LLM-powered feedback system, especially during consultations. To increase adoption, one GP suggested linking the tool to accreditation credits as a form of post-qualification training.

E4. Self-management for diseases and lifestyle

Both practice nurses and GPs felt that LLMs could improve existing self-management programs, through increased personalization and accessibility based on patient-specific information. Professionals thought that improved self-management programs could increase patient autonomy and lifestyle awareness, freeing up clinician time for more urgent patient care.

However, assistants and patient representatives were concerned about low patient engagement and the digital literacy of target groups such as chronic patients. They also raised safety risks for serious issues such as mental health, though one GP felt these tools could be a safer alternative to unsupervised commercial platforms.

Participants were divided on how such a tool should be organized. Most preferred a central initiative outside the GP workflow, while one GP favored a tool linked directly to the practice to increase patient engagement.

Discussion

Prioritization of LLM support

This interview study offers insights into 21 LLM applications across 5 categories in Dutch general practice. Through structured prioritization, we identified promising candidates for further development. The two highest-priority applications were summarizing consultations and triage support. Both were also prioritized in earlier research [24, 25] and are already widely adopted in practice [28, 29]. However, current digital triage applications are mostly rule-based or use traditional machine learning [30]. LLMs could enable new applications, such as conversational triage chatbots or real-time decision support during triage calls. The third most prioritized application, clarification of medical information around the consultation, was not prioritized in prior research. Participants particularly valued a patient-friendly summary of the consultation, extending automated notetaking from professionals to patients.

Our study extends prior LLM prioritization research [25] by capturing the priorities of practice nurses, GP assistants, and patient representatives alongside those of GPs. Although interest was shared across categories and stakeholder groups, participants often prioritized applications tied to their own role (e.g., assistants favored triage). Both practice nurses and patient representatives prioritized chronic disease management, reflecting a need to meet rising chronic care workload and patient demand [31, 32]. Among GPs, clinical decision support received high priority. This differs from an earlier survey, in which fewer GPs considered LLMs useful for clinical applications (such as differential diagnosis) than for nonclinical applications (such as consultation notes) [25]. Overall, our prioritization shows that interest in LLMs spans multiple areas of general practice, in line with their reported potential [14].

Application of LLM support

Although prioritization showed broad interest in LLMs, our qualitative analysis revealed three important considerations for their implementation: whether an LLM is needed, whether it relieves workload, and how its benefits weigh against its risks.

Assessing the need for LLMs

Not every application requires an LLM, and several factors influence whether an LLM is needed. First, the added value of an LLM differs by application: some workflows are already efficient and cannot be optimized much further. For example, participants said that consultation coding is currently not very time-consuming, so LLM support would add little value. Second, LLMs are not always the right technology: simpler or deterministic digital tools could support many workflows equally well. Participants frequently raised recalling and managing chronic patients as a challenge, yet this could be addressed through digitalization without LLMs. Third, existing systems may already suffice. For example, in the Netherlands, there is already a platform that streamlines referrals between providers, reducing the need for LLM-written referral letters. Taken together, these factors suggest that applying LLMs too broadly risks creating overly complex or unnecessary digital solutions. LLMs should therefore be treated as a possible solution, not a universal remedy. This view aligns with earlier findings that primary care stakeholders see AI as "just another tool in the toolbox", valued for its contribution rather than the technology itself [18]. Our application-level analysis offers a starting point for identifying where LLMs are the right tool.

Establishing workload relief

If an LLM is the right tool, the main benefit participants expected was workload reduction. However, participants also pointed out that an LLM intervention may generate work, thereby limiting these gains. This aligns with previous research suggesting that digital health interventions can increase a clinicians' burden rather than relieve it [33] [34] [35]. The concern was raised especially for clinical support applications, such as help with management plans or differential diagnosis, which may produce additional suggestions the professional has to review or act on. Patient-facing applications, such as a tool for sharing self-measurements with the practice, may similarly raise care demand or be difficult to fit into practice workflows.

Establishing whether an LLM actually relieves workload is an essential step before implementation.

Weighing benefits against risks

Even when an LLM is needed and relieves workload, its implementation carries risks that need to be weighed against the benefits. We highlight three main risks raised by participants.

The first risk is mistakes. LLMs are well known to hallucinate [36]. A human validation step may be needed to safeguard quality; for example, with ambient scribes, clinicians routinely revise the generated summaries to correct inaccuracies [37]. This human check costs time and thus limits the potential time savings of LLMs. Moreover, it is unclear what level or type of errors are acceptable in the first place. Medical professionals also make mistakes, yet it is not obvious that we should tolerate the same from an LLM [38]. Application design should consider the level and type of errors we are willing to correct or accept.

The second risk is reduced personal care and accessibility. Participants repeatedly stressed that digitalization through LLMs might reduce personal care. Personal care is an important driver of care quality and patient satisfaction in general practice [39]. Although participants noted that the freed-up time could be redirected to relational care, it is likely that the time will be used for other tasks given the high overall workload. Related, participants frequently mentioned decreased accessibility as a risk. Because the accessibility of digital tools in general practices depends on patient characteristics such as health and digital literacy [40], increased digitalization might exclude vulnerable patients groups. Application design should consider how to preserve personal contact and accessibility while retaining the benefits of digitalization.

The third risk is negative effects on professionals. In line with previous research, participants mentioned overreliance [41], automation bias [41], and deskilling [42] as concerns. Participants also noted that LLM support may conflict with core values of general practice, such as decision-making under uncertainty and gatekeeping. LLM might add uncertainty, create false certainty, or misalign with the values of the treating physician or healthcare context [43]. Application design should consider how to align with the professional autonomy, skills, and values of general practice professionals.

Implications

The risks of LLMs call for design choices, which interact with each other and with the expected benefits. For instance, assistant-led triage with LLM suggestions may reduce mistakes and retains personal contact, but limits time savings and may invite automation bias. By contrast, a fully automated chatbot maximizes efficiency but risks undetected errors, less personal contact, and lower accessibility. Rather than pointing to a single best design, these choices form a set of trade-offs that must be weighed. The design should be selected through co-creation with professionals and patients, who can judge which trade-offs are acceptable.

The relevant stakeholders depend on the application. Patient-facing tools should involve the vulnerable patient groups most at risk of being disadvantaged by digital technology. Broader implementation questions may require input from stakeholders beyond our sample, such as EHR vendors, regulators, and insurers. Finally, validation studies typically evaluate a single design. We suggest instead comparing multiple design variants within a single validation study, so that both the workload and the risk trade-offs identified here can be analyzed directly.

Strengths and Limitations

The strengths of our study are threefold. First, we introduce a new overview of LLM applications covering a substantial portion of general practice workflow, grounded in literature and validated with participants. Second, we included multiple stakeholder groups, adding the perspectives of patients and non-GP professionals. Third, we conducted an indepth application-level analysis to shape the priorities, going beyond a ranked list or higherlevel thematic analysis.

However, some limitations should be addressed. First, our sample may be subject to selection bias. We did not purposively sample for diversity in LLM use or professional experience. Although we included participants both with and without LLM experience, most showed an affinity for the topic. This was to some extent necessary, since envisioning future applications requires some familiarity, but it may have made the insights less critical. Second, participants reasoned about applications they had not used. Our findings therefore reflect expectations rather than evaluations. As a result, harder-to-imagine applications may have been undervalued. Moreover, as LLM tools develop rapidly, priorities may shift as applications mature or become adopted in practice. Third, although our fixed application list enabled structured comparison of prioritization and qualitative insights, we may have missed other high-impact applications. We mitigated this by validating the list with professionals beforehand. Fourth, the sample was small for a quantitative prioritization, particularly within stakeholder groups (n=6-12). Our prioritization results are therefore descriptive and sensitive to individual votes and should be interpreted alongside the qualitative findings.

Conclusions

GPs, assistants, practice nurses and patient representatives showed interest in LLM support across many areas of general practice. However, realizing LLM support requires three steps. First, the need for the LLM should be critically assessed against other alternatives that may be better suited. Second, it should be established whether the LLM actually relieves workload. Third, the application should be designed so its benefits outweigh the risks of mistakes, reduced personal care and accessibility, and negative effects on professionals.

Our application-level analysis and prioritization offer a starting point for this assessment and further design.
