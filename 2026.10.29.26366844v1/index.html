Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. Abstract

Background: Being not in education, employment or training (NEET) has far-reaching consequences for both health and economic outcomes. Identifying what contributes to, and can predict, becoming NEET is therefore necessary to mitigate negative downstream outcomes. A single measure of whether a young person is NEET can conceal differences in when disengagement occurs, whether it recurs, and what the antecedents are. Methods: We examined childhood educational, social and health characteristics in relation to three dimensions of later NEET: 1) number of occasions of NEET, 2) age at first observed NEET, and 3) single, sustained or recurrent (repeated with interruptions) patterns, using up to 11 repeated assessments between ages 16 and 33 in the Avon Longitudinal Study of Parents and Children (ALSPAC; imputed analyses, N=8,130). Negative binomial and multinomial models compared minimally, sociodemographically and mutually adjusted associations.

Results: After mutual adjustment, higher attainment at age 10-11 (Key Stage 2), number of childhood traumas up to age 17, persistent patterns of childhood conduct problems and adolescent depression remained associated with accumulated, sustained and recurrent NEET (e.g. attainment incidence rate ratio: 0.80, 95% CI: 0.75-0.85). NEET antecedents differed by age at first NEET: the widest range of factors predicted onset before 21, only depression predicted onset at 21-24, and onset at 25-33 was dominated by sex, with women twice as likely to experience being NEET. Family-disadvantage associations attenuated after mutual adjustment; complete-case results were consistent.

Conclusion: Conditional associations do not establish causal effects, mediation or individual predictive accuracy. However, educational, social and health needs overlap across different patterns of NEET participation, and these findings highlight the need for early educational and family support, integrated mental-health and employment services, and continuing support after re-entry.

What is already known on this topic NEET (not in education, employment or training) status is associated with childhood disadvantage and mental-health difficulties, but most evidence captures NEET at a single time point, potentially obscuring the relationship between antecedents with timing and recurrence.

What this study adds Using 11 repeated assessments across two decades of a birth cohort born in 1991/2, this study shows that childhood attainment, trauma, conduct problems and adolescent depression are associated with accumulated, sustained and recurrent NEET, and that the association between sex and NEET reverses direction after age 25.

How this study might affect research, practice or policy These findings support multi-agency approaches to NEET prevention that combine educational and mental-health support before school leaving with continuing support after NEET recurrence, rather than relying on single-point interventions.

Introduction

An estimated 1,012,000 UK 16-24-year-olds were not in education, employment or training (NEET) between January-March 2026 1 . NEET status is not only a labour-market indicator, but also a marker for current and future health and wellbeing. A systematic review and metaanalysis of 24 studies found that young people who are NEET have substantially higher odds of mental ill-health (odds ratio 1.28, 95% confidence interval 1.06 to 1.54), substance use problems (odds ratio 1.43, 95% confidence interval 1.08 to 1.89) and markedly elevated suicidality (odds ratio 2.84, 95% confidence interval 2.04 to 3.95) than their peers 2 , making NEET status a public-health concern.

Framing NEET status as a societal concern is not confined to the mental-health evidence.

Reduced educational and occupational engagement in adolescence is itself patterned by the structural inequalities that drive health inequalities across the UK more broadly, so that NEET status can be both a marker and a further driver of unequal life chances across multiple public-health domains, from mental health to health behaviours and long-term physical health 3 . Youth unemployment and disengagement have also been shown to leave a lasting 'scarring' effect on mental health and subsequent labour-market outcomes that persists years after a young person re-enters education or employment, suggesting that the consequences of a NEET episode are not necessarily confined to its duration 4.

NEET status is nevertheless conventionally measured as a single ever/never at one timepoint count, but NEET status encompasses experiences that plausibly differ in cause and consequence. An early critique of the NEET indicator, based on interviews with young people and caseworkers, identified three such groups: brief transitional gaps between stages of education or training; young parents who chose to prioritise caring for their children; and young people with multiple compounding risks such as homelessness, offending, or emotional and behavioural difficulties 5 . Collapsing these into a single headline rate obscures this heterogeneity and can push services towards reactive, target-driven responses that leave some of the highest-need young people, such as young parents, with comparatively little support 3 . The same conflation applies at the individual level. A single ever/never measure averages over differences in when these experiences begin and how often they recur. Distinguishing the timing and repetition of NEET, not only its occurrence, may therefore matter for identifying who needs support and when.

The evidence base nevertheless remains dominated by research that captures NEET status at a single wave rather than its timing or recurrence. A global scoping review of 159 studies identified substantial variation in how NEET and its associated characteristics are measured 6 . Materials and Methods

Pregnant women resident in Avon, UK with expected dates of delivery between 1st April 1991 and 31st December 1992 were invited to take part in the study [15] [16] [17] [18] . 20,248 pregnancies have been identified as being eligible and the initial number of pregnancies enrolled was 14,541. Of the initial pregnancies, there was a total of 14,676 foetuses, resulting in 14,062 live births and 13,988 children who were alive at 1 year of age. When the oldest children were approximately 7 years of age, an attempt was made to bolster the initial sample with eligible cases who had failed to join the study originally, adding a further 906 pregnancies not in the initial sample (913 additional children enrolled). The total sample size for analyses using any data collected after the age of seven is therefore 15,447 pregnancies, resulting in 15,658 foetuses. Of these 14,901 children were alive at 1 year of age. From the age of 30, there were an additional 101 new participants recruited from 101 pregnancies during Phase V enrolment. The total sample size for analyses using any data collected after the age of 30 is therefore 15,548 pregnancies, resulting in 15,690 foetuses. Of these 15,002 children were alive at 1 year of age. The present imputed analyses included 8,130 participants, with 5,270 in the secondary polygenic-score analyses.

ALSPAC study data were collected and managed using REDCap electronic data capture tools hosted at the University of Bristol 19 . REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) is a secure, web-based software platform designed to support data capture for research studies.

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the ALSPAC Ethics and Law Committee and the Local Research Ethics Committees. Informed consent for the use of all data collected was obtained from participants following the recommendations of the ALSPAC Ethics and Law Committee at the time. The completion of a questionnaire, either on paper or online, was considered to be written consent from participants to use their data for research purposes. For the majority of tests undertaken during face-to-face visits, verbal consent was obtained from participants (both parents and children as appropriate) prior to the start of any data collection; however, some tests required the completion of a written consent form.

Participants can contact the study team at any time to retrospectively withdraw consent for their data to be used. Study participation is voluntary and during all data collection sweeps, information was provided on the intended use of data. Please note that the study website contains details of all the data that is available through a fully searchable data dictionary and variable search tool: http://www.bristol.ac.uk/alspac/researchers/our-data/.

Measures

The outcome, NEET was self-reported at up to 11 assessments between ages 16 and 33.

We examined the number of observed NEET occasions (0-11), age at first observed NEET (16-20, 21-24 or 25-33), and pattern of observed NEET (single occasion, sustained across consecutive assessments, or recurrent across non-consecutive assessments; participants meeting criteria for both sustained and recurrent patterns were classified as recurrent), as per previous research19. Never-observed NEET was the reference category for multinomial models. These outcomes describe observed participation histories rather than exact durations of unemployment or inactivity. Further information on the derivation of the NEET variables is available in the supplement.

Exposures comprised of sex assigned at birth, ethnicity, maternal education and social class, all assessed at birth (gender identity was not assessed in ALSPAC and could not be examined separately), counts of maternal-depression (measured via the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale 21 on 8 occasions between child ages of 32 weeks' gestation and 8 years) and childhood financial-problems (assessed on 5 occasions between child ages of 32 weeks gestation and 7 years 22 ), childhood physical illness count up to age 16, number of childhood traumas up to age 17 23 , standardised Key Stage 2 attainment (a routine assessment in the UK at age 10-11), and counts of childhood conduct-problems and hyperactivity, assessed using the Strength and Difficulties Questionnaire 24 on 5 occasions between child ages 6.5 and 16 years, and adolescent-depression symptom occasions, assessed using the Short Mood and Feelings Questionnaire 25 on 4 occasions between child ages 10 and 16 years. We classified maternal education as A level or above (post-16 qualifications), O level (secondary-school qualification, typically taken at age 16), or below O level. A standardised educational-attainment polygenic score, derived from a large genomewide association study of educational attainment, was examined separately 26.

Statistical analysis

Negative binomial models estimated incidence rate ratios (IRRs) for accumulated NEET occasions. Multinomial logistic models estimated relative risk ratios (RRRs) for first-observed age category and pattern category (sustained or recurrent), each relative to never-observed NEET. These models included the exposure of interest and were adjusted for the number of completed NEET assessments; when the exposure was polygenic risk score for educational attainment, the model additionally included sex, ten ancestry principal components. Although sometimes described as unadjusted, these are minimally adjusted models. Sociodemographic models included sex, ethnicity, maternal education and social class, alongside assessment count. Fully adjusted models mutually included all non-genetic exposures alongside the exposure of interest and assessment count.

. The minimally adjusted models were interpreted as overall observed associations conditional on assessment count. The second models assessed associations also conditional on measured sociodemographic background. The final models assessed associations conditional on the broader measured educational, adversity, and health profile. We emphasised the final models when describing overlap between these domains, while retaining all adjustment stages to make attenuation visible. No stage was interpreted as identifying a causal effect, and changes between stages were not treated as a means of quantifying the extent of confounding or mediation. Multinomial relative risk ratios compare the probability ratio of an outcome category to the reference category across exposure values; they are not hazard ratios.

Missing data were addressed using multiple imputation by chained-equations with 50 imputed datasets, and estimates were combined using Rubin's rules 27 . Only antecedents and covariates were imputed, NEET status was not. To account for multiple testing across antecedents, p-values were corrected within each outcome using the false discovery rate (FDR) method, and associations are described as FDR-corrected unless stated otherwise.

Inference emphasised estimates and confidence intervals rather than solely based on statistical significance. Results across categories were described without claiming formal statistical differences between coefficients.

.

Results

Of the 8,130 participants, 20.5% (n=1,669) were observed as NEET on at least one occasion, with 3.5% (n=287) having 2 NEET occasions and 2.4% (n=198) having 3 or more.

The number of NEET occasions ranged between 0 and 10 (mean=0.31). Of these, 9.8% first became NEET between ages 16-20, 7.6% between 21-24, and 3.9% between 25-33. By pattern, 14.6% experienced a single NEET occasion, 3.9% sustained NEET, and 2.1% recurrent NEET.

Which childhood factors are associated with accumulated NEET occasions? Almost all early-life antecedents predicted a higher count of NEET occasions between ages 16 and 33 years in unadjusted models (Figure 1). The largest associations were for adolescent depressive symptoms (IRR 1.43, 95% CI 1.33-1.53), child and adolescent conduct problems (1.36, 1.29-1.43), low maternal education at birth (1.62, 1.40-1.86) and the number of childhood traumas (1.31, 1.26-1.37), with higher KS2 attainment associated with fewer NEET occasions (0.69, 0.65-0.73). A higher educational attainment polygenic score was also associated with fewer NEET occasions (0.89, 0.84-0.95). These estimates were similar when adjusted for sociodemographic characteristics. In the fully adjusted model, most family and social antecedents attenuated towards the null, including maternal depression (1.00, 0.96-1.04), maternal social class (0.92, 0.81-1.06) and financial problems (1.04, 1.00-1.08). Which factors are associated with sustained and recurrent NEET?

A similar pattern emerged across NEET types (Figure 3). Single-occasion NEET, the most common form, was weakly predicted overall: only childhood trauma ( 1.10, 1.03-1.17) and depressive symptoms (1.17, 1.06-1.28) survived adjustment and correction. Sustained NEET showed by far the strongest associations, with higher KS2 attainment the antecedent showing the strongest association (0.65, 0.56-0.75) alongside childhood trauma (1.31, 1.18-1.45), conduct problems (1.27, 1.12-1.45) and depressive symptoms (1.38, 1.20-1.60). Recurrent NEET was predicted by the same set at more modest magnitude: trauma (1.23, 1.06-1.42), conduct problems (1.28, 1.06-1.54) and depressive symptoms (1.34, 1.11-1.63) -with a large but imprecise association for ethnic minorities (2.08, 1.17-3.72) that fell marginally outside the corrected threshold. The polygenic score was associated with recurrent NEET (0.79, 0.64-0.96) but not with single-occasion NEET (0.95, 0.88-1.03). Sensitivity analysis restricted to complete cases

A complete-case analysis restricted to the 4,688 participants with fully observed exposure and covariate data produced fully adjusted estimates closely consistent with the imputeddata results above for all three outcomes (Supplementary Tables S2, S4, S6). Point estimates typically differed by 0.01-0.05 on the ratio scale, confidence intervals overlapped substantially, and no imputed-analysis association reversed direction. The largest difference was for conduct-problem occasions and first observed NEET at ages 25-33 (imputed 1.00, 0.86-1.17; complete case 0.77, 0.58-1.02). Restricting to complete cases therefore did not change which antecedents had associations with NEET.

Across all three sets of models, the same four antecedents recurred: childhood attainment, childhood trauma, conduct problems and adolescent depressive symptoms. Social and economic antecedents such as maternal education, social class, financial hardship and maternal depression were strongly associated with NEET in unadjusted models.

Discussion

We found that childhood attainment, trauma, conduct problems and adolescent depression were associated with accumulated occasions and with both sustained and recurrent NEET after mutual adjustment.

Age at first NEET showed distinct antecedent profiles across age bands, including a sex difference in risk that emerged only from age 25 onwards. The polygenic score predicted recurrence rather than single occasions of NEET. This pattern is consistent with the score indexing more persistent risk rather than any single NEET episode. The polygenic score estimates do not demonstrate a direct genetic effect or validate individual prediction. Together, these findings highlight the need to address early educational and health needs, the circumstances surrounding later disengagement, and support after reentry. It also reinforces the value of following NEET across adolescence and adulthood, enabling a more nuanced analysis of what antecedents predict accumulation, onset, recurrence and sustained occasions. The attenuation of family associations should not be interpreted as evidence that NEET is primarily an individual skills or mental-health problem. The first models describe how disadvantage clusters with NEET, whereas the mutually adjusted models condition on characteristics that may themselves reflect earlier social circumstances. A smaller coefficient can result from shared causes, adjustment for intermediate variables, measurement differences or correlated exposures. Without a causal model and appropriate identification assumptions, these changes cannot quantify mediation or isolate direct effects, a distinction sometimes summarised as the Table 2 fallacy 14 . The persistence of associations with low maternal education and financial difficulty further cautions against dismissing material circumstances.

This interpretation complements Gladwell and colleagues' analysis of the same age range, which found that cognitive ability accumulated across compulsory schooling was the dominant predictor of NEET status 10 . They tested childhood and family factorssocioeconomic status, aspirations, locus of control and risky behaviour-which were associated with NEET indirectly, through their effect on ability formation, rather than directly.

They found school engagement and the local youth unemployment rate were the main exceptions, with additional direct associations. Our childhood attainment associations extend across sustained and recurrent patterns. However, attainment is a marker of accumulated learning opportunities and circumstances as well as performance. This leaves out the broader skills acquisition accumulated during school, such as non-cognitive skills 28, 29 . Our results do not establish that raising test scores alone would prevent NEET; rather, as Our findings contrast with Pitkänen et al., whose Finnish register-based study of 99,137 young people found that parental socioeconomic resources largely accounted for associations between adverse childhood experiences and NEET at age 18 9 . Adjustment for parental education and household income attenuated the odds ratio for parental psychiatric hospitalisation from 1.86 to a non-significant 0.96, and for parental substance-abuse hospitalisation from 2.09 to 1.05, whereas low parental education remained strongly associated (odds ratio 2.94) after full adjustment 9 . Trauma remained associated with accumulated occasions and repeated NEET patterns here. Differences in adversity measures, adjustment sets, national context, institutional culture and follow-up windows may account for this divergence. A Canadian cohort study, born within a similar timeframe to the Finnish cohort above, found support for a specific mediating chain from childhood adversity through parental depression, parent-child conflict and adolescent behavioural difficulties to NEET status at age 24, offering a plausible mechanism for associations we also found even where socioeconomic adjustment attenuates 31 . As the cohorts are not separated by historical era, this contrast is more likely to reflect context-specific differences in social resources and adversity interact over development rather than a universal hierarchy of risk factors.

Adolescent depression was associated with first observed NEET in every age category and with single, sustained and recurrent patterns, and conduct problems also remained associated with repeated NEET. It is highly likely that a large proportion of those in these NEET groups will continue to experience mental health difficulties into adulthood. This argues for mental-health provision embedded alongside educational and employment support rather than offered separately. In England, Mental Health Support Teams (MHSTs) already provide this kind of embedded, early-intervention support within schools and colleges, working alongside education staff rather than through referral to separate clinical services 32 ; the 2025 Spending Review committed to extending MHST coverage to all schools and colleges by 2029 33 . Such provision is supported by evidence that NEET young people can remain committed to work while experiencing substantial mental health difficulties 11 . The barrier these associations point to is not necessarily motivation, but access to support that treats mental health and participation together.

Our repeated-pattern findings broadly accord with evidence linking persistent NEET with limited socioeconomic resources and negative life events 7 . Internalising symptom associations concerned early and late NEET, whereas adolescent depression here was associated with both sustained and recurrent classifications. Our study and Veldman et al.

. differ in observation schedules and definitions; our findings are complementary rather than a direct replication or contradiction. Our findings highlight the need for longer follow-up of NEET in future research in this area, which can reveal participation difficulties that are not captured at the point of leaving school, or even by mid-adulthood.

One policy implication is continued support for coordinated educational, clinical, and employment support, with linked-up placement opportunities to grow skills and confidence.

In a randomised trial among young people with first-episode psychosis, individual placement and support improved employment during the intervention 34 . That population differs from this cohort, and the trial cannot establish effectiveness for all young people with depressive symptoms. It nevertheless illustrates why retention, re-entry and wellbeing deserve attention alongside initial job attainment. Our findings motivate testing continued support rather than whether one successful placement resolves recurrent NEET.

The female-sex association with first observed NEET reversed direction after age 25, despite little association with the accumulated count. This finding cautions against treating the school-to-work transition as the only relevant period. Evidence from Denmark demonstrates substantial childbirth-related gender inequalities in employment and earnings 35 , making family formation one plausible explanation. However, these models did not test parenthood, caring responsibilities, or reasons for NEET status. The result cannot be interpreted as evidence that women's later NEET is caused by motherhood or is necessarily unwanted.

Childcare access, flexible employment and routes back into education or work are therefore hypotheses for further investigation, alongside health-related barriers. Equally, the association between childhood physical illness and recurrent NEET raises questions about accommodations and continuity of support. The aggregated ethnicity result warrants more detailed investigation of unequal opportunities, but cannot identify discrimination or a common experience across diverse ethnic groups. These estimates should inform questions about institutional access rather than individual employability.

A key strength of the study is the repeated assessments and coverage through age 33 that allow several dimensions of NEET to be examined within one cohort. However, the outcomes remain observation based. Consecutive NEET assessments do not establish continuous non-participation between waves, and first observed NEET need not be true onset. Missing waves can alter both the opportunity to observe NEET and its assigned pattern; controlling for assessment count cannot by itself remove informative non-response.

The sustained and recurrent groups are descriptive classifications, not estimated transition processes. The age categories for first observed NEET also differ in duration and in the number of underlying assessments, so observed differences between categories may partly reflect unequal opportunity to be classified within them rather than true differences in antecedents.

Some exposure windows also approach or overlap the earliest outcome observations: trauma was measured through age 17, while NEET observation began at 16. Reverse ordering therefore remains possible for early NEET. Attrition, imputation assumptions, residual confounding, and this regional cohort's historical context constrain inference and generalisability. The many exposure-outcome comparisons warrant caution, particularly where intervals lie near the null. Relative risk ratios also do not describe absolute probabilities, or the likely population benefit of an intervention, rather, the estimated discrepancies in risk between subgroups within the study sample.

In conclusion, this study contributes evidence that educational, social and health needs overlap across repeated patterns of non-participation, while timing reveals additional differences obscured by an overall count. Policies should be evaluated for their ability to sustain participation and wellbeing over time, with support available before school leaving, during later interruptions, and after re-entry.
