Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Antidepressants are widely prescribed, yet their biological mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Large-scale metabolomics and linked electronic health records provide a unique opportunity to characterise metabolic profiles associated with medication exposure. We investigated metabolic signatures of antidepressant exposure using 251 nuclear magnetic resonance biomarkers in UK Biobank (N = 222,911), with external validation in NESDA and ALSPAC (N = 6,082) and longitudinal analyses (n = 5,820, mean follow-up = 4.2 years). We identified 152 metabolites associated with current antidepressant use (PFDR < 0.05), characterised by elevated triglycerides and very-low-density lipoproteins. Current users also showed higher omega-3 fatty acid levels and lower omega-6-to-omega-3 ratios, both associated with lower depression risk. Metabolic profile scores significantly distinguished antidepressant users in both external cohorts (OR = 1.07-1.39, ROC AUC = 0.58-0.73). Longitudinal analyses identified nominal associations between antidepressant initiation and increases in nine lipid biomarkers (β = 0.144-0.229). These findings suggest metabolic signatures associated with antidepressant use may not be solely explained by underlying depression and are characterised by both potentially beneficial fatty acid alterations and adverse lipid profiles. Validation across independent cohorts and consistency with longitudinal findings strengthen our evidence, supporting insights into antidepressant mechanism and its relevance to cardiometabolic health.

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a debilitating psychiatric disorder with a high lifetime prevalence of approximately 10.8% 1 . The World Health Organisation has ranked depression among the largest contributors to global disability 2.

Antidepressants are the first-line pharmacological treatment for depression, with major classes including selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), selective norepinephrine re-uptake inhibitors (SNRIs), and tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) 3.

Antidepressant use has increased substantially in the global population 4,5 . For example, in England, 92.6 million antidepressant items were dispensed in 2024/25, representing an increase of nearly 50% over the past decade 6.

Despite the increased use of antidepressants, low remission rates remain a significant challenge. Around 30-50% of patients with MDD are treatment-resistant 7- 9 , and the biological mechanisms underlying the high variability in treatment response remain poorly understood. Improving clinical treatment therefore requires robust, replicable molecular evidence to help advance our understanding of the mode of action of antidepressants 10 . To date, few large population-scale molecular investigations of antidepressant use have been conducted. Previous findings have shown limited replication, and the causal effects of antidepressant use on molecular profiles remain underexplored. Metabolic psychiatry is a rapidly emerging field that examines systemic metabolic abnormalities involved in the pathophysiology of mental health conditions 11 , and may provide novel mechanistic insights into antidepressants currently in clinical use. Combined with advanced metabolomic profiling technologies, these data create opportunities to investigate metabolic changes associated with drug exposure. Metabolomic data linked to detailed clinical information remain scarce, with the UK Biobank (UKB) 19 , the Netherlands Study of Depression and Anxiety (NESDA) 23 , and the Avon Longitudinal Study of Parents and Children (ALSPAC) 24 being the major extant cohorts that can provide large-scale NMR-based metabolomic data alongside EHRderived information on the timing of antidepressant exposure. Importantly, plasma metabolites were measured at two timepoints in a subset of UKB participants 19 , supporting longitudinal assessment of metabolic changes and strengthening evidence for temporal associations 25 . Leveraging this unique opportunity, we used the UKB as a discovery cohort and sought to replicate our findings in NESDA and ALSPAC.

In our study, we utilised data from three large cohorts, the UKB, NESDA, and ALSPAC, to address the following aims: (1) to investigate metabolic signatures consistently associated with current antidepressant exposure by comparing current users with three control groups (never, past, and future users) in the UKB (N = 222,911, mean age = 56); (2) to interrogate the accuracy of the plasma metabolomic profiles in classifying individuals to antidepressant exposure status and antidepressant subclasses; (3) to validate the discovery results from UKB in two independent replication datasets, NESDA (N = 2,914, mean age = 41.9) and ALSPAC (N = 3,168, mean age = 24); and (4) to compare metabolic changes over time among participants who initiated antidepressants compared to sustained never users.

Out-of-sample classifications using metabolic profile score

To validate the associations between metabolite abundance and current antidepressant exposure, we created metabolic profile scores in three independent samples: (1) a held-out sample in UKB sample (internal replication), (2) the

Netherlands Study of Depression and Anxiety 27 (NESDA; external replication), and (3) the Avon Longitudinal Study of Parents and Children 28-30 (ALSPAC; external replication).

Internal validation in UKB

Current and never users in UKB were split into training (75%) and testing sets (25%).

In the training set, we repeated three case-control comparisons. A total of 138 metabolites showed significant differences between current users and all three control groups in the training set. These 138 metabolites were included in the LASSO (least absolute shrinkage and selection operator) regression, which identified 125 metabolite biomarkers with non-zero coefficients. A weighted sum score was generated using the LASSO weights. The score trained on the current and never users significantly classified all three comparison groups in the internal testing sample within UKB. Duration of antidepressant exposure

Among current antidepressant users in the testing set (n = 1,608), we conducted a simple linear regression analysis to assess whether the metabolic profile scores were associated with the duration of antidepressant exposure (mean = 8.3 years).

The score was significantly associated with duration of exposure (F(1, 1606) = 21.18, P = 4.51×10 -6 ), with each 1-SD increase in the score associated with a 0.63year increase in duration of exposure (95% CI: 0.49, 0.77; Fig. 5b). The score explained approximately 1.3% (R² = 0.013) of the variance in exposure duration.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the largest metabolome-wide association study of antidepressant exposure to date, examining 251 plasma metabolites with detailed characterisation of lipoprotein subclasses, lipid composition, and other metabolic biomarkers. We identified 152 metabolites uniquely associated with current antidepressant use across comparisons with three control groups, after accounting for depression status, depressive symptom severity, and lifestyle factors. Notably, 92% of metabolites significant in the main models remaining significant in minimally adjusted models including only age, sex, and technical covariates. Moreover, 98% remained significant in analyses restricted to individuals with depression, while all 152 remained significant after additional adjustment for cardiovascular risk factors.

Together, these findings suggest that the associations were largely robust across different model specifications. Comparisons with a previous metabolite-MDD study 13 showed both similarities and differences in metabolic associations, suggesting that the observed signatures may not be solely explained by underlying depression, although some may reflect biological pathways relevant to depression risk.

Our findings were further supported by out-of-sample classification in two independent cohorts spanning different age groups: NESDA (mean age = 41.9 years) and ALSPAC (mean age = 24 years). In both cohorts, the metabolic profile scores discriminated current from non-current users (ROC AUC 0.58-0.73). Higher scores were also significantly associated with longer duration of current antidepressant use. Furthermore, longitudinal analyses in UKB showed greater increases in nine lipid and lipoprotein biomarkers among antidepressant initiators compared with sustained-never users. All nine were directionally consistent with the cross-sectional metWAS findings and supplementary parametric g-formula analyses.

Although these associations did not survive FDR correction, they provided potential temporal evidence for antidepressant-associated metabolic changes over time.

Collectively, the external validation, associations with exposure duration, and longitudinal findings provide complementary evidence supporting the robustness of the observed metabolic signatures.

An important consideration is whether the observed metabolic signatures reflect antidepressant exposure rather than MDD itself. A previous study identified several fatty acid biomarkers with evidence of potential causal effects on MDD 13 . Notably, current users had higher omega-3 fatty acid levels and a lower omega-6 to omega-3 fatty acid ratio, both in directions associated with lower MDD risk based on MR estimates. These findings may be relevant to brain and cardiovascular health, as omega-3 fatty acids have been associated with neuronal preservation and protection against neurodegeneration, as well as lower cardiovascular mortality and major adverse cardiovascular events [31] [32] [33] [34] . Conversely, a high omega-6 to omega-3 fatty acid ratio has been associated with cardiovascular disease, cancer, and inflammatory diseases 35 . However, similarities between antidepressant-associated metabolic signatures and MR estimates for MDD were also observed. Current users showed a lower degree of unsaturation, while MR estimates suggested that lower levels were associated with higher MDD risk. Future research is needed to determine whether these similarities reflect effects of antidepressant use or shared biological pathways underlying depression.

Subclass analyses further suggest that these fatty acid signatures may differ between antidepressant classes. Despite broadly similar metabolic profiles between SSRI/SNRI and TCA users, class-specific differences were observed for docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) and omega-3 fatty acids to total fatty acids percentage.

Current TCA use was associated with higher levels of both biomarkers, which were associated with lower MDD risk based on MR estimates, while current SNRI/SSRI use was associated with lower levels of both. These differences perhaps partly reflect drug-specific effects on appetite and eating behaviours. Previous studies have reported differences in appetite and weight gain across antidepressants 36, 37 . For example, TCAs such as amitriptyline and nortriptyline, have been linked to weight gain, increased appetite and craving for carbohydrates 38, 39 , whereas venlafaxine (an SSRI) does not have a significant effect on weight 36 . Findings for SSRIs are more heterogenous 37 . These effects may contribute to the observed differences in metabolic profiles between subclasses, although further investigation is required.

Previous studies have consistently associated antidepressant use with higher plasma triglycerides [40] [41] [42] , a lipid marker associated with increased cardiovascular risk 43, 44 . We extend these findings by showing that current antidepressant users had higher triglycerides concentrations across VLDL, LDL, and HDL subclasses. Moreover, higher concentration across all VLDL lipid components further suggest that these associations extend beyond triglycerides to broader alterations in lipid composition. This pattern was further supported by longitudinal analyses, in which antidepressant initiators were associated with greater increases in VLDL lipids.

While HDL cholesterol is inversely associated with cardiovascular risk [45] [46] [47] , higher triglyceride content within HDL has been associated with coronary artery disease, myocardial infarction, and ischemic stroke 48, 49 , and may impair HDL structure and its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties 50, 51 . We also observed higher levels of glycoprotein acetyls, a marker of chronic low-grade inflammation 52 , and apolipoprotein B, which is implicated in the development of atherosclerosis and plaque formation [53] [54] [55] . Conversely, apolipoprotein A1, which has established antiinflammation properties 56, 57 , was also higher among current users. Together, our findings suggest wider disturbance in lipoprotein lipid composition, particularly involving triglyceride and VLDL, along with alterations in biomarkers relevant to inflammation and cardiovascular health.

There is growing evidence linking both MDD and antidepressant use to metabolic syndrome. A recent review estimated that immune-metabolic dysregulation is present in around 20-30% of individuals with MDD 12 . The metabolic signatures associated with antidepressant use in our study also showed similarities to comparable results previously reported in metabolic syndrome. Specifically, elevated circulating triglycerides and glucose are established features of metabolic syndrome 58 . This overlap is notable given that weight gain, increased BMI and glucose levels, and atherogenic dyslipidaemia are recognised adverse effects of several antidepressants 40, 59, 60 and are closely linked to metabolic syndrome [61] [62] [63] [64].

Previous research has linked antidepressant use to metabolic syndrome even after accounting for MDD and depressive symptom severity [65] [66] [67] , with some studies reporting stronger associations among TCA users 66, 67 . Consistent with this, we observed more extensive metabolic associations among TCA users than among SSRI/SNRI users.

Antidepressant utilisation among young people has increased substantially 6,68-70 . For example, in England, 7.9 million antidepressant items were dispensed to individuals aged 15-29 in 2024/25, representing a nearly 68% increase over the past decade 6.

Meta-analyses of antidepressant use among children and young adults under 25 have also raised concerns about an increased risk of suicidal ideation and behaviours 71, 72 . However, the metabolic effects associated with antidepressant use in this population remain poorly characterised. Here, we showed antidepressantassociated metabolic profiles in ALSPAC participants at age 24 (OR=1.13-1.39, ROC AUC = 0.61-0.65, P < 0.05). These findings suggest that some of the metabolic signatures associated with antidepressant use in older adults may also present in younger populations, consistent with previous studies linking SSRI use in young adults to increase BMI and a small increased risk of type 2 diabetes 73, 74.

However, the ALSPAC validation sample was modest, especially when the analyses were restricted to individuals with MDD. Furthermore, while metabolic profiles were measured at age 24, the antidepressant exposure definition in ALSPAC incorporated retrospective self-reported data collected at age 28. Therefore, these findings warrant further investigation in larger young-adult cohorts.

This study leveraged large-scale data, out-of-sample validations, and longitudinal data. However, several limitations should be considered. The imbalance between case and control sample sizes may have affected the classification models and contributed to their modest discriminative performance 75 . However, the precisionrecall area under the curve (PR AUC), which is particularly informative for imbalanced dataset 76 , exceed that of a naive classifier across all models. Another limitation is the potential influence of health-related and lifestyle confounders on the observed associations. To mitigate this, we included an extensive set of covariates in our main metWAS models and conducted sensitivity analyses accounting for MDD, lifestyle factors, and cardiovascular risk factors. In addition, reliance on a single baseline metabolomic measurement may not fully capture long-term metabolic patterns. We therefore utilised repeat assessment data to examine longitudinal associations between antidepressant exposure and metabolic changes, providing complementary evidence to the single cross-sectional findings. Finally, the predominantly European ancestry of the UKB population may limit the generalisability of our findings to other ethnic groups.

In summary, this study characterised metabolomic signatures associated with current antidepressant use, identifying higher triglycerides across lipoprotein subclasses and alterations in VLDL lipid components, with stronger associations observed among long-term users. These findings were supported by external validation in two independent cohorts and longitudinal analyses. Comparisons with previous MDD study revealed both similar and different metabolic associations, indicating both shared and distinct biological pathways. In particular, current users had higher omega-3 fatty acid levels and lower omega-6-to-omega-3 ratios, both associated with lower MDD risk, suggesting that antidepressant exposure may shift fatty acid profiles in directions that counteract metabolic risk factors for MDD. Our findings warrant further investigation into the potential value of monitoring metabolic profiles among patients prescribed antidepressants and contribute towards a better understanding of the impact of antidepressant use on metabolic health.

Methods

Study population

UKB is a large cohort study comprising over 500,000 participants recruited across the United Kingdom, aged 40-69 years 19 . Baseline assessments were conducted between 2006 and 2010. A subset of participants underwent a repeat assessment between 2012 and 2013 19 . We included 222,911 participants with baseline metabolomic profiling data and linked EHRs for the cross-sectional metWAS analyses. We then included 5,820 participants with both metabolomic data available at baseline and follow-up for longitudinal analyses. Ethical approval for UKB was obtained from the North West NHS Research Ethics Committee (11/NW/0382).

Access to the data for this study was granted under application #4844.

Metabolic biomarkers

Metabolomic profiling of plasma samples from UKB was performed by Nightingale Health Plc. using the high-throughput NMR spectroscopy platform. Detailed procedures of the Nightingale NMR pipeline have been described elsewhere 19 . A total of 251 metabolic biomarkers were derived from non-fasting ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) plasma samples, including absolute concentrations and ratios of circulating metabolites, lipids, and lipoprotein subfractions 19 . Nightingale grouped these biomarkers by types into 18 categories: amino acids, apolipoproteins, cholesterol, cholesteryl esters, fatty acids, fluid balance, free cholesterol, glycolysis related metabolites, inflammation, ketone bodies, lipoprotein particle concentrations, lipoprotein particle sizes, lipoprotein subclasses, other lipids, phospholipids, relative lipoprotein lipid concentrations, total lipids, and triglycerides 19.

A full list of metabolic biomarkers, groups, and formulae for deriving the composite biomarkers and ratios is provided in Table S26 in Supplementary 2.

In this study, we regrouped the metabolic biomarkers to facilitate comparisons of lipid components across lipoproteins. Biomarkers of lipoproteins were grouped according to their measured lipid components, while the original Nightingale classifications were retained for non-lipoprotein biomarkers, resulting in 16 metabolite groups. For example, cholesterol in large HDL was assigned to the cholesterol group rather than the lipoprotein subclass group, while cholesteryl ester in IDL was assigned to the cholesteryl ester group. A full list of the derived metabolite groups is provided in Table S14 in Supplementary 2.

Assessment of antidepressant exposure from electronic health records (EHRs)

Antidepressant exposure was defined using linked prescription and registration records from general practitioners (GPs) for 222,911 participants with available metabolomic profiling data. Prescription records included drug name, classification, quantity, and prescription date. Antidepressant records were extracted using drug names and Read v2/British National Formulary (BNF) codes, following the procedure established by Fabbri et al. (2021) 77.

Following the pipeline created by Davyson et al. 78 , antidepressant treatment episodes were defined as at least two consecutive prescriptions with gaps of less than six months. Prescriptions occurring more than six months after the previous record were considered the start of a new episode, and single prescriptions of antidepressant were excluded. Participants with available metabolomic data were categorised based on whether they had an antidepressant episode during, before, or after the baseline assessment: 1.

Current users (N = 11,271): had an antidepressant episode during the date of baseline assessment; 2. Past users (N = 31,283): had no current antidepressant episode, but had at least one past episode at least six months before the baseline assessment; 3. Future users (N = 24,324): had no current or past antidepressant episode, but had at least one future episode started after the baseline assessment; 4. Never used (N = 156,033): had a GP registration record but had no record of antidepressant use.

Metabolomic data imputation

Missing metabolomic data were imputed using the 'missRanger' package (version 2.6.1) 79 in R, which combines random forest prediction with predictive mean matching. Random forests have demonstrated superior performance compared to other machine learning models 80 and have been applied to other omics data 57 . After removing individuals with missing age and sex data and those with more than 60% missing metabolites, 488,083 individuals were included in the imputation. We specified 50 trees per forest to balance computational efficiency and predictive performance. Predictive mean matching with three nearest neighbours was implemented to preserve the original data distribution and reduce imputation bias by replacing predicted values with observed values from similar cases 82.

Cross-sectional metabolome-wide association study (metWAS) in UK Biobank

Case-control comparisons

We conducted three case-control comparisons between current antidepressant users (cases) and three control groups: past users, future users, and never users.

The association between each metabolite and antidepressant exposure was assessed using general linear regression. Metabolites that were consistently significant across all three comparisons (current/never, current/past, and current/future) were considered uniquely associated with current users. Metabolites were standardised using z-score transformation to allow direct comparison of effect sizes across models. Therefore, all reported beta coefficients represent the standardised mean difference (Cohen's d) between cases and controls. P-values were corrected for multiple testing using the false discovery rate (FDR) across all metabolic biomarkers within each case-control comparison (PFDR).

Covariates

In the main models, we included age, sex, assessment centre, NMR spectrometer, SES, smoking status, ethnicity, educational attainment, BMI, alcohol drinker status, and alcohol intake frequency as covariates. Depression diagnosis 26 and depressive symptoms severity (PHQ-4) were also included to account for potential confounding by MDD. See supplementary 1 for details about covariates.

Sensitivity analyses

Two additional sensitivity analyses were conducted: 1.

Restricting the main metWAS models to participants with depression only, to account for potential confounding between healthy and affected individuals.

2. Further adjusting for risk factors included in calculating cardiovascular risk 83 including type 1 and type 2 diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, kidney failure, kidney nephropathy, glomerulonephritis, migraine, systemic lupus, and erectile dysfunction, to ensure that metabolite associations were not fully driven by differences in cardiovascular risk. Further details about the covariates are provided in the Supplementary 1.

Comparison to MDD study

To further compare the metabolic associations observed in our study to those reported for MDD 13 , we examined the distribution of effect directions across metabolite groups using chi-square goodness-of-fit and two-sided exact binomial tests.

Metabolite set enrichment analysis (MSEA)

To investigate whether specific metabolite groups were enriched, MSEA was performed using 'fgsea' package (version 3.23) 84, 85 to estimate enrichment scores based on the effect sizes from the main metWAS models. To examine overall enrichment patterns across lipoprotein subclasses, we additionally grouped lipoprotein biomarkers according to four major lipoprotein classes: VLDL, IDL, LDL, and HDL classes. For example, triglyceride in very small VLDL was assigned to VLDL for this MSEA. A full list of metabolites in each group is provided in Table S14 in Supplementary 2.

In MSEA, effect estimates for each metabolite set were summarised using a normalised enrichment score (NES) 86 . The NES reflects the degree to which a set of metabolites is overrepresented at the top (positive effect sizes) or bottom (negative effect sizes) of the ranked list of metabolites. The NES is normalised to account for the differences in set size and statistical significance is assessed by controlling the false discovery rate.

Out-of-sample classifications using metabolic profile score

Internal validation in UKB

Following the association analyses, out-of-sample classifications were conducted to validate the classification potential of metabolic biomarkers in case-control comparisons using independent datasets. In the UKB baseline data, participants with current/never antidepressant status were split into training (75%) and testing (25%) sets. The 'caret' R package (version 7.0-1) 87 was implemented to ensure balanced representation of cases and controls. The three main metWAS models (current/never, current/past, current/future) were repeated in the training set, and only the metabolites consistently significant across all three case-control comparisons in the training set were included in the classification analyses. The metabolite values in both training and testing sets were standardised and residualised against covariates before further analyses. Covariates included age, sex, assessment centre, NMR spectrometer, SES, smoking status, ethnicity, educational attainment, BMI, alcohol drinker status, and alcohol intake frequency.

Penalised logistic regression using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) was applied to the metabolite residuals, with current/never status as the binary outcome. Model hyperparameters were optimised via repeated threefold cross-validation (five repeats). To address class imbalance, random oversampling examples (ROSE) 88 was applied. LASSO coefficients reflected the relative importance of each metabolite in classifying the outcome 89 . Non-zero coefficients from the training set were multiplied by corresponding residualised metabolite values in the testing set to generate a weighted composite metabolic score per participant.

Then, logistic regression was applied to use the score to classify current/never, current/past, and current/future status in the testing set. Areas under the curve (AUCs) and ORs were reported for classification performance 90 . The score was standardised using z-score transformation, therefore the reported OR represented the change in odds associated with a one standard-deviation (SD) increase in the score. Additionally, among current antidepressant users in the testing set, a linear regression analysis was conducted to assess whether the metabolic profile score was associated with the duration of antidepressant exposure.

External validation in different age cohorts

Two external cohorts were included as independent test sets to evaluate the reproducibility and generalisability of our trained model: NESDA 27 (mean age = 41.9) and ALSPAC 29 (mean age = 24). In each cohort, the non-zero coefficients from the UKB LASSO training were multiplied by corresponding metabolite values to generate a weighted composite metabolic score per participant. The score was standardised using z-score transformation. Further details about the external cohorts are provided in the Supplementary 1.

In NESDA, participants were classified as current antidepressant users (n = 723; case group) or non-current users (n = 2,191; control group) based on drug container inspection of medications used in the past month, with medications classified according to the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical classification system. The metabolic profile score was used to classified current and non-current users, adjusting for age, sex, and shipping batch.

In ALSPAC, participants were initially classified using a self-reported data on current antidepressant use at the time of blood collection during a research clinic visit age 24. Participants were categorised as current users of antidepressant at age 24 (n = 180; case group) or non-current users (n = 2749; control group). To increase statistical power, we conducted additional models incorporating data from a second self-reported questionnaire administered at age 28, which asked participants whether they had been prescribed medication for depression or anxiety within the past five years. In these additional models, the case group (n = 513) was defined as a "Yes" response to either: (1) antidepressant use at age 24, and/or (2) prescription . of medication for depression or anxiety within the past five years, assessed at age 28. The control group (n = 2,655) was defined by negative responses to both selfreported items. The metabolic profile score was used to classified current and noncurrent users, controlling for sex. Following adjustment for basic covariates, we conducted sensitivity analyses in both external cohorts by further adjusting for smoking, education, and BMI.

Antidepressant subclasses: Case-control comparisons and out-of-sample validation

In the UKB cohort, case-control metWAS and out-of-sample validation analyses were repeated for each antidepressant subclass to identify shared and class-specific metabolic signatures. Antidepressants were categorised into three groups: 1) SSRIs/SNRIs, 2) TCAs, and 3) other antidepressants. Categorisation was based primarily on BNF codes 77 , with drug names used as a supplementary resource when BNF codes were unavailable. Prescription records and treatment episodes were derived separately for each subclass: • SSRIs/SNRIs: current (n= 6,729), past (19,072), future (13,254), never (183,223) • TCAs: current (n= 4,221), past (20,815), future (18,387), never (178,855) • Other antidepressants: current (n= 459), past (3,890), future (3,916), never (214,013)

We also compared the metabolic associations observed in SSRIs/SNRIs and TCAs to those reported for MDD 13 . We examined the distribution of effect directions across metabolite groups using chi-square goodness-of-fit and two-sided exact binomial tests.

Longitudinal metabolomic analyses in UK Biobank

The final stage of this study incorporated data from the UKB baseline and repeat assessments, including both NMR-based metabolomic profiles and linked EHRs, to examine longitudinal associations between antidepressant exposure and metabolic patterns. A total of 19,877 participants had linked EHRs and metabolomic data available at both baseline and repeat assessment. Missing metabolomic data at the repeat assessment were imputed using the same approach described in the crosssectional metWAS.

Antidepressant exposure at the repeat assessment was defined using the same procedure as in the cross-sectional metWAS 78 . Participants were categorised as current (n = 343), past (n = 58), future (n = 0), or never (n = 5,677) users based on antidepressant treatment episodes at the repeat visit.

Based on antidepressant status at both baseline and repeat visits, participants were further classified into two groups: 1.

Control group (n = 5,677): participants who had a GP registration record but no record of antidepressant use in both baseline and repeat visits.

2. Antidepressant initiation group (n = 143): participants who were not current antidepressants users at baseline but had a current antidepressant episode at the time of the repeat assessment.

Linear mixed-effects model

We applied linear mixed-effects models to investigate whether changes in metabolite levels over time differed between controls and antidepressant initiators. Only the 152 significant metabolites identified across the three main metWAS models were included. Random intercepts were included to account for repeated measurements within individuals, with fixed effects for time (T0 = baseline, T1 = repeat assessment), group (controls, antidepressant initiators), and their interaction (time × group).

Covariates included age, sex, BMI, spectrometer, month of blood collection, and time of blood collection. Time of blood collection was categorised as morning (05:00 ≤ 12:00), afternoon (12:00 ≤ 17:00), evening (17:00 ≤ 21:00), or night (21:00 ≤ 05:00).

Analysis was conducted using 'lmerTest' R package (version 3.2) 91 . All metabolite values at T0 and T1 were standardised together by z-score transformation. The time × group interaction coefficient therefore represented the difference in the standardised change in metabolite levels over time between control group and initiator group.
