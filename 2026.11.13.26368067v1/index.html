Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Whose deposit is each link? How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic significantly expanded the use of wastewater monitoring for infectious disease surveillance. Before the pandemic, wastewater monitoring was primarily used for binary pathogen detection and notably to support polio eradication efforts. 1 During the pandemic, studies demonstrated that wastewater concentrations of SARS-CoV-2 RNA reflect COVID-19 incidence, 2, 3 bringing mainstream attention to its use for quantitatively tracking disease transmission dynamics. Wastewater monitoring has since expanded to a myriad of pathogens-including other respiratory viruses, 4 enteric viruses, 5 fungi, 6 bacteria, 7, 8 and arboviruses https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?4UTd8g -along with other purposes, such as tracking variants, 11, 12 identifying trends, 13 evaluating policies and nonpharmaceutical interventions, 14, 15 and inferring seasonal characteristics and drivers. [16] [17] [18] [19] [20] Presently, most wastewater monitoring efforts use quantitative polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based methods to detect prespecified, known viruses. 21 These "targeted" methods have shown significant utility for detecting and quantitatively tracking known pathogens. However, because assay design requires prior knowledge of the target genome sequence, targeted approaches are neither suited to detect novel or rapidly evolving pathogens nor feasibly monitor the full diversity of viruses in wastewater. By contrast, metagenomic sequencing characterizes nucleic acids across a sample without needing to prespecify targets. Shotgun metagenomic sequencing methods, referred to here as "untargeted" wastewater monitoring, involves sequencing all genomic material present in a wastewater sample. Nucleic acids from human pathogenic viruses comprise a small fraction of this material, however, so sensitive and consistent detection requires substantial sequencing depth or high total sequencing reads per sample. 22 Nonetheless, declining sequencing costs have increased feasibility for scaling wastewater metagenomic sequencing with most attention focusing on its capability to detect novel or unexpected pathogens.

In addition to its ability to detect novel or unexpected pathogens, wastewater metagenomic sequencing generates billions of reads of expected, endemic targets, meaning the data are also positioned to provide broader public health value. Virus-specific normalized read abundance from untargeted wastewater monitoring data have been shown to temporally correlate with targeted wastewater monitoring and clinical surveillance data for human pathogenic viruses, 23, 24 suggesting that wastewater metagenomic sequencing may be used to infer quantitative transmission dynamics of both widely-surveilled (e.g., SARS-CoV-2) and under-surveilled (e.g., rhinovirus) pathogens. If so, wastewater metagenomic sequencing could serve multiple public health functions, including early detection of emerging, unexpected, or hitherto unseen threats and routine, quantitative monitoring of multiple endemic pathogens in parallel.

Whether virus-specific metagenomic read abundance can reliably reconstruct underlying transmission dynamics remains unknown. Two quantitative statistics widely used to monitor transmission dynamics are the instantaneous epidemic growth rate ( r t ) and effective reproduction number ( R t ). 25, 26 Both r t and R t have been estimated from wastewater monitoring data, although only using data collected via targeted approaches. 27, 28 To our knowledge, no studies have evaluated whether untargeted wastewater monitoring data may be used to reliably infer transmission dynamics (as represented by r t and R t ). In this study, we aimed to assess the use of untargeted wastewater monitoring data for quantitatively tracking pathogen transmission dynamics. Using data from four distinct sites in the United States, we estimated r t and R t using untargeted wastewater monitoring, targeted wastewater monitoring, and clinical surveillance data for widely-monitored viruses. We then evaluated concordance among the time-varying estimates of pathogen transmission derived from each data source. Secondarily, we estimated r t for viruses only with available untargeted wastewater monitoring data to infer the transmission dynamics of less-monitored viruses. We found that wastewater metagenomic sequencing tracks transmission dynamics concordant with other data sources while expanding the viral targets we can reliably monitor.

Discussion

Wastewater metagenomic sequencing-or untargeted wastewater monitoring-is particularly well-suited to detect rare, albeit potentially consequential, novel and reemerging threats. Yet the data simultaneously offer routine public health value as most wastewater metagenomic reads belonging to viruses are of circulating, endemic pathogens. We demonstrated that untargeted wastewater monitoring may be used to quantitatively track transmission dynamics of routinely detected viruses while expanding the range of targets that may be reliably monitored. Estimates of growth rate ( r t ) and effective reproduction number ( R t ) derived from untargeted wastewater monitoring data were significantly concordant with those derived from clinical data and targeted, PCR-based wastewater monitoring data. Moreover, untargeted wastewater monitoring data covered more viral targets than other data sources, allowing transmission dynamics to be inferred for a wider number of targets.

Across distinct locations and viruses, we provided evidence that untargeted wastewater monitoring can recapitulate R t and r t as derived from clinical and targeted wastewater monitoring. The timing and magnitude of peak R t and r t estimates derived from untargeted wastewater monitoring data were overall relatively similar as when derived from other data sources during periods of increasing disease transmission. In addition, the relative timing of observed peak R t values derived from untargeted wastewater monitoring data during the 2024-25 season aligned with established temporal patterns based on historic clinical and targeted wastewater monitoring data. RSV typically peaks first, followed by IAV then IBV which we observed. 19, 34 The seasonality of SARS-CoV-2 is not as established but in past years has had a wintertime peak coinciding with RSV and influenza; 16, 19 we observed a peak in SARS-CoV-2 R t between that of RSV and influenza. NoV activity is typically elevated in winter and rotavirus in winter-spring, [35] [36] [37] [38] and we observed a wintertime peak in R t for NoV before rotavirus.

Our Bayesian permutation analysis then provided statistical evidence that the observed similarities in R t and r t estimates derived from untargeted wastewater monitoring and other data sources were significant. Other studies have calculated metrics similar to MAE, AOB, and correlation to assess similarity between two derived time series, although only between point estimates. 27, 39, 40 By using permutation within a Bayesian framework, we not only quantified the numerical discrepancy, agreement on epidemic direction, and temporal association between two time series but further provided statistical evidence that the observed metrics were significantly different than had there been no relationship between the two time series.

Nonetheless, there are shortcomings to our statistical approach which may explain why not all tests were significant. First, some clinical data were sparse at the county level so we used state-level data instead ( Table S2 ). Lack of concordance between estimates from untargeted wastewater monitoring and clinical data may be attributed to geographic differences between the localized and statewide population. Second, the duration of targeted wastewater monitoring was shorter than other data sources. R t estimates towards the end of the time window of a data source are less reliable; therefore, the end date of targeted wastewater monitoring occurring before that of clinical surveillance and untargeted wastewater monitoring data may have contributed to estimates derived from targeted wastewater monitoring sometimes lacking concordance with other data sources. Third, the permutation testing we adopted to infer significance of similarity depends on temporal variation in the data. Permuting a time series with its own time points randomizes that variation and enables principled construction of a null distribution and comparison. By contrast, time series with relatively low degrees of temporal variation (e.g., those that fluctuate minimally around R t = 1) do not differ much once permuted. Consequently, achieving significant differentiation between the observed and null posterior distributions is challenging with time series that do not exhibit substantial temporal variation. Lack of a complete year of concurrent surveillance (aside from Boston) may explain why estimates derived from untargeted wastewater monitoring were not always significantly concordant with other data sources despite visually appearing so. Lastly, we convolved latent infections to expected case reports using incubation period and reporting delay distributions for clinical data and fecal shedding distributions for wastewater data sources. While these distributions theoretically account for the delays unique to each data source, meaning inferred peak R t estimates should coincide across data sources, information to deduce these distributions-especially reporting and shedding delay distributions-is sparse. Lack of reporting and shedding delay data for viruses may further explain why derived R t estimates were not always concordant across data sources.

Most viruses included in this study only had untargeted wastewater monitoring data available. For the viruses with multiple surveillance sources, estimates from untargeted wastewater monitoring data were overall concordant when compared to other data sources. Therefore, we derived r t from untargeted wastewater monitoring alone for remaining viruses. Overall, r t estimates from untargeted wastewater monitoring data aligned with known epidemiological features of these viruses according to historical surveillance ( Table 1 ). Because many of these viruses are less prioritized compared to SARS-CoV-2, influenza, and RSV, future work should focus on better understanding the epidemiology of these less-studied viruses. Wastewater metagenomic sequencing offers a compelling means to do so as it does not rely on testing-seeking behaviors, clinical testing of specimens, or targeted assay design. For example, Smith et al. 41 retrospectively elucidated seasonal dynamics for a range of targets detected via wastewater metagenomic sequencing data using linear mixed effects models. Future studies should build upon this work to develop methods for prospectively identifying levels and trends or seasonal onset, peak, offset, and duration. Such methods have already been developed for targeted wastewater monitoring data; 13, [16] [17] [18] [19] extending this work to untargeted wastewater monitoring data would greatly expand its utility.

While our study highlights a compelling use of wastewater metagenomic sequencing, it has limitations. Foremost, longitudinal shedding concentration data are not available for most viruses. Viruses rarely detected may be widely present in a population but not shed into wastewater in detectable quantities. Moreover, viruses with low read counts may be widely present but shed in low quantities (e.g., respiratory viruses), making it more challenging to differentiate signal from noise. Second, metagenomic read counts are influenced by genome size, nucleic acid extraction efficiency, and sequencing depth, and it is possible reads may be incorrectly mapped to reference genomes or that reference genomes may be sparse. However, estimates derived from untargeted wastewater monitoring were concordant with those derived from the other data sources for the sequencing depth and normalization used. Still, future work should repeat analyses using other normalization approaches or probe-based hybrid capture "semi-targeted" wastewater monitoring data which reduce sequencing depth by enriching for viral targets. 24, [42] [43] [44] [45] [46] [47] [48] [49] Third, the frequency of targeted wastewater monitoring was higher than that of clinical and untargeted wastewater monitoring data which may have introduced more day-to-day variability in derived estimates. Nonetheless, derived estimates from targeted wastewater monitoring data remained similar at downsampled frequencies ( Figs. S30-31 ) although future work could investigate optimal metagenomic sequencing frequencies for inferring transmission dynamics. Lastly, EpiNow2 was readily adaptable to other types of surveillance data but ultimately built for clinical data. While EpiNow2 generates real-time R t estimates, these estimates are highly affected when recent observations are outliers as are more common in environmental data ( Fig. S32 ). Real-time outlier detection methods have been proposed for some targeted wastewater monitoring program setups, 50 so further developing methods for other program setups would support prospective monitoring. Evidently, if wastewater metagenomic sequencing expands, developing real-time estimation tools tailored specifically to the data would support public health adoption. Estimation tools for targeted wastewater monitoring data exist but are often data intensive, requiring PCR and sample metadata we did not have. 39, 51, 52 No tools for untargeted wastewater monitoring data yet exist but could account for sequencing depth and data processing time lags unique to untargeted wastewater monitoring data.

Notwithstanding, our study is the first of our knowledge to estimate r t and R t from untargeted wastewater monitoring data and evaluate concordance with existing surveillance data using permutation analysis within a Bayesian framework. We highlight a practical use case for wastewater metagenomic reads for tracking transmission dynamics of known viruses, including those not widely monitored via other surveillance means. As interest and planned investment in wastewater metagenomic sequencing grows nationally, our work emphasizes that broader public health value that may be derived from the data, ranging from early detection of novel pathogens to routine surveillance of endemic pathogens.

Methods

We estimated r t and R t for several human pathogenic viruses using clinical surveillance, targeted wastewater monitoring, and untargeted wastewater monitoring data. r t , defined as the instantaneous rate of change of log-transformed case incidence, describes the speed at which cases are increasing ( r t > 0) or decreasing ( r t < 0) in a population at time t. https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?2bwUvr R t , defined as the average number of secondary infections caused by a single infection at time t , describes whether an epidemic is growing ( R t > 1) or shrinking ( R t < 1). 25 For viruses with two or more surveillance data sources available, we then assessed concordance among the time-varying r t and R t estimates derived from each data source. An overview of the data sources, r t and R t estimation approaches, and concordance metrics is provided below. We conducted the analysis for the following locations in the United States during periods with routine untargeted wastewater monitoring: Boise, Idaho (November 2024-March 2026); Boston, Massachusetts (September 2024-March 2026); Riverside, California (December 2024-March 2026); and Ottumwa, Iowa (February 2025-March 2026). These sites were selected because they had at least one year of routine untargeted wastewater monitoring data, alongside at least six concurrent months of routine targeted wastewater monitoring data, from the data sources described below. All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.6.0). Data and code are available at https://github.com/petal-code/casper_rt_paper. 53 We used Claude Code Opus 5.5 to assist in drafting analytic code, replicating analyses across multiple sites and viruses, and structuring the GitHub repository. All code was manually reviewed and validated by the authors.

Overview of surveillance data sources

In this study, we used clinical surveillance, targeted wastewater monitoring, and untargeted wastewater monitoring data to estimate r t and R t . Human pathogenic viruses included in this study, along with available surveillance data sources, are listed in Fig. 1.

Clinical surveillance data were obtained from Epic Cosmos (Epic Systems Corporation, Verona, WI, USA). Epic Cosmos is an electronic health record data aggregation platform that includes data from over 300 million patients across more than 2,000 hospitals and 52,000 clinics; it is representative of patients seeking healthcare in the United States. 29 Previously, Epic Cosmos data were shown to be less prone to week-to-week variability compared to sentinel, voluntary reporting systems (e.g., the National Respiratory and Enteric Virus Surveillance System) and attainable at localized spatial scales. 35 For viruses in Fig. 1 , aside from influenza A and B virus (IAV/IBV), we obtained weekly totals of patient encounters resulting in an ICD-10 (International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision code) diagnosis ( Table S1 ). For IAV and IBV, we obtained weekly totals of positive lab results since ICD-10 influenza diagnoses do not always differentiate between IAV versus IBV. We obtained data for the corresponding county (or state, when county-level data were sparse) of wastewater treatment plants included in this study during the period of untargeted wastewater monitoring ( Table S2 ).

Both targeted and untargeted wastewater monitoring data were obtained from the same wastewater treatment plants at each location ( Table S2 ). Targeted wastewater monitoring data between July 2024-September 2025 were obtained from Boehm et al. 21 ; we used the subset of data concurrent with untargeted wastewater monitoring for each site. Refer to the data descriptor publication for complete methodological details but, briefly, concentrations for all viruses were measured in wastewater solids in units of gene copies per gram of dry weight (cp/g-dry) approximately three times per week using droplet digital reverse-transcriptionpolymerase chain reaction (ddRT-PCR). 21 For this analysis, we (1) imputed non-detect measurements with half the assay limit of detection (LOD = 1,000 gc/g-dry) and ( 2) normalized human pathogen viral nucleic acids by pepper mild mottle viruses (PMMoV) RNA concentrations in the same sample. 21 PMMoV is an endogenous control originating in the human diet; PMMoV-normalization serves to correct for differences in human fecal strength and viral recovery across wastewater samples. [54] [55] [56] Untargeted wastewater surveillance data were obtained from Justen et al. 23, 24 from the same wastewater treatment plants with available targeted wastewater monitoring data. Routine untargeted wastewater monitoring began at study sites between September 2024-February 2025; we obtained data through March 2026. Refer to the data descriptor for complete methodological details but, briefly, wastewater influent samples were collected and sequenced approximately one time per week using Illumina NovaSeq platforms. 23 For this analysis, we only used sequencing data from the University of Missouri protocol. 23 Bioinformatic processing included shallow taxonomic profiling on a subset of reads and deep vertebrate-infecting virus profiling on all reads following a published metagenomics pipeline by Bradshaw et al. 57 ( https://github.com/securebio/nao-mgs-workflow ). For this analysis, we (1) imputed samples with 0 read counts for a viral target with half the LOD, herein defined as the lowest possible number of read counts (LOD = 1 read), and (2) normalized virus read counts by scaled PMMoV counts (PMMoV counts ✕ total read pairs / number of reads profiled) to account for fluctuations in human fecal strength, viral recovery, and sequencing depth. 23 Because human pathogenic viruses are rare considering all microbes present in a wastewater sample, we assumed absolute clade counts are approximately proportional to relative abundance. Additionally, we opted to normalize virus read counts by PMMoV read counts for consistency with the targeted wastewater monitoring data. Preliminary work comparing normalization approaches for wastewater metagenomic sequencing data suggests normalization by tobamovirus read counts, the genus of PMMoV, may be most preferable although PMMoV normalization was still among the best performing normalization markers. 24 Assessing temporal association across surveillance data sources For viruses with surveillance data from multiple sources ( Fig. 1 ), we evaluated the null hypothesis that weekly surveillance signals (i.e., clinical counts, concentrations of viral nucleic acids, or virus-specific read counts) are not temporally correlated. We used nonparametric Kendall's tau correlation test as surveillance time series were not always normally distributed (Shapiro-Wilk, p < 0.01). We excluded any wastewater time series where >75% of samples were below the LOD. Virus presence or absence was instead reported for these rarely detected viruses in subsequent analyses, rather than r t or R t estimates. Furthermore, because targeted wastewater monitoring data were reported multiple times per week, we computed weekly average concentrations prior to assessing correlation. For each virus, we assessed correlations at each location and between each pair of available data sources. Therefore, we used an adjusted alpha value of 0.05 divided by the total number of tests conducted (i.e., the number of locations times the number of distinct data source pairs with sufficient detections) to account for multiple testing when interpreting p values for each virus.

Estimating instantaneous epidemic growth rate ( r t )

We estimated r t using all available data sources for viruses as r t does not require assumptions about disease transmission. r t is defined as the logarithmic derivative of smoothed incidence. 26 The clinical surveillance data represent incident infections; we assumed wastewater measurements similarly represent incident infections as wastewater signals have been previously shown to positively correlate with disease incidence. 4, 23 To smooth each surveillance time series, we fit a generalized additive model (GAM) using the mgcv R package. 30 Specifically, we used a negative binomial distribution for clinical data, which were discrete counts, and a tweedie distribution for both targeted and untargeted wastewater monitoring data, which were continuous and positive. Additionally, we removed outlier wastewater measurements prior to smoothing; set k , the dimension basis of the smooth term, generously (i.e., the number of observations remaining in the time series divided by 4); and used REML (restricted maximum likelihood) as the smoothing parameter estimation method with the outer BFGS (Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno) optimization algorithm. 30 To remove wastewater outliers, we first calculated the median and median absolute deviation (MAD) of log 10 -transformed wastewater measurements above the LOD in the time series. We then calculated the modified z-score for each log 10 -transformed measurement using this median and MAD and defined outliers as measurements with a modified z-score above 3.5. 58 We assumed all defaults otherwise. Refer to Fig. S32 for smoothed time series without outlier removal for comparison.

GAMs implemented using the mgcv R package have an empirical Bayesian interpretation as the smoothing parameter estimated via REML uses a frequentist approach and the uncertainty calculation to obtain credible intervals (CrIs) uses a Bayesian approach. Thus, for each GAM fit, we extracted 1,000 posterior draws and estimated the median and 90% CrI each day to produce a smoothed time series. Next, to estimate daily r t from each smoothed time series, we approximated the logarithmic derivative of the smoothed time series S t using Equation 1 with a daily time step. We approximated the logarithmic derivative each day using all 1,000 posterior draws to calculate the median and 90% CrI of the r t estimate. Estimating R t requires an epidemiological model to link an observed surveillance signal to latent infections in the population. Therefore, for each location, we estimated R t only for viruses with sufficient data to inform model parameters: SARS-CoV-2, IAV, IBV, RSV, NoV, and rotavirus. For each virus, we estimated R t from each available surveillance data source using the renewal equation model in a Bayesian framework, as described by the R package EpiNow2 ( Equation 2 ). 31, 32 The United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) similarly used EpiNow2 to estimate R t from emergency department visit data between 2022-May 2026. 59 According to the renewal equation, latent infections each day I t are given by the total infectiousness of infected individuals on day t , multiplied by the effective reproduction number R t . Total infectiousness is described by past incidence and an infected individual's infectivity profile or generation time w probability mass function (pmf), both of which depend on the time since infection 𝜏.

Latent infections are then convolved to expected case reports D t c according to Equation 3. Specifically, 𝜁 is the infection-to-reporting delay pmf, which is a combination of incubation period and reporting delays for clinical data, and 𝛼 c is a scaling factor representing ascertainment of cases.

Next, expected case reports D t c are mapped to observed case reports C t c using a negative binomial distribution, where 𝜓 is an overdispersion factor ( Equation 4 ).

To control the smoothness of R t estimates, we used the default Gaussian process with the default length scale and magnitude parameters. Note we did not remove outlier wastewater measurements prior to estimating R t because Gaussian process smoothing is less sensitive to extrema compared to GAM smoothing. Namely, D t ww and C t ww instead represent the expected and observed wastewater signal, respectively, arising from latent infections I t in this adapted framework ( Equation 5-6 ). The wastewater signal is PMMoV-normalized concentrations of viral nucleic acids using targeted wastewater monitoring data and PMMoV-normalized virus clade counts scaled to account for sequencing depth using untargeted wastewater monitoring data. EpiNow2 can only handle integer counts; therefore, we multiplied wastewater signals in a time series by a constant factor and rounded such that they represented integer counts analogous to clinical data. When both clinical and wastewater data were available for a given site and virus, we multiplied wastewater signals by a factor that resulted in the median rounded wastewater signal being on the same order of magnitude as the median clinical count. When only wastewater data were available, we multiplied wastewater signals so the median rounded wastewater signal was above one and on the same order of magnitude as the other wastewater data source, if both wastewater data sources were available. Assessing concordance in time-varying estimates derived from different surveillance data sources Specifically, for both r t and R t , we calculated MAE, AOB, and Kendall's tau between all pairs of posterior draws derived from data source a and b . Doing so generated an observed posterior distribution for each concordance metric and each pair of data sources. Since each surveillance data source has unique biases and limitations, no surveillance data source represents a gold standard. Therefore, to assess whether two surveillance data sources generated significantly similar r t and R t estimates than would be expected by random chance, we generated null posterior distributions for each pair of data sources using Bayesian permutation analysis. Specifically, we permuted each posterior draw across time points of data source b and recalculated each metric between data source a and b perm , generating null posterior distributions. For each pair of data sources and concordance metric, the null posterior distribution represented the posterior distribution under a null hypothesis of no temporal concordance or relationship between the two data sources. We then calculated Bayesian permutation p values which represent the proportion of posterior draws in which the null scenario was more similar than the observed scenario. For MAE, values closer to 0 indicate similarity, so we calculated permutation p values as the proportion of null draw pairs less than observed draw pairs. In contrast, AOB and tau coefficients closer to 1 indicate similarity; we calculated permutation p values as the proportion of null draw pairs greater than observed draw pairs. We interpreted permutation p values < 0.05 as suggesting significant concordance between two data sources as this indicated the null hypothesis was not rejected in less than 5% of posterior draw pairs.

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV)

Rhinovirus

Rhinoviruses may have multiple peaks within a year, often in spring and early fall. 73 We observed multiple peaks when observing daily derived r t estimates ( Fig. S26 ). Timing of peaks differed by rhinovirus species.

Rotavirus

Rotavirus A is the predominant type with rotavirus C occasionally detected and rotavirus B being rare. 74 Rotavirus activity is typically elevated in winter-spring. 35, 38 Rotavirus A and C r t estimates were highest in winter/spring. Rotavirus B was rarely detected among sites except Ottumwa.

Aichivirus (AiV)

AiV has no known distinct seasonality. 75 AiV r t estimates were generally stable most of the year.

Astrovirus (AstV)

AstV has previously exhibited elevated activity in late winter/spring. 76 r t estimates for AstV were generally highest in fall.

Sapovirus

Sapovirus has previously exhibited elevated activity in colder months. 77 r t estimates for sapovirus were generally highest in fall.

Parvovirus B19

Parvovirus B19 community transmission has been increasing in the United States following decreased transmission during the COVID-19 pandemic. 78 Parvovirus B19 was rarely detected across sites.

Human bocavirus (HBoV)

HBoV generally peaks in late autumn/winter. 79 Relative to other viruses, HBoV r t estimates did not widely fluctuate but were generally highest in fall.

Human metapneumo virus (HMPV)

HMPV generally peaks in late winter/spring. 19, 34 HMPV r t estimates were generally > 0 in fall/spring.

Estimating time-varying effective reproduction number ( R t )

When we estimated daily r t and R t from two or more surveillance data sources, we assessed concordance among the time-varying estimates between all pairs of data sources. We used three complementary concordance metrics: (1) mean absolute error (MAE), (2) agreement on binary (AOB), and (3) correlation. MAE ( Equation7) quantifies numerical agreement between two time series. AOB ( Equation8) assesses agreement on epidemic direction between two time series; we used thresholds ɸ of 0 (AOB0) and 1 (AOB1) for r t and R t , respectively, as these thresholds are used to assess whether an epidemic is growing or shrinking for each metric. In Equations 7-8 , y represents the derived time-varying estimate each day t from data source a and b and n represents the total number of concurrent days between these same data sources. Correlation evaluates whether two time series are temporally associated. Given that time series estimates may not always be normally distributed, we used Kendall's tau correlation to calculate correlation coefficients. Positive tau values indicate positive temporal association between the daily time-varying estimates derived from data source a and b whereas negative tau values indicate negative temporal association.
