Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 2 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Introduction

Enteric pathogens are a leading cause of the global burden of disease, with the greatest burden falling on young children in low-resource settings. 1 Although this burden is often recognised through diarrhoeal illness and diarrhoea-associated mortality, many enteric infections in early childhood are subclinical. 2 These infections may nevertheless have lasting consequences, including malnutrition, linear growth faltering, 3, and impaired cognitive development. 5 Preventing exposure is difficult because enteric pathogens move through overlapping transmission pathways, including waterborne, foodborne, person-to-person, and other faecal-oral routes that vary across social and ecological contexts. Nutrition may also influence infection risk, as undernutrition can compromise gut barrier function and increase vulnerability to enteric infection. 6 As a result, it remains difficult to determine which pathogens may be most affected by water, sanitation, and handwashing (WASH), and nutrition interventions, and at what point in early childhood those effects emerge.

The WASH Benefits Bangladesh cluster-randomised trial provided an important opportunity to examine these questions. The trial evaluated individual and combined WASH and nutrition interventions on child growth and diarrhoea, with children followed from birth to approximately 24 months. 7, 8 Prior analyses showed that combined WASH interventions reduced caregiver-reported diarrhoea by 31-38%, Giardia infections by 27-30% (aged ~31 months), 9 and soil transmitted helminth infection by 10% (~30 months for index children and 7 years for non-index children) 10 using stoolbased testing. Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing in a sub-study at age ~14 months demonstrated that the WASH interventions reduced viral pathogen infections, but found no differences in bacterial or protozoan pathogens. 11 These results suggest that WASH may interrupt multiple faecaloral transmission pathways, but stool-based testing at limited time points provides an incomplete picture of cumulative pathogen exposure and intervention effects over time. A richer characterization of pathogen-specific transmission could help clarify whether WASH affected enteric infection in Bangladesh and why this trial showed stronger intervention effects than contemporaneous trials of similar interventions fielded in Kenya and Zimbabwe. 12, 13 Here, we report results of multiplex IgG antibody testing for 12 enteric pathogens in the Bangladesh birth cohort. Our objective was to estimate the effects of improved WASH and nutrition interventions on enteric pathogen seroconversion during the first two years of life, a key window for enteric pathogen seroconversion in high transmission settings. 14 Because IgG responses integrate exposure over time, serology offers a complementary approach for measuring pathogen-specific force of infection in trials with infrequent specimen collection. 15 We hypothesized that WASH could reduce seroconversion by interrupting environmental and person-to-person transmission. We also hypothesized that the nutrition intervention could reduce seroconversion through two mechanisms: exclusive breastfeeding through six months followed by continued breastfeeding with complementary food through 24 months could reduce enteric pathogen exposure and, if exposed, may lead to a more robust innate immune response, pre-empting a systemic IgG response. We hypothesized that there would be no interaction between WASH and nutrition interventions for most of the pathogens. A companion trial conducted in Kenya addressed the same study objectives. Methods

Study design and participants

The WASH Benefits Bangladesh study was a seven arm, cluster-randomised trial conducted in rural districts of Gazipur, Mymensingh, Kishoreganj, and Tangail in central Bangladesh (figure 1a). The study targeted communities with low groundwater iron and arsenic and no major ongoing or planned WASH or nutrition programs. The area is predominantly agricultural, and households are commonly organised into compounds where multiple families share a courtyard and may also share a pond, water source, and latrine. Pregnant women in their second or third trimester were eligible to enrol if they planned to remain in the study village for the following two years, regardless of where they delivered.

Only one pregnant woman was enrolled per compound; in the case of twin births, both children were enrolled. Details of the study design and rationale 7 , and primary outcomes results on diarrhoea and child growth 8 have been published previously. Study enrolment occurred between May 2012 and July 2013.

The trial included six intervention arms: water treatment (W), sanitation upgrades (S), handwashing promotion (H), nutrition, combined WASH, combined WASH + Nutrition, and a double-sized control arm. The water intervention included chlorine tablets and a safe drinking water storage vessel. The sanitation intervention included improvements via a double-pit latrine with a water seal, child potties, and hoes for faeces disposal at the compound level. The handwashing intervention included handwashing stations placed near the latrine and kitchen, stocked with detergent soap and dispensers.

Within the combined intervention arms, the sanitation intervention was implemented first, followed sequentially by handwashing and water treatment. The nutrition intervention consisted of ageappropriate infant and young child feeding counselling plus lipid-based nutrient supplements provided twice daily from 6 to 24 months of age. Interventions were initiated during pregnancy so that children were exposed from birth. The trial's environmental enteric dysfunction (EED) sub-study assessed the effects of interventions on EED among a subsample of children who underwent intensive biological sampling 16 . EED sub-study clusters were selected according to the feasibility of specimen collection and transport to a central laboratory. The EED sub-study included children in the control, combined WASH, Nutrition, and combined WASH + Nutrition. For this analysis, we used a 2x2 factorial design across these four arms to preserve statistical power and maintain a consistent definition of the WASH intervention package.

Randomisation and masking

In the WASH Benefits Bangladesh trial, compounds were enrolled within 720 clusters, grouped into geographically matched blocks of eight clusters 7 (figure 1a and 1b). Within each block, the trial randomly allocated clusters to one of the seven arms. For the EED sub-study, a total of 450 clusters were randomly allocated to the four arms: WASH, WASH + Nutrition, Nutrition and double-sized control arm.

Because the interventions had distinct visible components, neither participants nor field staff could be masked to assignment; however, data collection and intervention delivery were conducted by separate teams. Here, to minimise analytic bias, an investigator (PAA-T) performed masked statistical analyses from the raw datasets, using a re-randomised, uninformative assignment variable in place of the true group labels until final estimates were generated.

Children in the EED sub-study provided longitudinal blood specimens at three visits, with median ages of approximately 3, 14, and 28 months 16 , from December 2012 after intervention initiation through March 2016 (figure 1). In addition, dried blood spots (DBS) were collected from non-EED children in the birth cohort at the final study visit, at a median age of approximately 22 months. For the primary endpoint analysis, we included all children with available DBS at the final study visit, combining EED children measured at visit 3 with non-EED children measured once at their final visit; the overall median age at this endpoint was approximately 24 months. Throughout the paper, we refer to visit 3 or final visit analysis as the 24-month/primary analysis and visit 2 as the14-month/secondary analysis. DBS were collected on TropBio filter paper, with six extensions, each calibrated to absorb approximately 10 μL of whole blood. These were then dried at ambient temperature overnight, individually sealed with desiccant in plastic bags, and stored at -80°C at the International Centre for Diarrhoeal Research, Bangladesh (icddr,b) before being shipped to the Kenya Medical Research Institute (KEMRI) at ambient temperature and stored at -80°C.

One dried blood spot extension of approximately 10 μL from each participant was eluted to 1:40 overnight at 4°C with 200 µl of Buffer B (containing 1X phosphate buffer saline (PBS), 0.50% polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), 0.80% polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP), 0.30% Tween-20, 0.02% sodium azide, 0.50% casein and 3 µg/mL Escherichia coli extract). Following elution, samples were diluted in Buffer B to a final serum dilution of 1:400 prior to analysis using a multiplex bead-based assay. 17 Serum dilutions were centrifuged in a microcentrifuge at 4,000 RPM for 5 minutes immediately before use to pellet any residual E. coli extract particulates. Bloodspot dilutions were then incubated with antigen-coupled microsphere beads, and fluorescence was measured using a Bio-Plex 200 instrument equipped with Bio-Plex Manager 6.1 software (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA). Targets (antigen-coupled beads) included Norovirus (GI.5 and GII.3, GII.4, GII.6, GII.17 virus-like particles), Campylobacter previously. 18 Each assay plate contained duplicate wells for Buffer B-only to serve as blanks, as well as one negative control serum dilution and four pooled positive control sera with known antibody reactivity at varying concentrations for quality control purposes. Results were expressed as median fluorescence intensity minus background (MFI-bg).

Quality assurance procedures included monitoring positive control performance using Levey-Jennings plots across 77 plates; plates were repeated if control values fell outside ±2 standard deviations of the established mean. Control samples were run in duplicate to evaluate within-plate and between-plate variability. Plates were flagged for repeat testing if control coefficients of variation exceeded 20% or if bead counts were consistently low (fewer than 20 beads per analyte).

Seropositivity cutoffs

Antigen-specific seropositivity cutoffs were defined using Receiver-Operating-Characteristic (ROC) analysis of known positive/negative reference specimens for protozoan and helminth targets (Cp17, Cp23, VSP-5, LecA, Es33, T24) or using a longitudinal pre-exposed cohort approach for bacterial and viral pathogens. 19 In the pre-exposed cohort approach, we used IgG responses from all timepoints measured and identified incident IgG increases of ≥100-fold from an initial low value (≤300 MFI-bg).

We enforced the large increase between measurements to reduce the possibility of including measurements that were mid-boost since the timing of blood collection was independent of infection.

Pre-boost values were taken to represent the seronegative distribution, and cutoffs were set as mean + 3 SD of pre-boost log10 IgG (imputing MFI-bg ≤0 to 1). Cutoffs were derived using both WASH Benefit trial cohorts (Bangladesh, Kenya) because samples were processed identically in the same lab using the same bead couplings and reagents (supplementary figure 1). For pathogens with multiple antigens in the assay, children were classified as seropositive if positive for any antigen.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was IgG seroprevalence at visit 3 of all eligible children (~median age 24 months) (figures 1c and 1d). We evaluated seroprevalence for the 12 enteric pathogens: Norovirus GI/GII, Campylobacter, LT-ETEC, Shigella/EIEC, non-typhoidal S. enterica, typhoidal S. enterica, V. cholerae, Cryptosporidium, Giardia, E. histolytica, T. solium, and S. stercoralis. Seroprevalence at visits 2 and 3 was interpreted as a measure of cumulative incidence of seroconversion. Enteric pathogen seroprevalence and seroconversion rates coincide in this narrow age range during the window primary infection in high transmission settings since IgG responses reflect past exposure and persist beyond acute infection. 14, 20 Secondary outcomes included IgG seroprevalence at visit 2 (~median age 14 months) among children enrolled in the longitudinal EED sub-study, and the count of seropositive pathogens per child (range of 0 to 12).

Statistical analyses

Sample size was determined by the number of available banked specimens. For pooled factorial comparisons, we estimated minimum detectable effects assuming 80% power, two-sided significance level of 0.05, an intracluster correlation coefficient of 0.15, and 110 clusters per comparison group, with an average of eight children per cluster. Conservatively assuming 50% seroprevalence in the control group, the minimum detectable relative reduction was approximately 20%, corresponding to an absolute reduction of 10 percentage points. These calculations were based on the number of clusters in the EED sub-study and did not incorporate potential gains in precision from geographic matching. 21 We conducted an intention-to-treat analysis based on the 2x2 factorial design: WASH (WASH and WASH + Nutrition) versus no WASH (Nutrition and control) and Nutrition (Nutrition and WASH + Nutrition) versus no Nutrition (WASH and control). Across all samples tested, we estimated age-and visit-specific IgG seroprevalence by intervention group. We conducted a complete-case analysis in which the primary analysis was restricted to children with available DBS specimens at the final study visit, at a median age of 24 months, and the secondary analysis was restricted to EED sub-study children with available DBS specimens at visit 2, at a median age of 14 months.

We estimated marginal average seroprevalence and risk differences at visit 3 (median age 24 months) with a linear regression model with main effects for WASH and Nutrition interventions and indicators for randomization blocks. We used clustered-robust standard errors to account for repeated measurements within each study cluster. We assessed WASH x Nutrition interaction with an interaction term in the model and if there was no evidence of additive interaction (P>0.1, pre-specified), we pooled arms to estimate each main effect separately to improve precision. The main effect for each intervention was averaged over the levels of the other intervention. Conditional means and effects were reported in cases of interaction between WASH and nutrition interventions. We computed two-sided permutation p-values by permuting assignments within randomization blocks (10,006 replicates) and controlled the false discovery rate across 12 pathogens using a Benjamini-Hochberg approach allowing for 5% false discovery.

We repeated the analysis at visit 2 among EED sub-study children (median age 14 months) to assess early intervention effects. The EED sub-study was not balanced by randomisation block, so models excluded block indicators but otherwise followed the primary analysis approach. Lastly, we estimated differences between groups in the number of pathogens seropositive at the final visit using linear regression with WASH and Nutrition indicators, randomization-block indicator, and cluster-robust standard errors, testing for interaction and pooling arms when appropriate as in the primary analysis.

Results

Study population and characteristics

The trial randomised 720 clusters and enrolled 5551 pregnant women living in 5551 compounds. The pre-specified analysis of IgG outcomes (Zenodo: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23671136) focused on the four factorial WASH and Nutrition arms, including 450 clusters and a total of 4817 IgG measurements, including 2453 collected at the primary endpoint (median age of 24 months) (figure 2).

At the primary endpoint, DBS samples were available from 180 clusters (897 children) in the control arm, 90 clusters (534 children) in the WASH arm, 90 clusters (495 children) in the Nutrition arm, and 90 clusters (527 children) in the WASH + Nutrition arm. At the visit 2 secondary endpoint, 1370 IgG measurements were available from children in the EED sub-study. Baseline characteristics were well balanced across intervention groups with minimal differences (table 1, supplementary table 1).

Intervention adherence

Adherence to the WASH intervention was high and consistent through the entire follow-up period, with markedly higher coverage of WASH indicators in compounds that received WASH interventions (supplementary table 2). Adherence to the nutrition intervention was also high, with children in the arms that included the nutrition intervention consuming 93-94% of expected sachets at years 1 and 2 follow-up (supplementary table 2).

Age-specific IgG seroprevalence

IgG seroprevalence curves show that enteric pathogen transmission was intense during the first years of life (figure 3). Maternal IgG contributions were evident for many pathogens resulting in a decline in seroprevalence during the first 6-12 months before a subsequent increase due to incident infections.

Due to the longevity of IgG responses, seroprevalence curves likely approximate cumulative incidence of seroconversion during this early age window. For the highest transmission pathogens, Norovirus GI/GII, LT-ETEC, and Campylobacter, over 90% of children had seroconverted by age 24 months and there was no evidence for reductions due to WASH at any age. In contrast, age-seroprevalence curves rose more slowly for several enteric pathogens among children that received improved WASH, reflecting lower transmission due to the WASH intervention. This was most clear for Shigella/EIEC, Giardia and non-typhoidal S. enterica (figure 3). Patterns in age-seroprevalence were broadly similar when stratified by the Nutrition intervention (supplementary figure 3).

Intervention effects at the primary endpoint

For pathogens without evidence of WASH x nutrition interaction, marginal comparisons at the final visit (median age ~24 months) were consistent with reductions in seroprevalence to Shigella/EIEC (-4.5%, 95% CI -7.6% to -1.5%), non-typhoidal S. enterica (-4.0%, 95% CI -6.5% to -1.5%), V. cholerae (-0.8%, 95% CI -2.2% to 0.5%), Cryptosporidium (-2.9%, 95% CI -6.5% to 0.7%), and Giardia (-3.4%, 95% CI -6.7% to -0.2%), though differences were not significant at the 5% level after correcting for multiple testing (figure 4). Analyses to estimate the effect of nutrition intervention showed no marked differences between groups in pathogen-specific seroprevalence at the final visit (supplementary table 3, supplementary figure 2).

Secondary outcomes

Secondary analyses that compared groups at visit 2 (median age 14 months) showed lower seroprevalence to non-typhoidal S. enterica among children that received WASH (-5.0%, 95% CI -8.4% to -1.6%, multiple comparison adjusted p-value = 0.05) but no other marked differences (figure 5). For the effect of nutrition, age-seroprevalence curves were consistent with a modest delay in the increase in IgG seroprevalence for Shigella/EIEC, Giardia and non-typhoidal S. enterica (supplementary figure 3), with largest conditional effect difference between groups (nutrition vs control) for non-typhoidal S. enterica (-6.4%, 95% CI -9.8% to -3.0%, supplementary table 4, supplementary figure 4). The number of seropositive enteric pathogens detected at 24 months were broadly similar between children in all intervention groups (median of 5 pathogens seropositive), with slight reductions among children who received the WASH intervention (mean difference -0.1, 95% CI -0.19 to -0.03) (supplementary figure 5).

Discussion

In this longitudinal analysis of the WASH Benefits Bangladesh trial, IgG seroprevalence increased sharply by age 2 years for many enteric pathogens, consistent with high transmission and accumulating pathogen exposure during early childhood. This study helps fill an important gap in WASH trials: stoolbased PCR provides evidence of active infection at time of sampling, but infrequent stool collection can miss intermittent, short-duration or earlier infections. By measuring IgG responses to 12 enteric pathogens, we were able to characterise cumulative pathogen-specific exposure and estimate how WASH and nutrition interventions affected force of infection over the first two years of life. Over 94% of children 24 months old were seropositive for the highest transmission enteropathogens, including Norovirus GI/GII, LT-ETEC, and Campylobacter, with no benefit provided by WASH or nutrition interventions. Improved WASH led to modest reductions in seroprevalence to Shigella/EIEC, nontyphoidal S. enterica, Cryptosporidium and Giardia. These results are consistent with an overall reduction in enteric pathogen transmission and caregiver-reported symptomatic diarrhoea among children born into WASH compounds, even though a majority of children in intervention compounds were IgG seropositive to ≥5 pathogens before their second birthday. Improvements in household WASH conditions likely reduced enteric pathogen transmission broadly, and may have delayed the timing of primary infections, but did not entirely prevent enteric pathogen infection during the study. The PAASIM and MapSan Mozambique controlled trials found no overall neighbourhood and compoundlevel WASH effect on enteric pathogen infections at 12 and 24 after intervention, respectively, in an urban setting. 22, 23 However, exploratory analysis provided suggestive evidence of reduced Trichuris and Shigella infections among children born into the study by 24-month follow up visit suggesting that WASH may have delayed pathogen exposure and accumulation of enteric infections. 22 These findings add to the broader body of evidence from the WASH Benefits Bangladesh trial by showing how serology can complement single-timepoint stool-based assessments of enteric pathogen infection. Earlier analyses using PCR 9,11 and microscopy 10 found mixed effects of WASH across pathogens, with limited reductions in most bacterial and some parasitic infections but evidence of reductions in selected enteric viruses, including norovirus. This IgG analysis suggests modest WASHassociated reductions in cumulative exposure to selected bacterial and protozoan pathogens, including Shigella/EIEC, S. enterica, Cryptosporidium, and Giardia. Together, these findings demonstrate that WASH can interrupt multiple faecal-oral transmission pathways and may reduce transmission across broad pathogen taxa. However, the magnitude of these differences was generally modest, consistent with household-level interventions being protective but insufficient to substantially disrupt enteric pathogen transmission in this setting. The timing of infection also appears important: WASH effects on enteric pathogen infection outcomes were more apparent during the second year of life, when children likely have greater mobility, environmental contact, and cumulative exposure risk. However, this pattern should be interpreted cautiously because the visit 2 analysis was limited to the EED sub-study and had lower statistical power.

The reduction in Shigella/EIEC IpaB seroprevalence associated with improved WASH contrasts with a recent analysis that found no effect of combined WASH + Nutrition on Shigella/EIEC IgG immune repertoire development measured through a bacterial display assay. 24 Differences could arise from differences in analytic sample and assay. The prior analysis compared the combined WASH + Nutrition arm with the control arm in a subset of 120 children, whereas this analysis used a larger subset of the birth cohort (n=2453 samples) and compared children who received WASH with those who did not within the factorial design. Additionally, the prior analysis used peptide arrays to tile epitope-level IgG responses across several proteins (IpaA, IpaB, IpaC, IpaH) with relatively muted IpaB response and may have not been able to distinguish the modest reduction in transmission measured here on the Luminex platform. Further analyses using the bacterial display assay found poor alignment with the Luminex IgG responses for proteins with conformational epitopes with additional (potential) challenges of non-specific epitope binding, though overall age-dependent patterns for high transmission Norovirus GI/GII were broadly consistent between the assays. 25 Our findings also highlight both the value and the limitations of serology for measuring enteric pathogen transmission in early childhood. IgG responses integrate exposure over a longer period and can provide a clearer picture of the cumulative force of infection through age-seroprevalence curves. This was particularly informative for pathogens with substantial levels of seroconversion that had not yet saturated near 100% by age 2 years, such as Shigella, Cryptosporidium, and Giardia. For example, prior PCR-based analyses found that Cryptosporidium infections were too rare at the final visit to support robust arm comparisons 9 , whereas IgG responses provided sufficient information to compare cumulative exposure across intervention groups. However, the same feature that makes IgG useful for capturing cumulative exposure can limit interpretation for pathogens with extremely high force of infection, where asymptomatic carriage of multiple enteric pathogens is common and children may be repeatedly exposed over time. 26, 27 Prior serologic studies, including work on norovirus in young children in low-resource settings, have similarly shown that cumulative exposure can be substantially higher 2, 28, 29 than suggested by PCR prevalence at a single visit. In our study, the high Norovirus GI/GII seroprevalence (~80%) at 14 months contrasted with lower PCR point prevalence (~23%) 11 , underscoring how serology can provide a broader picture of cumulative burden. Thus, PCR may be preferable for detecting short-term reductions in infection for very high-transmission pathogens, while serology may be especially useful for estimating cumulative burden and intervention effects on pathogens for which seroprevalence remains in a more intermediate range, below 100%.

The nutrition findings should be interpreted alongside prior trial results showing a ~40% reduction in diarrhoea in the nutrition-alone arm. 8 Although the nutrition package included lipid-based nutrient supplements and breastfeeding promotion, which could plausibly reduce exposure or susceptibility to enteric infection, we found no broad reductions in pathogen-specific seroprevalence. Breastfeeding was already highly prevalent across groups at follow-up, leaving limited scope for the intervention to further increase breastfeeding practices. The most pronounced reduction was for non-typhoidal S. enterica seroprevalence at the 14-month visit, near the end of the breastfeeding promotion, while effects for other pathogens were small and not statistically significant. Together with no evidence of reduction in PCR-detected enteric pathogens due to nutrition alone 9, 11 these findings suggest that the large reduction in diarrhoea in the nutrition group may reflect courtesy bias.

Our findings support previous evidence that children remained highly exposed to faecal contamination and enteric pathogens 30 despite good adherence to household WASH interventions 31 and some reduction in diarrhoea or enteric infections. 32 These findings raise questions about whether basic household WASH interventions can adequately interrupt diverse transmission pathways, including foodborne, animal faecal, person-to-person and broader environmental exposure routes. 33 The results also underscore the importance of directly measuring enteric pathogen infection, rather than relying only on behavioural, process or environmental indicators or self-reported health outcomes. This study has several limitations. Serologic measures provide integrated markers of cumulative pathogen exposure, but they do not indicate active infection, pathogen shedding, or the precise timing of infection because IgG responses may persist after exposure. Seroprevalence may also reflect variation in host antibody responses, not exposure alone. In particular, nutrition, breastfeeding, and immune development potentially related to the early-life microbiome may alter systemic antibody responses following exposure; however, microbiome composition was not measured in this study. Serologic saturation for highly prevalent pathogens also limited our ability to evaluate intervention effects for pathogens with very high transmission intensity. DBS measurements may be affected by variation in specimen quality, storage conditions, or antibody recovery, although laboratory qualitycontrol procedures were used to reduce potential measurement error. Since DBS specimens were unavailable for some eligible children, the complete case analysis assumed outcomes missing completely at random. We also assessed IgG responses, and some mucosal pathogens are non-invasive (such as V. cholerae) and largely induce IgA and IgM responses. 34 Induction of systemic IgG responses in such cases often requires repetitive exposures, and as such our results may underrepresent force of infection compared to those relating to invasive enteropathy (example S. enterica, Campylobacter, Shigella/EIEC). In addition, the trial was conducted in low-resource rural communities in Bangladesh, and cluster selection based on logistical feasibility may limit generalizability to settings with different transmission intensity, intervention coverage, or environmental conditions.

This study also has important strengths. We leveraged a rigorously conducted cluster-randomized trial with high intervention adherence and longitudinal follow-up during a critical period of early childhood.

Multiplex IgG testing provided an efficient way to assess overall pathogen transmission and pathogenspecific impacts of the interventions in a trial with infrequent specimen collection. By complementing prior PCR and microscopy analyses from the same trial, this study provides new insight into how WASH may affect cumulative pathogen exposure over time.

In conclusion, longitudinal multiplex serology provided new evidence on early-life exposure to multiple enteric pathogens in the WASH Benefits Bangladesh trial. Although WASH effects were modest and pathogen-specific, the generally protective pattern for multiple bacterial and protozoan pathogens suggest that improved WASH may reduce cumulative exposure to some enteric pathogens over time, but insufficient to substantially disrupt transmission in this high-exposure setting. These findings support the use of serology as a complementary tool for evaluating enteric pathogen transmission and intervention effects in large-scale trials, particularly low-to-medium prevalence pathogens, while underscoring the need to combine serologic and stool-based measures to capture both cumulative exposure and short-term infection dynamics.
