Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. Abstract

Background: High vascular transcriptomic discrimination may obscure dependence on comparison populations, gene selection, and score scale. We examined these properties separately.

Methods: GSE100927 compared surgical atherosclerotic tissue with lesion-free deceased-donor arteries: 104 arrays formed 95 complete-label groups, versus 96 arteries reported originally; independent donor identities remained unresolved. Within held-out folds, pooled area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve (AUROC) was decomposed into nine case-bed-control-bed comparisons. Seven fixed rules underwent ten repeated three-fold splits. The primary ridge model used sample-wise gene ranks and training-only selection of 200 genes. External tests involved 32 paired plaque/intact patients in GSE43292 and 29 early/advanced specimens in GSE28829. Post-result explorations added random and disjoint gene-panel controls, feature stability, frozen-score accounting, source-only threshold learning, and individual-failure analyses.

Results: Primary mean held-out-fold pooled AUROC was 0.9968, versus a within-bed mean of 1.0000 and contrast -0.0032; the empirical ceiling constrained this contrast to be nonpositive. Paired external AUROC was 0.877 (conditional 95% interval 0.792-0.947), with plaque ranked higher in 29/32 patients. Histological AUROC was 0.986 (0.942-1.000). All primary external probabilities fell below 0.5. Both source-derived thresholds also gave zero external sensitivity. Twenty outcome-independent random panels retained source AUROCs of 0.9932-0.9992, while external ranking varied substantially. Across 30 primary fits, 619 genes were selected at least once and 45 every time. Fixed-score failure analyses distinguished shared from model-specific discordance.

Conclusions: Strong source ranking did not establish a unique gene signature or transferable technical thresholds. 1 Introduction

Panvascular research seeks to understand disease across anatomically distinct vascular territories. Transcriptomic studies have identified both shared and bed-associated expression patterns in human arteries, providing a basis for investigating common disease signals and local biology [1]. Recent conceptual frameworks also emphasize this dual requirement. FLOW proposes integrating heterogeneous vascular beds within a systemic modeling framework, whereas the tri-ecological account of device-vessel matching makes the relevant biological context dependent on the particular bed, lesion, and patient [2, 3]. These proposals motivate explicit tests of transfer; they do not establish that a molecular classifier trained in one tissue comparison will retain its meaning in another.

A recent immune-world-model preprint evaluates intervention-dependent state transitions across biological scales using computational benchmarks [4]. Our static comparison-population audit examines a complementary measurement problem and does not test intervention forecasts or clinical efficacy.

The availability of public arterial expression cohorts has already enabled multi-bed biomarker studies. Wu and colleagues combined arterial datasets to derive candidate genes, while Cui and colleagues evaluated C1Q-related signatures across carotid and other arterial tissues [5, 6]. Consequently, another gene list or another high pooled AUROC using these cohorts would provide limited independent information. A separate question concerns the comparisons that create the reported performance: which diseased specimen is being ranked above which comparator, and under what anatomical and sampling distribution? AUROC can be interpreted as the probability that a positive observation receives a higher score than a negative observation, with half credit for ties [7]. In a multi-bed cohort, that probability combines comparisons within the same bed and comparisons between different beds. Its anatomical composition is therefore part of the estimand. Related work on matched classification studies shows why the comparison population must be considered when interpreting accuracy [8]. This issue is distinct from training leakage: correctly nested feature selection does not by itself determine whether the resulting comparisons answer the intended biological question.

A second distinction separates ranking from the transport of a decision threshold. A model may preserve tissue ordering after a change of platform, recruitment, or comparator while shifting its entire score distribution. Moreover, a healthy-donor comparison, plaque versus relatively intact tissue from the same patient, and early versus advanced histology represent different endpoints. Treating them as interchangeable external replications can obscure both preserved information and important failures.

We therefore conducted an anatomical comparison-population audit. The primary question concerned the difference between pooled disease-versus-donor discrimination and within-bed discrimination; the prespecified contrast was pooled AUROC minus the equally weighted mean of the three within-bed AUROCs. An exact nine-cell decomposition under a common held-out-fold scoring model evaluated that comparison. Anatomical holdouts, patient-paired lesion ranking, and an external histological contrast then examined distinct transport questions. The protocol identified strong diagonal discrimination with a narrow reweighting range as evidence against marked composition sensitivity in the evaluated samples, without specifying a one-sided positive-inflation hypothesis or an equivalence margin. Preserved external ranking accompanied by threshold failure would identify a separate limitation of score transport. 1 and 16 advanced lesions [11]. Early lesions included pathological intimal thickening or intimal xanthoma; advanced lesions included thin-or thick-fibrous-cap atheroma. Public metadata did not establish an independent donor count. This dataset tested a cross-sectional histological contrast, not longitudinal progression. 2.3 Expression processing and common vocabulary

Official processed matrices, sample metadata, and platform annotations were downloaded from GEO on 25 October 2026, with source URLs and SHA256 hashes retained. The deposited GSE100927 matrix contained 47,264 probe rows before our mapping and filtering; the source paper described a 20,000-probe filter. This study therefore analyzed the deposited processed matrix anew rather than exactly reimplementing that filtered analysis. Probes were kept only when their annotation mapped unambiguously to one gene symbol. Multiple eligible probes for a gene were summarized by the median within each sample. The common vocabulary contained 15,235 genes present in the three platform annotations and available matrix probes. Thus, target input availability informed the vocabulary, although target expression distributions and outcome associations did not inform feature selection. This is declared use of target input information, not a claim of completely untouched inductive development.

The primary representation was each sample's fractional gene ranks within that vocabulary. Ranks were calculated before median aggregation of repeated complete-label groups. No gene-level missingness remained in the analyzed matrices, so fitted imputation was unnecessary. For the native-expression sensitivity, all matrices were retained on their deposited scale because their values already occupied a finite log-intensity range; no additional logarithmic transformation was performed. Gene centering and scaling were fitted on each training set and applied unchanged to its held-out or external data. These operations did not reverse the original normalization. GSE100927 had been normalized against an across-array reference; the Affymetrix datasets had undergone cohort-level RMA processing. Consequently, the study did not reproduce a prospective raw-array pipeline in which every preprocessing operation was learned without held-out samples. Source-only secondary scaling and selection address a narrower leakage risk. Methodological work has shown that batch and source confounding can remain problematic even when cross-validation is otherwise carefully organized [12]. We did not pool cohorts for batch correction, fit a target transformation, or recalibrate on external outcomes.

The five expression-based specifications were evaluated in each of the three leave-one-bed-out tasks and in both external cohorts; the prevalence and bed/sex controls were restricted to mixed-bed evaluation.

In each anatomical holdout, the four trainable specifications used only the other two beds, while the fixed C1Q mean-rank score was applied directly. The primary ridge model additionally underwent six directional single-source-bed transfers and control-proxy exclusion analyses. The latter excluded source controls sharing a numeric control suffix and sex with target controls; this proxy was not treated as authenticated linkage. For external evaluation, the four trainable specifications were fitted once to all 95 source label groups and applied unchanged to both targets, alongside the fixed C1Q score.

In GSE43292, the principal external statistic was the fraction of patients whose plaque score exceeded their intact-tissue score, with half credit for ties. It differs from marginal AUROC, which compares every positive tissue with every negative tissue. Patient clustering was retained when resampling the latter, consistent with the need to distinguish clustered from independent ROC observations [16]. We used 2,000 patient-block bootstrap draws for conditional intervals and an exact binomial interval for pair concordance when there were no ties. GSE28829 intervals used 2,000 class-stratified specimen bootstrap draws; unresolved donor dependence was not incorporated.

Additional metrics were average precision, sensitivity, specificity, balanced accuracy at the unchanged score threshold 0.5, and Brier score for probability-producing models. The threshold was a technical operating point, not an established clinical decision rule. Brier scores described the selected tissue samples; no population-risk calibration or clinical net benefit was inferred. Bootstrap intervals were conditional on the fitted source model and excluded training, feature-selection, annotation, and preprocessing uncertainty. At empirical boundaries, fixed-score resampling can give degenerate intervals: sensitivity [0, 0] when every observed positive scores below 0.5, specificity [1, 1] when every observed negative scores below it, and AUROC [1, 1] under perfect observed separation. These intervals reflect the support of the observed scores and do not establish population certainty.

Eight prespecified sensitivity specifications changed feature count to 50 or 500, ridge C to 0.01 or 1, representation to native expression, or identity handling to exclusion of all ambiguous records, all 104 arrays with complete-label grouping in folds, or possible-shared-control proxy grouping. Negative controls reran selection, scaling, and fitting for 99 within-bed label permutations and 99 unrestricted permutations. They diagnosed pipeline behavior under different label nulls; they were not tests of anatomical causation. Thirty stratified 80% subsets, using within-stratum flooring, each contained 75 label groups and reran the primary pipeline. Their ranges provided separate fitting-stability evidence, not confidence intervals.

2.7 Post-result exploratory extensions

After inspecting the original results, we recorded an extension on 26 October 2026 to examine representation specificity, feature stability, score geometry, source-only threshold selection, and individual failures. These analyses were not part of the pre-fit primary plan. A preliminary threshold based on ten-repetition averaged source scores had already been inspected and is disclosed in the extension record; it is distinct from the first-repetition recipe below. The original model, primary estimand, and external predictions were retained.

We drew 20 sets of 200 genes from the 15,235-gene vocabulary without consulting outcomes, using seed 20261026. The same sets were used throughout the original 30 folds and in full-source external fits. A disjoint control selected training-set feature ranks 201-400, excluding that fit's top 200. All controls used the original scaling, ridge penalty, class weighting, and splits. Panel-level fold means and their distribution describe variation across representations, not independent cohorts or confidence intervals. No externally favorable panel was selected for promotion. We also replayed the 30 original primary fits to recover membership and coefficients, requiring agreement with their saved held-out scores. Pairwise Jaccard overlap and selection frequency were descriptive; we did not train a new "stable" signature using all-fold selections.

Ranking metrics for the new logistic controls used unrounded linear logits. Independent checking found that one random panel returned probabilities rounded to 1 for every GSE43292 tissue, creating artificial ranking ties. A dated correction applied logit-based AUROC, average precision, and pair ordering uniformly to all new controls. Probabilities remained the inputs to threshold and Brier diagnostics. The pre-correction outputs were retained; this numerical correction neither changed the random panels nor selected a model from target outcomes.

For the unchanged full-source primary model, we decomposed each logit into its intercept and the sum of coefficient-weighted, source-standardized feature values. Source predictions in this display were in-sample references, not validation estimates. We summarized class-specific contributions and the fraction of selected values with absolute source-standardized z > 3, a descriptive displacement measure rather than a validated out-of-distribution detector. Named-feature displays used the largest absolute full-source coefficients, without target-outcome selection. Exact paired logit differences additionally cancel the intercept and source center; their attribution describes the classifier, not a causal mechanism.

Two technical thresholds were learned from source cross-fitted scores: maximum Youden J and the largest candidate attaining source sensitivity at least 0.90. Candidates were adjacent distinct-score midpoints plus 0 and 1. Youden ties within 10 -12 were resolved by proximity to 0.5, then the smaller threshold. Full-source thresholds used one held-out score per group from the original first three-fold repetition and were applied to the original frozen external probabilities. Scores from three separately fitted fold models were pooled only for threshold learning; this procedure remains sensitive to foldto-fold scale and does not replace the within-fold AUROC estimand. For each anatomical holdout, a separate inner three-fold cross-fit was confined to the other two beds; the outer fit then scored the excluded bed. Source sensitivity constraints are optimization criteria, not clinical targets or unbiased source performance estimates. Target threshold-response curves were descriptive, and no target-best rule was adopted.

Finally, we displayed ordering signs for all 32 patient pairs under five expression rules. Fixed-score deletion of both tissues from each patient assessed influence on marginal AUROC and pair concordance; deletion of individual GSE28829 specimens assessed a separate histological contrast. These operations did not refit a model and are not cross-validation or confidence intervals. Failures selected for detailed contribution displays were explicitly retrospective. Supplementary Figures S1 and S2 show additional fold-level cell variation and repeated source-score behavior from the original saved results.

3 Results

3.1 Source structure and identity uncertainty

The 104 deposited source arrays produced 95 complete-label groups under the primary convention (Figure 1). Nine repeated-label pairs had rank-profile correlations of 0.9835-0.9924. This similarity was consistent with closely related profiles but could not identify the duplication mechanism or authenticate a donor. Sex composition also differed across disease strata: male proportions were 74% in carotid, 96% in femoral, and 77% in infrapopliteal disease groups, compared with 80% in each control stratum. These are properties of the recorded groups, not estimates of disease prevalence.

3.2 The primary model did not show pooling inflation

Primary mean held-out-fold pooled AUROC was 0.9968, with ten-repetition means ranging from 0.9939 to 0.9985. Mean within-bed AUROC was 1.0000, giving ∆ mix = -0.0032; repetition values ranged from -0.0061 to -0.0015. The prespecified contrast therefore showed pooled discrimination slightly below within-bed discrimination, with no positive pooling inflation in these evaluated samples (Figure 2). All three diagonal AUROCs were 1 in every primary-model fold, so A pooled ≤ 1 mathematically constrained ∆ mix = A pooled -1 ≤ 0. Its sign is consequently not independent evidence of population-level anatomical invariance. The mean cross-bed share of comparison pairs was 66.8%; the observed pooled AUROC remained 0.0032 below the diagonal mean.

The nine-cell mean matrix localized the small shortfall: femoral cases compared with carotid controls had mean AUROC 0.9766, and infrapopliteal cases compared with carotid controls had 0.9967; the other cells were 1.0000. Mean per-fold mixture extrema were 0.9743 and 1.0000, giving a mean span of 0.0257. The extrema of the mean matrix were instead 0.9766 and 1.0000, and the smallest individual fold cell was 0.8750. Individual primary cells contained only 12-36 case-control pairs sharing observations. These results support strong observed within-bed separation, not a formal claim of anatomical equivalence. The separate pooled AUROC after averaging each label group's ten held-out primary-model scores was 0.996923; the supplement distinguishes this descriptive value from the primary equal-fold mean of 0.996775.

The estimand distinction was more apparent for some simpler controls. Bed/sex logistic regression had pooled AUROC 0.5375 and within-bed mean 0.4709. C1Q scores had corresponding values 0.9244 and 0.9482; the six-gene model had 0.9693 and 0.9926. Centroid and forest pooled AUROCs were 0.9860 and 0.9931, respectively, with within-bed means near 1.0000. The prevalence intercept remained at 0.5000. Primary balanced accuracy was 0.9549 and Brier score 0.0358 in the source evaluation.

3.3 Anatomical ranking transported more consistently than thresholds

For the primary model, leave-one-bed-out AUROCs were 1.000 for carotid, 0.996 for femoral, and 1.000 for infrapopliteal targets. Corresponding balanced accuracies were 0.850, 0.820, and 0.962 (Figure 3). The six directional single-bed transfers had AUROCs from 0.980 to 1.000, but balanced accuracies ranged from 0.500 to 0.962. In particular, carotid-to-infrapopliteal transfer achieved AUROC 1.000 while assigning every target score below 0.5.

Excluding possible shared controls removed eight or nine source controls in the leave-one-bed-out scenarios. The primary AUROCs remained 1.000, 0.996, and 1.000; balanced accuracies changed to 0.950, 0.800, and 0.885. Directional exclusions removed four or five controls. These analyses show sensitivity of operating characteristics to source composition while leaving the uncertainty about donor linkage unresolved.

3.4 Paired lesion ranking and the unchanged technical threshold diverged

In GSE43292, the frozen primary model achieved marginal AUROC 0.877 (conditional 95% interval 0.792-0.947) and average precision 0.870. Plaque scored above intact tissue in 29 of 32 patients, with no ties: concordance 90.6% (exact 95% interval 75.0-98.0%). The mean paired probability-score difference was 0.00728 (patient-bootstrap interval 0.00187-0.01665). Three patients had reversed ordering (Figure 4).

All 64 primary scores were below 0.5: 0/32 plaque tissues were classified positive and 32/32 intact tissues negative. Sensitivity was therefore zero, specificity 1.000, balanced accuracy 0.500, and Brier score 0.4926. The degenerate fixed-score bootstrap intervals at these boundaries do not establish future sensitivity or specificity with certainty. Other models illustrated that ranking and operating-point behavior were separable. C1Q marginal AUROC was 0.828; six-gene, centroid, and forest AUROCs were 0.846, 0.873, and 0.874. The six-gene model retained sensitivity 0.938 with specificity 0.531, whereas forest sensitivity was 0.125 with specificity 1.000. No threshold metrics were assigned to C1Q or centroid scores.

3.5 Histological discrimination did not establish progression prediction

In GSE28829, primary advanced-versus-early lesion AUROC was 0.986 (conditional specimen-bootstrap interval 0.942-1.000), with average precision 0.989. Unknown donor dependence was not incorporated in this interval. Again, every score was below 0.5: 0/16 advanced specimens were classified positive and 13/13 early specimens negative, producing sensitivity zero, specificity 1.000, balanced accuracy 0.500, and Brier score 0.4182 (Figure 5). C1Q, six-gene, centroid, and forest AUROCs were 0.952, 0.971, 1.000, and 0.846. The centroid's perfect observed ordering and the boundary threshold metrics have degenerate empirical bootstrap intervals; these do not establish zero uncertainty outside these specimens. This contrast contains no longitudinal observations from which to infer disease progression.

3.6 Specification and pipeline checks

Mean pooled AUROC across the eight alternative specifications ranged from 0.9937 to 0.9981; all mean composition contrasts remained nonpositive (Figure 6). Excluding every ambiguous-label record left 86 arrays and mean pooled AUROC 0.9937. Retaining all 104 arrays with complete-label groups held together yielded 0.9958; conservative control-proxy grouping yielded 0.9957. Native-expression AUROC was 0.9972.

Within-bed and unrestricted permutation means were 0.5027 and 0.5057, with maxima 0.7154 and 0.6853, respectively. Both maxima were below the observed first-repetition mean AUROC of 0.9985. Across 30 complete-pipeline subset runs, pooled AUROC ranged from 0.9853 to 1.0000 and within-bed mean AUROC from 0.9947 to 1.0000. These diagnostics support reproducible source ranking under the tested perturbations; they do not remove original-study selection, processing, or identity limitations.

3.7 Random panels preserved source discrimination but differed after transport

The exploratory representation controls exposed information not apparent from source model comparisons alone (Figure 7). The 20 random panels had mean-fold source AUROCs of 0.9932-0.9992 (median 0.9961), spanning the primary value 0.9968. The disjoint next-ranked panel yielded 0.9955. These results show that high source discrimination was not unique to the particular top-200 panel in these data; random feature choice is not a label-permutation null, because the selected genes can still contain real or confounded class-associated information.

External random-panel AUROCs ranged from 0.6123 to 0.8623 in GSE43292 and from 0.5337 to 0.9904 in GSE28829. Their medians were 0.7671 and 0.8317. The next-ranked panel yielded 0.8379 and 0.8558, respectively. Thus, similar near-ceiling source performance concealed much wider variation under changed tissue comparisons. These descriptive draws do not establish an optimized or universally superior signature. For the saturated random panel, raw-logit AUROC was 0.7207 and pair concordance 29/32 despite all 64 probabilities rounding to 1; this illustrates why numerical score precision must be distinguished from biological ranking failure.

3.8 Stable ranking coexisted with variable selected-gene membership

Across the 30 recovered primary fits, 619 different genes were selected at least once; 14,616 were never selected, 103 appeared in at least 24 fits, and 45 appeared in all 30 (Figure 8). The 435 pairwise feature-set Jaccard values ranged from 0.3468 to 0.7167, with mean 0.4850. These overlapping fits are dependent, so the distribution is not an uncertainty interval. Agreement between recovered and original held-out predictions verified that this display describes the original fitting procedure. Membership and coefficient direction of source-selected named features were shown without reusing their stability to build a new classifier. High source ranking therefore did not imply a uniquely identified molecular signature.

3.9 Frozen-score accounting localized scale displacement without identifying its cause

The same full-source model placed mean logits at 0.2808 in the source in-sample reference, -8.3072 in GSE43292, and -3.4185 in GSE28829 (Figure 9). Because overall means depend on class composition, the figure also separates every cohort's two classes. The intercept plus signed feature contributions exactly reconstructed these logits. Across the selected 200 genes, the proportion of gene-sample values with absolute source-standardized z > 3 was 0.12% in the source, 23.21% in GSE43292, and 21.71% in GSE28829. These are fractions of correlated feature values, not percentages of independent patients or clinical error rates. They document displacement relative to source scaling while leaving platform, procurement, normalization, and comparator changes inseparable.

3.10 Source-only threshold learning did not restore external sensitivity

The first-repetition source cross-fit produced thresholds of 0.6173 for maximum Youden J and 0.6922 for the source sensitivity constraint. Both remained above every primary-model probability in both external datasets, yielding zero sensitivity and balanced accuracy 0.500, as did 0.5 (Figure 10). Nested anatomical evaluations showed that threshold effects were context-dependent: maximum-J balanced accuracy changed from 0.850 to 0.800 in carotid, from 0.820 to 0.880 in femoral, and remained 0.962 in infrapopliteal targets. The corresponding sensitivity-constrained values were 0.850, 0.780, and 0.962. Thus, these particular source-only recipes did not solve external transport; the experiment does not establish that every possible source recipe must fail or that target recalibration would be clinically valid.

3.11 Patient-level failures were partly shared across scoring rules

All five expression rules ranked plaque above intact tissue in 26 of 32 patients; at least one rule reversed the ordering in the remaining six (Figure 11). Primary ridge reversals involved deposited patient identifiers 12, 17, and 21. Fixed-score deletion of one known patient changed primary marginal AUROC to values between 0.8689 and 0.8959 and pair concordance to 0.9032-0.9355. Deleting one histology specimen yielded primary AUROC 0.9844-0.9949. These ranges describe sensitivity to individual records, not uncertainty bounds or additional validation. In the source repeated predictions, 87 groups had no 0.5-threshold error, five had errors in some repetitions, and three were misclassified in every repetition (Supplementary Figure S2). Exact contribution displays for the three reversed patient pairs expose the fitted score arithmetic without attributing failure to an unmeasured clinical characteristic.

4 Discussion

This study separated three properties often compressed into a single external AUROC: the anatomical composition of comparisons, preservation of tissue ordering, and transfer of an operating threshold. For the fixed primary model, the prespecified comparison placed pooled AUROC 0.0032 below the equally weighted within-bed mean. Leave-one-bed-out ranking also remained high. A separate limitation concerned score transport: both external contrasts retained ranking information while the unchanged 0.5 threshold classified every primary-model target tissue as negative.

The composition contrast describes how the comparisons contributed to the observed statistic. Approximately two thirds of held-out case-control pairs crossed beds, yet the nine-cell decomposition localized the small departure from perfect separation to particular off-diagonal comparisons. Because the observed diagonal mean was already 1, a positive ∆ mix was mathematically impossible in these primary-model folds. The nonpositive contrast therefore cannot by itself demonstrate population-level mixture invariance; the cell matrix and reweighting span supply complementary descriptive information.

A high pooled result warrants an estimand audit, while near-ceiling source data limit resolution for smaller composition effects and cannot establish absence of anatomical effects in other populations, disease stages, or measurement systems.

Our analysis does not introduce a new AUROC formula or demonstrate that shared vascular signatures are novel. Earlier work has already explored both multi-bed gene discovery and C1Q-related cross-cohort discrimination [5, 6]. The contribution is the combination of an explicit empirical comparison-population decomposition, transparent identity conventions, and separately interpreted anatomical and tissuecomparator stress tests. The source results favor a strong transferable ranking signal within this selected donor-versus-surgical-tissue setting. The repeated-label alternatives help show that its magnitude did not depend entirely on one grouping convention, while stopping short of proving patient independence.

The paired external analysis adds a different biological perspective. Marginal AUROC 0.877 and 29/32 concordant patient pairs both indicate retained plaque-associated information, but they answer different questions. The first aggregates comparisons across all plaque and intact tissues; the second asks whether the lesion is ranked higher within the same person. The latter reduces some between-person differences by design, but the relatively intact sample remains tissue from a patient with established vascular disease. Neither statistic establishes diagnostic accuracy for an asymptomatic population or identifies an intervention-responsive molecular state.

The unchanged technical 0.5 boundary assigned every specimen to the negative class in one source-study arterial-bed transfer and in both changed external contrasts. The exploratory source-only threshold experiment extended this observation beyond an arbitrary single cutoff: two source-derived rules also had zero external sensitivity, while their effects differed across anatomical holdouts. These are failures to separate changed tissue labels, not failure tests of a validated clinical rule for an unchanged endpoint. A balanced, penalized classifier's probability is not automatically an individual's clinical risk. Target intercept adjustment or recalibration would answer a different question; neither was performed or established as clinically effective.

The random-panel and feature-membership results further constrain molecular interpretation. Nearperfect source discrimination survived outcome-independent feature sampling and replacement by a disjoint ranked panel, while external rankings and selected-gene sets varied. A broad expression contrast can therefore make a particular panel appear compelling without showing that it is necessary or biologically specific. The present controls do not distinguish a distributed disease-associated program from cellular composition, procurement, or other shared class differences. Likewise, coefficient accounting connects numerical displacement to actual prediction failures but does not establish the biological cause of that displacement. Both observations favor evaluating representations against explicit alternatives before assigning significance to named genes.

Earlier breast-cancer expression analyses provide a methodological precedent for comparing named signatures with size-matched random gene sets [18]; those results do not validate vascular biology or the present classifier.

The external differences cannot be attributed specifically to comparator definition. Platforms, source normalization, tissue handling, recruitment, sampling, and lesion spectrum changed together. Samplewise ranking removed a common within-sample scale dependence but could not remove gene-specific platform behavior or make different tissue definitions exchangeable. Strong ordering after transport is therefore evidence of an association shared across these particular contrasts, not proof of a universal vascular representation. Poor threshold transport likewise cannot identify which component of the domain change was responsible.

This distinction matters for broader panvascular and interventional proposals. The full-watershed perspective recognizes organ-specific vascular architectures within a systemic framework [19]. Our findings are compatible with retaining that local context when evaluating molecular measurements. They do not validate a device-vessel compatibility score. Similarly, proposals linking intravascular imaging and digital twins emphasize multimodal interpretation and acknowledge unresolved generalization across vascular regions [20]. A tissue classifier supplies neither a patient-specific dynamical model nor evidence that choosing an interventional device from its output improves outcomes. Such applications would require relevant imaging, hemodynamics, longitudinal measurements, and prospective clinical evaluation.

Several limitations govern the strength of the evidence. First, the source unit discrepancy is unresolved: 104 deposited arrays, 95 complete labels, and 96 arteries reported in the original methods cannot be treated as equivalent counts. Unknown cross-bed donor overlap may remain after all tested conventions. Second, source-wide original normalization means that training-only secondary operations do not deliver a fully inductive raw-data validation. The common vocabulary also used target annotation and probe availability. Third, surgical versus deceased-donor procurement is inseparable from class in the source data and remains so under arterial-bed holdout. Bulk expression may reflect cellular composition, tissue collection, preservation, and other unrecorded factors; a sample-level preservation map was unavailable. The analysis neither isolates the contribution of these factors nor identifies a causal molecular mechanism.

Fourth, the external sample sizes were modest. GSE43292 uncertainty respected known pairing, whereas GSE28829 donor dependence could not be reconstructed. Fixed-score bootstrap intervals omit source-model and preprocessing uncertainty. Perfect empirical ordering and boundary sensitivity or specificity can produce degenerate intervals, which do not imply population certainty. Subset refitting addressed a separate source-stability question without supplying a comprehensive confidence interval. Fifth, small source strata and near-ceiling discrimination limit resolution for distinguishing models. Fixed baselines reduced selection flexibility but do not constitute an exhaustive comparison of modern representation learning. Finally, prior access to public cohorts and publication-informed panels limits claims of untouched external validation. The random-panel, stability, threshold, geometry, and influence analyses were added after original results and must be interpreted as exploratory. Their internal records, including the sigmoid-saturation correction, improve traceability without converting them into preregistered confirmation.

The appropriate next step is to evaluate the same estimands in datasets with authenticated donor identifiers, explicitly separated acquisition batches, and comparable tissue definitions. Raw-data prospective preprocessing and truly locked multicenter sampling would test whether source ranking survives beyond the present archive. If a clinical application is proposed, the target population, decision threshold, and consequences of errors should be specified independently of these selected tissue labels.

In conclusion, pooled AUROC was slightly below the equally weighted within-bed mean in this anatomical comparison-population analysis. Source and external tissue ranking remained strong while the unchanged technical threshold did not separate either changed external contrast. Exploratory random-panel and membership analyses further showed that strong source ranking did not identify a unique gene signature. Reporting these properties separately, together with the empirical ceiling, sample-identity uncertainty, and preprocessing provenance, gives a more defensible account of vascular transcriptomic transport than pooled AUROC alone.
