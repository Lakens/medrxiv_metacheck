Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

run_results$checked says what the run of each script shows about the code, in five groups, so that a script that does not reproduce is kept apart from one the check could not test: ran (it ran to the end); code_error (it did not reproduce: it stopped with an error of the code or the deposit, could not be read, or needs a file the deposit does not hold); dependency (a package or program was not available: a package that could not be installed or was not loaded, a program outside R and Python, a system library, or R crashing in a package’s compiled code); time (it did not finish within the time limit for one script, or was not started because the time for all scripts had run out); and not_checkable (it could not be checked automatically: it needs a person, the internet, a key or another setting of the authors’ computer, one platform such as Google Colab, Windows or OpenSAFELY, more memory than the run allows, a file that was not downloaded, a screen, or a program that runs it, or it is a file of a package of the code that Python runs only as a module of that package). It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Objectives. Online reviews constitute a source of information on the experience of users in community pharmacies. The way in which pharmacists engage with them remains poorly documented. The objective was to describe the frequency, determinants, and content of the responses provided by French pharmacy teams to reviews published on Google Maps. Methods. Cross-sectional observational study. Public reviews from nearly all French community pharmacies (approximately 20 000) were collected automatically between September and October 2026. For each review, the rating, the text of the review, the time since its publication, and the text of the response, if it existed, were collected. Factors associated with the response were studied using logistic regression. Sentiment of the reviews and the tone of the responses were automatically coded according to a predefined grid. The presence of reviews generated by artificial intelligence, as well as potential interventions on the ratings, were explored. Results. In total, 1 008 024 extractions (729 520 reviews and 278 504 responses) concerning 19 985 pharmacies were analysed. A response was present for 38% of reviews and 67.5 % of pharmacies had responded at least once. The response rate was higher for negative reviews (one or two stars) than for positive reviews (five stars) (adjusted OR 1.35; 95 % CI [1.33-1.38]; adjusted risk difference +3.6 percentage points; 95 % CI [3.3-3.8]), and these responses were about three times as long (adjusted geometric mean ratio 3.03; 95 % CI [3.01-3.06]). The tone of the responses was very widely judged to be appropriate. Comments generated by artificial intelligence seem to be appearing progressively and represent approximately 3-4% in 2026. The removal of poor ratings appears to be marginal. Conclusions. Community pharmacists regularly and appropriately respond to online reviews, including, and in particular, less positive ones. These results could serve in the development of specific professional recommendations aimed at securing this practice.

Introduction

Online review platforms play a growing role in the choice of a healthcare professional or establishment (1) (2) (3) (4). Online reviews provide valuable information on patient experience, although they are unlikely to be suitable indicators of clinical quality or a sound basis for ranking healthcare professionals. (5). Among these platforms, Google Maps (Google LLC, Mountain View, CA, USA) allows users to associate a rating and freely written comments with each establishment. The community pharmacy, as a local business and a primary care setting, is not exempt from this public evaluation. With nearly 20 000 pharmacies in France (6), the pharmacy network constitutes one of the healthcare structure groups most exposed to online reviews. Several studies have used Google reviews to describe user experience in community pharmacies. In Saudi Arabia, an analysis of more than 17 000 reviews across 512 pharmacies shows that the propensity to write a review depends on the user's perception (7). In Canada, a study of pharmacy reviews in British Columbia identifies professionalism and waiting time as the main themes expressed (8). In China, the themes addressed in comments on online pharmacies mainly concerned logistics, the price of medicines, customer service, and the effect of the medications (9). These studies focus on what users say, but they do not provide information on what pharmacists do with this information (10) (11) (12) (13). Similarly, while European and French literature is beginning to take an interest in the digital transformation of health and the influence of review platforms on patient choice, research remains largely focused on the global perception of e-health and largely centered on content analysis of user comments, leaving the professionals' uptake of this information largely unexplored (14) (15) (16). To date, precise and documented national data are lacking on how community pharmacists in France actively manage their e-reputation, particularly through the writing of responses to comments, thus leaving an analytical gap regarding the communication strategies adopted by professionals in response to user feedback. The healthcare professional's response is an integral part of the exchange via these social platforms. Experimental data obtained for physicians indicate that the presence of a response mitigates the effect of negative reviews on users' choice intentions (4). However, responding exposes the healthcare professional to a specific risk. Confirming that a person visited the establishment, or mentioning their care, may constitute a breach of professional secrecy, even when the user has made these elements public themselves (17). In France, the pharmacist is bound by professional secrecy and ethical rules governing their communication. The code of ethics for pharmacists was completely overhauled in March 2026 and recognises digital tools as information and communication supports while maintaining safeguards (18). The pharmacist may communicate by any means, including the internet, regarding their skills, their background, and their conditions of practice. This communication must be fair and honest, without third-party testimonials and without compar-2020- 21 ison with other pharmacists. Concepts of fair communication and professional dignity imply, for example, that the tone of responses, especially to negative reviews, affects the dignity of the profession. In this context, it is interesting to provide an overview of how pharmacy teams appropriate online reviews. To our knowledge, this work has not been described quantitatively and no national-scale study has been conducted. The main objective of this work is to estimate the proportion of Google Maps reviews that received a response from the pharmacy in France. Secondary objectives are to identify the characteristics of the reviews and pharmacies associated with the existence of a response, to describe the tone and content of these responses, and to explore the appearance of comments generated by conversational tools as well as potential data manipulations.

Materials and methods

Study design and data

This was a cross-sectional, descriptive, and analytical observational study based on publicly accessible online data. The 20 003 community pharmacies were identified in the extraction of the open data National File of Health and Social Establishments (Fichier National des Établissements Sanitaires et Sociaux FINESS) published on data.gouv.fr (version dated 9 September 2026). Their Google Maps reviews were collected automatically between September and October 2026: rating (one to five stars), text, age of the review, and, where applicable, the text and age of the pharmacy team's response, as well as the average rating of each profile were collected. Duplicates were removed (Supplementary Methods S1).

Description of responses

The analyses focused on reviews containing a readable textual comment. A review was considered to have received a response when the latter contained at least one word. The response rate (Wilson 95 % CI) and the length of responses in number of words (median and interquartile range, IQR) were calculated for France as a whole and by region (Supplementary Methods S2.1).

Primary endpoint

The primary endpoint was the presence of a response from the pharmacy team to a review. Reviews were classified as negative (one or two stars) or positive (five stars). The association between this polarity and the probability of response was estimated using a mixed-effects logistic regression, with a random intercept per pharmacy, adjusted for the length and age of the review, the pharmacy's average rating, and its region. The results are expressed as adjusted odds ratios (OR, 95 % CI) and adjusted risk difference (percentage points). The length of the responses was compared between negative and positive reviews using a linear mixed model on the logarithm transform of the number of words, with the same adjustments, and expressed as an adjusted geometric mean ratio (aGMR) (Supplementary Methods S2.2). For the three panels, the denominator consists of reviews containing a textual comment. Raw, unadjusted indicators.

Review rating

Nature and tone of responses

The review-response pairs were automatically coded by a large language model (LLM) run locally (Gemma 4 (19), 12 billion parameters; temperature 0; constrained JSON output), without data transmission to a third party. For each pair, the model coded the sentiment of the review, as well as the primary function of the response (a grid of thirteen inductively constructed categories, Table 1), its tone, the appropriateness of this tone for the review, and its degree of personalisation. The analysis of the response tone was conducted on a sample stratified by rating of 81 953 pairs; global estimates were weighted by the inverse of the sampling fractions (Supplementary Methods S3).

Detection of AI-generated reviews

A binary classifier (human vs AI) was obtained by fine-tuning pre-trained BERT encoders on a balanced corpus sampling from 103 338 human reviews published before 2021 (prior to the public large adoption of ChatGPT (20)) and 80 000 reviews generated by four open-source LLMs, either created de novo (80 %) or rewritten from human reviews (20 %). The corpus was split by pharmacy into training, validation, and test sets (70/15/15 %). Six encoders were compared. To limit incorrect AI attributions, the decision threshold was calibrated to aim for a false positive rate of 1 %. The selected model, BERT large, was applied to reviews published after 2021, and the proportion of flagged reviews for each pharmacy was compared to this baseline (Supplementary Methods S4).

Estimation of removed one-star reviews

In Germany, Google Maps indicates in brackets the number of reviews removed following complaints of defamation. We used aggregated data (rating histogram and mention of removal) from 3 430 German pharmacies to construct and validate a model of the expected distribution of ratings in the absence of removal. The expected number of one-star reviews for each pharmacy was predicted from two-to-fourstar reviews (anchor category) using a Dirichlet-multinomial model fitted on listings without a mention of removal, and then compared to the observed number (deficit score). The model was validated by comparing listings with and without mentions, and then applied to French pharmacies (Supplementary Methods S5).

Results

Data description

In total, 729 520 reviews concerning 19 985 community pharmacies were analysed. Among all the reviews, 278 504 (38.2 %) had received a response from the pharmacy team and 67.5 % of the pharmacies had responded at least once.

The number of comments per pharmacy varied strongly by region (Figure 1A). The highest medians were observed in Île-de-France (32 comments per pharmacy; IQR 0-58), Provence-Alpes-Côte d'Azur (27; IQR 7-51) and Auvergne-Rhône-Alpes (26; IQR 10-47). The lowest were observed in Pays de la Loire (12; IQR 0-35) and Bourgogne-Franche-Comté (15; IQR 5-28), and across the overseas regions (medians between 6 and 9). The distribution was highly skewed, with some pharmacies accumulating several hundred comments (up to 1 540 in Île-de-France). The distribution of ratings was bimodal, with a characteristic J-shape and a very clear imbalance in favour of positive reviews (Figure 1B). Five-star reviews represented 69 % of the reviews (506 956) and one-star reviews 18 % (130 562), while intermediate ratings were rare: two stars 3 %, three stars 2 %, four stars 8 %. The average rating was 4.08/5 and the median rating was 5/5. Although increasing, the flow of reviews remains generally quite modest, with median numbers of a few comments per year per pharmacy (Figure 1C).

Response rate and response length

Among reviews containing a comment, the response rate was 38 % for France as a whole, and the median length of responses was 21 words (Figure 2A). The metropolitan regions formed a homogeneous group, with response rates between approximately 33 and 43 % and median lengths of 18 to 25 words. The overseas regions were distinguished by lower response rates (approximately 13 % in Mayotte, 22 % in Martinique, 28 % in Guadeloupe and 31 % in La Réunion), but by longer responses (median lengths of 27 to 37 words). The width of the confidence intervals, however, calls for caution for these regions due to their small sample sizes. The raw response rate varied little according to the review rating (Figure 2B): it was 38 % for one-star reviews, 39 % for two stars, 33 % for three stars, 32 % for four stars and 39 % for five stars. Reviews with intermediate ratings therefore received a response slightly less often. This profile was generally found in most regions, with significant dispersion between regions. In contrast, the length of responses decreased markedly with the rating (Figure 2C): the median fell from 44 and 42 words for one-and two-star reviews to 34 words for three stars, and then to 18 and 17 words for four and five stars.

Factors associated with response

For equal characteristics and within the same pharmacy, negative reviews (one or two stars) were more frequently responded to than positive reviews (five stars) (adjusted OR 1.35; 95 % CI [1.33-1.38]). The probability of response increased with the length of the review: a doubling of the number of words was associated with a 17 % increase in the odds of response (OR 1.17; 95 % CI [1.16-1.18]). Conversely, it decreased with the age of the review, with the odds of response falling by 19 % per year (OR 0.81; 95 % CI [0.81-0.82]). Higher-rated pharmacies responded more often: each additional point of average rating multiplied the odds of response by 2.51 (95 % CI [2.27-2.77]). Regional differences persisted after adjustment.

Compared to Auvergne-Rhône-Alpes (reference), the propensity to respond was lower in most regions, particularly in Corsica (OR 0.46; 95 % CI [0.25-0.86]), Grand Est (OR 0.61; [0.50-0.76]), Centre-Val de Loire (OR 0.66; [0.50-0.87]), Brittany (OR 0.69; [0.54-0.90]), and Hauts-de-France (OR 0.71; [0.58-0.87]). The gap was more moderate in Normandy (OR 0.75; [0.58-0.97]), Île-de-France (OR 0.79; [0.66-0.95]), Occitanie (OR 0.80; [0.65-1.00]), and Nouvelle-Aquitaine (OR 0.81; [0.66-0.99]). Pharmacies in Provence-Alpes-Côte d'Azur, Pays de la Loire, and Bourgogne-Franche-Comté did not differ significantly from those in the reference region. The most significant effect, however, remained that of the pharmacy itself.

The variance of the random intercept was very high (σ2 = 8.24), which corresponds to a median odds ratio of 15.5: for the same review, the odds of response differed by a median factor of 15.5 between two randomly selected pharmacies. The proportion of response variability attributable to the pharmacy (intraclass correlation coefficient) reached 71 %. Compared to this heterogeneity, the effects of review polarity, length, and age were modest. The decision to respond therefore seems to depend primarily on a policy specific to each pharmacy.

Detection of reviews written by LLMs

AI-written review detector

A classifier was trained on two classes (human vs AI) (Figure 4A) on a training set of 20 000 reviews sampled from human reviews (reviews written before 2021) and reviews generated by four open models: Mistral 7B, Qwen 2.5 7B, Llama 3.1 8B, and Gemma 4 12B (Supplementary S4). The decision threshold was calibrated on the validation set for a false positive rate of 1 %. Six encoders were compared, each with five random seeds: CamemBERT (base and large), CamemBERTa-v2, ModernCamemBERT, EuroBERT-210m, and UltraBERT-150M (Supplementary Table S1 and Supplementary Figure S3). The best performing, camemBERT large, achieved an AUC of 0.99 on the test set. At the learned threshold, its sensitivity is 90.4 % with an observed false positive rate of 1.05 %. As expected, the prediction is less performant on short reviews (≤ 15 words) and on reformulations (Supplementary Figure S2).

Evolution of the proportion of flagged reviews by rating

The classifier was applied to 625 585 pharmacy reviews (Figure 4B). From 2021 to 2023, the proportion of flagged reviews remained between 0.8 and 1.9 % depending on the rating, close to the false positive rate set during calibration. It subsequently increased for all ratings starting from 2024. In 2026, it reached 3.6 % for 5-star reviews, 3.3 % for 2-star, 3.0 % for 1-star. The increase appears slightly stronger for 5-star reviews and for 1-and 2-star reviews. Intermediate ratings remained the least affected. AI is therefore likely used primarily at the two extremes of the scale, for both glowing reviews and complaints. It should be noted that these rates combine true and false positives and therefore do not directly measure actual prevalence. The latter clearly remains mod-est. Finally, the years of posting are approximate, as they were reconstructed from time-classes since publication displayed by Google, and the year 2026 is incomplete.

One-star review deficit Sample of German pharmacies.

To study one-star review deficits-a proxy for pharmacy team intervention to manipulate ratings-we analyzed the rating histogram of German pharmacies. In Germany, Google displays on each business profile a range indicating how many reviews were removed over the past 365 days following defamation complaints (e.g., "11 to 20 reviews"). This provides an external benchmark of review removal against which we can validate a deficit score. We fitted a model to the German data and applied it to French pharmacy data to explore potential rating manipulation. Among the data of the 3 430 German pharmacies collected, the histogram was most often reconstructed from the length of its bars (89 %), with a resolution of up to 488 reviews per bar unit; 335 of these listings (10.0 %) displayed a removal notice. The main analysis was restricted to the 1 819 pharmacies whose histogram resolution was at most 5 reviews per bar unit and which had at least one 2-to 4-star review. These pharmacies had a median of 125 reviews (interquartile range 82-178), and 232 of them (12.8 %) displayed a removal notice.

Reference model.

We built a Dirichlet-multinomial regression model that learns from pharmacies without a notice how many one-star reviews are expected relative to the number of 2-to 4-star reviews, a category assumed to be unaffected by removals (Supplementary S5). In this model, the 5-star reviews, genuine or not, play no role. For each pharmacy of the sample, the observed number of one-star reviews is compared with this expectation, and the resulting mid-p value is converted into a z-score that follows a standard normal distribution in the absence of removals. Fitted on the 1 587 pharmacies without a notice, the z-scores of reference pharmacies were centred close to zero (Figure 5A) but the model indicated strong between-pharmacy heterogeneity in the proportion of one-star reviews. Their mid-p values were not uniformly distributed (Figure 5B): values below 0.25 were under-represented and values between 0.55 and 0.85 over-represented. Accordingly, only 3.4 % of reference pharmacies had p < 0.05, fewer than the nominal 5 %. In addition, the z-score increased with the number of reviews (Spearman ρ = 0.42 in the reference population), including among pharmacies whose histogram gave one-star counts similar to the one expected (ρ = 0.54). From these observations, two conclusions follow. First, the test is conservative in the lower tail, so the deficits reported below are unlikely to be overstated. Second, the model overestimates the expected proportion of one-star reviews for small listings and underestimates it for large ones, so individual scores are not directly comparable between listings of very different sizes.

Comparisons between populations were therefore also made within a common volume band (see below).

Discussion

Main results

This study describes, on a national scale, the way in which French pharmacies respond to reviews published on Google Maps. Out of nearly 730 000 reviews from pharmacies across the entire country, slightly more than one in three reviews (38 %) had received a response, and two in three pharmacies had responded at least once. The decision to respond depended primarily on the pharmacy itself: the pharmacy explained 71 % of the response variability, far ahead of the characteristics of the review. For a given pharmacy, negative reviews received a response slightly more often than positive reviews, and responses approximately three times longer.

Responses to positive reviews were mostly brief and little personalised thank-you messages. Responses to negative reviews employed more varied registers: apologies, justification, and explanation of regulations. According to the automated coding, the tone was appropriate in more than threequarters of cases, but approximately one in eight responses to a one-star review was judged inappropriate. The proportion of reviews flagged as written by an artificial intelligence remained close to the detector's background noise until 2023, then doubled in 2026, while remaining modest, in line with other areas of online health (21). Finally, the analysis of German data, where Google flags removed reviews, shows that these removals leave a measurable trace in the distribution of ratings. This trace was not detectable across the population of French pharmacies.

Heterogeneous appropriation

The observed response rate indicates that responding to reviews has become a common practice in pharmacies, although it is not generalised: one third of pharmacies had never responded. The heterogeneity between pharmacies is the most striking result. For the same review, the response rate varied by a median factor of 15 between two randomly selected pharmacies. Responding is therefore less a reaction to the content of the review than a policy specific to each pharmacy: some respond to almost all reviews, others to none. This policy may reflect the time available, the digital appetite of the team, the size of the pharmacy, or the use of an external provider. Our data do not allow us to distinguish between these explanations. The highest-rated pharmacies responded more frequently. Several interpretations are possible and are not mutually exclusive: an attention paid to the online image that translates into both responses and a better rating, a specific effect of responses on subsequent reviews, or common characteristics (size, location, organisation) favouring both. The crosssectional design does not allow for a definitive conclusion. Similarly, the decrease in response with the age of the review is consistent with a recent diffusion of the practice, but also with the absence of retrospective responses to old reviews.

Responding to negative reviews

Negative reviews represent the primary challenge of responding: it is for these that a response can mitigate the effect of the review on readers (4), and it is also where the deontological risk is highest. Pharmacists seem to be aware of this: their responses to negative reviews were longer, more personalised and more frequently oriented towards explanation or apology. A portion of the dissatisfaction relates to legal constraints (prescription dispensing, substitution, reimbursement conditions) that the pharmacist cannot remove. The response then becomes a tool for education for the author and future readers. However, one in five responses to a one-star review had the primary function of defending or justifying oneself, and 12 % were judged inappropriate. A defensive or ironic response, or one that blames the customer, can damage the dignity of the profession which the code of ethics requires to be preserved (18). Above all, a response that corrects the facts carries the risk of disclosing elements of the patient's care. The response to a review is public and permanent, and the fact that the user themselves made their visit public does not waive professional secrecy (17). Our grid did not specifically code the disclosure of information covered by secrecy (22). Factual corrections (9 % of responses to one-star reviews) and justifications nonetheless constitute the situations where this risk is most likely. Conversely, the invitation to continue the exchange privately, which allows the complaint to be handled without public exposure, remained rare (4 %).

Influence of the emergence of conversational AI tools

The proportion of reviews flagged by the detector increased from 2024 across all ratings, reaching approximately twice the 2021-2023 level by 2026. As the threshold was calibrated for 1 % false positives, the excess attributable to reviews written or rewritten by an AI is in the range of 1 to 3 points depending on the rating. This emerging phenomenon therefore remains minority. The increase affected both ends of the scale: glowing reviews, which could correspond to complimentary reviews or satisfied users assisted in drafting, and one or two-star reviews, which suggest complaints formatted by an AI. These two uses do not have the same impact: a real review reformulated by an AI remains the testimony of an experience, whereas a review generated entirely from scratch reflects none. The detector does not allow them to be distinguished. The same tools are likely used by pharmacies to draft responses. We have not measured this. The high proportion of poorly personalised responses could reflect either preprepared response templates or automatically generated responses. An AI-generated response raises the same ethical questions as a response written by the pharmacist, for which the pharmacist remains responsible. Even when wellconfigured, AI tools are not immune to deviating from the rules (23). The risk of a standardised response, or a response that indiscriminately incorporates elements of the review, should be taken into account in any future recommendations.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study covers almost all French pharmacies, identified using a national database, and all accessible reviews. To our knowledge, this is the first national description of pharmacists' responses to online reviews. Mixed models allowed for the separation of effects specific to reviews from those specific to pharmacies. The coding of responses and the detection of AI-written reviews were performed using locally executed models, without data transmission to a third party. The code and prompts have been made available. This study has several limitations. Only reviews published and visible at the time of collection were observed. Reviews filtered or removed by the platform escaped analysis, which prompted the complementary analysis of German data. Furthermore, the tone, function, and adequacy of the responses were coded by a language model and not by human evaluators. The judgment of adequacy reflects the criteria defined in the prompts, rather than the perception of users or the regulatory body. Inadequate responses may be underestimated, as the model may have judged responses that were polite but problematic regarding professional secrecy as acceptable.

The detector for AI-written reviews was trained on texts produced by four open models. Its sensitivity to texts produced by commercial assistants, which are likely the most used, is unknown, and it is less performant on short reviews and reformulations. The estimation of the one-star review deficit relies on histograms largely reconstructed from bar lengths, on an assumption of stability for 2-to-4-star ratings, and on a model whose fit varies with the volume of reviews. Finally, we do not know who writes the responses (pharmacist owner, team member, service provider), nor whether responses modify subsequent reviews or the choice of users.

Perspectives

These results argue for professional support. A professional recommendation could specify what a response may and may not contain (22). It could also help in adopting a tone appropriate for negative reviews and provide a framework for the use of AI tools to draft responses. Several complementary studies would be useful: • a dedicated coding of potential breaches of professional secrecy or reglementation in the responses; • a study of the perception of responses by users; • a longitudinal follow-up on the evolution of practices, the proportion of reviews written by AI and the withdrawal of reviews; • a qualitative survey of pharmacists regarding their motivations, their organisation and their use of service providers or AI.

Conclusion

French community pharmacies have widely adopted the practice of responding to online reviews, but they have done so in a very heterogeneous manner: the decision to respond depends primarily on a policy specific to each pharmacy. Responses are most often tailored, remaining brief for positive reviews and more elaborate for negative reviews, yet a significant proportion of responses to the most critical reviews remain defensive or inappropriate, potentially posing a risk of breaching professional ethics. The emergence of AI-generated reviews and the possibility of having negative reviews removed further complete this picture. These results could serve to develop professional recommendations to secure these communication practices between community pharmacies and their patients.

S3. Automated coding of the nature and tone of the responses

S3. Automated coding of the nature and tone of the responses

S3.1. Categorization grid for functions

The functions of the responses were classified using a grid of thirteen categories that were mutually exclusive at the level of the main function (Table 1). This grid was constructed inductively, based on the reading of a sample of responses, and supplemented by themes described in the literature and by informal inquiries from health professionals. It covers the following registers: • relational: thank you, valorization of the team, apologies, invitation for a private exchange; • corrective: announcement of an improvement, factual rectification; • informative: explanation of regulations, practical information, education on the role of the pharmacist; • defensive: contesting the authenticity of the review, justification.

A "generic brief response" category grouped short and standardized responses without content specific to the review, and an "other" category allowed for the classification of responses that did not fit into any of the previous categories.

S3.2. Language model and parameterization

The coding of review sentiment and response categories was performed automatically by a large language model (LLM), and not by human coders. We used the Gemma 4 model (12 billion parameters, quantized version gemma4:12b-it-qat), run locally with the Ollama software (version 0.40.2): no data was transmitted to a third-party service. For each evaluation, the model received the review rating, its text, and the pharmacist's response, accompanied by an instruction that defined each category and the coding rules described below. Its output was constrained to a structured format (JSON) where the possible values were limited to the planned modalities. The temperature was set to 0 to make the coding deterministic. The pairs for which the model had not returned a valid output after three attempts were resubmitted during a second pass. Reviews without a response were classified as "no response" and were not submitted to the model.

S3.3. Coded dimensions and coding rules

For each evaluated review-response pair, the model coded seven dimensions: two concerning the review and five concerning the response (Table S1).

Main and secondary functions.

Each response was assigned a single main function, defined as its dominant function, and zero, one, or several secondary functions among the remaining twelve categories. The same category could not be both main and secondary: any duplicates returned by the model were removed. Thanking was to be retained as the main function only when the response was essentially limited to thanking; as soon as it also contained apologies, an explanation, or a rectification, that other function became the main one.

S4.6. Application to the corpus

S5. Modeling of removed low-rated reviews

S5. Modeling of removed low-rated reviews

S5.1. General principle

Platforms do not publish the reviews they remove. To estimate the scale of these removals, we used a situation where some of this information is visible: in Germany, Google Maps displays a mention on an establishment's profile indicating, in brackets, the number of reviews removed following complaints of defamation (" . . . Bewertungen wurden aufgrund von Beschwerden wegen Diffamierung entfernt "), over the last 365 days. These German data allowed for the construction and validation of a model of the expected distribution of ratings in the absence of removal. This model was then applied to French pharmacies, for which no such mention exists.

S5.2. Sample of German pharmacies

German pharmacies were identified using the official directory aponet.de, via a postal code search for the city centers of the 215 most populous cities in Germany. The 25 pharmacies closest to each search point were selected, and then duplicates and pharmacies without data were eliminated, totaling 3 430 pharmacies. For each, the corresponding Google Maps profile was searched using its address. When multiple profiles were suggested, the one whose name most closely resembled the aponet name was selected (string similarity after removing generic terms). Similarity matches below 0.6 were marked as doubtful and excluded.

The collection, carried out on October 31, 2026, with an automated browser (Playwright, German interface), only concerned aggregated data displayed publicly, without any review text: average rating, total number of reviews, distribution of 1 to 5-star reviews, and the text of the removal mention. This mention was converted into the bracket displayed by Google (none, 1, 2-5, 6-10, 11-20, 21-50, >50 reviews removed). Only profiles whose histogram contained at least 20 reviews were analyzed.

S5.3. Reconstitution of rating distributions

For each pharmacy, the distribution of ratings (1 to 5 stars) was recorded from the Google Maps listing, as well as any mention indicating that reviews had been removed over the last 365 days, along with the range of the number of reviews concerned (1, 2-5, 6-10, 11-20, 21-50, >50). When the displayed histogram did not directly provide the counts, the bar lengths were converted into proportions and then scaled to the total number of reviews, while preserving this total (largest remainder method). The resolution of this reconstitution was measured by the number of reviews represented by one bar unit. Listings without a legible histogram were excluded. The main analysis was restricted to listings where the resolution was at most 5 reviews per bar unit and which had at least one review of 2 to 4 stars.

S5.4. Deficit of one-star reviews

Principle.

Ratings were grouped into three categories: one-star reviews, an anchoring category grouping ratings from 2 to 4 stars, assumed to be minimally affected by potential deletions, and five-star reviews. The principle is to estimate, based on the anchoring category, the number of one-star reviews expected in the absence of deletions, and then to compare it to the observed number.

S2.1. Descriptive indicators

The descriptive analysis focused on reviews containing a textual comment. Reviews without text (rating only) or those whose text was not usable were excluded. A review was considered to have received a response when the "pharmacy team response" field contained at least one word. The length of each response was measured by the number of words, after space normalization, counting each sequence of non-whitespace characters as a word. Indicators were calculated for all of France and by administrative region. For each region, we recorded the number of pharmacies and the number of commented reviews. The response rate was defined as the number of reviews that received a response relative to the number of commented reviews in the region; it is presented with its 95 % confidence interval (95 % CI) calculated using the Wilson method. Among reviews that received a response, the length of the responses was described by the median and the interquartile range (IQR). These indicators are descriptive and do not account for the clustering of reviews by pharmacy.

Probability of response.

The unit of analysis was the review. The outcome measure was the presence of a response from the pharmacy team (yes/no). Reviews were classified as negative (one or two stars) or positive (five stars) (review polarity). The association between review polarity and the probability of response was estimated using a mixed-effects logistic regression, with a random intercept per pharmacy (FINESS number) to account for the non-independence of reviews from the same pharmacy. The model was adjusted for: • the length of the review (logarithm of the number of words plus one) ; • its age (in months, derived from the relative date displayed by Google) ; • the average rating of the pharmacy ; • its region.

S4. Detection of AI-generated content

To estimate the proportion of reviews written or rewritten by an LLM, we trained a binary classifier (human vs AI) on a corpus combining authentic reviews and generated reviews. These generated reviews included both entirely created reviews and AI-rewritten human reviews.

S4.1. Reference human reviews

Human reviews were drawn from the corpus by keeping only those published before 2021, more than a year before the date ChatGPT was made publicly available. Before this date, the use of an LLM to write a review can be considered negligible. The texts were normalized (Unicode NFC form, spaces and line breaks reduced to a single space). Reviews of fewer than three words and duplicates were excluded. We thus obtained 103 338 human reviews from 13 811 pharmacies.

S4.2. Generation of AI reviews

In total, 80 000 reviews were generated using four open-source LLMs from different families, executed locally with Ollama (version 0.40.2): Mistral 7B, Qwen2.5 7B, Llama 3.1 8B, and Gemma 4 12B (QAT version). The use of multiple model families aimed to prevent the classifier from learning the style of a single generator. For each review, the generator and a reference human review were drawn at random, and the sampling temperature was drawn uniformly between 0.6 and 1.2. Two generation modes were used: • Creation (80 %) : the model wrote a Google review for a pharmacy, using the star rating of the reference human review.

In 25 % of cases, the instruction was minimal, imitating a rushed user (« Write a Google review for a pharmacy, k stars »). In other cases, it specified a target length and one or two styles drawn from twelve: colloquial language, enthusiastic or annoyed tone, natural spelling mistakes, lack of capitalization, emojis, SMS abbreviations, elderly person unfamiliar with the internet, regular customer, mention of a specific detail (price, medication, employee's first name), comparison with another pharmacy, etc. Target lengths (one sentence, 2-3 sentences, 4-6 sentences, a paragraph of 8-12 sentences) were drawn with probabilities of 0.51, 0.32, 0.14, and 0.03, respectively, chosen to reproduce the length distribution of human reviews.

• Reformulation (20 %) : the model rewrote the reference human review according to an instruction drawn from four (clarify, correct mistakes, make more professional, make more convincing). This mode simulates "AI-assisted" reviews.

Post-processing.

The prompt requested that only the review text be produced. Typical LLM artifacts were then removed from the outputs, as they would have provided the classifier with trivial clues: introductory formulas (« Here is. . . »), reasoning tags, Markdown formatting, stars and ratings such as « 4/5 », and enclosing quotation marks. The same normalization as for human reviews was then applied.

S4.3. Constitution of datasets

From these two corpora (human and LLM), the two classes were balanced by random undersampling of the majority class, and then the corpus was split by groups into training, validation, and test sets (70/15/15 %). All reviews from the same pharmacy, as well as reformulations of a human review and their source review, always fell into the same set to avoid any information leakage between sets.

S4.4. Training and calibration

The classifier was obtained by fine-tuning a pre-trained encoder model for sequence classification (Transformers library, version 5.20.2). The hyperparameters are summarized in Table S2. The best checkpoint was selected based on the area under the ROC curve (AUC) in validation. Since falsely accusing a human author is the most costly error, the decision threshold was not set at 0.5: it was calibrated on the validation set at the 99 th percentile of probabilities assigned to human reviews, targeting a false positive rate of 1 %.

Model.

The counts n i = (n i1 , n i,anchor , n i5 ) for pharmacy i were modelled using a Dirichlet-multinomial distribution: where the anchor category serves as the reference (β anchor = 0) and the dispersion parameter ϕ i captures between-pharmacy variability beyond multinomial variability.

The covariates x i described the internal shape of the anchor category (smoothed log-ratios of 3-star to 2-star counts and of 4-star to 3-star counts) and its size (log(1 + n i,anchor )). The model was fitted by penalised maximum likelihood (L 2 penalty, L-BFGS-B algorithm) on a reference population: pharmacies whose listing carried no notice of review removal. Each reference pharmacy was evaluated using leave-one-out cross-validation, i.e. with a model refitted without it.

S5.5. Validation

Calibration was verified on the reference population: proportion of reported pharmacies, uniformity of the mid-p distribution, and absence of trend in the z score according to the number of reviews. S5.6. Sensitivity analyses

Four sensitivity analyses were conducted: 1. a conservative analysis, where each page receives the maximum number of one-star reviews compatible with the resolution of its histogram (read count increased by half a bar unit) ; 2. a restriction to pages whose histogram gave the exact counts ; 3. the analysis of all pages, without resolution criteria, using a model without covariates ; 4. an anchoring category restricted to 3 and 4-star ratings, to account for potential deletions of 2-star reviews.

S5.7. Effect on the displayed rating

For pharmacies with a mention, a corrected rating was calculated by reintegrating the removed reviews as one-star reviews, using first the lower bound and then the upper bound of the displayed range. Since the mention covers the last 365 days and the histogram covers the entire lifespan of the listing, this correction is indicative.
