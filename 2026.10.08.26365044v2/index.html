To fix Exact P-Values

What we found

We found 2 imprecise p values out of 17 detected p values.

Show details

Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (e.g., “decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

P-Value Text
p < 0.05 Azithromycin, cefuroxime, clarithromycin and roxithromycin declined by 0.073, 0.031, 0.008 and 0.004 percentage points per month, respectively (all BH-adjusted p < 0.05).
p < 0.05 Among Not Recommended combinations, cefpodoxime proxetil/clavulanic acid increased by 0.023 percentage points per month (BHadjusted p = 1.1 × 10 -4 ), whereas cefixime/ofloxacin, cefixime/clavulanic acid, cefpodoxime proxetil/ofloxacin and amoxicillin/dicloxacillin declined (all BH-adjusted p < 0.05).

The APA manual states: Report exact p values (e.g., p = .031) to two or three decimal places. However, report p values less than .001 as p < .001. However, 2 decimals is too imprecise for many use-cases (e.g., a p value meta-analysis), so report p values with three digits.

American Psychological Association (2020). Publication manual of the American Psychological Association, 7 edition. American Psychological Association.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 1 non-significant p value that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

Text Sentence
p = 0.497 The increases in lymecycline and cefpodoxime proxetil/clavulanic acid were no longer significant (BH-adjusted p = 0.497 and 0.241, respectively), whereas the increase in minocycline remained significant (BH-adjusted p = 9.5 × 10 -6 ).

REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Introduction:

An estimated 1.14 million deaths worldwide were attributable to bacterial antimicrobial resistance (AMR) in 2021, with substantial variation by age and location 1 . AMR surveillance in India is supported by established hospital and laboratory networks, including the Indian Council of Medical Research Antimicrobial Resistance Research and Surveillance Network (ICMR AMRSN) 2 and the National Centre for Disease Control's National AMR Surveillance Network (NARS-Net) 3 . The ICMR AMRSN 2024 report documented 99,027 culture-positive isolates and described contributions from 21 regional centres 2 , while NARS-Net analysed 195,077 deduplicated priority bacterial isolates from 54 reporting laboratories 3 . These networks include hospital outpatients as well as admitted patients, but their findings reflect the populations accessing participating facilities and the clinical decisions to obtain cultures.

Alongside resistance surveillance, monitoring antibiotic prescribing can help identify stewardship priorities. The World Health Organization's Access, Watch and Reserve (AWaRe) classification 4 supports monitoring of antibiotic-use composition. The former WHO benchmark called for at least 60% of antibiotic consumption to comprise Access antibiotics 5 and the 2024 United Nations political declaration on AMR established a target for Access antibiotics to account for at least 70% of global human antibiotic use by 2030 6 . In India, an analysis of privatesector antibiotic sales in 2019 estimated consumption of 5.07 billion defined daily doses, with Watch and Access antibiotics accounting for 54.9% and 27.0%, respectively 7 . Global analyses have also documented large geographic differences in antibiotic consumption and a disproportionate rise in Watch antibiotic use, particularly in low-and middle-income countries 8, 9.

Prescribing composition and organism-and specimen-specific susceptibility describe different aspects of stewardship and examining them together can identify priorities for further review.

Digital healthcare platforms offering outpatient pharmacy and home-based diagnostic services provide an opportunity to examine these questions within a common care pathway. Prescription records can describe antibiotic prescribing, while cultures from home-collected urine specimens can characterise resistance among users of the diagnostic service. Their common platform infrastructure and observation period enable complementary characterisation of prescribing and resistance, although the two streams need not represent the same individuals or clinical episodes.

Where geographic and temporal coverage overlaps, these data may also support locally focused stewardship review.

Here, we analysed routinely collected outpatient prescription records and diagnostic microbiology from specimens collected exclusively at home through Tata 1mg during 2023-2025. We characterised platform coverage, AWaRe prescribing composition, urinary resistance profiles and temporal trends, and contextualised resistance estimates against hospital-based surveillance reports. Exploratory analyses examined ecological lead-lag associations between prescribing and resistance using nationally pooled monthly series. Our objective was to evaluate how routinely collected prescribing and microbiology data can complement established AMR surveillance and identify stewardship priorities within the platform.

Discussion

.

This study analyses routinely collected digitised prescriptions and diagnostic microbiology data from a large digital healthcare platform in India, providing a community-facing view of antibiotic use and resistance that is currently missing in the conventional hospital-based AMR surveillance. To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine both antibiotic prescribing and microbiological resistance using routinely generated data from a digital healthcare platform in India. The study identifies a low Access prescribing share across eligible platform geographies, substantial variation in urinary resistance profiles, and changes over time that were not apparent from published reports alone. The analysis of prescription and resistance from the same platform also enabled exploratory assessment of temporal associations and local co-occurrence of prescribing and resistance changes.

Across all the eligible geographies, access antibiotics accounted for 28.7% of recorded antibiotics, compared with 47.0% for Watch antibiotics and 11.9% for Not Recommended combinations. The low Access share was geographically widespread and every state and union territory contributing at least 1,000 antibiotic records remained below the former 60% reference benchmark. This pattern is broadly consistent with the previously published results from India.

Koya and colleagues reported 5.07 billion defined daily doses of systemic antibiotics consumed in India in 2019, with Watch antibiotics accounting for 54.9% and Access antibiotics for only 27.0% of total consumption 7 . Farooqui and colleagues similarly showed increasing communitylevel antibiotic consumption in India, along with growing use of newer and broader-spectrum classes 10 . Internationally, Klein and colleagues found that per-capita Watch consumption increased by 90.9% between 2000 and 2015, compared with 26.2% for Access antibiotics, with a larger increase in Watch use in low-and middle-income countries 9 . Our record-based estimates are not directly comparable with defined daily doses, but both studies identify a predominance of Watch antibiotics. Browne and colleagues estimated that antibiotic consumption rates in South Asia increased by 116% between 2000 and 2018, alongside substantial national and subnational variation in antibiotic use 8 . These findings identify an important stewardship opportunity within the platform, although they do not establish whether individual prescriptions were clinically appropriate. The WHO Access targets are population-level benchmarks, whereas the WHO AWaRe antibiotic book provides guidance by infection syndrome, including antibiotic choice, dose, route and duration 11 . Increasing Access use therefore requires consideration of diagnosis, susceptibility and patient characteristics.

The resistance findings also show why stewardship cannot be reduced to increasing the Access share alone. Among urinary E. coli, resistance varied substantially within the Access group, from relatively low levels for nitrofurantoin and amikacin to substantially higher levels for trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole and ampicillin. K. pneumoniae showed a different profile, coli, although the populations and time period differed 14 . Catalán and colleagues similarly identified heterogeneous temporal patterns across pathogen-antibiotic pairs in the global ATLAS database, including methodological artefacts that could alter apparent trends 15 . These findings support considering resistance levels and trajectories together within a geography as a relatively low pooled proportion can coexist with an increase that warrants continued monitoring. A descriptive pincode level analysis provided a more local view of the two data streams.

Our exploratory lead-lag analyses revealed that for urinary E. coli resistance to Watch-class antibiotics preceded resistance to Access-class antibiotics by approximately 3-6 months for several combinations. Ceftazidime and ceftriaxone resistance preceded trimethoprimsulfamethoxazole resistance by around 4 months, while ceftriaxone resistance preceded tetracycline and ampicillin resistance by approximately 3-6 months. These findings are conceptually aligned with our earlier indicator-antibiotic framework in which some antibiotics showed increasing patterns earlier relative to critically important antibiotics and could be used as potential early warning signals 14, 16 . A systematic review of hospitalised adults in Europe found that most reported associations between antibiotic use and resistance occurred within 0-6 months 17 . In the present platform dataset, the lead-lag structure suggests that selected Watchresistance trends may warrant prospective evaluation as candidate leading indicators, using a prespecified lag and an independent dataset. However, these associations are not causal and several factors like co-selection, plasmid linkage, clonal expansion, diagnostic selection, seasonality, changing case mix, and antibiotic exposure outside the platform may all contribute.

Several limitations affect interpretation of these findings. The data represent platform-mediated healthcare interactions and were not sampled to represent the Indian population. Findings should therefore be interpreted within the online digital healthcare platform. Since urine predominated the diagnostic samples, the downstream resistance analyses was done on urinary isolates.

Although the platform contains home-collected samples, collection location does not establish where infection was acquired or exclude previous hospital exposure. Therefore, the resistance estimates should therefore not be interpreted as estimates of community-acquired resistance.

Patients selected for culture may differ from other platform users in previous antibiotic treatment, recurrence, disease complexity or treatment failure. The aggregated diagnostic records lacked prior-antibiotic exposure, clinical indications and repeat-testing identifiers, so we could not quantify these differences or assess the contribution of repeated samples from the same patients. In an independent survey of users of the same platform, 46.7% of respondents in the doctor-recommended antibiotic subgroup reported that a doctor had recommended a diagnostic test before prescribing 18 . This finding provides context for diagnostic use within the platform population, although recommendations for any diagnostic test do not establish the frequency of culture testing or the reasons patients were selected for culture. Resistance proportions should therefore be interpreted as describing the tested isolates, rather than the broader platform outpatient population. The independent survey also found that 58.0% of respondents reported that their last antibiotic use was doctor-recommended, while 42.1% reported always completing the full antibiotic course 18 . These findings highlight variation in how antibiotics are obtained and taken, which prescription records alone cannot capture. However, these self-reported practices cannot be used to quantify antibiotic consumption or adherence in the analysed prescription cohort. Recorded prescriptions should therefore be interpreted as a measure of antibiotic activity through the platform, rather than a direct measure of individual antibiotic exposure.

Since aggregated data was analysed, the individual-level prescriptions and diagnostic results could not be linked.

Their common platform origin therefore does not establish that patients contributing diagnostic samples were exposed to the antibiotics recorded in the prescription dataset. Prescription-resistance associations should consequently be interpreted as ecological associations between recorded prescribing and resistance, without attributing a subsequent resistant isolate to a particular prescription or purchase.

The time-series analyses also have limitations. Cross-correlation identifies temporal ordering but cannot distinguish direct selection from co-selection, seasonal disease patterns, local outbreaks, shifting diagnostic intensity, or changes in platform coverage. We used prewhitening to reduce the influence of serial correlation, but lagged associations may still reflect co-selection, seasonal disease patterns, local outbreaks, changes in diagnostic testing or shifts in platform coverage.

These signals should therefore be considered exploratory and require independent validation before use as early warning indicators.

Methods

Study Design

We conducted a retrospective observational time-series study using routinely collected digitised prescription and diagnostic microbiology data from Tata 1mg, a large digital healthcare platform in India. Prescription records contained aggregated by antibiotic, state and month, while diagnostic data contained aggregated by organism, specimen type, antibiotic, state, pincode and month. The two streams are from a shared digitally mediated outpatient care pathway (TATA 1mg) but were analysed as aggregated records without individual patient level linkage. Records generated between 1 January 2023 and 31 December 2025 were included for analysis. Antibiotic names in both the datasets were standardised and mapped to WHO AWaRe 2025 classification 4.

The prescription cohort included all prescription-validated antibiotics recorded during the study period with a valid prescription date and a positive prescription count. Products that did not represent antibacterial agents were excluded from the downstream analysis. Products without a corresponding entry in the WHO AWaRe 2025 lists were retained as Unclassified.

To quantify antibiotic activity relative to overall platform use, monthly prescription antibiotic counts were expressed as a proportion of all prescription medicines recorded during the corresponding month. In contrast, analyses of AWaRe composition and individual antibiotic trends used total antibiotic prescriptions within the relevant month or state-month as the denominator. These proportional measures reduced the influence of secular changes in platform activity and enabled comparison of prescribing patterns over time.

All eligible prescription antibiotics were included in national analyses. State-level analyses were restricted to states and union territories with a valid location and at least 1,000 prescription antibiotics during the study period.

The diagnostic dataset included diagnostic specimens were collected exclusively from patients' homes and processed in Tata 1mg laboratories during the study period. Resistance analyses were restricted to culture-positive bacterial isolates and downstream analysis were restricted to culture-positive urine samples. Since aggregated data were analysed no attempt was made to reidentify any patient.

Data sources

This study comprehensively analysed a novel dataset from Tata 1mg, a large online pharmacy and digital health platform that has operated in India since 2015. Routinely generated aggregated data for antibiotic prescriptions and diagnostic microbiology were analysed for the period 2023 to 2025.

Prescription dataset:

The antibiotic prescription data were derived from orders placed through the Tata 1mg platform that required a valid prescription. Under the platform workflow, users upload prescriptions to purchase medicines, which are then digitised, reviewed, and confirmed by an internal team of experts. The medicine names and corresponding antibiotic names from these prescriptions were at a pincode and day-level for the analysis.

Diagnostic dataset:

The diagnostic data pertaining to the culture results were obtained from These data sources enabled integration and analysis of antibiotic use and antimicrobial resistance patterns within a large digitally mediated, community-linked healthcare setting in India.

Data Pre-processing

Diagnostic specimen labels were harmonised into urine, sputum and blood categories. Organism names were cleaned by removing specified report annotations, standardising spelling and taxonomic abbreviations, and applying explicit mappings, longest-prefix matching and, where necessary, approximate matching against a reference vocabulary using a similarity threshold of 0.75. Entries consisting of colony-count information rather than organism names were treated as missing, and recognised negative-culture descriptions were standardised as No growth.

Laboratory antibiotic names were cleaned to remove embedded codes, susceptibility descriptors and extraneous characters, with explicit rules for spelling variants and combination agents.

Antibiotics in both datasets were classified using the WHO AWaRe 2025 classification 4 . For prescription data, antibiotic names were standardised for case, spacing, component separators and recognised generic-name variants. Fixed-dose combinations were classified only when the complete combination matched a WHO entry. Medicine names were first matched to the Not Recommended list and subsequently to Access, Watch and Reserve groups. Medicines that could not be matched were retained as Unclassified.

For the diagnostic dataset, antibiotic names were standardised using manually reviewed spelling corrections.

All prescription medicines were included in national analyses. State-specific prescription analyses were restricted to the 25 states and union territories contributing at least 1,000 prescription antibiotics.

Descriptive analysis

Descriptive analyses were performed separately for the diagnostic and prescription datasets. For each dataset, the geographic coverage was summarised by states and districts represented, and temporal coverage by study months and observed state-month activity. In the diagnostic dataset, we described culture outcomes, specimen types, organism distributions, and state-level contributions using counts and percentages. Resistance was summarised for major organismantibiotic combinations as the proportion of resistant isolates among tested isolates, as in previous studies 14, 16, 19 . Monthly resistance proportions were displayed without smoothing, with 95% percentile bootstrap intervals derived from 1,000 resamples of states within each organismantibiotic-month combination. For each organism, figures included the ten antibiotics with the largest number of interpretable susceptibility results.

The prescription dataset was summarised by total antibiotics, geographic and temporal coverage, top contributing states, and proportional distributions across AWaRe categories and antibiotic classes. Monthly prescription shares were displayed as centred three-month moving averages, with 95% percentile bootstrap intervals derived from 1,000 resamples of states within each month and the same smoothing applied to each bootstrap series.

Comparison with published surveillance network reports

To contextualise platform-derived resistance estimates, we compared resistance among urinary Escherichia coli isolates collected through Tata 1mg during 2024 with the corresponding hospital-outpatient estimates reported in the National Centre for Disease Control's NARS-Net 2024 annual report 3 . An equivalent comparison with ICMR AMRSN was not possible because its 2024 report did not provide outpatient-specific susceptibility estimates for urinary E. coli 2.

Comparisons were restricted to antibiotics reported in both datasets. These comparisons were descriptive and differences in patient characteristics, geographic coverage, culture selection, isolate deduplication and laboratory testing practices were not harmonised or adjusted for.

Longitudinal trends for antibiotic prescriptions and resistance

Trends in antibiotic prescriptions and resistance were estimated using state-level generalised linear mixed models fitted to monthly count data. Models were fitted using the glmmTMB package 20.

Prescription trends were analysed using beta-binomial generalised linear mixed models with a logit link, with the proportion of prescriptions attributable to each antibiotic as the dependent variable, time in months as a fixed-effect predictor, and state-level random intercepts and slopes.

Separate models were fitted for up to ten most frequently prescribed products within each of the Access, Watch, Reserve and Not Recommended categories. State-months with antibiotic activity but no recorded prescriptions for the selected product were assigned a zero numerator. States with antibiotic activity in at least 75% of study months were included. If the initial prescription model failed to converge, a random-intercept-only beta-binomial model was attempted, followed by binomial models with random intercepts and slopes and then random intercepts only.

Temporal effects were expressed as average marginal changes in antibiotic share, reported in percentage points per month, and p-values were adjusted within each AWaRe category using the Benjamini-Hochberg method 21 . Sensitivity analyses using all medicine prescriptions as the denominator was performed.

Resistance trends were analysed for urine isolates, specifically for each organism-antibiotic combination using beta-binomial generalised linear mixed models with a logit link, with the resistance percentage as the dependent variable, time in months as a fixed-effect predictor, and state-level random intercepts and slopes. For each organism-antibiotic combination, states were included if they had observations in at least 75% of the calendar months between the first and last observations for that combination and represented at least two pincodes. If the initial model could not be fitted successfully, a binomial model with the same random-effects structure was attempted, followed by random-intercept-only beta-binomial and binomial models. Temporal effects were summarised as average conditional marginal changes in antibiotic prescribing share or resistance, expressed in percentage points per month. Fitted probabilities incorporated statelevel random intercepts and, where present, random slopes. For each observed state-month, the time derivative of the fitted probability was calculated using the common time coefficient plus the state-specific random slope and the latter was set to zero for random-intercept-only models.

Derivatives were converted to percentage points and averaged with equal weight across the observed state-months included in each model. p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-

Hochberg method within each AWaRe category, separately for each prescription denominator, and within each organism for resistance.

Integrating antibiotic prescriptions and resistance datasets

Antibiotic prescription and diagnostics datasets obtained from the Tata 1mg platform were merged to generate a unified monthly analytical dataset for downstream temporal analyses. The diagnostics dataset was filtered to include only culture-positive urine samples. For each organism-antibiotic combination, the resistance percentage was calculated as the proportion of resistant isolates among all isolates tested. The prescription dataset was independently processed and aggregated at the month and antibiotic-level to obtain normalised monthly prescription volume. Following aggregation, the prescription and diagnostic datasets were aligned using a common temporal and antibiotic framework. Specifically, pincode-wise resistance estimates for each organism-antibiotic combination in a given month were linked with the corresponding monthly prescription count for the same antibiotic and month. Since the microbiology data were retained at the organism level, whereas prescription data were available at the antibiotic level, the merged dataset preserved organism-specific resistance observations and antibiotic-specific prescription for the corresponding month and geography. Thus, the resulting linkage was temporal and platform-level rather than patient-level, and no individual record linkage between prescriptions and diagnostic results was performed.

Lead-lag analyses of prescription and resistance

Lead-lag relationships were assessed using merged monthly time series of antibiotic prescriptions and organism-specific resistance. Analyses were performed separately for each organism-antibiotic pair. Antibiotic prescriptions were expressed as normalised monthly shares, and resistance was expressed as the monthly proportion of resistant isolates among those tested for that antibiotic. A complete calendar-month grid was retained for each series pair. Missing months remained missing and were not interpolated, ensuring that reported lags represented true calendar intervals. At least 24 paired months were required. The prescription series was prewhitened using an automatically selected ARIMA model implemented in the forecast package 22 and the same filter was applied to the resistance series. Cross-correlations between the residual series were evaluated from -12 to +12 months. Negative lags indicated that prescribing preceded resistance; positive lags indicated that resistance preceded prescribing. The same prewhitened cross-correlation framework was applied to pairwise antibiotic-resistance series within each organism to identify temporally ordered resistance signals across antibiotics.

Findings were interpreted as ecological temporal associations, not causal effects. Software pandas 23 (version 2.3.3) was used for data cleaning and aggregation, NumPy 24 (2.2.6) for numerical calculations and bootstrap resampling, and Matplotlib 25 (3.10.8) for figures. In R, data.table 26 (1.15.4) and the tidyverse packages dplyr and tidyr 27 (1.1.4 and 1.3.1, respectively) were used for data management, and zoo 28 (1.8-12) for monthly time indices.

Generalised linear mixed models were fitted with glmmTMB 20 (1.1.7), and automated ARIMA selection and prewhitening used forecast 22 (8.21).
