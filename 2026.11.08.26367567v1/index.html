Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. Introduction

Accurate and timely cause-of-death (CoD) data are critical for informing public health decisionmaking, resource allocation, and health-system accountability [1]. In many low-and middleincome countries (LMICs), a substantial proportion of deaths occur outside health facilities and without medical certification, resulting in inaccurate data for national statistics and global burden of disease estimation, and inadequate data to guide policies aimed at reducing mortality [2, 3].

Sub-Saharan Africa carries a disproportionately high burden of unregistered community deaths, compounded by underdeveloped civil registration and vital statistics (CRVS) systems [4, 5]. Delays and incompleteness in death reporting, compounded by logistical barriers, contribute to poor-quality mortality data [4, 5]. These gaps limit the ability of local governments and global health decision-makers to predict and detect emerging outbreaks, monitor progress towards Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), and understand the definitive burden of infectious and non-communicable diseases (NCDs) contributing to mortality [6, 7].

South Africa presents a paradox in mortality reporting; despite relatively high national CRVS coverage, substantial subnational heterogeneity persists, particularly in rural settings where deaths occur at home, medical certification of cause of death (MCCOD) is less accessible, and sociocultural norms influence death reporting [8]. In rural settings, most deaths that occur at home are witnessed by family members, and verbal autopsy (VA) has thus become a widely used tool to determine the probable cause-of-death [9]. Although VA is a valuable, pragmatic tool for understanding population-level mortality patterns, especially in settings with incomplete civil registration systems, it has several limitations, primarily when applied to individual-level diagnosis or detailed clinical analysis [10].

Minimally invasive tissue sampling (MITS) has emerged in the last decade as a culturally acceptable alternative to complete diagnostic autopsy (CDA) [11, 12]. Process evaluation framework

The Vukamberi project was assessed using a process evaluation framework integrating the Medical Research Council (MRC) Framework for Developing and Evaluating Complex Interventions [26], with Proctor's Implementation Outcomes Framework [27]. The MRC framework guided assessment of the intervention context, implementation mechanisms, and pathways through which inputs (community engagement, mortuary partnerships, trained field teams, laboratory capacity) enabled delivery of the community-based MITS workflow. Seven implementation outcome domains were specified for this evaluation. Four are Proctor constructs (adoption, fidelity, feasibility and acceptability). Reach, timeliness and adaptations were added from complementary sources to capture features specific to community-based post-mortem surveillance. Reach was taken from the RE-AIM framework [28] and corresponds to Proctor's penetration construct; timeliness is a core attribute in the CDC guidelines for evaluating public health surveillance systems [29] and was included because MITS must be performed soon after death, before decomposition compromises sample quality; and adaptations were documented as recommended by the MRC framework, which treats adaptation to context as central to complex intervention delivery [26]. The seven domains were operationalised as follows: reach (timely death notification and eligibility screening), adoption (family consent and enrolment), fidelity (adherence to MITS standard operating procedures), feasibility (procedural completion and sample adequacy), acceptability (cultural appropriateness of the process, including elder involvement in consent), timeliness (intervals from death to notification and sampling), and adaptations (modifications introduced in response to operational and sociocultural barriers). This integrated approach enabled assessment of whether community-based MITS was feasible and of how specific mechanisms and contextual factors shaped implementation outcomes across phases (Fig 3).

Data collection

Data were collected prospectively from study-specific operational and implementation records, including monthly death notification logs; field team operational and case reports; consent documentation forms; in-house laboratory sample quality records and results; and monthly surveillance summary reports compiled by the study team. Implementation challenges and adaptive responses were documented during weekly team review meetings and mapped to implementation outcome domains.

Data analysis

The operational phases were sequential, and our assessment of each of Proctor Quantitative implementation domains (reach, adoption, fidelity, feasibility, acceptability, and timeliness), drawn from Proctor's Implementation Outcomes Framework [27] and the complementary sources described above, were summarised using descriptive statistics, including frequencies, proportions, medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), and ranges where appropriate. Enrolment trajectories were analysed monthly to characterise programme rampup and phase-specific performance. Implementation challenges and adaptive strategies were categorised as operational, logistical, sociocultural, or project-level, and mapped against the implementation domains and the phase in which they occurred, enabling a temporal analysis of barrier-adaptation relationships. To describe change in implementation performance over time, the reach, adoption and feasibility indicators were additionally disaggregated by implementation year (Year 1: July 2024-June 2025; Year 2: July 2025-June 2026), and the enrolment cascade was tabulated cumulatively by quarter. The protocol was open to decedents of all ages from inception, and the age distribution of the enrolled series was described to establish whether reach extended across age groups. Age groups followed the study protocol: stillbirth, neonate (under 28 days), infant (28 days to under 1 year), child (1-16 years) and adult (older than 16 years).

Procedural completion and sample collection

All enrolled participants had tissue and, where obtainable, fluid samples successfully collected in accordance with the MITS standard operating procedures. Specimen collection was nearcomplete for the principal diagnostic compartments.

Among the 206 enrolled cases, formalinfixed tissue was obtained from the brain in 203 (98.5%), right lung in 205 (99.5%), left lung 204 (99.0%) and the liver in 204 (99.0%); the four core solid organs were jointly sampled in 201 cases (97.6%). Kidney tissue was obtained in a minority of cases (left 29 [14.1%], right 24 [11.7%]); kidney biopsy was attempted for all cases but was frequently constrained by available field time relative to the core diagnostic organs. For microbiology, EDTA whole blood was collected in 203 cases (98.5%), blood culture bottles (aerobic and anaerobic) in 201 cases (97.6%), nasopharyngeal swabs in 205 (99.5%; collected but not yet tested, owing to cost constraints), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) in 180 (87.4%), with paired solid-organ microbiology specimens obtained in 200 cases (97.1%). Rectal swabs were not collected. The study team decided against them when developing the protocol because the budget could not cover the multiplex molecular testing needed to distinguish enteric pathogens that contributed to death from normal gut flora. Sample adequacy for the core specimen set was therefore high.

Sample processing and analysis

The collected specimens are distinct from completed analyses with laboratory processing, verbal autopsies, medical records linkage and determination of cause of death (DeCoDe) processes continuing. As of June 2026, microbiological processing on the in-house culture bench had been finalised for 108 of 206 cases (52.4%). The outstanding cases reflect laboratory throughput at the reporting cut-off, and processing was continuing at the close of the study period. Procedural completion tracked cumulative eligibility across the study quarters (Table 3). Histology samples were submitted for 198 cases, of which 100 (50.5%) have been completed. VA and TeleVA interviews were completed for 143 of 206 enrolled cases (69.4%). Clinical records from the Clinic-link HDSS project include ante-mortem laboratory results, diagnosis, medication, clinic and hospital visits. Records were retrieved for 50 of the 92 decedents residing within the HDSS at the time of death (54.3%; 24.3% of all 206 enrolled cases); no Clinic-link record exists for the remaining 114 decedents, who were resident beyond the HDSS (106) or were former residents of the study area (8). DeCoDe multidisciplinary CoD panel determinations have been finalised for 100/206 (48.5%). Full laboratory analysis and CoD findings will be reported in a dedicated epidemiological manuscript.

Implementation challenges and the adaptive strategies introduced

Drawing the domains together, implementation across the four phases was shaped by a recurring set of challenges: notification arriving more than 48 hours after death, emotional sensitivity in acutely bereaved families, cultural hesitancy about disfigurement and disturbance of the spirit of the deceased, mortuary-level access barriers, a traditional leader's restriction on entry to 14 villages, laboratory turnaround delays, and the MITS kit supply gap of January to mid-March 2026. Each was met with an adaptation introduced at an identifiable point in the study and traceable to a change in the implementation outcomes reported above: the incentivised multi-channel notification network restored reach and reduced out-of-window notification; elder mediation and transparent non-disfigurement messaging raised consent; formal mortuary agreements and mortuary-based consenting preserved reach and procedural completion where household access failed; and the in-house culture bench shortened laboratory turnaround. Taken together these adaptations describe an implementation model in which community-based MITS is sustained by the notification, consent and laboratory systems built around the sampling procedure (Table 6).

Discussion

Principal findings

This study presents, to our knowledge, one of the first systematic process evaluations of establishing community-based all-ages MITS surveillance across all ages. Over 24 months of enrolment, all 408 notified community deaths were screened, a MITS procedure was completed in all 206 consented cases with target-organ sampling adherence of 96.6%, and 65.2% of eligible families consented, indicating that community-based MITS is operationally feasible within an established rural surveillance platform. Enrolment increased from fewer than five procedures per month in the pilot phase to 12-26 per month from June to November 2025 and recovered to 14-15 per month from April 2026 after the kit supply gap, consistent with iterative adaptation to context-specific barriers within the HDSS infrastructure [24, 26, 27]. The evaluation also extends the MITS evidence base: CHAMPS and most published MITS programmes have focused on stillbirths and child deaths, whereas Vukamberi applied the procedure to community deaths at all ages, including adults. The diagnostic validity of MITS against complete diagnostic autopsy was established in hospital deaths in Mozambique, with cause-of-death concordance of 75.9% in adults, 89% in children, 83% in stillbirths, 68% in neonates and 68% in maternal deaths [30] [31] [32] [33]; whether that performance holds for community deaths sampled a median of 37 hours, and up to 72 hours, after death will be addressed by the Vukamberi cause-of-death analysis.

Death notification and timeliness

Screening of all notified deaths was maintained throughout the study, although this measures coverage of notified deaths rather than of all deaths in the catchment (see Limitations).

CHAMPS and comparable programmes typically target notification within 24-72 hours for paediatric deaths in both communities and health facilities [16]. Our 48-hour window for allage community deaths was chosen because high ambient temperatures in the study area accelerate body decomposition and bacterial overgrowth, and because of the culturally grounded practice among elders of waiting two to six hours after apparent death before calling the mortuary, which is linked to beliefs of possible 'miraculous recovery' [24, 34]. Only 26 deaths (6.4%) were ineligible because of delayed notification, a pattern consistent with community-based MITS programmes in other rural settings, including India and Pakistan, where cultural practices around death disclosure, geographic isolation and limited informant awareness contribute to delayed reporting [19, 20]. The multi-channel notification network, particularly incentivised community and mortuary informants, was the main strategy that sustained screening and reduced out-of-window notifications across study phases.

Consent, refusal and elder mediation

The 65.2% consent rate among eligible cases is lower than the 82% consent reported for paediatric deaths in a Mozambican hospital setting [17] but was achieved for community deaths of all ages within a 48-hour window and is consistent with the high hypothetical acceptability of MITS reported in this community during the Vukamberi formative study [24].

Qualitative studies on MITS implementation from Kenya, Mozambique and rural South Africa identified fears of organ harvesting, spirit disturbance and burial delay as the main barriers to consent; all three were documented in Vukamberi field notes and addressed through transparent communication and elder mediation [18, 24, 34]. The Kenyan acceptability study similarly found that community health workers and elder community figures were key facilitators, supporting the transferability of elder-mediated consent across sub-Saharan African settings [18]. Recent MITS implementation in Zambezia Province, central Mozambique, also found that advocacy involving provincial government, community leaders and traditional healers was critical for sustained acceptability [35].

The dominant driver of non-enrolment was family refusal (48.2% of non-enrolled eligible cases), most often reflecting grief-related unreadiness. This pattern aligns with evidence from consent research in acute bereavement contexts, which identifies emotional state at the time.

of approach as a primary determinant of participation, independent of attitudes toward the study itself [36]. Most refusals reflected a mismatch between the 48-hour operational window and a family's capacity to engage in the consent process, and few families expressed principled opposition to MITS. This has implications for programme design: advance community sensitisation that allows households to indicate openness to participation before bereavement, and flexible return-visit protocols, may increase enrolment without compromising consent. The decline in refusals as community familiarity grew across phases supports this interpretation, although refusal reasons were recorded at summary level and could not be linked to decedent characteristics.

A distinctive feature of Vukamberi implementation was the strategic use of elder household members as consent mediators. Our earlier qualitative study in this community documented how elder authority in Xitsonga-and Sepulana (Northern Sotho)-speaking communities in rural Mpumalanga is deeply embedded in death ritual governance; elders hold decision-making power over whether the deceased's body may be examined, and their endorsement is both socially required and practically decisive for family consent decisions [34]. This authority is not static: the same study found that younger household members increasingly defer to elders on decisions made around the time of death, such as whether the body may be examined, even in households where elders have less say in everyday matters. Elder involvement was documented in 277/316 (87.7%) of eligible cases and was consistently associated with improved family receptiveness in field notes where initial hesitancy was documented. This finding is consistent with evidence from the CHAMPS PICK-CHAMP participatory communityentry workshops, in which participants across five sites indicated that support for MITS was contingent on the procedure not interfering with burial practices and rituals, and in which engagement with community leaders was central to establishing acceptability [37]. The sociopolitical barrier of local chief's access restriction in 14 villages was managed through mortuary-based consenting, an adaptation that preserved reach by drawing on a structural feature of rural South African mortuary practice, in which families are reliably present in the.

peri-death period. A comparable emphasis on rapid identification of deaths and on prior institutional relationship-building was reported from Zambezia Province, central Mozambique, where these were identified as preconditions for timely sampling [35].

Procedural fidelity and supply-chain vulnerability

Target-organ sampling adherence of 96.6% (199/206 consented cases) indicates high procedural fidelity, supported by established mortuary partnerships and consistent team training. The 48-hour eligibility window was set on operational grounds, reflecting ambient temperature, the absence of refrigeration in most households and local burial practice, rather than on a published post-mortem interval threshold. The most significant operational disruption was the MITS kit supply gap from January to mid-March 2026, which resulted in 25 missed eligible cases at a time when families were willing to consent. Cost-evaluation work in LMICs has identified MITS kits and consumables, alongside labour and testing, as principal cost drivers [38]; our experience shows that they are also a key operational vulnerability and underscores the need for buffer stock and procurement redundancy as projects move from externally funded pilots to integrated surveillance systems. Because the scientific value of surveillance depends on population-representative sampling, such gaps introduce nonrandom missingness: a seasonal block of community deaths is absent from the series for reasons unrelated to the characteristics of those deaths.

Point-of-care testing

Point-of-care (POC) testing was performed at the time of MITS sampling in 205 of 206 cases (99.5%). Integrating POC testing for HIV, hepatitis B surface antigen, hepatitis C antibody and syphilis (RPR/TPHA) into the MITS procedure adds minimal procedural time and provides immediate serological context directly relevant to interpreting cause-of-death pathways, which may be useful for sites without rapid access to laboratory serology.

Implications for implementation and policy

Viewed through an implementation science lens, the Vukamberi trajectory illustrates core principles of effective complex intervention implementation: staged rollout with deliberate preparatory investment prior to procedures; iterative adaptation in response to identified barriers; and system-level capacity building, including in-house culture bench establishment and mortuary partnership formalisation that improved project performance over time [26]. The preparatory phase (May-December 2023), focused on community engagement, stakeholder mapping, and formative acceptability work, was important for building the social licence necessary to conduct post-mortem procedures in bereaved households in a context where mistrust of biomedical research remains a real concern. Our Phase 1 qualitative study reported that MITS was broadly acceptable in this community, with community members expressing willingness to participate provided the procedure was non-disfiguring, respectfully conducted, and that results would be shared back with families and the community; concerns about spirit disturbance and organ harvesting were raised and were specifically addressed through the pictorial consent aids and community information sessions developed as part of that preparatory phase [24]. This mirrors the PICK-CHAMP community-entry model, in which structured pre-implementation engagement was used to surface alignment and tension between surveillance activities and community priorities before mortality surveillance began [37]. The Africa CDC Continental Framework for Mortality Surveillance explicitly calls for integration of community mortality surveillance into national health information systems; the Vukamberi experience offers an operational example of how this could be done at district level [39].

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, the evaluation integrated MRC process evaluation guidance with Proctor's Implementation Outcomes Framework, allowing implementation outcomes to be linked to specific mechanisms and contextual factors; to our knowledge, it is . among the first systematic process evaluations of community-based MITS and the first to cover deaths at all ages within an HDSS. Second, implementation outcomes were derived from records generated prospectively as the intervention ran (notification logs, consent documentation, field and case reports and laboratory quality records) rather than reconstructed retrospectively, so barriers and the adaptations made in response are dated and can be read against the performance indicators of the period in which they occurred.

Third, reporting follows the Standards for Reporting Implementation Studies (StaRI) statement (S1 Checklist) [40].

This evaluation also has limitations. First, implementation outcomes were abstracted from monthly summary reports rather than case-level records, so reasons were not documented for 23 ineligible and 3 non-enrolled cases, and refusals could not be stratified by decedent age, sex or duration of terminal illness; these should be captured routinely in future surveillance. Second, study notifications were not compared with deaths recorded independently by the HDSS, so the completeness of death ascertainment, overall and by age group (including infant deaths), could not be quantified; screening coverage refers to notified deaths only, and ascertainment was incomplete in February 2026, when only two mortuaries reported. Third, the traditional leader's restriction of access to 14 villages and the kit supply gap of early 2026 may have introduced selection bias into the enrolled series, which may affect the representativeness of future cause-of-death estimates. Fourth, the enrolled series was predominantly adult and included no stillbirths, neonates or infants, so feasibility for these groups could not be assessed. Fifth, cause-of-death determination was incomplete at the reporting cut-off, so the diagnostic yield of community-based MITS is not reported here. Sixth, the evaluation was conducted by the implementing team, which may have influenced the interpretation of field notes; the use of prospectively recorded operational data reduces, but does not remove, this risk. Finally, the single-site design limits generalisability, although the Agincourt setting shares structural features with many surveillance sites in sub-Saharan.

Africa, including rural geography, high HIV burden, incomplete civil registration and strong community leadership.

Conclusion

The Vukamberi project demonstrates that community-based MITS surveillance is feasible, operationally achievable, and generates actionable scientific data within an established HDSS in rural South Africa. Elder-mediated consent, mortuary partnerships, multi-channel incentivised death notification and in-house laboratory capacity may be transferable to sites facing comparable barriers in the African region, and reliable procurement of consumables is a precondition for continuous surveillance. Africa CDC's Continental Framework for Mortality Surveillance calls for community mortality surveillance to be integrated into national health information systems [39]; the Vukamberi experience illustrates what such integration requires in practice. Cause-of-death findings from the complete cohort will provide a pathologyinformed community mortality dataset for rural South Africa, offering a resource for validating verbal autopsy, informing public health priorities and tracking the epidemiological transition in a setting of growing non-communicable disease burden.
