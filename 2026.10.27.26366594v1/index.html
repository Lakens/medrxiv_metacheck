How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on 226 Psychological Science papers with 5,019 hand-coded p-values, 438 of them imprecise (scienceverse/validation, branch stat_p_exact). Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). Of the misses, 13 are starred table notes (“*p < .05”) that the module leaves out on purpose and 11 are p-values it did not find; of the wrong reports, 20 are p-values the ground truth does not contain (such as “ps < .05”) and 6 use ≤ or ≥. These figures measure agreement with the hand-coding, which has known errors in both directions (scienceverse/validation#48).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. Some differences come from the ground truth: it lacks p-values with a correction name (“p Bonf = .061”), and counts partial eta squared written with its subscript after it (“η 2 p = .05”), “p = .05”, and some table notes and figure legends (“† p < .10, *p < .05”), which this module leaves out, as non-significant p-values. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. A TRO uses them for each trov:TransparentResearchPerformance. * run_results$exit_status: the exit status of the process that ran the script (0 = ended normally); NA when the process did not report one (a time limit, or Docker failing to start the container). * artifacts: one row per file, with stage (deposit: as downloaded from the repository, hashed from the downloaded file; before_run: the run folder just before the first script ran, after metacheck’s own changes, such as rewritten paths and unpacked archives; after_run: only the files the scripts made (status new), changed (changed) or removed (removed), as an unchanged file is the one listed before the run), path (relative to the run folder, as valid UTF-8, a byte of another encoding written as <xx>), size (bytes), sha256, mime_type (by extension, NA when unknown), source (the file’s address in the repository, for deposit). The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. A TRO uses the rows as trov:ResearchArtifacts and each stage as a trov:ArtifactArrangement. * modifications$sha256_before, modifications$sha256_after: the hash of the changed script as downloaded and as it ran; NA when the script is not at the same path in both (a script made from an R Markdown file). * environment: the system that ran the code (trov:TransparentResearchSystem in a TRO): metacheck_version, started_at, ended_at, host (R version, platform, operating system), sandbox, network_isolated (TRUE only when every Docker run had --network none; the process sandbox does not stop the network), internet, timeout_per_script, total_timeout, python_local (the Python run outside Docker and its version), and docker: runs (for each kind of run, r_run, r_install, python_run, python_install, the image and the docker run arguments, such as --network none, --read-only, --cap-drop ALL, no-new-privileges, the process limit and the memory and processor limits) and images (for each image, its ID and digests from docker image inspect, its creation time and the R and Python versions its environment declares).

It is NA for files run inside another script, files that are not an analysis, files not read, and a Docker failure. summary_table counts each group (repro_ran_n, repro_code_error_n, repro_dependency_n, repro_time_n, repro_not_checkable_n), and the report’s first sentence gives them. The traffic light reflects the static readiness signals by default; a script in code_error that stopped with an error makes it red, as a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure, and a script in one of the other three groups keeps it from being green. Calls that need a screen, which the run does not have (dev.print(), dev.copy2pdf(), dev.copy2eps(), savePlot(), windows(), win.graph(), x11(), quartz(), View()), are replaced before the run by calls that need none, which modifications records as screen_call_replaced. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Abstract

Family caregivers of cancer patients, especially advanced cancer patients, face a significant load of care and also decline in life quality. Caregiving challenges are related to feelings of guilt, selfcriticism, and shame. Self-compassion is also linked to reduced negative emotions and increased effective coping strategies. Due to time constraints and limited access to in-person support, webbased training can provide an accessible and cost-effective option for caregivers. This single-blind randomized controlled trial evaluates whether web-based self-compassion training based on Compassion-Focused Therapy improves quality of life and reduces the burden of care among Family Caregivers of patients with advanced cancer. A total of 96 caregivers aged 18 and above in oncology and palliative care units will be randomly assigned to the Compassion-Focused Therapy (CFT) group or the Treatment as Usual (TAU) group. The CFT group will receive eight sessions of web-based self-compassion training over four weeks (two sessions per week), while the TAU group will receive usual care. Data will be collected using the Self-Compassion Scale (SCS), the Caregiver Burden Inventory (CBI) to assess caregiving burden and the Caregiver Quality of Life Index-Cancer Scale (CQOLC) to assess life quality in three phases: baseline, immediately after completion of the training and four weeks after completion of the training. In addition, two researcher-developed instruments will be used: The Compassion-Focused Therapy Satisfaction Questionnaire and the Compassion-Focused Therapy Adherence Scale. Data will be analysed using linear mixed-effects modelling in R to evaluate the effectiveness of the CFT treatment. This study may assess whether web-based self-compassion training leads to a reduction in caregiving burden and an improvement quality of life for family caregivers of patients with advanced cancer.

Introduction

Based on statistical data, after cardiovascular disease, cancer is the most life-threatening disease in the world [1]. In Asia, for example, among individuals aged 0-85 years, incident cancer cases are estimated to be around 16,162,830 between 2022 and 2045 [2, 3]. In Iran, incident cancer cases in 2024 were 156,463 [4], and this number is predicted to increase to about 210,701 in 2027 [5].

Cancer diagnosis, disease management, and treatment are extremely stressful for patients that constantly expose them to various types of financial and non-financial pressures [6] [7] [8]. This disease affects both patients and their family caregivers [8].

Family caregivers are essential in managing the physical and psychological symptoms associated with cancer [9]. Family caregivers include spouses, relatives, or friends who devote significant time (months or years) to providing health care and take on physically, socially, emotionally or financially demanding activities [10, 11]. Caring for patients presents a variety of challenges for caregivers [12]. Including: career and financial challenges [13], physical fatigue [14], decreased quality of life [15], psycho-emotional conflicts [16], Pressure to gain social acceptance [17], mental fatigue [18], lack of social support [19], social isolation [20], feeling ignored [21], stress process [22], cancer taboo [23], and heavy caregiving burden [24]. Caring for a terminally ill patient is one of the most difficult aspects of providing care [20]. The relatives of patients suffer from psychological distress, emotional problems and caregiving burden throughout the course of the disease [25].

Caregiving burden is a sophisticated and multidimensional meaning that includes negative emotions experienced by caregivers due to the social, economic, psychological, and physical, aspects of the care process [26]. Patient-related factors such as: male gender [27], patient functional status [28], poor physical and mental health [29], number of comorbidities [30], confusion [31], degree of dependency [30], and younger age [27] and caregiver-related factors such as: lower education [32], duration of care [30], longer hours of care [32] and limited support [32] both affect the severity of caregiving burden. Caregiving responsibilities have negative impacts on the mental health and quality of life of caregivers. There is a negative association between burden of care and quality of life [33]. As the burden of care increases, the life quality decreases significantly [34].

Quality of life consists of physical and psychosocial dimensions [35]. Caregiving burden affects both the quality of life of caregivers and their patients [36]. Because it reduces the quality of care that caregivers provide to patients and affects the recovery process of patient from both physical and psychological aspects [37]. Therefore, implementing targeted interventions to reduce the caregiving burden and enhance the quality of life of family caregivers seems to be very necessary.

According to the findings of the studies, interventions such as caregiver education and psychosocial support are necessary to reduce caregiving burden, because psychosocial factors have a significant impact on the severity of caregiving burden [38]. Also, the implementation of psychoeducational interventions is suggested as a useful and cost-effective strategy for reducing the caregiving burden and enhancing the quality of life of family caregivers. [8]. Cognitivebehavioral therapy [39], acceptance and commitment therapy [40] and self-compassion training [41] are among the psychological interventions that have been utilized to reduce the burden of caregiving for caregivers of patients with cancer. Among them, self-compassion training has been examined in numerous studies (Table 1).

According to a study, self-compassion training for caregivers of older adults with dementia has been associated with reduced caregiving burden and increased use of effective coping strategies to manage caregiving stress [42]. Furthermore, a study conducted by Neff et al. (2015) showed that parents of children with autism who possess higher levels of self-compassion reported lower levels of stress and depression, as well as greater life satisfaction and hope. [43].

Self-compassion means being supportive and kind to ourselves when we experience pain or suffering. It is an effective way to deal with distressing thoughts and feelings, which ultimately associated with mental and physical health [54]. Self-compassion is a form of communication with oneself in difficult situations that includes three main components: 1-being aware of when we are in distress and responding in a balanced way to difficult experiences; 2understanding common humanity in difficult moments and recognizing that we are not alone in our suffering; and 3-the ability to be kind and understanding to ourselves in difficult moments [55]. Two main self-compassion training have been developed so far:1. Mindful Self-Compassion training [56] and 2. Compassion-Focused Therapy [57]. Gilbert, the theorist behind Compassion-Focused Therapy [58], believes that CFT is effective, particularly for individuals with severe selfcriticism and shame by activating the soothing and safety system (rather than the threat system) [57]. Compared with cognitive behavioral therapy, compassion-focused therapy considers positive emotions to be more important during the therapeutic process and emphasizes improving the way individuals relate to their psychological problems [59]. The findings of three studies have shown that increased self-compassion is linked to reduced feelings of guilt, self-criticism and depressive symptoms, and promotes psychological well-being [60] [61] [62]. Self-compassion may be a better therapeutic approach than traditional cognitive therapies for self-critical individuals because it activates their soothing system more effectively [63].

Family caregivers of cancer patients have reported that they often feel guilt and selfblame due to an inability to provide sufficient, high-quality care to the patients and to support them [64]. These caregivers often neglect their own personal and health needs and even feel guilty when taking care of themselves or enjoying personal moments [44, 65]. Research has shown that caregiver guilt is positively correlated with caregiver burden; therefore, a dyadic "patientcaregiver" assessment approach may reduce caregiver guilt and alleviate caregiver burden [66].

Self-compassion can reduce the intensity of guilt by cultivating a more compassionate relationship with yourself [67]. Therefore, self-compassion training may be effective in decreasing caregiving burden and increasing their quality of life by reducing these negative emotions.

Self-compassion training can be delivered to caregivers in person or online. Using digital platforms to develop health interventions has several advantages over traditional methods, including maintaining participant anonymity, wider accessibility, and reduced cost and time [68].

Web-based interventions are very suitable and valuable due to time flexibility and easy access when face-to-face meetings are not possible [69]. According to findings of two systematic reviews, psychoeducational interventions delivered via videoconferencing can overcome barriers to attending in-person sessions and help improve caregiving skills, self-care, and the well-being of informal caregivers [70]. Furthermore, digital health interventions can address some limitations of traditional face-to-face interactions and enhance caregivers' access to self-care services [71].

However, several studies in Iran have examined the effectiveness of self-compassion training on caregivers or parents of cancer patients in person [41, 52, 72, 73]; few studies have investigated self-compassion training as an internet-based intervention for cancer patient caregivers. Although research on internet-based interventions for cancer caregivers is expanding, the impact of these interventions on "caregiver burden" and "quality of life" remains unclear. [74].

Therefore, the development of comprehensive and tailored web-based interventions to reduce caregiving burden and improve the quality of life of caregivers of cancer patients in Iran seems essential. The main goal of this study protocol is aimed at investigating the effectiveness of webbased self-compassion training on the caregiving burden and quality of life of family caregivers of patients with advanced cancer.

The hypotheses of this study state that following an 8-session CFT program, caregivers in the CFT group will report: A. more progress in self-compassion than those in the TAU group as assessed by the Self-Compassion Scale (SCS) B. greater reduction in caregiving burden than those in the TAU group as assessed by the Caregiver Burden Inventory (CBI). C. greater increase in quality of life than those in the TAU group as assessed by the Caregiver Quality of Life Index-Cancer Scale (CQOLC).

The study population will consist of caregivers whose patients are admitted to the oncology and palliative care wards of two hospitals affiliated with the Iran University of Medical Sciences (Rasoul-e Akram and Firouzgar hospitals). In this study, patients with advanced cancer will be identified based on the diagnosis of the treating oncologist. After identifying eligible patients, their family caregivers will be assessed for inclusion and exclusion criteria. Inclusion criteria for caregivers included being 18 years of age or older of each gender [44], providing direct care to patients with stage III or IV cancer for at least six months [78], acting as the primary caregiver of a patient with cancer [47], being able to read and write [49], being able to communicate verbally and complete questionnaires, having a smartphone [47], being able to use a smartphone, having access to stable internet and virtual platforms [79], being able to complete educational assignments, having no history of psychiatric disorders [80], and not taking psychiatric medications when the study was being done (according to caregiver self-report). Exclusion criteria will also include death of the patient with cancer, receiving other psychological treatment during the study or within the 6 months prior to enrollment, failure to complete more than three of the seven recommended tasks, and the caregiver's desire to withdraw from the study. If family caregivers meet the inclusion criteria, the research team will provide them with detailed information about its objectives, methodology and beneficial and adverse effects. Written informed consent is essential for all caregivers before enrollment and the collection of baseline data.

After completing the baseline assessments, caregivers will be randomized equally to the CFT or TAU group (1:1 ratio) using a four-way block randomization sequence generated by R software by an independent statistician. An independent researcher who has no involvement in participant recruitment or outcome assessment will ensure allocation concealment by keeping the randomization sequence and group allocation hidden from other members of the research team until the initial assessments are completed. After randomization, the data analyst will be the only person to remain blinded to group allocation, because caregivers and researchers cannot be blinded. Unblinding will occur only if required for participant safety or requested by the Research Ethics Committee. All instances of unblinding will be documented.

Feasibility and accessibility Measures

To assess the feasibility of the CFT program, the rate attendance at virtual sessions, the rate of caregiver attrition, and the completion of assignments will be calculated by the researcher. To assess the acceptability of the CFT program, caregivers' satisfaction will be measured using an item at the end of the study (How helpful was the CFT program?) responses will be scored on a 5-point Likert scale from1 = (completely helpful) to5 = (completely unhelpful).

Procedures

At baseline, family caregivers will complete demographic, Self-Compassion Scale (SCS), Caregiver Burden Inventory (CBI), and Caregiver Quality of Life Index-Cancer (CQOLC) questionnaires. These questionnaires will take approximately 15 to 20 minutes to complete.

In order to implement the intervention, a web-based educational system will be designed so that participants can access the educational content through different tools, like computers, tablets and cellphones. The usability, efficiency and technical performance of the system will be evaluated and approved by relevant experts before the study. Caregivers in the intervention group will receive the web-based self-compassion training via this platform. The educational content will include narrated educational videos, audio files (podcasts), and structured educational modules.

The duration of each educational video will be approximately 15 to 20 minutes and each audio file will be a maximum of 15 minutes. The intervention program will consist of eight educational sessions, which will be delivered over four weeks, two sessions in a week. For each session, practical exercises and assignments appropriate to the educational content will be provided to participants to provide the opportunity to apply the learned skills in real caregiving situations.

In order to facilitate communication with participants and resolve potential technical issues, a communication group will be created within one of the internal messaging software platforms. In addition, the researcher will be in regular contact with participants via phone calls and online communication throughout the intervention period to answer questions and potential issues, as well as to monitor their level of participation and adherence to the training program.

TAU group participants will receive only usual care during the study period and will not be provided with any educational intervention related to the study. Outcomes will be assessed using online questionnaires at two time points: immediately after completing of the training and four weeks after completion of the training. In addition, two researcher-developed instruments: The Compassion-Focused Therapy Satisfaction Questionnaire to assess participants' satisfaction with the intervention and the Compassion-Focused Therapy Adherence Scale to assess the researcher's adherence to the training program's content will be used. In this study, usual care was selected as the comparator because it reflects current clinical practice for family caregivers of patients with advanced cancer and enables assessment of the incremental effect of the self-compassion education intervention. Caregivers and the general public were not involved in the planning of this study.

TAU group

According to the routine care protocols of both hospitals, all caregivers of patients with advanced cancer will receive patient care training, which include: information about the disease and its progression, symptom assessment and management, proper medication administration, daily patient care, nutritional care, effective communication with the patient, management of common caregiving challenges, and how to contact and communicate with the healthcare team. Caregivers who wish to receive psychological supports may consult the hospital-based psychologist.

Caregivers in this group will receive only patient care training and will not undergo psychological interventions during the study period.

Compassion-Focused Therapy (CFT)

The self-compassion training intervention in this study will be based on the Compassion-Focused therapy approach designed by Paul Gilbert [81]. This approach aims to strengthen selfcompassion, reduce self-criticism and shame, and improve emotional regulation ability, providing individuals with a set of training and exercises. This training program will be designed and implemented in the form of eight sessions, each session having specific educational objectives and practical exercises tailored to improving self-compassion skills.

In the initial session, caregivers will be introduced to the general structure of the program, the basic concepts of compassion and self-compassion, types of compassion, and how important is it to cultivate self-compassion in everyday life. The second session is dedicated to introducing the characteristics of compassionate people, and the personal and psychological benefits of selfcompassion.

In the third session, topics such as the multiple mind, the old and new brain, the evolutionary functions of the brain, the concept of the "tricky brain," and the difference between the threat-oriented mind and the compassion-oriented mind will be discussed. The goal of this session is to increase the awareness of participants about the origins of their emotional and behavioral reactions. In the fourth session, participants will be introduced to the three emotion regulation systems: the threat system, the arousal system, and the safety and comfort system. It will also be examined which of these systems are activated in difficult and stressful caregiving situations and the safety and comfort system can be strengthened and activated through selfcompassion exercises. Sessions five through seven focus on teaching and practicing practical self-compassion skills. In these sessions, techniques such as mindfulness, being compassionate to others and ourselves, compassionate reasoning, the multiple selves technique, Rhythmic Relaxation Breathing, three-chair technique, compassionate attention, compassionate perception, compassionate imagery, compassionate feeling, compassionate behavior, compassionate letter writing are introduced and practiced practically. Finally, in the eighth session, techniques for applying self-compassion as a routine in daily life and performing activities throughout the day with a focus on self-compassion will be introduced. The content validity of the self-compassion training program will be reviewed and approved by 10 Iran University of Medical Sciences experts and faculty members, including supervisors and referees, faculty members of the psychiatry nursing department, clinical psychologist, oncologist, and specialists in the field of cancer and palliative care.

Protocol Adherence

To ensure intervention fidelity, first, all study personnel will receive standardized training before participant enrolment and the educational intervention will be implemented by a researcher who has undergone the necessary specialized training in the field of self-compassion. Second, the researcher's adherence to the intervention protocol during the implementation of the intervention will be assessed by supervising experts using the researcher-developed Compassion-Focused Therapy Adherence Scale. Corrective feedback will be provided if any deviations from the protocol are observed. Third, all produced educational content, including narrated educational videos, podcasts and structured educational modules will be reviewed and approved by experts before use. Any necessary revisions will be made based on the feedback provided.

Primary Outcome Measure

Self-Compassion Scale (SCS) developed by Neff in 2003 [82], has 26 items with 6 subscales: Self-Kindness with 5items, Self-Judgment with 5 items, Common Humanity with 4 items, Isolation with 4 items, Mindfulness with 4 items, Over-identification with 4 items. Each question is scored on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (almost never) to 5 (almost always). The Cronbach's alpha in the original scale ranged from 0.75 to 0.81 and 0.92 for the total scale. The scale showed high test-retest reliability (0.93). In the Iranian version, Cronbach's alpha for the total scale reported as 0.78 and for subscales ranging from 0.79 to 0.93 [83].

Secondary Outcome Measures

Caregiver Burden Inventory (CBI) , developed by Novak and Guest in 1989 [84] is a 24 items that consists of five subscales: developmental burden, time-related burden, social burden, emotional burden and physical burden. All subscales contain 5 items except physical burden which consists of four items. These items are scored on a 5-point Likert scale from 0 (never) to 4 (almost always). Subscale scores range from 0 to 20 for developmental, emotional, time-related, and social burden, and from 0 to 16 for physical burden. Higher scores indicate more caregiver burden. The Cronbach's alpha for the original version of the CBI has been reported to be acceptable ranging from 0.69 to 0.87 and 0.80 for the total scale. In the Iranian population, the Persian version showed excellent reliability, 0.93 for total Cronbach's alpha and 0.70 to 0.91 for subscales. At a two-week interval, test-retest reliability has also been reported to be acceptable (ICC = 0.96) [85].

Caregiver Quality of Life Index-Cancer (CQOLC) design by Weitzner et al. [86] which is a 35-item instrument with multiple dimensions for cancer caregivers to measure their quality of life, including 10 items for caregiving burden, 7 items for disruption of daily living, 7 items for positive adjustment, 3 items for financial concerns and 8 items for other psychological and physical factors such as sleep disturbance. Each question is scored using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 4 (very much). The total score ranges from 0 to 140. The original version of the CQOLC demonstrated good psychometric properties, with a Cronbach's alpha coefficient of 0.90 and a test-retest reliability coefficient of 0.95 [87]. The Persian version of the CQOLC that was translated and validated by Khanzari et al. [88] includes four subscales: emotional burden subscale with 14 items, lifestyle disruption subscale with 9 items, positive adjustment subscale with 8 items and financial concerns subscale with 3 items. An additional item (35) assesses the family's perceived interest in caregiving and is included in the total score but not assigned to any subscale.

The internal consistency of the Persian version has been reported to be satisfactory, 0.89 for the total Cronbach's alpha and 0.72 to 0.90 for the subscales.

Compassion-Focused Therapy Satisfaction Questionnaire (CFT-SQ) is a researcherdeveloped questionnaire that will be used to assess participants' satisfaction with the educational content. This questionnaire is adapted from the Mindfulness Program Satisfaction Questionnaire (MPSQ), which is itself derived from the 8-item Client Satisfaction Questionnaire [89]. It consists of eight questions that scored on a 4-point Likert scale (1 to 4) and the total scores range from 8 to 32. According to Matsubara et al. [90], the Cronbach's alpha for both the Cebuano and Waray versions of the CSQ-8 was higher than 0.80, which is acceptable.

Compassion-Focused Therapy Adherence Scale(CFT-AS)

as a researcher-developed scale based on the MBCT-C Adherence Scale will be used to assess the researcher's adherence to the training program's content. MBCT-C-AS is an unpublished scale developed by Semple and Sears [91] . This researcher-developed scale consists of 20 questions, each scored on a 3-point Likert scale ranging from0 =Not at all to2=Clear. Higher scores represent more adherence by the researcher. The researcher-developed scale evaluates the researcher's adherence to the core principals of teaching self-compassion, as well as the structure and sequence of the sessions.

Statistical Strategy

First, we will screen the data for completeness, accuracy of entry, and coding; we will then analyze the data distribution and identify potential outliers using descriptive statistics and visualizations such as histograms and box plots. Statistical model assumptions will also be checked prior to the main analysis. Valid data points will not be excluded solely on the basis of unusual scores. We will analyse the data using linear mixed-effects modelling in R. Data will be imported from SPSS into R after data collection is completed. Participants will be coded according to study allocation (Compassion-Focused Therapy [CFT] vs. Treatment as Usual [TAU]). Outcome variables will be converted from wide to long format to represent the three assessment time points: baseline (T1), right after the intervention (T2), and one-month follow-up (T3). Separate linear mixed-effects models will be fitted for each outcome variable, including selfcompassion, caregiver burden, and caregiver quality of life. In these models, study group, assessment time, and the group × time interaction will be specified as fixed effects, and to account for the correlation between repeated measurements for each individual, a random intercept will be included for each participant. The main focus of the analysis will be the group × time interaction, which will assess whether changes in outcome measures over time differ between the CFT and TAU groups.

Models will be estimated using the maximum likelihood method with the lmerTest package in R.

Type III tests for fixed effects will be used to evaluate whether outcome scores are different between two groups, changes over time, and show different patterns of change between two groups. We will use partial eta-squared (η²p) or standardized regression estimates to report effect sizes, where appropriate. Estimated marginal means will be calculated using the emmeans package. Post hoc comparisons will examine between-group differences at each assessment point, within-group changes across assessment times, and between-group differences in change from before intervention to immediately after completion of the training, before intervention to 4 weeks after completion of the training, and immediately after completing of the training to 4 weeks after completing of the training. Holm-adjusted p-values will be used to control for multiple comparisons.

Participants will be analyzed based on their initial assigned group, in accordance with the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle. Furthermore, mixed-effects models will be used to handle missing outcome data, assuming that the data are missing at random. We will perform Sensitivity analyses, such as per-protocol analysis, to evaluate the robustness of the results and variables such as age, caregiving burden score, and quality of life score will be reported as mean and standard deviation, while variables such as gender, marital status, and education level will be reported as frequency and percentage. No interim analyses or stopping guidelines are planned.

Discussion

As cancer progresses, patients become increasingly dependent on the support of others [14]. Among them, family caregivers play a vital and essential role in providing care [94]. This . heavy responsibility is often associated with a significant caregiving burden, reduced quality of life [95, 96] and frequent experiences of feelings such as shame, guilt, and self-blame in caregivers [64, 97]. Therefore, quality of life and caregiving burden are two important targets for supportive interventions.

Self-compassion, as an important psychological mechanism, activates the safety and comfort system [57] and is effective in reducing shame [60], guilt [98], and self-criticism [99]. CFT, which was designed for individuals experiencing shame and self-criticism [57], provides a suitable framework for this intervention. Delivering this intervention in a web-based format offers significant advantages over traditional face-to-face methods, including greater efficiency, time flexibility, lower cost, wider access, and the possibility of training in discrete time intervals [100].

This study has important methodological strengths, including the design of a randomized clinical trial, focusing on a vulnerable population of family caregivers, using a digital approach to increase accessibility, and planning for a one-month follow-up. Also, we will use standardized instruments to measure burden of care and life quality, which will strengthen the validity of the results. However, the implementation of this study faces three major limitations. First, there is the possibility of self-report bias due to the use of questionnaire instruments. To reduce this limitation, the presence of the researcher in the initial briefing sessions, providing clear instructions, emphasizing the confidentiality of information, and selecting valid and reliable instruments have been considered. Second, virtual education may reduce the motivation and commitment of caregivers to perform home exercises. To address this issue, regular telephone follow-ups, sending daily or weekly reminders via SMS, and creating a virtual group to share experiences, challenges, and successes are planned to strengthen peer and researcher support. Third, the need for stable and adequate-speed internet access. This limitation will be managed by considering stable internet access as one of the study entry criteria and providing the necessary technical training to address common problems in the virtual group.

Finally, this study aims to fill the gap in providing evidence-based interventions for family caregivers of these cancer patients in Iran.

Clinical Implication

If web-based Compassion-Focused Therapy proves effective, the consequences of this study could provide preliminary evidence supporting the use of this approach in nursing support interventions and palliative care services. Furthermore, the findings could help in designing and developing digital support programs aimed at reducing burden of care and improving the life quality for these caregivers.
