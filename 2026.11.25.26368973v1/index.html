Reporting p values imprecisely (e.g., p < .05) reduces transparency, reproducibility, and re-use (e.g., in p value meta-analyses). Best practice is to report exact p-values with three decimal places (e.g., p = .032) unless p values are smaller than 0.001, in which case you can use p < .001.

To check Non-Significant P Value Check

What we found

We found 5 non-significant p values that should be checked for appropriate interpretation.

Show details

Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard), also Internal Review Board - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term); not IEC followed by a number, which is a technical standard - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee/Commission, in the singular or plural - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee, Bioethics Committee/Commission, Ethikkommission - Ethical Review Authority/Unit/Office/Agency - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Human Research Protection Office/Program, Office for Human Subjects Protection - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee(s) on/for (Medical and Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt - Ethics review that was not needed: Ethics review was not required, did not require further ethical review, exempt from formal human research ethics review, no new institutional ethics review

A template line the authors have not filled in (“[Add ethics approval … here.]”) is not a statement.

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

When a statement is expected

The module sorts each paper by where its data came from (the internal .data_source(); the signs and how the rule was tested are described in R/data-source.R): - own: the authors collected data from people or animals, for this paper, in an earlier study of their own, or (a protocol) will collect them; - existing_records: records about individual people that the authors obtained for this study, such as hospital records or a cohort; - existing_unclear: existing data about people from a source the module could not identify; - open_resource: an open resource any researcher can use, such as UK Biobank or NHANES; - none: no data about people or animals (a review, simulation, commentary, theory, tutorial, coding of published papers, or figures about groups); - uncertain: the signs were missing or pointed both ways; - no_text: the paper has no text.

A statement is expected for own, existing_records and existing_unclear. A paper without a statement in these classes, or in uncertain, is flagged. The module reads the text, and the results of power, stat_p_exact, stat_check, repo_check, data_check and prereg_check when they ran before it (run it after them); without stat_p_exact, it finds the p-values in the text.

On 199 PsyArXiv and medRxiv preprints labelled after the rule was fixed, the module decided right whether a statement is needed for 86.9% (the earlier live-data search: 83.4%). Its main errors: other people’s studies described in the words of a new study, records and specimens described only as counts, and open resources the paper does not call open.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). When the paper says anywhere that its tests are one-sided, a p-value that fits a one-sided test in either direction counts as consistent too: half the two-sided p, or 1 minus that half for an effect against the predicted direction. Equivalence tests (TOST) are left out, as their p-values do not test the difference. A statistic written with “<” or “>” is read as a bound, and in a sentence about equal variances an F-test’s two-sided p (twice the one-tailed p) counts as consistent. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Version 0.4.4.3 reported 419 p-values as imprecise, 388 of them correctly (93%), and missed 50 (89% found). How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability).

Provenance, kept so that a TRACE Research Object (TRO) declaration of the run can be written later from a stored result (metacheck #404; the helpers are in R/provenance.R). Only when code was run (execute = TRUE): * run_results$started_at, run_results$ended_at: when each run of a script started and ended (ISO 8601, UTC, milliseconds); NA for a script that was not started. A script run again in a later pass has the times of its last run. The package libraries metacheck installs in the run folder are left out, as are metacheck’s own files there (a script’s runner, and the statistical output it writes in statistical_output/). Files metacheck itself copied or made between passes (see modifications, output_copied, file_placed) show in after_run too. Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Abstract

Background. Increased physical activity (PA) has been associated with decreased risk of several cancers in epidemiologic studies, but the intermediate molecular mechanisms remains poorly characterized.

Leveraging genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary statistics for 22 self-reported and accelerometer-based PA measures, plasma levels of 2,940 proteins (N = 34,557), and multiple site-specific cancers, we performed a two-stage proteome-wide Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis. In Stage 1, we estimated the effects of each PA measure on plasma protein levels and in Stage 2, we evaluated the effects of PA-associated proteins on cancer risk.

We identified 687 PA-protein associations involving 503 unique proteins at FDR<0.05. Across 80 PA-responsive proteins curated from intervention-based studies, we nominally replicated 83% using our MR approach. Approximately 40% of PA-associated proteins were differentially expressed in response to exercise in transcriptomic datasets primarily localizing to muscle tissues, and in immune cell populations. In stage 2, 213 (of 503) PA-related proteins demonstrated significant effects on at least one cancer (FDR<0.05) highlighting them as proteomic intermediates of the effect of PA on cancer. For example, for prostate cancer (PCa), we observed that reductions in sedentary time and increases in light-intensity activity were associated with higher MSMB and lower TNF levels, which in turn reduced PCa risk.

This study applies a two-stage Mendelian randomization framework to link PA, circulating proteins, and cancer risk, enhancing biological interpretation of epidemiologic PA-cancer associations and nominating a focused set of PA-associated proteins as potential targets for further investigation.

Introduction.

Cancer prevalence and mortality in the United States presents a major health burden, with over two million new cancer diagnoses and 611,720 deaths in 2024. Epidemiological and genetic studies, including genome-wide association studies (GWAS), have identified several risk factors and genetic susceptibility loci associated with different cancers 1, 2 . Leveraging the findings from such studies for prevention, remains a central goal of cancer epidemiology. Among such factors, physical activity (PA) has shown consistent evidence for reduced risk of several disease [3] [4] [5] , including reduced risk of cancers 6, 7 . PA is a multifaceted behavioral exposure rather than a single, uniform exposure. It encompasses the overall volume, intensity, and temporal distribution of activity, as well as sedentary behavior, which carries distinct behavioral, metabolic, and circadian correlates. These dimensions may relate to cancer risk through overlapping as well as distinct biological processes, and their effects might not be concordant. However, there is a limited understanding of the biological mechanisms through which different facets of PA influence carcinogenesis. In this respect, delineating the intermediate molecular processes that connect PA to cancer is crucial to deepen etiologic understanding, prioritize pathways for intervention, and inform prevention strategies. Characterizing the molecular correlates of PA therefore requires attention to these different facets rather than treating PA as a monolithic exposure.

A key consideration in delineating these mechanisms is the nature of the PA exposure that is relevant to cancer. Cancer is a long-latency disease, developing over years or decades through the gradual accumulation of inflammatory, metabolic, immune, and proliferative changes. Consequently, the PA-related exposure most relevant to carcinogenesis is not any single episode of exercise, but the sustained, habitual pattern of activity and sedentary behavior maintained over the life course. This distinction is critical and notable for etiologic and mechanistic understanding. Much of the existing evidence of the effects of PA on cancers, derives from candidate biomarker and exercise intervention studies, which characterize acute or short-term responses to prescribed, often higher-intensity exercise, and are frequently limited in size or to selected biomarker panels. While such designs are well suited to defining the immediate physiology of exercise, the transient responses they capture may not reflect the durable biological differences that distinguish habitually active from habitually inactive individuals. An approach that captures the molecular signatures of long-term, habitual PA is therefore needed to interpret its effects on cancer and to understand the intermediate molecular phenotypes that cascade the effects.

With the advent of high throughput technologies, circulating plasma proteins have gained prominence as molecular intermediates for integrative analyses of cancers [8] [9] [10] . Correlated SNPs were excluded based on measures of LD (R² > 0.1) and only SNPs with the most significant p-value were retained as genetic instruments. Subsequently, the instrumental variables for each PA were analyzed to estimate the effect of PA on protein using generalized summary statistics based Mendelian randomization (GSMR; see below).

Removing pleiotropic SNPs: We employed Heterogeneity in Dependent Instruments (HEIDI) 21 to identify and address potential pleiotropy in genetic instruments. Briefly, the HEIDI outlier removal method tests for heterogeneity in the causal estimates derived from individual genetic instruments by measuring the inconsistency in the estimated effects across variants. To retain statistical power to detect heterogeneity, we used the default threshold of 0.01 meaning that even in the absence of pleiotropic outliers, only ~1% of the genetic instruments will be removed by chance, which is unlikely to result in a substantial decrease in power of the subsequent MR analysis.

Mendelian Randomization We performed MR using GSMR 21 which can efficiently handle summary-level data from GWAS and uses the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method to estimate the effect of a risk factor on the outcome. Using GWAS summary statistics from both the risk factor and outcome, GSMR identifies genetic instruments via clumping, removes potentially pleiotropic SNPs (via HEIDI) and uses the final list of genetic instruments to perform MR. GSMR further accounts for mild LD (r 2 < 0.1) between the variants beyond LD clumping, estimated from an appropriate ancestrymatched reference panel. MR inference relies on instrumental variable assumptions: (1) genetic instruments are associated with the exposure (PA traits or protein levels); (2) instruments are independent of confounders of the association between exposure and outcomes; and (3) instruments influence the outcome only through the exposure (no horizontal pleiotropy). To evaluate the robustness of our analyses to violations of these assumptions, we calculated the F-statistic for instrument strength and also compared the significant results with those obtained from MR-Egger. For two-sample MR, we additionally assume comparable ancestry and alignment of genetic effects between exposure and outcome GWAS and correct for bias from sample overlap. TSMR Stage 1: Effect of PA on plasma proteome

We performed summary-based MR using different measures of PA as the exposure and circulating plasma protein levels as outcomes (Figure 1A). We collected a list of 22 different measures of PA, measured using accelerometer 22 and questionnaires 23 (Table S1) from two studies with available GWAS summary statistics. Given that effects of genetic variants on PA can be confounded by BMI and other anthropometric factors, we further conditioned on 4 anthropometric traits (BMI, WHR, body fat percentage and whole body fat mass) 24, 25 to obtain estimates of direct effects of SNPs on PA. For GWAS of plasma protein levels, we used publicly available GWAS summary statistics reported by UK Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project (UKB-PPP), across 2,940 proteins measured using Olink 8 . We utilize the reported GWAS in 34,557 individuals of European ancestry from the discovery cohort. The details of this proteomic GWAS have been reported previously 8 . For each PA, we defined a false discovery rate threshold of 5% to identify statistically significant PA-protein associations.

TSMR Stage 2: Effect of PA-related proteins on cancers

We performed summary-based MR using PA-related plasma protein levels as exposures and 8 different cancers, with publicly available summary statistics, as outcomes (Figure 1B). We collected data from five different studies with available GWAS summary statistics. Sample sizes ranged from 14,803 to 156,319 cases and from 27,065 to 944,762 total individuals of predominantly European ancestry. We further analyzed available subtypes of lung cancer, which included squamous cell carcinoma, adenocarcinoma, and lung cancer among smokers, and renal cell carcinoma, which included clear cell and papillary (Table S1). Similar as above, for each cancer, we defined a false discovery rate threshold of 5% to identify statistically significant protein-cancer associations.

Correction for sample overlap

MR estimates can be subject to potential biases due to overlap between exposure and outcome samples. In our study, GWAS for both physical activity and proteins included individuals from the UK Biobank. To account for this sample overlap, we used MRlap 26 , a software package, to correct for bias in PA and protein associations. For associations with significant difference in effect size before and after correction (p diff < 5x10 -3 ), we used the bias corrected estimates from MRlap.

TSMR Stage 1: Effect of PA on plasma protein levels.

Genetically proxied habitual PA recovers exercise-responsive proteins.

We first assessed whether genetically proxied habitual PA recovers proteins known to respond to exercise, benchmarking the validity of PA instruments before proteome-wide analysis. Of 195 curated exercise-responsive proteins, 84 had available UKB-PPP genetic instruments. Their strongest associations yielded 74 significant PA-protein associations (p<0.05) involving 72 unique proteins, and 21 associations at p<1×10 -3 (Table S2-3). This significantly higher than expected by chance. Even under the conservative assumption that all 22 PA measures are independent, fewer than 57 of the 84 proteins would be expected to show a nominal association, and the observed enrichment remains significant (p=1.3×10⁻ ). Of the 74 significant associations 43 involved accelerometer-derived activity measures, 27 involved self-reported leisure screen/sedentary time and 4 involved moderate-to-vigorous PA, indicating that genetically proxied habitual activity and sedentary behavior, can recover experimentally identified exercise-responsive proteins as well. Several associations were directionally relevant with the known biology of sustained activity. For example, leptin (LEP), an adipokine that declines with habitual activity 27, 32 , was inversely associated with devicemeasured activity, including total log acceleration (TLAC_mean; β=-0.12, p=9×10⁻ ), light intensity activity (LIPA_Mins_mean; β=-0.17, p=2×10⁻ ) and evening activity between 6-8PM (TLAC_10_mean; β=-0.21, p=2×10⁻ ) with the direction of effects being consistent with PA-related improvements in energy balance and cardiorespiratory fitness. This benchmarking demonstrates that MR can realistically recapitulate experimentally observed proteomic effects of PA, supporting its utility as an in silico framework for testing potentially causal hypotheses in molecular exercise biology.

Proteome-wide effect of PA. Encouraged by the high level of corroboration of intervention-based associations by MR results, we next expanded our investigation to identify the effects of PA measures on a proteomic scale. Across PA traits, the median F-statistic for instrument strength was > 22 indicating low bias due to weaker instruments. We identified 687 unique associations between PA and proteins (Figure 2A-B), comprising 503 unique proteins (FDR < 0.05). Analysis using MR-Egger produced similar estimates for significant associations, with the correlation between estimated effects being 83.1% (p< 2x10 -16 ) and only 24 (4.7%) exhibiting nominal evidence (p-value of intercept < 0.05) of pleiotropy. Approximately half of the proteins (50.2%; n = 253/503) were significantly associated with at least one accelerometerbased PA measure (mean 49 proteins per measure), and 59.2% (298/503) with selfreported leisure screen/sedentary time (LST_mean), whereas self-reported moderateto-vigorous activity (MVPA_mean) showed no FDR-significant associations (Table S2). Total log acceleration (TLAC_mean), an index of overall activity volume, had the most associations (176 proteins at FDR < 0.05) the strongest association being with ITGAV (β=0.23, p=3.4x10 -11 ). Among the self-reported measures of PA, the most significant association was of LST_mean with NOTCH3 (β=-0.15, p=8.2x10 -12 ). Sedentary behavior was not simply the inverse of activity. Of 45 proteins associated with both self-reported sedentary behavior and at least one accelerometer-based PA measures, some tracked coherently across modalities. For example, ITGAV was inversely associated with sedentary time (LST_mean; β=-0.08, p=6.2×10⁻ ) and positively with activity volume. Similarly, MYOM3, a muscle-associated protein 33 , exhibited the lower association with sedentary time (β=-0.12, p=6.4×10⁻ ) and higher with evening activity (TLAC_10_mean; β=0.17, p=4.1×10⁻ ), supporting consistent PArelated effects across measurement modalities. There were 138 proteins associated with multiple PA measures, including previously discussed proteins like LEP which also exhibited response to PA in interventional studies, and several members of the tumor necrosis family (TNF, TNFRSF11A), growth factor (VEGFB) and multiple immunoregulatory proteins (CD22, CD72, C2). This indicates that distinct facets of habitual activity converge on a shared set of inflammatory, vascular, and immune proteins while retaining facet-specific signals.. Sensitivity to sample overlap.. Because the PA and protein GWAS both drew on UK Biobank, we assessed bias from sample overlap using MRlap 26, 34 . We found minimal evidence of substantial bias due to sample overlap, with MR estimates before and after correction for sample overlap being largely similar (r pearson = 0.64). Of the 687 total PAprotein associations, 17.8% had nominal differences (p diff < 0.05) between the MR estimates before and after correction by MRlap, and with a low proportion (5.6%) of PAprotein associations having stronger differences (p diff < 0.005), that merited correction by MRlap (Supplementary Figure , Table S4).

In silico analysis in transcriptomic and pathway data.. We contextualized the 503 proteins using ExerGeneDB 35 a database of genes differentially regulated following exercise, PA and related exposures, across several laboratory experiments in humans and mice. Among the 503 PA-related proteins, nearly half (48.1%)corresponded to genes differentially expressed after exercise in at least one human bulk RNA-seq experiment, predominantly in muscle (97.5%) and colorectal mucosa (2.5%; (Table S5). TSMR Stage 2. Effect of PA-associated proteins on cancer.

We next tested whether the 503 proteins associated with habitual PA and sedentary behavior were associated with cancer risk, using summary-based MR with these proteins as exposures and 8 cancers and subtypes as outcomes. Of the 503 proteins, 213 (42.3%) had a significant effect on at least one cancer (FDR<0.05; Figure 3A). Because these proteins are supported in both stages, associated with habitual PA in Stage 1 and with cancer risk in Stage 2, we refer to them as candidate proteomic intermediates linking genetically proxied habitual PA to cancer, while noting we do not directly test formal mediation. The number of associated proteins varied across cancers, averaging about 27 per cancer and largely tracking the size of the corresponding GWAS: prostate cancer had the most (n=94), followed by breast (n=87) and colorectal cancer (n=56; Figure 3B).

Consistent with habitual PA acting through broadly shared as well as tissue-specific biology, 74 proteins were associated with more than one cancer or subtype. Of these, B2M and CD74, both reported as Tier-1 cancer genes in COSMIC 36 , were associated with 6 cancers or subtypes, while 5 proteins (BTN3A2, MUC2, BTN2A1, IFNL1 and SELE) were associated with 5 cancers or subtypes. Additionally, several of these proteins had opposing effects on different cancers suggesting context dependent effects. Prostate cancer (PCa)

Of the 213 candidate intermediates, 94 were associated with PCa risk (Figure 4A; Table S6), the largest set among the cancers examined. Among these, several have established roles in prostate biology and PCa. This includes, Microseminoprotein Beta (MSMB), a protein secreted by the prostate gland 37, 38 which was associated with lower risk for PCa (β = -0.21, p < 10 -125 ), consistent with its reported protective role. In stage 1, we found that longer duration of light-intensity activity was associated with higher MSMB levels (β=0.13, p=9.48x10 -5 ) describing a coherent chain in which greater habitual light activity corresponds to higher MSMB and, in turn, lower PCa risk. We also identified a positive association between genetically predicted TNF levels with PCa risk (β=0.13, p=3.3x10 -05 ) 39, 40 , while total activity volume was inversely associated with TNF (TLAC_mean; β=-0.13, p=3.6x10 -04 ) from stage 1. Higher sedentary time was associated with higher TNF (LST_mean; β=0.07, p=2.2×10 -03 ), opposite in direction to activity and consistent with a coherent pattern in which greater habitual activity, and less sedentary time, lowers TNF and thereby PCa risk.

The 94 PCa-associated proteins were enriched for cancer relevance across independent resources. Nine (9.6%) were Tier-1 cancer genes in COSMIC and 21 (22.3%) were candidate cancer driver genes in the Network of Cancer Genes 36, 41 and 48 (51.1%) exhibited differential expression compared to matched normal tissues in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)-prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD; Figure 4B; Table S7) 42 . Additionally, 11 genes of the corresponding proteins were identified to be essential for cell proliferation in at least CRISPR knockout experiments 43 in prostate cancer cell lines, indicating their relevance to PCa (Figure 4D).

Discussion

Increased PA is consistently associated with reduced risk of several common cancers 49 , but the intermediate molecular phenotypes and mechanisms that connect PA to carcinogenesis remain incompletely characterized. Multi-omics, especially proteomics, provide a powerful approach to interrogate such intermediate processes because circulating proteins reflect integrated responses across tissues, cell types, and signaling networks and are directly relevant to both tumor biology and therapeutic targeting. In this study, we used a two-stage Mendelian randomization (TSMR) framework 14, 15 to link 22 accelerometer-and questionnaire-based PA traits to 2,940 plasma proteins and multiple cancers. We identified 503 proteins whose levels were associated with PA and, among these, 42% exhibited inferred effects on cancer risk. By anchoring on germline instruments, our analysis prioritizes proteins most likely to reflect sustained, habitual activity rather than the transient response to acute exercise. This distinction between habitual and acute exposure is the conceptual basis of the study. Genetic instruments for PA are fixed from conception and index a lifelong propensity toward higher activity and lower sedentary behavior, whereas exerciseintervention studies measure short-term responses to prescribed, often higher-intensity exercise. These are related but non-interchangeable exposures, and for a long-latency disease such as cancer which is primarily driven by cumulative rather than momentary influences, the habitual exposure is the more relevant one. Our benchmarking analysis supports the validity of this approach. Of 84 curated exercise-responsive proteins with genetic instruments, 72 showed nominal MR association with at least one PA measure, an enrichment robust even under the conservative assumption of fully independent measures. Because the genetic and experimental approaches rest on different designs and biases, this convergence of two distinct lines of analyses, supports the validity of PA-focused MR while not implying that genetically proxied liability is equivalent to a deliberate change in activity.

Consistent with PA being a multifaceted exposure, accelerometer-derived activity and self-reported sedentary behavior produced overlapping but distinct proteomic signatures, and sedentary behavior was not simply the inverse of activity. The implicated proteins were enriched for inflammatory, metabolic, immune, and vascular pathways and were differentially expressed following exercise in transcriptomic data, predominantly in muscle and across immune cell populations, situating them in biology plausibly relevant to carcinogenesis. Therefore, linking these proteins to cancer illustrated the value. For prostate cancer, MSMB and TNF provided coherent chains from habitual activity to risk, but across the 94 prostate-associated proteins the implied direction of the PA effect was evenly split between risk-lowering and risk-raising; for RCC, the majority of proteins, including APOL1, implied a non-protective direction. These patterns are consistent with the weak and inconsistent epidemiologic associations of PA with these two cancers, and they underscore that the prioritized proteins represent testable, context-specific hypotheses rather than uniform evidence that activity lowers risk at any given site. Multi-cancer proteins such as B2M, CD74, and MUC2, the last with opposing directions across sites, further point to shared but tissuedependent pathways.

Several limitations have direct consequences on interpretation. First, MR estimates for a behavioral exposure are difficult to interpret causally. Second, forward MR cannot fully exclude reverse or bidirectional effects or shared upstream determinants at either stage. Third, horizontal pleiotropy is likely widespread for a behavioral trait. Fourth, the two-stage design screens rather than formally partitions mediation: proteins supported in both stages are candidate intermediates, and because the same protein GWAS informs both stages, Stage 2 results are best read as prioritization within a pre-selected set rather than independently calibrated tests. Despite these caveats, our work underscores the interpretive gains of embedding PAcancer epidemiology within a proteogenetic framework. By systematically linking different facets of PA to plasma proteins, cell types, pathways, and cancer outcomes, we move beyond simplistic understanding of PA on cancers, to a more nuanced understanding of which biological programs are altered by PA and how they may translate into reduced cancer risk. This framework is explicitly hypothesis generating. Our analysis attempts to shorten the search space from proteome-wide scale to a tractable set of potentially PA-associated candidates with genetic and functional support that merit mechanistic experiments, validation in intervention studies, and evaluation as potential biomarkers or therapeutic targets. This study provides a prioritized catalog of PA-responsive proteins and pathways that can guide experimental follow-up, inform the design and monitoring of PA interventions, and ultimately help to refine prevention strategies that leverage exercise as a modifiable, low-cost tool in cancer control.
