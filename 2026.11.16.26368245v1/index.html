Power Analysis Check

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). When the sentence of a test says it is one-sided (“one-tailed”, “one-sided” or “directional”), a p-value that fits a one-sided test counts as consistent (statcheck’s OneTailedTxt setting; #695). Statcheck might still miss one-sided tests that are described in another sentence, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: Validated on three hand-coded sets of papers (scienceverse/validation: 249 Psychological Science, 250 Economics and 250 MDPI papers). Of 1,783 non-significant p-values in the ground truth, version 0.4.4.3 found 1,719 (3.6% missed), and 94.8% of its 1,814 detections were in the ground truth. How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

A link to a file-sharing service (Google Drive, Dropbox, OneDrive, Box, WeTransfer, MEGA, iCloud Drive, Baidu Netdisk, SURFdrive; see [file_share_links()]) gets a warning and makes the light yellow: such services are not repositories, as files can be changed or deleted at any time, get no DOI, and the link stops working when the account is closed. The report advises a stable repository such as Zenodo instead. Their files are not read, and summary_table$file_share_n counts the links.

Deposits in archives for sequence and expression data (GEO, the INSDC archives BioProject, SRA, ENA and DDBJ, and ArrayExpress; see [seq_archive_links()]), mostly named by accession number, are read for whose deposit they are. The authors’ own are listed with what they hold ([seq_archive_size()]) and are not downloaded or checked as data and code; summary_table$archive_own_n counts them.

R packages on CRAN ([cran_links()], [cran_info()]) are the paper’s own when a paper author is a package author and the paper presents the package; the paper’s own are listed with their version and not checked (summary_table$cran_own_n). A Handle link (hdl.handle.net/...) that leads to a repository read here is checked as a link to that repository ([handle_links()]).

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. Abstract

Brazil is one of the higher meat producer and consumer worldwide. Recently, the sustainability of the meat market is questioned on regard to its high contribution to climate change and public health concerns such as obesity. Hence, further public policies are likely to regulate the meat market and simultaneously reduce meat production and consumption. However, in the context of middle-income countries marked by strong inequalities such as Brazil, the nutritional effect of such market regulation is uncertain. Indeed, one can hypothesize that meat intake plays a protective role against poor nutritious food intake and weight gain among underprivileged settings. To test this hypothesis, this study analyzes household cross-sectional yearly surveys collected from 2018 to 2023 in the 27 Brazilian State capitals. As expected, multivariate estimates show education-specific associations between meat intake and obesity. Among the most educated individuals, declaring not consuming any meat the previous day before the survey is associated with lower body mass index (BMI) and obesity risk, as observed in previous epidemiological studies. By contrast, declaring consuming meat the previous day before the survey appears to be protective against weight gain among poorly educated individuals. We also observe education-specific meat substitution behaviors that help explain these findings. In facts, meat is more likely to be replaced by sweets and ready-to-eat meals among poorly educated individuals compared to more educated ones. We conclude that, everything being equals, meat market regulation policy might worsen public health concerns for under-privileged individuals. Important investments in the education sector and anti-poverty programs are thus needed to limit the raise of nutritional inequalities.

1. Introduction

Meat is an essential source of high-quality dietary protein and further micro-nutrients such as iron, zinc, selenium, vitamin B12, and vitamin D [1, 2]. However, the unprecedented levels of meat production and consumption worldwide highly contribute to global warming [3]. Worldwide livestock production accounts for 66% of greenhouse gas emissions emitted by the agricultural sector and is responsible for 33% of global greenhouse gas emissions [4]. Beyond environmental impacts, excessive meat consumption is also associated with obesity and non-communicable diseases including certain cancers [2, 5, 6]. In fact, as projections indicate that half of the global population will be overweight and one fourth will classified as obese by 2035, the health risks tied to such dietary habits become even more concerning [7]. Consequently, for public health and environmental concerns, there is growing interest in reducing meat intake in diets too rich in meat.

Regulating the meat market by price incentives through the implementation of taxes or quality norms appears to be a relevant public policy for reducing both meat-based public health expenditures and carbon footprint [8, 9]. Evidence shows that meat taxes consistently reduce meat consumption and related greenhouse gas emissions, especially among high meat eaters. As expected, these taxes seem particularly effective when targeting beef, which is the higher source of greenhouse gas emissions [10] [11] [12].

Nonetheless, the nutritional benefits of meat market regulations are questionable for underprivileged sub-populations. Indeed, price-based instruments like tax are known to be socially unfair, by similarly taxing the rich and the poor despite of strong differences in economic (e.g., purchasing power) and socio-cultural (e.g., health and nutrition knowledge and interest) patterns. While it is predictable that most consumers will adjust their diet in response to higher meat price, the specific changes they make (i.e., opting for healthier or unhealthier meat alternatives) are uncertain and may result in different nutritional and health outcomes depending on individual socioeconomic status (SES). Even if individuals with lower SES are usually more responsive to unhealthy food taxes due to a higher price elasticity [13, 14], substitution effects to affordable non-taxed unhealthy alternatives might also be observed [13]. One may therefore hypothesize that individuals with lower SES may be more likely to turn to energy-dense unhealthy meat alternatives (e.g., snacks, sweets and soft-drinks), leading to negative health outcomes such as weight gain and obesity.

The existing public health literature on the meat-obesity association suggests a negative association between meatless diet and obesity. However, while potential different effects according to SES are rarely explored, mots of studies are limited by sample selection biases enable to overstate the protective effects of meat less diet against obesity. While some studies simply compare nutritional outcomes of meat eaters and vegetarians [15] [16] [17], even if the latter are known to be more educated and more prone to have healthier habits than the average, others rely on non-representative cohort data mainly composed of women with a high interest for food and nutrition [18]. Consequently, one can consider that male and poorer individuals represent a black spot in the literature analyzing the meat-BMI association. To clarify this gray area in the literature, a recent study investigates potential heterogeneity in the meat-BMI association according to SES relying on a nationally representative household survey collected in France [19]. Interestingly, while they find that higher meat intake is associated with higher sugar and fat intake and higher BMI among individuals with upper SES, as demonstrated by previous studies, they reversely observe that meat intake is protective against poor nutritious food intake and weight gain among lower SES' individuals. SES-specific effects in the meat-BMI association might be even more concerning among developing countries characterized by substantial income inequalities. This article therefore aims to explore this potential heterogeneity in the case of urban Brazil, relying on repeated cross-sectional surveys from 2018 to 2023 collected in each State capitals. Our focus on the Brazilian adult population is relevant in several respects. Indeed, being a middle-income country with around 1/4 of its population under the poverty line, Brazil is also one of the world's leading meat producers and consumers [20]. The place of meat is so central in the Brazilian food culture that it is consumed similarly in all social strata of the population [21]. Moreover, obesity prevalence in Brazil is worrying (22.5% in 2021), with a stronger increase among lower social groups [22]. Hence, it is expected that regulating the meat market will produce important SES-specific impacts in Brazil, with potential differences in meat substitution strategies along the social ladder. For a better control of obesity trends and the rise of nutritional inequalities, it is therefore needed to know what will be the potential nutritional effects of reducing meat in diet for all, considering potential SES-specific substitution responses.

The rest of the article is structured as follows. In Section 2, we describe the data and methods we use to estimate SES-specific associations between meat intake and BMI, controlling for several individual, household and area factors. Then, we present the results in Section 3 and finally provide a discussion in Section 4.

2. Methods

>Methods

2.1 Data and sample

The study relies on the repeated cross-sectional by-phone survey Vigitel Brazil (Surveillance System for Risk and Protective Factors for Chronic Diseases by Telephone Survey) that continuously monitors the frequency and distribution of significant risk and protective factors for noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) among adults living in the 27 Brazilian State capitals quasi-annually [23, 24]. Vigitel provides probabilistic samples of the adult population (≥18 years old) residing in households with at least one landline telephone [23, 24]. Due to difficulties imposed by the COVID-19 pandemic on data collection, the Vigitel survey was canceled in 2022, and reduced (in terms of sample size) in 2020, 2021, and 2023. 1After merging, the repeated cross-section sample includes more than 100,000 adults who were yearly surveyed from 2018 to 2023 (except for 2022), i.e., more than 20,000 observations per survey year.

The Vigitel dataset is particularly appropriate for our research question because it has the advantage of providing across time individual information on reported food consumption (the previous day before each survey), sociodemographic characteristics (e.g., age, gender, and race/color), and anthropometric measurements (i.e., self-declared height and weight). As recommended by the survey administration, our statistical and econometric analyses are systematically balanced using the weights provided in the survey. To avoid body weight measurement errors, we restrict the sample to non-pregnant and nonlactating adult individuals and excluded adults over the age of 65 years to limit the effects of metabolic aging on weight gain. Hence, our analyses are representative of the urban adult population aged from 18 to 59 years and living in the 27 Brazilian State capitals (i.e., the 27 largest cities).

2.2 Estimation model

To test for specific meat substitution behaviors according to individual SES, we performed linear regressions of several nutritional outcomes on educational attainment, meat consumption, their mutual interactions, and a comprehensive set of covariates. Specifically, for each considered dependent variable, we regressed the following pooled ordinary least square (OLS) estimation model (i.e., pooling five cross-sectional surveys from 2018 to 2023): 𝑌 𝑖𝑎𝑡 = 𝛽 0 + 𝛽 1 𝐸𝑑𝑢 𝑖𝑎𝑡 + 𝛽 2 𝑀𝑒𝑎𝑡 𝑖𝑎𝑡 + 𝛽 3 𝐸𝑑𝑢 𝑖𝑎𝑡 * 𝑀𝑒𝑎𝑡 𝑖𝑎𝑡 + 𝛽 4 𝑋 𝑖𝑎𝑡 + 𝑇 𝑡 + 𝐴 𝑎 + 𝑇 𝑡 𝐴 𝑎 + 𝜀 𝑖𝑎𝑡 𝑌 𝑖 refers to two types of dependent outcomes that characterize the nutritional profile of an individual i living in a large city a and surveyed in the year t. We considered the following: (i) the individual nutritional status, measured by BMI (in kg/m²) and obesity status (i.e., a binary variable with a value of 1 if BMI is higher than 30 kg/m², 0 otherwise); (ii) the individual consumption of food items that are commonly considered as unhealthy when excessively consumed, including sweets (e.g., industrialized flavored yogurt, sweet cakes/biscuits, desserts, ice creams, chocolates/candies, and other sugars), snacks (e.g., packaged savory snacks/chips, savory crackers or salted biscuits and other salted snacks), readyto-eat meals (e.g., instant noodles, packaged instant soup, frozen lasagna, and other ready-to-eat frozen meals) and carbohydrates (e.g., pasta, rice, Brazilian corn couscous, and fresh corn). For each food item, the surveyed respondent must declare whether it was consumed or not the previous day before the survey.

In the set of independent variables, 𝐸𝑑𝑢 𝑖𝑎𝑡 refers to educational attainment (in years of completed schooling) and 𝑀𝑒𝑎𝑡 𝑖𝑎𝑡 refers to the meat eating status. We used educational attainment as proxy for individual SES for two main reasons: i) income and occupation data (i.e., other components of SES) are not available in Vigitel surveys; ii) the level of education provides a more reliable and stable image of the individual's social position and cultural knowledge. Unlike income and occupation, educational attainment is consensually preferred in pooled cross-sectional studies to measure SES because this factor does not widely vary across time among adult populations [25]. 2 To measure meat eating status, we used a four-categories variable distinguishing respondents that declared no consuming any meat (reference category) from respondents that declared consuming only processed meat (e.g., sausages, salami, mortadella, and ham), only raw meat (e.g., beef, pork, chicken, and fish) and both raw and processed meat, the previous day before survey. To explore the association between nutritional outcomes and meat consumption according to educational attainment, we introduced an interaction term between the two factors of interest (𝐸𝑑𝑢 𝑖𝑎𝑡 * 𝑀𝑒𝑎𝑡 𝑖𝑎𝑡 ). Using this interaction term, we could determine whether the associations between meat consumption and nutritional outcomes were significantly different across education status.

We included the main indirect determinants of nutritional outcomes in the set of control variables, 𝑋 𝑖 , excluding direct drivers like calorie intake. Concretely, we controlled for demographic factors such as age and its square (to account for metabolic changes due to aging), sex, race/color (i.e., white, black or mixed, and other), marital status (i.e., married, single, and separated or widowed). We also controlled for individual habits including daily smoking (a binary-response variable) and physical activity practice (in minutes per day).

Finally, 𝑇 𝑡 and 𝐴 𝑎 refer to time fixed-effects (for the five survey years, ranging from 2018 to 2021 and 2023) and area fixed-effects (for the 27 State capitals of Brazil), respectively. 𝑇 𝑡 𝐴 𝑎 is their mutual interaction to neutralize baseline local specificities and potential shocks that may influence nutritional outcomes across cities and time, and make all survey waves comparable. Both fixed effects enable to deal with some forms of endogeneity, especially mitigating omitted variable bias due to time-and areaspecific unobserved factors such as inflation periods, economic cycles, cultural patterns, availability of infrastructure and public services, and environmental condition.

2.3 Model assumptions regarding endogeneity issues

Even if our pooled time-and area-fixed effects model allows to deal with important sources of estimation bias (e.g., local inflation in meat price and other food that may simultaneously affect meat intake and nutritional outcomes), it potentially remains sensitive to other sources of endogeneity that may lead to erroneously over-or under-estimate the education-specific effects of meat intake on nutritional outcomes.

First, time-and area-fixed effects cannot mitigate individual-level omitted variable bias, given that some unobserved individual drivers of nutritional outcomes might be correlated with meat intake (e.g., food preferences and health awareness, experience in composing meatless meals, and family shocks). For instance, a positive correlation between education and healthier lifestyle and nutrition could overstate the protective effect of meatless diet on nutritional outcomes. However, it seems that health awareness are disconnected to meat intake in Brazil where meat consumption is broadly and culturally accepted and consumed in all social groups [21]. As shown in Table 1, non-meat eaters tend to be less educated and slimmer on average than meat eaters in the sample, thus rejecting the risk of an overstating bias. Another issue regarding unobserved individual heterogeneity relies on time-varying shocks or traumatizing events that may affect individuals' diet and BMI, such as family/emotional drama (e.g., divorce, death, job loss, or income-specific inflation response). While some of these shocks may understate the nutritional differences across education levels, for instance price shocks causing weight gain through reduced purchasing power and dietary adjustments [26, 27], other shocks may overstate these differences, such as sudden illnesses that lead to both weight loss and reduced meat consumption.

Unfortunately, we have no means at our disposal to control for such endogeneity sources, except for local meat inflation. 3 Hence, we have no other choice to assume that both over-and under-stating effects compensate and neutralize each other.

Further endogeneity-related bias might be due to the presence of unobserved heterogeneity within area. For instance, individuals with higher education levels may disproportionally live in wealthier neighborhoods with abundant health services and healthier food stores (e.g., selling plant proteins), both factors being theoretically negatively correlated with meat intake and BMI [28]. Given the lack of data, we were unable to accurately disentangle the real effect of educational attainment from the effect of local amenity exposure. However, it can be reasonably assumed that, among urban adults in Brazil, educational attainment is colinear to neighborhood wealth; which renders our result relatively robust to such a source of unobserved heterogeneity. Moreover, it has been shown in the US that food supply differences across neighborhoods (in terms of products' availability and price) contribute by only 10% to nutritional inequality between low-income and high-income households [29]. In future studies, however, it will be interesting to introduce measurements of local amenities into the model to test for potential heterogeneous effects.

Finally, potential reverse causality problems in the association between meat intake and obesity might also bias OLS regressions. A first risk of reverse causality comes from the expected gap in energy needs between larger and slimmer bodies: higher BMI implying greater calorie intake. Indeed, the basal metabolic rate (BMR) of an individual with obesity is significantly higher [30]. Hence, individuals with higher BMI may need to purchase more meat to satisfy their BMR. This point is however not a major concern in our study context insofar as there is no theoretical causal effect of educational attainment on 3 We replicated Eq.1 controlling for year-and city-based price consumption index variations relative to city-based price consumption index variations and observed similar findings (results are available in Table S1 in Supplementary Materials). an individual's BMR. A more concerning risk of reverse causality though may rely on the sudden adoption of a low-meat diet by people with obesity. One can assume that individuals diagnosed by a doctor as being with obesity are more likely to receive nutritional advice recommending the reduction of fatty meat in diet. Yet, educational attainment is positively correlated with health services' attendance. As shown by Jinnette et al. [31], individuals with higher education more frequently attend the doctor and are more willing to follow their advice. Hence, there could be some individuals with obesity that have decreased their meat purchase over the period, especially among higher education groups.

Theoretically, such a reverse causality may only understate the protective nutritional effect of meat reduction in diet among higher education groups, and then understate the expected opposite nutritional effects of meat consumption according to educational attainment. Consequently, this form of reverse causality is neither a major issue in our study context.

4. Discussion

In Brazil, meat consumption is determined by important factors such as purchasing power, food availability and affordability, rising prices and social inequalities [37] [38] [39]. SES is a crucial determinant of food access in Brazil [38]. Individuals in higher-income strata consume more meat, as shown by population-based studies in Brazil, where individuals with higher economic status reported higher total meat [40, 41], processed meat [42, 43, 44], and red meat consumption [44, 45]. As well, Ferreira et al. showed that food insecurity correlates with lower meat intake, noting that severe food insecurity reduced red meat consumption by 26.9% compared to food security [45]. Nevertheless, meat intake still exceeded EAT-Lancet recommendations across all income levels and food security statuses [45], a trend linked to the deep cultural integration of red meat in Brazil [46, 47].

Based on a representative sample of the Brazilian adult population, this study showed educational attainment trends regarding the association between meat consumption and obesity. Although meat consumption the day before the survey was positively associated with BMI and obesity in groups with higher levels of education, our results show that meat intake is associated with lower BMI among poorly educated individuals. Consistent with expectations, this study suggests that educational status acts as a moderating variable in the link between meat consumption and body weight. To our knowledge, this study is the first to show opposite relationships between meat consumption and BMI in lower-and higher-educational-attainment groups in the Brazilian context, converging with recent results found in high-income countries like France [19]. Another important finding of the study is that meat-substitution behaviors may also depend on educational attainment. Among poorly educated individuals, meat is more likely to be replaced by sweets and ready-to-eat meals than among more educated individuals.

Our analyses also revealed another issue in the relationship between meat consumption and education level: possible health risky meat substitutes among individuals with lower education levels in the sample. Our results estimated a tendency to replace meat with ultra-processed foods (sweets and readyto-eat meals) among individuals with lower education levels who reported not consuming meat the day before the survey. Replacing meat, a source rich in high-quality protein, iron, and zinc, with ultraprocessed foods (high in sugars, saturated fat, and additives) has serious consequences. This type of substitution can accelerate weight gain and increase the risk of obesity [48]. Consuming UPFs raises the population-level risk of developing type 2 diabetes, hypertension, metabolic syndrome, and cardiovascular diseases, particularly among vulnerable segments of society [49]. This also exacerbates social and health disparities, as groups with lower SES are driven to choose UPFs due to their lower cost and availability, thereby exposing this population to the development of non-communicable chronic diseases [50, 51].

In Brazil, previous studies have shown consistent increases in the prices of healthy foods and decreases in the prices of UPFs [52, 53]. Caldeira at al. showed that Brazil has seen prices of ultra-processed foods fall between 2018 and 2024, and they are projected to become the cheapest food group by 2026 [54]. scenario hinders the adoption of healthy, sustainable diets, particularly among lower-SES populations [54].

Popkin et al. [55] discuss the need for robust public policies that make healthy foods more affordable and competitive than UPFs. These policies include taxing UPFs and subsidizing fruits, vegetables, and other fresh foods. Some countries now try to implement this approach [55, 56]. Pereda et al. [57] report that policies combining UPF taxation with subsidies for unprocessed and minimally processed foods are fairer, as they help reduce inequalities in access to healthy foods and minimize potential negative impacts on the well-being of individuals of low SES.

Regarding environmental concerns, Cruz et al. [58] showed that both beef and UPFs had a significant linear association with carbon and water footprints in crude and adjusted models. The authors also noted that the greatest reduction in environmental footprints would occur when both beef and UPFs purchases are decreased, resulting in a 21.1% reduction in carbon footprint and a 20% reduction in water footprint. However, evidence from a recent modeling study in Brazil shows that healthy, sustainable diets are currently more expensive than the average diet, despite their benefits for health and the environment [59].

From this perspective, enhancing food sustainability in contexts of high social inequalities is a great challenge. As suggested by our results, under-privileged individuals may replace meat with affordable ultra-processed foods conducting to aggravate health inequalities. Complementary findings reported that meat costs for socially vulnerable population groups may affect consumption of other food groups, such as fruits and vegetables [40]. In this context, social inequalities complicate a consensus on meat consumption recommendations for the Brazilian population that are nutritionally, culturally, ecologically, and socially appropriate [40].

5. Policy implications

Developing national dietary recommendations based on sustainability is challenging in contexts of strong social inequalities, since restricting meat consumption may increase health inequalities and obesity concerns among vulnerable groups. To promote healthier, more sustainable diets, public health interventions must account for regional and socioeconomic disparities, as well as the cultural significance of meat. Policies should prioritize the human right to food and consider the socioeconomic and environmental factors that influence and limit access to meat.

The Dietary Guidelines for the Brazilian Population have sustainability as one of their guiding principles. These Guidelines recommend prioritizing natural or minimally processed foods over ultraprocessed foods [59]. In this regard, public efforts should aim to increase the physical and economic availability of unprocessed and minimally processed foods, especially in vulnerable areas. Strengthening family farming and discouraging ultra-processed foods through fiscal measures are important for creating food environments that promote health and sustainability.

6. Limitations and strengths

The findings of this study should be analyzed in light of certain limitations. The first limitation is the cross-sectional nature of the data, which does not allow the identification of causal effects. Another limitation of this study is the self-reported nature of consumption frequency, a characteristic of the Vigitel survey. As well, the Vigitel survey does not provide income and food security data; consequently, educational attainment served as the primary proxy for SES, which may not fully capture economic hardship or food access. Despite such methodological limitations, the study highlights the importance of using large national databases, such as the Vigitel survey, to conduct comprehensive and representative analyses. In this sense, this is one of the first national-sample studies addressing educational inequalities and meat consumption in Brazil.

This study offers important insights into the relationship between education level and meat consumption.

Our results have broader implications for public health in the country, including the development of public policies and targeted health interventions to reduce socioeconomic inequalities and promote access to healthy, sustainable foods.

Further studies should evaluate meat substitution strategies and the motivations behind them in greater depth. Research also should simulate and evaluate the potential impact of fiscal policies, particularly targeted taxation on ultra-processed foods, including ultra-processed meat products, to assess their effectiveness in shifting consumer behavior, improving population diet quality, and mitigating socioeconomic and health disparities.
