Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Full text

Abstract

Physical exercise lowers blood pressure, but guidelines provide broad recommendations that rarely account for age, comorbidities, medications and hypertension severity. A structured, queryable resource of exercise interventions for hypertension is lacking, while manual curation is labor-intensive and difficult to scale. We present PAHD (Physical Activity for Hypertension Database), built with large language models (LLMs). An LLM screened 2,548 PubMed records through title-and-abstract and full-text assessment, selecting 255 studies. LLMs extracted arms, exercise protocols, participant characteristics, and outcomes, with two LLMs cross-checking study structure and each value linked to a verbatim quote. Programmatic integration generated 17,273 standardized records from 253 studies, one per reported comparison. Against adjudicated benchmarks, screening achieved 86.3% recall and 80.6% precision, and extraction 90.1% recall and 90.5% precision for blood pressure values. A clinician-facing portal retrieves studies matched to patients' hypertension stage and comorbidities. PAHD is a reusable, traceable evidence resource for personalized exercise interventions in hypertension.

Introduction

Hypertension is characterized by persistently high blood pressure in the systemic arteries [1]. Blood pressure (BP) is recorded with two values: systolic BP, the pressure exerted on artery walls when the heart contracts, and diastolic BP, the pressure when the heart relaxes between beats [2]. Under the American College of Cardiology (ACC) / American Heart Association (AHA) guidelines [3], an individual is considered hypertensive when systolic BP is at least 130 mm Hg or diastolic BP is at least 80 mm Hg. These thresholds classify nearly half of US adults as hypertensive [4], underscoring its importance as a public health problem. The economic burden is substantial: projections from 2010 to 2030 estimate that total direct costs attributed to hypertension will nearly triple (from US $130.7 billion to US $389.9 billion) and indirect costs from lost productivity will almost double (from US $25.4 billion to US $42.8 billion) [5].

Effective prevention and treatment of hypertension are therefore critical to reducing disease burden and improving population health.

Physical activity (PA) is a recommended non-pharmacological intervention to control BP [1, 5]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that regular PA improves cardiovascular fitness, lowers BP, and enhances quality of life [5] [6] [7]. Current guidelines on PA and hypertension management [3, 8, 9] are designed for general populations. For instance, CDC guidelines advise at least 150 minutes per week of moderate-intensity aerobic activity, plus muscle-strengthening activity on at least 2 days per week [9], while the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) suggests 30 minutes per day of continuous or accumulated exercise [8]. These guidelines, while valuable, are largely generic: even recent consensus advice that tailors the type of exercise to the blood pressure level [10] rarely accounts for individual-level factors such as age, comorbidities, or medication use, all of which shape the safety and efficacy of an exercise protocol.

Safe and effective exercise prescription requires tailoring protocols to individuals. A promising solution is to collect effective PA interventions for subpopulations, characterized by their individual-level attributes, into a structured, queryable knowledge base, which in turn requires a systematic and comprehensive review of the existing literature on PA interventions for hypertension. Prior efforts, such as PAHFKB for heart failure [11], highlight both the utility of such resources and the barriers to scaling them: paper screening and data extraction were performed manually, demanding extensive human effort. Given the growing volume of hypertension research and the need for continuous updates, such manual approaches are not sustainable.

Large language models (LLMs) make such automation feasible. They can answer freetext questions and extract information from documents without being trained for the specific task [12], and they have been used to turn scientific papers into large databases of structured records [13, 14]. With careful task design, prompts and output schemas, they can take over much of the repetitive work of reading hundreds or thousands of papers, which makes it possible to build, and keep updating, a structured and personalized knowledge base of exercise interventions for hypertension with far less human labor.

Recent work applies LLMs to the steps of evidence synthesis. In a study that each used ten published articles, Claude 2 extracted pre-specified data elements with an accuracy of 96.3% [15], and in six ongoing systematic reviews, LLM-assisted extraction was at least as accurate as extraction by humans alone [16]. The agreement of GPT-4 screening with human reviewers ranged from none to moderate, depending on the review stage and the language, but it was almost perfect for full texts with highly reliable prompts [17].

TrialMind links study search, screening and the extraction of clinical outcomes for metaanalysis [18]. Most of these tools automate one step of one review, and few evaluations keep a test set apart from prompt development [19, 20]. However, these approaches have not established a reusable resource that keeps arm-level exercise protocols, baseline characteristics and results, each with a source quote. Such a resource is essential for ensuring evidence traceability, enabling comparisons across heterogeneous studies, and identifying the exercise protocols that were tested in patients with similar clinical characteristics. Meta-analyses of exercise and BP likewise rank exercise modes by their average effect [21]; they cannot show which protocol suits a patient with a given stage, comorbidity or medication.

To the best of our knowledge, no structured database currently exists for exercise interventions specific to hypertension. In this work, we fill that gap. Our contributions are: (i) a two-step LLM screening procedure (title and abstract, then full text); (ii) an evidence-grounded extraction procedure in six steps, in which two LLMs cross-check the structure of each study and every extracted value carries a verbatim source quote; (iii) PAHD, a standardized database of 253 studies with one record per reported comparison; and (iv) a clinician-facing web portal for profile-specific evidence retrieval. We evaluated screening and extraction against two benchmarks, each annotated independently by two annotators and adjudicated, and the portal with automated tests of its usability, reliability and summary faithfulness.

Results

Pipeline overview

Our PAHD construction has four stages: screening, information extraction, database construction, and a web portal (Fig 1). Large language models (LLMs) screen the studies and extract their content; a programmatic pipeline then builds the database without further LLM calls. In the screening stage, we retrieved candidate studies from PubMed with a Boolean query designed to miss as few relevant studies as possible. The study, interventions, arms and results steps then extract the remaining details, guided by the data schema, and every extracted value is paired with a verbatim quote from the source. In the information extraction stage, six steps each extract one part of every eligible study, and together they collect the data items that systematic reviews collect from each study, as listed in the Cochrane Handbook: its methods, participants, interventions, outcomes and results [23]. The skeleton step identifies the arms, that is, the groups of participants that a study compares, and the interventions they receive. The outcomes step identifies the outcomes and the time points at which they were measured. Together, these two steps define the structure of a study, on which every later step depends. Two LLMs (gpt-6-luna and Google gemini-3.8-flash [24]) therefore extracted the structure independently. Where they disagreed, both reviewed the two answers blindly against the paper, and a third LLM (Anthropic Claude Sonnet 5.5 [25]) adjudicated the disagreements that remained. The four other steps then fill in the details with gpt-6-luna: the study step extracts facts about the study as a whole, such as its design, region and sample size; the interventions step extracts the protocol of each intervention, with its frequency, intensity, session duration and type of exercise; the arms step extracts the baseline characteristics of each arm, such as age, sex, body mass index (BMI), medications and comorbidities; and the results step extracts every reported result of each outcome. Every extracted value is paired with a verbatim quote from the paper, so that it can be checked against the source.

In the database construction stage, four programmatic steps turn the extractions into the database. The tables step stores them in linked tables, one per entity of the data model (Fig 2): papers, arms, interventions, time points, outcomes, baseline statistics and results, connected by the identifiers that the skeleton and outcomes steps assigned. The standardization step removes results that cannot be used, such as comparisons between two baseline values, and maps free-text values to standard categories and units. The joining step combines the tables into one table, and the quality control step checks every record for internal consistency, corrects clear errors by rule and flags the rest. Each record of the final table is one comparison reported in a paper: an outcome, the two sides being compared (groups, time points or both), the statistics of each side and the difference between them. These records form PAHD.

Corpus construction and study selection

The identification and selection of candidate studies are summarized in the PRISMA flow diagram [26] (Fig A in S1 Appendix). In the title-and-abstract step, the LLM judged each study from its title and abstract against five eligibility criteria: (1) the population included people with hypertension or elevated BP; (2) a structured exercise training program with multiple sessions was evaluated; (3) the effect of exercise could be isolated from other components of the intervention, such as diet; (4) the study was a completed intervention study with reported results; and (5) BP was reported as an outcome, with a statistically supported comparison involving an exercise arm. Information extraction

All 255 eligible studies went through the six extraction steps. Each request to an LLM contained one whole paper: its text, converted from PDF to Markdown so that tables keep their rows and columns, and its figures as images, so that values shown only in a figure could also be read by the LLMs, all of which accept images as input. The prompts were developed on 14 of the included studies, which were kept out of the test set of the extraction benchmark. The two LLMs that extracted the structure of each study rarely disagreed. On the arms and interventions (skeleton step), they disagreed on at least one item in 23 of the 255 papers, and on the outcomes and time points (outcomes step) in 115; these disagreements were resolved by a blinded review by both LLMs or by the adjudicating LLM (Methods). Across all steps, the pipeline produced 53,782 evidence quotes. A programmatic check found 98.9% of the quotes taken from the text in the paper itself; the others were read from figure images (Note B in S1 Appendix). Calls for different papers can run in parallel (Table B in S1 Appendix).

Baseline characteristics were mostly captured. A baseline statistic is one reported value of one characteristic for one arm, such as the mean age of the exercise arm with its standard deviation, or the number of women in the control arm. The pipeline found 87.8% of the benchmark's baseline statistics (recall), and 81.0% of the statistics it extracted matched a benchmark statistic (precision), for an F1 of 84.3% (78.7-89.2%).

The exercise prescription of each intervention was usually captured (Table 3; Fig 3b). For each intervention, the pipeline copies the verbatim protocol description and extracts five prescription parameters as short texts: frequency, session duration, intensity and modality (the FITT components) and the total duration of the program. To score the content, we compared each of the 410 extracted parameters (82 test interventions × 5 parameters) with the benchmark text.

A pair counted as correct when the two texts were the same after normalization, when the extraction contained the benchmark text, or when their numbers and units matched; the remaining pairs were judged by a second LLM (Methods). By this measure, 81.2% (76.5-85.1%) of the parameters were correct.

Frequency was the most accurate parameter (92.7%), followed by session duration (85.4%), modality (80.5%) and intensity (75.6%). The verbatim protocol descriptions were faithful to the paper: 99.8% of the sentences of the extracted descriptions were found in the paper text, and the extracted descriptions recovered 90.5% of the benchmark protocol sentences that appear in the paper (Table 3).

A row of a results table usually holds several numbers: for example, the mean SBP of the exercise arm at baseline and after training, each with its standard deviation, and the difference between the arms with its p-value. We therefore scored the BP results number by number, as data points, rather than as table rows. A data point is the statistic of one group at one time point (such as the mean SBP of the exercise arm after 12 weeks), the change of one group between two time points, or one difference between groups. A data point counted as correct when its outcome, groups and time points matched the benchmark and its value was equal. Discussion

To our knowledge, PAHD is the first structured database of PA interventions for hypertension. We built it with an LLM-assisted pipeline and paired it with a clinicianfacing portal for profile-specific evidence retrieval. The pipeline combines two-step screening with a six-step, evidence-grounded extraction procedure, and it converts heterogeneous papers into a uniform record: one row per reported comparison. The resulting resource spans the demographic, comorbidity, medication, and FITT dimensions relevant to individualized prescription, and its extracted effects recover the expected antihypertensive signal of exercise, supporting face validity.

Three design choices drive the quality of the resource. First, decomposing extraction by the parts of a study (its structure, interventions, arms and results) keeps each model call scoped to a small, unambiguous slice of the paper, which we found necessary to obtain reliable per-arm and per-outcome values. This design also yields stable identifiers that make assembly deterministic and auditable. Fixing the structure of each study first, with two LLMs cross-checking each other and a third LLM adjudicating the disagreements that remained, limits structural errors in the later steps. Second, pairing every extracted value with a verbatim source quote grounds the output in the text and provides a direct handle for verification, which is important because modern LLMs cannot be assumed to be free of hallucination [33]. Third, standardization and quality control are deterministic and logged, and clear errors such as reversed signs were corrected by rule or by review against the paper. Against the extraction benchmarks, the pipeline recovered the structure of the studies almost perfectly and BP results with an F1 score of 90.3%, while the weaker fields point to concrete targets for refinement.

Compared with prior manually curated resources such as PAHFKB, PAHD reduces the human effort required to screen and extract from a large literature and is designed to be re-run as new trials appear, addressing the timeliness limitation of static, hand-built knowledge bases. Compared with LLM pipelines built for a single review question, PAHD keeps the arm-level protocols, baseline characteristics and results of every included study in one reusable table. Relative to general-purpose LLM assistants, the portal grounds its output in retrieved, peer-reviewed evidence and restricts the language model to explaining rather than selecting studies, which improves traceability at the cost of the fluency an unconstrained model might offer.

Our study has several limitations. First, the corpus is restricted to English-language, randomized controlled trials indexed in PubMed and published from 2005 onward, so relevant evidence in other designs, languages, or sources is not represented, and studies without accessible full text were excluded. Second, only the study structure was crosschecked by two LLMs; the other steps rely on a single LLM. Third, the benchmarks are modest in size (36 test studies for extraction), each relied on a single adjudicator, and results were evaluated for BP outcomes only, so the accuracy for other outcomes is unknown. Fourth, several fields are extracted less accurately and should be interpreted with caution: the number completing each arm, exercise intensity, and the dispersion and number analyzed of BP results. Fifth, the portal's matching is rule-based and uses only the hypertension stage, comorbidities and exercise category. Its summaries cover the protocols that the user selects, may add general background that is not in the data, and in one of 30 tests used prescriptive wording. As this study primarily focused on developing the evidence database and establishing the technical feasibility and reliability of the portal, its evaluation was limited to automated testing. Formal clinician evaluation and assessment of patient outcomes were beyond the scope of the present study and remain important directions for future research. Finally, PAHD is a research and clinicaldecision-support resource, not a medical device; its output summarizes existing evidence and is not a substitute for individualized clinical judgment.

Several directions follow naturally. The corpus could be broadened beyond Englishlanguage randomized controlled trials and refreshed on a schedule to keep the database current. Importantly, the database provides an evidence-grounded foundation for developing an interactive patient-clinician chatbot that integrates clinical characteristics with patients' exercise preferences, motivations, and physical limitations to support clinicians in delivering reliable, personalized physical activity recommendations. More broadly, the pipeline is not specific to hypertension: because it is organized around the generic structure of a randomized controlled trial, the same framework could be applied to other chronic conditions in which physical activity is therapeutic, such as obesity, type 2 diabetes, and heart failure, laying the groundwork for scalable, personalized, evidencebased guidance across chronic disease management.

Methods

Data sources and corpus construction

PubMed was selected as the primary data source given its broad coverage of peerreviewed biomedical literature and high-quality metadata. To scope the database, we first specified PICO terms [34]: the population is people with hypertension; the intervention involves physical exercise; the comparison is a no-intervention or sedentary control versus exercise; and the outcome includes, at minimum, blood pressure. Expanding these terms with synonyms and related concepts produced a Boolean query that combines three groups of terms (hypertension; exercise or control conditions; blood pressure) with AND, and the terms within each group with OR (Table A in S1 Appendix). We then applied filters on publication date, text availability, article type, language, species, and participant age. We retained studies published between 2005/01/01 and 2025/12/31 with accessible abstracts and full texts, restricted to randomized controlled trials, written in English, and conducted in adults (≥19 years, per PubMed age-group definitions). Randomized controlled trials were chosen because they provide well-defined exercise protocols and study populations; meta-analyses and systematic reviews were excluded because they summarize rather than report the arm-level granularity the database requires. The resulting study metadata (PMID, title, abstract, MeSH terms, journal, publication year, and publication type) formed the input to screening. Exact record counts at each stage are reported in Results and Fig A in S1 Appendix.

Study screening

Screening used an LLM (OpenAI gpt-6-luna) in two steps with the same five eligibility criteria (prompts in Figs B-D in S1 Appendix). We chose gpt-6-luna, for screening and for most extraction steps, to balance capability and cost, given the large number of calls.

The criteria asked whether: (1) the study population included individuals with hypertension or elevated BP (prehypertension); (2) a structured exercise training program with multiple sessions over days or weeks was evaluated as an intervention; (3) the effect of exercise could be isolated from other intervention components, such as diet, for the hypertensive population; (4) the study was a completed intervention study with reported results; and ( 5) BP (e.g., SBP, DBP, MAP, ambulatory BP) was reported as an outcome, with a statistically supported comparison involving the exercise arm. For each criterion the model returned a decision and a supporting quote, plus a one-sentence explanation for any decision other than "Yes."

The title-and-abstract step used only the title and abstract, with the model's extended reasoning turned off (reasoning effort "none"). The model could answer "Yes," "No," or "Unclear." It was told to prefer "Unclear" whenever the abstract was silent or ambiguous, because a wrong "No" removes a study for good. Studies with at least one "No" were excluded. The full-text step used the full text of the paper, with the title-and-abstract decisions as context only, and a low reasoning effort. Because the API cannot read PDF files directly, each paper was first converted to Markdown with the Mathpix API [35].

Mathpix was chosen because it reliably converts tables and mathematical expressions with sufficiently high quality; most results in our corpus are reported in tables, and a poor conversion of tables would cause values to be lost or attributed to the wrong group. The model re-judged every criterion and had to answer "Yes" or "No." A study was retained only if all five criteria were "Yes." Both steps followed the same decision rules. For example, a study of the acute response to a single exercise session failed criterion 2. A multi-arm trial passed criterion 3 if one arm differed from another only by exercise.

Statistical support for criterion 5 could be a p-value, a confidence interval, a test statistic, a significance symbol defined in a table legend or footnote, or an explicit statement of significance or non-significance, provided that the paper states its significance level.

Information extraction

Extraction ran in six steps: skeleton, outcomes, study, interventions, arms and results.

Each step had its own prompt (Figs E-O in S1 Appendix) and a fixed JSON schema.

Together, the steps collect the data items that the Cochrane Handbook lists for data collection in systematic reviews [23]: methods, participants, interventions, outcomes and results. We did not ask a model to extract a whole paper in one pass. Research papers are hierarchical: a study defines arms, interventions, time points and outcomes, and every result is indexed by them. Enumerating all of these while also filling in every value imposes a large combinatorial load, which in our experience degrades accuracy and increases misattributed values. We therefore fixed the structure of each study first (the skeleton and outcomes steps). Later steps received this structure as compact entity cards with stable identifiers, and each call was scoped to one paper, one intervention or one outcome. The identifiers also make the later joins deterministic and auditable.

Every request contained the whole paper: the Markdown text produced by Mathpix Errors in the skeleton or the outcome list propagate to every later step. The skeleton and outcomes steps were therefore run with two independent extractors, OpenAI gpt-6-luna and Google gemini-3.8-flash, both with a medium reasoning effort. A program then aligned the two outputs. Arms were matched one to one by the similarity of their names (including every name under which an arm appears in the paper), equal numbers of participants, the similarity of the interventions they receive and their position; interventions by their names and by the arms that receive them; and outcomes and time points mainly by their names and the table labels under which they appear, never matching, for example, a systolic with a diastolic outcome. Each disagreement between the aligned outputs became an item, for example a different design, an arm or outcome that only one model lists, a different division of the study into arms, an uncertain match, a different number of participants in an arm, a link between an arm and an intervention that only one model lists, or a time point that only one model treats as the baseline. In a crosscheck, both models reviewed every item blindly: they saw the two answers in random order, without knowing which model had written which answer, and judged them against the paper. An item was resolved when the two reviewers agreed. Otherwise, it went to a final adjudicator (Anthropic Claude Sonnet 5.5), which weighed the answers and the reviewers' arguments against the paper text. Disagreements were uncommon. In the skeleton step, 23 of 255 papers had at least one disagreement (47 items; 20 resolved by the cross-check and 27 by adjudication). In the outcomes step, 115 papers had at least one disagreement (357 items; 256 resolved by the cross-check and 101 by adjudication). The final skeleton and outcome list of each paper were then assembled by program.

The four other steps extract the details against this structure with gpt-6-luna (medium The model also receives the table footnotes, so that significance symbols can be resolved.

All steps follow the same grounding rules. Every extracted value is paired with a verbatim evidence quote and its location in the paper. The model must keep the paper's own wording, numbers and units, and must not convert, compute or infer values; normalization is left to the deterministic steps that follow.

A field can carry a status ("not reported," "not applicable" or "unclear") instead of a value, so that missing information is not guessed. A program checks each quote against the paper text, allowing for formatting differences such as LaTeX markup. The output of the results step is also checked against the entity cards and the paper: every arm, intervention and time point that a result cites must exist and be linked to the outcome, each arm must receive the interventions listed with it, a result must not compare a side with itself, every number must have a supporting quote that is found in the paper, and the same group must not carry different values in different rows. Failed checks are logged as warnings rather than fixed; the quality control step later turns some of them into flags, for example a time point that is not linked to the outcome.

Database construction

The database construction stage turned the extraction outputs into the database in four deterministic steps, without further LLM calls. The tables step flattened the outputs into linked tables, one per entity: papers, arms, interventions, time points, outcomes, baseline statistics and results. The tables are keyed by the stable identifiers and keep the raw values and their evidence.

The standardization step filtered and standardized these tables. It removed result rows that compared two baseline values or held no result, comparisons between two control conditions or between medication-only arms, the one study in which no arm received exercise, and one study that reported BP only as correlations with other measures. Each intervention was mapped to a PA category: aerobic, resistance, flexibility, neuromotor (e.g., yoga or tai chi) or other forms (e.g., breathing exercise or inspiratory muscle training). Non-exercise conditions were labeled by type, such as usual care or no intervention. Frequency and session duration were expressed in consistent units, and a weekly exercise volume was computed from them. These PA mappings were made by rules and then reviewed by hand for every intervention. Countries were mapped to ISO 3166-1 alpha-3 codes [36]. Baseline characteristics were mapped to standard vocabularies: race and ethnicity to the US OMB categories [37], medications to WHO ATC classes [38] and comorbidities to ICD-10 titles [39]. P-values, however written (e.g., "P<0.001," "NS"), were parsed into an operator and a number; when a paper stated no significance threshold, 0.05 was assumed.

The joining step joined the tables into one flat table with one row per reported comparison. Each row carries the study context, the protocol and baseline characteristics of each side, the statistics of each side and the contrast. Rows were oriented so that the exercise side comes first, and differences follow one sign convention: intervention minus comparator, and later minus earlier time point. Each arm was assigned BMI categories (WHO cut-offs [40]) and a hypertension stage (2025 AHA/ACC guideline [3]) from its mean baseline values. Comparisons reported more than once, for example in a table and in the text, were merged when compatible and otherwise kept as distinct results; the few conflicting duplicates were resolved by hand.

The quality control step checked the joined table record by record. Rules tested each record for internal consistency, and each failed test adds a named flag: the reported difference does not match the two group values; the direction of a difference cannot be determined; an interval does not contain its estimate, or contradicts the p-value; a number does not appear in the evidence quotes or the paper; a resting BP value is implausible; the number analyzed exceeds the number assigned; a value repeated in several places differs; the stated direction contradicts the values; or a standard deviation, standard error or interquartile range is negative.

Clear errors with a deterministic fix, such as a reversed sign convention or swapped interval bounds, were corrected automatically, and the value as reported was kept in the record. Flags on BP outcomes were reviewed by hand against the paper. The reviewer could use a general-purpose LLM chat assistant (for example, ChatGPT) to locate information in a paper or to explain symbols and footnotes, but made every decision and was responsible for the result. In the final table, 15,389 records (89%) passed every check and 390 (2%) were corrected by rule, mostly to harmonize the sign convention of reported differences. Of the flagged BP records, 392 were confirmed or corrected by hand and 55 were removed. The remaining 1,102 records (6%), 89% of them on non-BP outcomes, keep an open flag. Numbers that an LLM read from a figure image cannot be checked against the text, so a separate column marks the 169 records in which some numbers come only from a figure. Every fix, edit and open flag is recorded in the quality-control status and flag columns of the database.

Screening validation

We built a screening benchmark of 500 studies from the pool of 2,548. To include enough eligible studies, sampling was stratified by keyword flags in the title, abstract and MeSH terms. Stratum S1 held studies with both a hypertension and an exercise term (880 in the pool; 225 sampled), S2 an exercise term only (920; 200) and S3 no exercise term (748; 75). Each study carries a stratum weight, the inverse of its stratum's sampling fraction (3.9, 4.6 and 10.0), so that weighted estimates describe the whole pool.

Two annotators judged each study independently on the same five criteria, blinded to the stratum and to the pipeline's decisions. A human adjudicator resolved every disagreement against the paper. The annotators and the adjudicator could use general-purpose LLM chat assistants, for example to locate information in a paper or to explain symbols and footnotes, but made every judgment themselves and were responsible for the final labels.

We summarized agreement before adjudication with raw agreement, Cohen's κ and Gwet's AC1.

We scored the end-to-end screening decision: a study counted as included only if it passed both screening steps. We report recall, precision, F1, F2, specificity and work saved, the proportion of the pool not passed on to extraction. All estimates are weighted by the stratum weights, with 95% confidence intervals from a stratified bootstrap of studies. We also report the title-and-abstract step alone, and results for studies whose label was agreed or adjudicated (Note C in S1 Appendix).

Extraction validation

The extraction benchmark comprised 50 studies drawn from the included set, stratified by type of exercise so that every type is represented. One was a meta-analysis and was excluded, leaving 49. Thirteen studies had been used during prompt development (development set). The other 36 form the held-out test set, our primary analysis set. Two annotators annotated each study independently with the same workbook, field definitions and PDF. Their annotations were aligned record by record. A human adjudicator then resolved every disagreement cell by cell against the paper, choosing either value or typing a correction. As for the screening benchmark, the annotators and the adjudicator could use LLM chat assistants in this way, but every value and decision was theirs.

Results were adjudicated for BP outcomes only (SBP, DBP, mean arterial pressure, pulse pressure and their variants), so the evaluation of the results step is restricted to them.

The pipeline names entities independently of the benchmark. Before any value was compared, each extracted arm, intervention, time point and outcome was therefore matched one-to-one to a benchmark entity, using names, linked entities, sample sizes and position. The adjudicator reviewed every uncertain or unmatched pair among the arms, interventions, time points and BP outcomes. Values were compared after normalization, using documented rules that equate only formatting differences (e.g., "p<0.01" and "P < .01").

For specific extracted entities, we report precision, recall and F1. For study-and armlevel fields, we report end-to-end accuracy, in which a value of a missed entity counts as wrong, and macro-F1 for closed lists with rare classes. The FITT parameters are free text.

A pair counted as correct when the texts were equal after normalization, the extraction contained the benchmark text, or their numbers and units matched. The remaining pairs were judged by an LLM (gemini-3.8-flash). This judge was not validated against a human rater, so we also report bounds that count every judged pair as wrong or as correct. To check where the verbatim protocol passages came from, we located each extracted and each benchmark passage in the Markdown text of the paper, by covering it with matching stretches of at least 20 characters after normalization, and assigned each located stretch to its paragraph and, through the Markdown headers, to its section. We report the proportion of each passage that was found, the sections it came from, and the overlap between extracted and benchmark passages by paragraph and by character (Note D in S1 Appendix). BP results were scored as data points rather than table rows: the statistic of one group at one time point (or its change), and each between-group contrast.

A data point was correct when its outcome, groups and time points matched and its value was equal. We also report the proportion of benchmark data points whose value and dispersion were both correct (meta-analysis-ready), and the proportion of extracted values printed in the paper (grounding). Each benchmark data point that was not metaanalysis-ready was assigned one error category: a dispersion error (the value is correct, but its dispersion is missing or different), a sign error (the benchmark value with the opposite sign), a misattribution (the value was extracted, but for another group, time point or outcome), a transcription error (the data point was extracted with a different value) or an omission (the value was not extracted at all).

Confidence intervals are 95% percentile intervals from a paper-level cluster bootstrap with 2,000 replicates. In a sensitivity analysis, we removed the entities that the adjudicator judged to be defensible alternative readings rather than errors. In an ablation, we scored each single extractor (gpt-6-luna, gemini-3.8-flash) of the skeleton and outcomes steps and compared it with the cross-checked output. Both are reported in Note D in S1 Appendix.

Web portal

The web portal is a Shiny for Python application [41] that searches the PAHD table. Its search pool contains the comparisons that involve an exercise arm and a blood pressure level measured at rest, over 24 hours (ambulatory), at home or centrally (3, 117 comparisons from 248 studies). Blood pressure outcomes that are not such a level, such as responses during an exercise test, changes reported as separate outcomes, variability and the proportion of patients reaching blood pressure control, were left out by matching their names against a list of words. For each study the portal shows one result, chosen in this order: the kind of blood pressure outcome (standardized SBP or DBP, then other resting or office blood pressure including mean arterial pressure, then ambulatory, home or central blood pressure); the kind of comparison (exercise against control, then the exercise arm over time, then exercise against another exercise arm); a single arm before a pooled set of arms; systolic before diastolic before mean pressure; the primary analysis before an intention-to-treat analysis; the full sample before a subgroup; and finally the significance of the p-value (p < 0.05 first, then the smaller p-value). A study's hypertension stages are those of its arms (2025 AHA/ACC stage of the mean baseline blood pressure), and its comorbidities are the ICD-10 labels in PAHD.

The journal's Journal Impact Factor is taken from Clarivate's Journal Citation Reports and is shown as not available for journals without one. The user sets the number of studies to show (one to five), the patient's stage and the matching mode. The stage is selected, or derived from a typed blood pressure reading with the 2025 AHA/ACC cut-offs[3] (Stage 2: systolic ≥140 or diastolic ≥90 mm Hg;

Stage 1: 130-139 or 80-89; elevated: systolic 120-129 and diastolic <80; otherwise normal). Matching is rule-based. The pool is first restricted to studies that include the stage; if there is none, all stages are searched and the user is informed. In comorbidity mode, studies that report at least one selected comorbidity come first, ordered by the number of selected comorbidities they report and then by significance. If there are fewer studies than requested match, the list is completed with the remaining studies whose comorbidity profile is most similar to the query, measured by the cosine similarity of sentence embeddings (all-mpnet-base-v2) [42, 43]. In exercise-type mode, studies with a selected exercise category (aerobic, resistance, flexibility, neuromotor or other) are ordered by significance. Age, sex, race, region, body mass index and medication are not used for matching.

Users can type comorbidities and exercise types instead of choosing from the lists. A typed entry is embedded and compared with the embeddings of each option label and of a short list of synonyms, abbreviations and lay terms written for it (for example "T2DM"

or "heart attack"). The best option is used if the cosine similarity is at least 0.55.

Otherwise, the closest option by fuzzy string matching (similarity of at least 0.6) is used, and an entry without a match is ignored. The synonym lists and the 0.55 cutoff were written after a first set of 58 typed entries showed mapping errors; the mapping was then tested on a second set of 67 entries that had been written, and frozen, before these changes.

The user can select some of the displayed protocols and request a summary. A fixed prompt gives the language model, for each selected protocol, the title, journal and impact factor, the exercise category, frequency, intensity, session duration and modality, the blood pressure outcome and its comparison, the p-value with its stated significance, the change in the statistics and the type of comorbidity match. The model (Qwen3.8, qwen/qwen3.8-27b [32], served by Groq [44]; temperature 0.1; at most 400 tokens) is asked for 120 to 170 words that explain in general terms why this type of exercise lowers blood pressure, what the statistics mean, the trade-offs between protocols and a brief safety note. It must not repeat the protocol specifications, invent protocol-specific facts or reinterpret p-values, must call the items "protocols" rather than recommendations, and must end with a fixed sentence that discloses that the text is AI-generated. The language model has no role in selecting or ranking studies.

Web portal evaluation

We evaluated the portal with code, without human raters or an LLM judge. The test code calls the functions that the server uses to run a query and to build and request a summary.

The 440 test queries (five results each) combined each of the four stages and an unknown stage with each of the 64 comorbidity options (including none) and each of the 14 exercise categories, plus 50 random combinations of two or three comorbidities with a random stage.

For usability, we recorded the proportion of queries that returned the requested number of studies and the proportion for which the stage had to be relaxed. Typed-entry mapping was scored on the second set of 67 entries (57 with a correct option and 10 without) as top-1 accuracy, the proportion of entries with a correct option that were mapped to it, and as the false-mapping rate, the proportion of entries without a correct option that were mapped to an option anyway. Query and summary times were compared with responsetime limits of 1 s and 10 s.

For reliability, every query was checked against eight rules: the portal returns at most the requested number of studies; it lists no study twice; it honors the requested stage, unless no study of that stage exists, in which case it says so; it returns only studies of the requested exercise type; it lists studies that report a requested comorbidity before similar ones; it orders the studies as documented; every row has a blood pressure outcome; and every displayed value equals the value in the PAHD table. We also compared the coverage of the requested comorbidities with that of an independent program that works directly on the PAHD table. The coverage of a study is the proportion of the requested comorbidities that it reports, for example 0.5 for a study that reports one of two requested comorbidities. For each query, attainment is the summed coverage of the returned studies divided by the largest summed coverage that any set of studies of the stage could reach; an attainment of 1 means that no better list exists.

We also checked that every study with an exact comorbidity match was returned whenever there was room for it. Stage assignment was tested on 14 typed blood pressure readings: values just below, at and above each cut-off, readings in different formats, and two readings that cannot be read.

The portal's blood pressure filter was compared, outcome name by outcome name, with the definition of resting SBP and DBP used by the quality control step (Cohen's κ).

Determinism was tested on 40 sampled queries: each was repeated, typed with its labels in a different case or spacing, and given with its labels in a different order.

For faithfulness, we generated 30 summaries for selections of one, two or three protocols (ten each) from the test queries, balanced between significant and non-significant results, and checked them by rule. We extracted every number from each summary and checked that it appears in the protocol data given to the model; a rounded form of a number in the data (for example, 10.6 for 10.62) counted as a match. A statement of significance attributed to a protocol had to agree with its p-value, every protocol or PMID mentioned had to be one of the selected ones, and every exercise type mentioned had to appear in a selected protocol. A summary had to end with the disclosure sentence, contain no prescriptive wording (such as "recommend," "prescribe" or "you should"), have 120 to 170 words (with a tolerance of 20%) and contain no headings or tables.

The evaluation tests whether the portal behaves as specified, not whether its output improves clinical decisions.

Ethics and intended use

This study used only data reported in published articles and involved no human participants; ethical approval and informed consent were therefore not required. PAHD is a research and clinical-decision-support resource intended to summarize published evidence for clinicians; it is not a medical device and does not provide individualized medical advice. Its output should be interpreted alongside, and not as a substitute for, clinical judgment.
