Power Analysis Check

No power analyses were detected.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

For a sensitivity power analysis, this sentence would look like:

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine, Daniel Lakens and Cristian Mesquida

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 128 papers with 246 instances of power statements, 203 were correctly detected (true positives), 22 were missed (false negatives) and 21 were incorrectly detected (false positives). Overall, among all instances flagged as power statements, 90.6% were correct (positive predictive value).

How Preregistration Check works

Retrieve information from preregistrations in a standardised way, and make them easier to check.

The Preregistration Check module identifies preregistrations on the OSF and AsPredicted based on links in the manuscript, retrieves the preregistration text, and organizes the information into a template. The module retrieves text from the OSF with its API, and from AsPredicted with ResearchBox’s API when a ResearchBox key is set (see rbox_token()), or else from the preregistration’s web page. The information in the preregistration is returned. An AsPredicted link that could not be read is named in the report, with the reason.

The module can’t extract information from non-structured preregistration templates (i.e., where the preregistration is uploaded in a single text field) and it can’t retrieve information in preregistrations that are stored as text documents on the OSF.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download information from other preregistration sites, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Marginal Significance works

List all sentences that describe an effect as ‘marginally significant’.

The marginal module searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The list of terms is a subset of those listed in a blog post by Matthew Hankins. The module returns all sentences that match terms describing ‘marginally significant’ results.

Some of the terms identified might not be problematic in some contexts, and there are ways to describe ‘marginal significance’ that are not detected by the module.

How StatCheck works

Check consistency of p-values and test statistics

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. More information on the package can be found at https://github.com/cran/statcheck. The module only returns Statcheck results for t-tests and F-tests, as these are the only tests which have been validated, see https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/.

Statcheck was developed by Michèle Nuijten and Sascha Epskamp.

Statcheck considers p = 0.000 an error, as you should report p < 0.001. Furthermore, p < 0.03 is an error if the p-value was 0.031, and one should simply report exact p-values (p = 0.031). Statcheck might miss one-sided tests, and falsely assume the p-value is incorrect. For more information, see StatCheck.

This module only checks statistical results reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process statistics reported only in tables.

This module was developed by Daniel Lakens and Lisa DeBruine

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 685 tests with 34 instances of inconsistent reporting, Statcheck correctly detected 34 of them, and incorrectly identified 26. Therefore 0% of true instances were missed, and 43% of detections were false positives. See https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tcxaj_v1/ for more details of the validation.

How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.) or reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold” or “legend”). Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

Validation: In a sample of 225 papers containing 405 instances of non-exact p-values, the module correctly detected 269 cases (true positives) and incorrectly identified 78 (false positives). It missed 136 instances of imprecisely reported p-values (false negatives) and correctly identified 4557 cases of precisely reported p-values (true negative). Additionally, 78% of positive detections were correct (positive predictive value).

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

If you want to extend the package to be able to download files from additional data repositories reach out to the Metacheck development team.

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. Every sheet of a spreadsheet that holds a table is read: a file with several such sheets has its columns under source_file = "file.xlsx [sheet]", and the sheet column names the sheet of any spreadsheet with several sheets; a sheet of notes or a codebook is treated as documentation when the file has another table. Following DDI, a column is described by orthogonal facets rather than a single type: representation (numeric/text/datetime/code — how it is stored), measurement_level (Stevens: nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio), concept (what it measures: reaction_time/age/gender/likert/id/…), role (identifier/measure/condition/timestamp), unit, and a quality state (ok/empty/constant). Concepts are detected by name+value rules (the demographic concepts via data_check_demographic()); when llm_use(TRUE) the model fills concepts and measurement levels the rules left blank. Qualtrics survey exports are recognised (data_check_is_qualtrics()): their extra header rows are stripped so columns type correctly, and the reserved response-metadata columns (StartDate, Duration, Finished, …) are tagged.

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE): columns the rules cannot resolve (ambiguous 3–20-unique integers, ambiguous character columns) fall back to continuous / text respectively. When llm_use(TRUE), ambiguous columns and unresolved file types (other) are sent to llm() for optional refinement, and every file is assigned a study group (ex1, pilot2, shared, …) so that multi-study repositories can be recognised (used by psychds_check). Without an LLM the study group is left NA (unknown).

Column extraction requires files to be readable from disk. Files fetched by repo_check from OSF/GitHub/etc. without a local copy (file_location is NA) are classified but not column-extracted; pass local_path to point at a downloaded copy of the repository.

For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this means column extraction never happens unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (dataset listing and metadata do not need one). Without a key, every Dryad file stays classified but un-downloaded, column_n is 0, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures. A one-time session message is also shown (see repo_check) the first time a Dryad dataset is encountered with no key set.

Every downloaded text file the authors wrote (code, settings, survey definitions such as Qualtrics .qsf files, documentation, outputs) is searched for keys: API keys, access tokens and private keys, which let anyone who downloads the file use a paid service at the authors’ cost. Of a tabular data file only the column names and first 3 rows of the table as read are searched: its values come from participants, but a key the authors set for a whole survey (as embedded data in a Qualtrics survey flow) is exported in every response. The search uses the rules of the secret scanner betterleaks (https://github.com/betterleaks/betterleaks, MIT licence) of high and medium confidence. A key found turns the traffic light red; the report and the keys table give its file, line and type, and only its beginning. Other files over 25 MB and binary files are not searched. A key is never tested with its provider. Every module’s result has any whole key replaced by “keyremoved” (see module_run()).

How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. Full text

Abstract

Background: The World Health Organization (WHO) has recommended that quality of care (QoC) should be timely delivered within safe conditions based on patients' needs and preferences. However, low-and middle-income countries face challenges such as understaffing and inadequate infrastructure, resulting in poor QoC. In 2012, Rwanda leveraged the pre-existing performance-based financing (PBF) to introduce continuous quality improvement (QI) towards accreditation recognition of district hospitals (DH). Despite these efforts, no DHs accredited by 2022.

Objective: To explore healthcare professionals' (HCP) perspectives on achieving the delivery of standard QoC in Rwandan DHs.

Methods: Using an interpretative epistemology, this qualitative study conducted in-depth semi-structured interviews with 14 participants purposively sampled HCPs from Masaka (urban) and Remera-Rukoma (rural) DHs. Interview data were audio-recorded, manually transcribed, and analyzed with thematic content analysis.

Results: The analysis revealed three main themes: perceptions about continuous QI towards accreditation recognition, barriers to achieving accreditation, and strategies to achieve the recognition. Participants from both hospitals expressed that the accreditation program required more efforts than the PBF approach. They also highlighted that the DHs were still at the awareness stage and had not clearly implemented the quality standards due to challenges such as low staff involvement and motivation, staff turnover and shortage, and limited budget for accreditation. Recommended strategies to achieve the recognition included staff motivation through increased incentives and salaries, cascaded accreditation rollout, integrating accreditation program into pre-service curriculum.

Conclusions: This qualitative study highlighted the challenges, such as limited budget and low staff motivation, impeding progress towards DH accreditation recognition. Cascaded accreditation rollout, enhanced staff incentives and salaries, and integration of accreditation program into pre-service curriculum were key strategies suggested for Rwanda Ministry of Health and DHs to achieve the required quality standards and recognition.

.

Introduction

Quality standards of healthcare services are critical for achieving the best patient's health outcomes through the delivery of effective, safe, and timely evidence-based interventions based on patients' needs and preferences within the same quality standards across all patients 1, 2 . However, low quality care-including, but not limited to, inaccurate diagnosis, unnecessary or inappropriate treatment, and medication errors-can lead to increased morbidity and mortality, reduced trust in health systems, and substantive wasted expenditures for health systems and patients 3, 4.

In low-and middle-income countries (LMICs), low quality of care is responsible for eight million deaths per year, causing a loss in the economic welfare of $6 trillion 3 . Healthcare providers in these countries often deliver less than 50% of the recommended evidence-based healthcare and around one third of patients experience poor communication, disrespectful care, long waiting times, and short consultation. Less than 25% of people in the LMICs are satisfied with care services provided in their healthcare systems as opposed to 50% of people in high-income countries (HICs) 3.

In Rwanda, quality of care in hospitals also faced patient safety issues. For instance, in 2019, 10-20% of treatment delivered to patients was not needed for specific diseases, due to inaccurate diagnoses 5 . In addition to existing high workload and limited staffing levels (Rwanda Public Health Bulletin, 2024), other examples were the commonly reported incidents which included medication errors (25.3%), loss to follow-up (25%), and surgical site infections (20%) 5 . In 2016, patient waiting time for seven hospitals varied from three hours 32 minutes to seven hours 33 minutes across all hospital services. By 2019, this waiting time was still high with some hospitals having over six hours 6 , reflecting poor delivery of timely care 7 . Additionally, at least 75% of health professionals were dissatisfied with their salaries and benefits (Rwanda Public Health Bulletin, 2024), reflecting that providing quality healthcare by generally dissatisfied staff is difficult.

In response to the challenge of low quality of care, Rwanda aligned healthcare services with quality standards by leveraging the pre-existing performance-based financing (PBF) system and initiated continuous quality improvement (QI) towards accreditation recognition in all district hospital 8 . This program was implemented five risk areas including leadership process and accountability, competent and capable workforce, safe environment for staff and patients, clinical care, and improvement of quality and safety 9 . Each of these domains has a set of quality standards and each standard is organised into three levels of effort. Level one focuses on the availability of written organisational policies and clinical protocols as well as staff awareness of the documents. Level two is about the implementation of what is described in the guiding documents and level three focuses on data-driven decision-making to continuously improve quality of healthcare 8, 9.

However, since 2012, none of the hospitals have yet received accreditation recognition for providing healthcare services meeting quality standards, reflecting ongoing challenges of achieving the standard quality of care. In addition, while healthcare staff working in the hospitals are on the frontline providing quality health services, there was a need for improving their willingness to implement quality standards along with their routine activities among their top priorities 5 , which can help with maximising healthcare effectiveness, timeliness, and patient safety 7.

. The most recent assessment conducted in 44 district hospitals-including those that were recently upgraded to Level Two Teaching Hospitals, referral hospitals, and Provincial Hospitals-found that 61% of the hospitals were recognized for having met the requirements for level I and II, and 36% of the hospitals for level I only, while achieving a recognition for level III remains a significant challenge 10.

As the healthcare professionals are a critical workforce in the active implementation of the quality standards, exploring their perspectives 11 on how to overcome the challenges and achieve the quality standards is essential for a successful system redesign and implementation of measures for accelerating the attainment of the standard quality of care in the district hospitals in Rwanda. This study therefore aimed to explore healthcare professionals' perspectives on how to achieve standard quality of care services in district hospitals, in order to inform enhancement of the healthcare quality in Rwanda.

Methods

(a) Research paradigm and researcher reflexivity

This study used the constructivist and interpretivist approaches 11, 12 to explore views of healthcare professionals about appropriate strategies for achieving the standard quality of healthcare.

As the researchers could be seen as part of the research participants and then considered as an 'insider' 13 , the investigator critically reflected on values and beliefs that could influence the research findings 14 . The interviewer allowed the participants to express their thoughts based on their own experience and knowledge. Additionally, the interviewer 'bracketed' their views to mitigate any background impact, used a reflexive diary throughout the research process by assuring participants that there were no correct answers 15.

(b) Design and Setting

This study used a qualitative study design with in-depth semi-structured interviews to obtain rich information from the participants. This design was chosen because it enables to gain deeper insights into people's experiences and allows the opportunity to explore people's views, beliefs, and perceptions in depth 16 . The study was conducted at two district hospitals, Masaka Hospital in Kigali city and Remera-Rukoma in rural area.

(c) Recruitment

Purposive sampling was used by selecting different types of healthcare professionals that included medical doctors, nurses/midwives, allied health professionals, and supervisors of health services in order to ensure information rich cases are selected so as to enable a deeper understanding of the study subject 17 . Participants were recruited through snowball sampling through recommendation of previous participants 18 . Participants were included in the study if they were over the age of 18, worked at the district hospital, were familiar with and had been actively involved in the healthcare accreditation activities.

(d) Data Collection

We conducted 14 qualitative individual in-depth interviews with the healthcare professionals for 30-45 minutes each, from January 04 to February 08, 2022.

. To test recruitment procedures, comprehensiveness of the interview guide questions and if they were collecting the right concepts that answer the research question 19 , we conducted a pilot testing with the first two participants who fulfilled the inclusion criteria. The interview guide was not revised because the pilot results showed that the questions were comprehensive and collected the right data. The pilot testing participants were then included in the data analysis as there were no changes made to the interview guide.

The interview was recorded with a smartphone sound recorder and supplemented with notes taken at each interview. The researcher aimed to reach data saturation which is the point in the research when no new information is discovered in the data analysis 20.

(e) Data Analysis

The interviews were transcribed verbatim and translated the transcripts into English, where necessary. A thematic content analysis was used to uncover themes, sub-themes, and emerging concepts 21 . The codes that described a specific pattern were organised into a broader theme. The reviewed patterns and themes were reorganised into codes, subthemes, and themes, and presented in a codebook (Table 1).

Ethical Considerations

All participants confirmed their participation in the study by signing their informed consent. This research was approved by the University of Liverpool (ETH-101121-MPH256), Rwanda National Health Research Committee (NHRC/2021/PROT/041), Rwanda National Ethics Committee (836/RNEC/2021), and authorized by Rwanda Ministry of Health (20/8035/DPMEHF/2021). Informed consent-in which confidentiality and anonymity were guaranteed-was sought and obtained from each participant before data collection.

Results

(a) Perception about quality improvement towards accreditation recognition Accreditation standards

Almost half of the participants from both urban and rural facilities said that before the continuous quality improvement (QI) towards accreditation of district hospitals (DH), the staff were used to the pre-existing performance-based financing (PBF) approach which was much easier than the accreditation process as staff could prepare for the PBF assessment just before it started and get good performance scores. The hospitals were first given some standards known as "patient safety goals" of quality improvement (QI) in combination with the PBF system and thereafter, started the full accreditation process.

"…. we previously had a PBF system, where the scoring and evaluation systems were not tough like in accreditation; they used to check the completeness of patients' medical files, and these files were completed in the last days or one month just before the evaluation; people could get good scores … in districts, the accreditation started as patient safety goals … thereafter, we were given all the standards …" (I1).

Support for accreditation programme

Many participants highlighted that donors and partners such as Ingobyi Activity supported some QI activities, including equipment provision and staff training in maternal and child health programme, but not directly to the accreditation programme. The Ministry of Health (MOH) also provided accreditation facilitation and biomedical equipment to all the DHs regardless of geographic location. The local district administration also supported the DHs in quality of care (QoC) in many ways including analysis of patients' suggestions and complaints and provision of a report with recommendations to the hospital administration. Using a portion of financial resources from their own revenues from healthcare services delivered to patients, the hospitals included a budget line for accreditation into their annual plan and budget. A few participants indicated that the budget allocated to accreditation activities was small but did not estimate its percentage based on the overall budget.

Accreditation status

Overall, the status of accreditation within the DHs was still at the awareness stage or level one across both rural and urban settings. The implementation was done, but it was not yet secured as mentioned by most of the participants: "… the current status, I think, we are still at the awareness stage. I can't say that the implementation is clear, we are still dealing with awareness, we are still learning the . policies and procedures, plans; we also do implementation, but it is not yet at 100% looking at the current status ..." (I1).

Estimated time of achieving accreditation recognition

The healthcare professionals had different views about when the hospitals would be recognised, with the shortest time predicted being two years from the time of conducting this study and the longest period being 20 years. Some participants said it would take a long time to be accredited, but it was not easy for them to predict when their hospital would achieve the recognition as there were many challenges to address. Other participants felt the question was very hard to answer and preferred not to answer it as the existing challenges could not allow them to predict at all.

"… (laughter), uh! That question is difficult! It's like asking me when Jesus will come. If we had capacities, enough staff, money … it cannot take time to be accredited (I10).

(b) Barriers to achieving accreditation recognition

Limited financial resources

The hospitals were struggling with implementing the accreditation activities due to a limited budget. Most of the participants said that the hospitals used a small portion of their own revenues to prepare only for the external accreditation assessment. This challenge made it difficult for them to comply with the standards within the existing budget constraints as stated by many participants especially those from rural areas.

"… the limited budget restricts us to comply with standards … The hospital does not have enough resources for covering the cost of most of the things ..." (I10).

Low compliance with policies/protocols

Participants said that there was low compliance with the quality standards due to high workload, staff shortage and turnover, inadequate infrastructure and equipment, and low salaries and financial (PBF) incentives. The staff turnover and shortage limited the ability to deliver quality care to patients and required the DHs to usually assign accreditation responsibilities to staff who already had other responsibilities. For instance, risk area two could be given to the Human Resource Manager, who was already overburdened with daily HR management activities and ended up not finding time for the accreditation duties. The QI Officer therefore had to work on all the accreditation activities alone as said by almost half of the participants from both facilities. In addition, the incentives, which was expected to increase with improved accreditation performance, did not change upon achieving better performance, demotivating the staff. Linking this incentive with the hospital's performance demotivated staff. For instance, if a hospital did not provide the required facilities for effective hand hygiene and marked low during an assessment, the staff ended up receiving a reduced PBF amount. Most of the participants from both urban and rural DHs said that it was not clear how the staff were penalised for organisational responsibilities.

"… By the time of the accreditation, people were promised a top-up upon achieving a level. But achieving level one, and even level two, they did not receive the promised incentives. This affected the performance, we lost level two on the following assessment … people reduced their commitment in the accreditation activities … there are many things to improve …" (I13).

. Most of the participants from both settings highlighted that the low financial motivation was the leading factor for staff turnover. The high workload, low salaries and PBF incentives were behind the low motivation for the existing staff, which also triggered resistance to accreditation requirements for some staff. However, other staff were involved under the leadership's influence, but they immediately relaxed upon leadership change, especially when the efforts of new leaders were weak.

Additionally, a few participants emphasised that due to the existing challenges across the urban and rural DHs, the staff used to present false documents to surveyors during the assessment, which implied that the accreditation process was more in words than in practice; reflecting the hospitals' inability to maintain achieved successes.

"… our barriers across the district hospitals are similar. Today we succeed, tomorrow we fail … a hospital is unable to stabilise the implementation. It seems as if the accreditation is in words, but not in practice … I can say that all of this leads us, during the accreditation assessment, to present things, false documents …" (I14).

Low leadership involvement

Some hospital leaders might not put effort into the accreditation activities, which decreased performance in some risk areas, discouraging other staff who work hard to maximise their performance. Some participants noted that the poor performance that was frequently seen in risk areas one and two, which are mainly for the leaders, was behind the leadership's weakness.

"… If the leadership is sleeping, we also, we sleep ... if I miss people who are always there to encourage me, I end up disregarding the accreditation … If the risk area 4 has the highest score, but the lowest scores are reported in the administrative risk areas … what does it mean? The leadership doesn't care about that …". (I9).

(c) Recommended strategies to address the barriers to achieving accreditation recognition

Recommendations for addressing barriers to accreditation of healthcare quality mainly focused on aligning salaries and financial incentives for healthcare professionals current market prices and enhancing leadership involvement in cascaded rollout of the healthcare quality programme and integrating the healthcare quality accreditation into pre-service curriculum.

Staff motivation and commitment

Most of the participants recommended the MOH harmonise PBF incentives and salaries across hospitals according to the living standards to ensure that staff are not moving from one health facility to another.

"… if the leaders can increase financial motivation for accreditation levels …they achieve this level and you provide this amount, it can help to maximise the performance. And for the staff turnover, it wouldn't be there as people are already motivated ..." (I12).

Increased leadership involvement

Many participants believed that the MOH needed to consider the use of a cascaded approach for the accreditation rollout. For instance, the process could start with a cohort of hospitals.

until they are fully accredited, support another cohort up to accreditation recognition, and continue the same process until all the hospitals are accredited.

So, in my opinion, I suggest one thing, the Ministry of Health can identify like 5 district hospitals, 2 provincial hospitals, and 1-2 referral hospitals, and support those hospitals in the accreditation process up to level three, and after having these fully accredited, then focus on supporting another group of hospitals; but lifting all the hospitals as a whole, it becomes like stressful, you end up finding that no one is progressing. I don't support the idea of lifting all the district hospitals to level three. No. Those who have not achieved level one, should focus on achieving that level and then move to the next level instead of just moving on to level three, without being stable at previous levels. Imagine a student who fails on a simple exam, can that student succeed on a more difficult exam? He/she can't. That's another challenge that discourages the hospitals.

Most participants from both rural and urban settings also emphasised that the MOH could consider integrating accreditation standards into pre-service curriculum for students in health-related fields, for them to start practicing in the hospitals when they already know what accreditation is and what is expected from them.

Another suggestion is to deliver the accreditation course to all students who will potentially come across accreditation, who will work in healthcare facilities, physicians, nurses, laboratory, all of them; particularly in district hospitals. In that way the students would come into hospitals when they are already aware of what accreditation is and what is expected from them.

Discussion

This study explored barriers to the accreditation process across the district hospitals (DH) and the strategies needed to speed up the process towards the accreditation recognition. The participants perceived the continuous quality improvement (QI) towards accreditation recognition as a difficult implementation process because of different challenges. Additionally, the fact that the DHs were still struggling with improving the awareness of the process, the participants were generally unsure of the expected timeline for achieving the accreditation recognition.

The barriers to the progress towards achieving this recognition as highlighted by most of the participants included limited funding sources for the continuous QI activities as the DHs did not have external support directly related to accreditation. Instead, these hospitals needed to find a budget from their own revenues which were even insufficient for addressing existing operational problems. Other studies similarly found that the high cost of implementing accreditation standards was one of the great barriers to effective implementation, which requires sufficient quality equipment and adequate health infrastructure 22, 23 . Another barrier was low staff motivation due to low staff incentives and salaries, leading to low compliance with the standards. Due to the low compared to the cost of living, the hospital staff had to leave the hospital to work for other institutions where they received more incentives and higher salaries. This turnover created an additional workload on the remaining staff with some staff having multiple responsibilities, and finally contributed to a decline in the performance of QI activities within the DHs. This result supports other qualitative studies, where it was found that the staff shortage and turnover were key barriers to the implementation of hospital accreditation programmes and a hospital tends to lose its best performing employees when it is unable to motivate them 23, 24.

This study found that the DH staff sometimes presented incorrect documents during the accreditation assessment. This issue was mainly due to the existing challenges including the low motivation, high workload, insufficient facilitation, and limited equipment and materials which restricted the staff to comply with standards as required. As the accreditation evaluation was also linked to the PBF incentives, the staff presented false documents to surveyors just to please their leaders and receive the incentives. Besides, the behaviour of presenting incorrect documents to surveyors might have been a custom as some participants mentioned that staff used to complete documents just before the PBF evaluation. This practice implies that during the accreditation assessments, the staff continued to present things that were not in their routine practice. A qualitative study conducted to explore challenges of hospital accreditation model in Iran also found that hospital staff used to present false documentation to surveyors, mainly due to existing challenges including lack of incentives and staff shortage 25.

This study also found that cascading the implementation of the accreditation programme across the DHs could be a good approach to speed up achieving the DH accreditation recognition. With this strategy, the MOH could focus efforts on one cohort of DHs up to the accreditation recognition and progressively continue with other cohorts until all DHs are accredited. Rwanda had initially introduced the full accreditation programme with five DHs, with the target of helping them to achieve level one after two years. However, after four of the five hospitals achieved level one, the programme was immediately extended to all the DHs 8 , which the participants thought was too heavy for the national health system.

Most participants also believed that integrating the hospital accreditation programme into pre-service curriculum would facilitate fresh graduates to start clinical practice in the DHs when they are already aware of what accreditation is and required quality expectations. This strategy aligns with the WHO recommendation of integrating QI and assurance into the preservice curriculum for health professional programmes 7.

Strengths and Limitations

To ensure the trustworthiness of these research findings, this study used different strategies including verbatim transcription to show that the data were collected from the research participants 26 . The credibility of the research findings was reflected in the fact that the investigator had a previous experience in healthcare quality. This opportunity allowed him to ask pertinent questions to get a deeper understanding of the participants' insights 27 . To ensure dependability, the details of the findings were clearly described, allowing repeatability of the work 28 . As the data collection reached saturation, the results can be transferred to other DHs 26 . However, it may not be feasible to use the findings in other health facilities including private and national referral hospitals as the scope of work is different. The confirmability of the findings reflected in the fact that participants were asked to express their thoughts freely as there were no correct or wrong answers 28, 29 . However, due to the COVID-19 pandemic restrictions, the investigator conducted telephone interviews as face-to-face data collection was not possible. The researcher could also take note of non-verbal information such as facial expressions through video call interviews, but all the participants did not prefer to use any video call platforms.

Conclusion

The district hospitals (DH) in Rwanda had been running a hospital accreditation programme since 2012. However, until 2022 there were not even one of the hospitals accredited for providing standard quality of care. The research participants from both rural and urban DHs highlighted major barriers to achieving accreditation recognition, including low staff involvement, staff turnover and shortage, and inadequate infrastructure and equipment. The recommended strategies for achieving and maintaining the recognition included cascaded accreditation rollout, financial staff motivation, leadership involvement and commitment through cascaded rollout and integration of accreditation programme into pre-service curriculum.
