Meta-scientific research has shown nonsignificant p values are commonly misinterpreted. It is incorrect to infer that there is ‘no effect’, ‘no difference’, or that groups are ‘the same’ after p > 0.05.

It is possible that there is a true non-zero effect, but that the study did not detect it. Make sure your inference acknowledges that it is possible that there is a non-zero effect. It is correct to include the effect is ‘not significantly’ different, although this just restates that p > 0.05.

Metacheck does not yet analyze automatically whether sentences which include non-significant p-values are correct, but we recommend manually checking the sentences below for possible misinterpreted non-significant p values.

P=0.63 Because the stage-specific mortality gradient did not change across the study period (likelihood ratio test for stage-by-period interaction, P=0.63), stage-specific HRs estimated from 2005-2018 were applied to 2021-2023 death counts.

Power analyses need to contain the following information to be interpretable: the type of power analysis, the statistical test, the software used, sample size, critical alpha criterion, power level, effect size, and an effect size metric. In addition, it is recommended to make sure the power analysis is reproducible (by sharing the code, or a screenshot, of the power analysis), and to provide good arguments for why the study was designed to detect an effect of this size.

For an a-priori power analysis, where the sample size is determined, reporting all information would look like:

An a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that for a Cohen’s d = 0.5, an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired power level of 80% required at least 64 participants in each group.

A sensitivity power analysis for an independent samples t-test, conducted using the pwr.t.test function from pwr (Champely, 2020), indicated that with 64 participants in each group, and an alpha level of 0.05, a desired power level of 80% was reached for an effect size of d = 0.5.

How Ethics Approval Check works

This module searches for statements of ethics approval, IRB approval, institutional review board approval, and related terms.

Patterns are designed to capture:

Human research committee names - Institutional Review Board / IRB (US standard) - Independent Ethics Committee / IEC (ICH-GCP international clinical trial term) - Ethical/Ethics Review Board (generic) - Ethics (Advisory/Review) Committee/Board/Panel/Sub-committee - Research Ethics Committee/Board/Panel (UK/Canada/Australia) - Medical Ethics Committee - Human Subjects/Research Committee / Human Research Ethics (Advisory) Panel - Committee for/on the Protection of Human Subjects - Committee on (Health) Research Ethics - Office of Research Ethics - Abbreviations: IEC, REC, REB, ERB, METC, DEC. REC/REB/ERB/DEC are short enough to collide with unrelated jargon (e.g. REC as a recognition-heuristic model abbreviation, DEC as a decision-task label, Reb as an author surname), so these four require an ethics/approval/committee/board/panel/institutional/ protocol word within ~30 characters in the same sentence. IRB and IEC are kept unconstrained as they are not observed to collide in practice. - Dutch: Medisch-ethische toetsingscommissie (METC) - French: Comité d'éthique / Comité d'Ethique

Animal research committee names - Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee / IACUC (US standard) - Any sentence containing animal(s) and committee (or board/panel) within ~5 words of each other — catches Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee, Animal Ethics Committee, Animal Welfare Board, Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body, etc. - UK: Animal Welfare Ethical Review Body / AWERB - Dutch: Dierexperimentencommissie / DEC (see DEC constraint above); Instantie voor Dierenwelzijn / IvD - German: Tierversuchskommission / TvK (advisory committee under §15 Tierschutzgesetz) - French: Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale / CEEA; Comité d'éthique animale - Spanish: Comité de ética en experimentación animal / CEEA

Approval phrasing - Ethics/Ethical (approval|clearance) [was] (granted|obtained|received|given|secured) - Ethically approved - Approved by the (ethics|irb|institutional|independent|local|review|research) ... - This/The study/protocol/experiment/procedures/methods/research [was] (approved|reviewed) by - Study was ethically approved - (Followed|met|follow) the ethical (guidelines|standards|requirements) - In accordance with ... ethical (guidelines|standards) - Ethics protocol - Approved under (protocol|reference|number)

Waiver / exemption - Ethics/Ethical (waiver|exemption) - IRB (waiver|exemption|exempt) - Exempted from (ethics|irb|institutional review) - Waived by the (ethics|irb|institutional|review) (committee|board) - Deemed exempt

Declaration of Helsinki - Declaration of Helsinki / Helsinki Declaration

Patterns are NOT designed to capture: - General ethical considerations or ethical dilemmas in study content - Mentions of “ethics” in theoretical or philosophical contexts - Author approval of a manuscript (e.g. “all authors approved the final version”) - Ethical behaviour as a measured variable (e.g. “ethical decision-making”) - URLs containing “ethics” in path components

Live data collection is detected by the internal helper .detect_live_data(), which searches for sentences indicating that data were collected directly from human or animal participants. Papers without any live-data signal are not flagged as missing ethics approval. The helper uses conservative patterns to avoid flagging secondary data analyses, computational experiments, or economics papers that analyse existing survey data. See .detect_live_data() for the full list of patterns and intentional exclusions.

A paper that states that no human or animal subjects took part (“no human subjects were involved”, “did not involve human participants”, or people taking part “not as subjects”) is not expected to have an ethics statement, whatever participant phrase it also has (see .detect_no_participants()). A negation near a participant phrase alone (“No participants were excluded”) does not count: it reports a study with participants.

How Power Analysis Check works

This module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. If specified by the user, it also uses a large language model (LLM) to extract information reported in power analyses, including the statistical test, sample size, alpha level, desired level of power, and magnitude and type of effect size.

The Power Analysis Check module uses regular expressions to identify sentences that contain a statistical power analysis. Without the use of an LLM, the module uses regular expressions to classify the power analysis as a-priori, sensitivity or post-hoc. With the use of an LLM, it checks if the power analysis is reported with all required information.

The regular expressions can miss power analyses, or fail to classify them correctly. The type of power analysis is often difficult to classify, which can easily be solved by explicitly specifying the type of power analysis as ‘a-priori’, ‘sensitivity’, or ‘post-hoc’. Note that ‘post-hoc’ or ‘observed’ power is rarely useful. The LLM can fail to identify information in the paper, and will not have access to information in paragraphs in the paper other than those that contain the word ‘power’. This package was validated by the Metacheck team on articles in Psychological Science.

How StatCheck works

The Statcheck module runs Statcheck. Statcheck searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern, and identifies APA reported statistical tests. How Exact P-Values works

List any p-values reported with insufficient precision (e.g., p < .05 or p = n.s.), reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000), or impossible (below 0 or above 1, e.g., p = -.22).

This module uses regular expressions to identify p-values. It will flag any values reported as p > ? or p < numbers greater than .001. It will also flag p-values reported as exactly zero (e.g., p = .000, p = 0.00), which are mathematically impossible — p-values are never exactly zero and should instead be reported as p < .001.

A p-value that is not the result of a test is not flagged: a table note (“* p < .05, ** p < .01”), a figure legend (“Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < .05)”, “Values in bold are significant at p < .05”) or a stated threshold (“p < .05 was considered significant”, “thresholded at p < .001”). GROBID often puts such notes into the running text, so each p-value is judged by the words in its own clause (the text up to a “;” or a full stop), and never left out when a test statistic belongs to it (“F(1, 7) = 8.03, # p < .05”). The column not_a_test of the table says why a p-value was left out (“marker”, “threshold”, “legend” or “named”).

A p-value with the name of a correction or a test after the p (“pFWE < .05”, “padj < 0.05”, “p Wilcoxon = .02”) is flagged only when a test statistic comes before it in its clause (“t(37) = 7.17, p FWE < .05”). Without one it is mostly the rule that decided what was significant (“genes with padj < 0.05 were selected”); such a p-value is “named” in not_a_test. Notes in other forms, such as a marker after the p-value (“p < .05 (*)“), are still flagged.

A p-value below 0 or above 1 is impossible; it is listed apart and not also as imprecise. Often the statistic before it, or its minus sign, was copied into it (“t(134) = -2.32, p = -.13”, “Z = 3.091, P = 3.091”). A value above 1 is listed only after a test statistic in its clause: without one, a “P” above 1 was nearly always another quantity (a proportion, pH, a group’s mean, a model parameter). A number in scientific notation whose power of ten was lost when the PDF was read (“p = 2.4 x 10 -9” read as 2.4) is not listed. A “p” that is the subscript of another symbol (“r p = .64”, “²p = .02” for partial eta squared, “Δp = .33”), or a quantity with a unit, a logarithm or a ratio (“P = 2.9 µm”, “-log10 P = 7.5”, “Ca/P = 1.67”), is not read as a p-value at all (see extract_p_values()). Each p-value has its own row, also when the same one is written twice in a sentence.

This module sometimes flags false positives when sentences discuss how p-value thresholds were set. It can also miss an imprecise p-value that is reported against a threshold (“decreased at the p < .05 significance threshold”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables (as opposed to a table’s footnote text, which the module does see and tries to exclude — see above).

This module was developed by Lisa DeBruine and Daniel Lakens

Read more in the metacheck manual.

How Non-Significant P Value Check works

This module checks for imprecisely reported p values. If p > .05 is detected, it warns for misinterpretations.

The nonsignificant p-value check searches for regular expressions that match a predefined pattern. The module identifies all p-values in a manuscript and selects those that are not reported to be smaller than or equal to 0.05. It returns all sentences containing non-significant p-values.

In the future, the Metacheck team aims to incorporate a machine learning classifier to only return sentences likely to contain misinterpretations. If you want to help to improve the module, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

A p-value in a table note (“† p < .10, * p < .05”) or a figure legend (“dashed lines indicate non-significant paths (p > .05)”) is left out, as in stat_p_exact; the column not_a_test says why. A p-value next to words that state a threshold is kept, because such sentences often report a result against that level (“not significant at the conventional threshold (p = .054)”).

This module only checks p-values reported in the running text of the manuscript. It cannot (yet) process p-values reported only in tables.

How Codebook Check works

This module checks whether the data columns in a repository are documented in a codebook or README. It locates codebook/readme files, extracts the variable definitions they contain, matches those definitions against the data columns extracted by data_check, and reports documentation coverage: how many data columns are documented, and which documented variables are never used.

The Codebook Check module consumes the columns and file classification produced by data_check (and, transitively, repo_check). Files classified as codebook or readme are parsed with parse_codebook(), which reads structured tables (CSV/TSV/Excel with a variable-name column and a label column), embedded labels in haven files (SPSS/Stata/SAS), and plain text from rich-text formats (docx/pdf/rtf/odt). Embedded haven labels from data files are also harvested directly.

Each data column is then matched against the parsed variable definitions with match_column_labels(), using normalised-name matching with experiment-group scoping, haven-label priority, and rule-based label-equivalence merging.

Beyond the variable label, the module harvests the DDI-style per-variable properties a source supplies: the value labels / code list (the 1=“Strongly disagree”…5=“Strongly agree” mapping — from SPSS/Stata value labels and from a codebook “values”/“coding” column), the missing-value scheme (which codes denote missingness, from haven declared missings and labels that read as “refused”/“n/a”), and question text and columns when present. These are carried onto the matched data columns and exported into the Psych-DS variableMeasured (as a schema.org code list plus namespaced metacheck: fields).

The module defaults to rules-only when llm_use(FALSE). When llm_use(TRUE), three optional LLM tiers run: parsing unstructured codebook text that the rules could not handle, fuzzy-matching still-unlabelled columns to still-unmatched codebook variables, and merging conflicting label definitions into a single canonical label.

How Repository Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories.

The Repository Check module lists files on the OSF, GitHub, ResearchBox, DSpace (PsychArchives and other legacy- and DSpace 7-based installations), Zenodo, Dataverse, Figshare, Dryad, ReShare, 4TU.ResearchData, Mendeley Data, DataONE (Arctic Data Center, KNB, and other member nodes), and LDbase based on links in the manuscript.

Listing a repository’s files never requires an API key, but some hosts answer faster or more completely with one: OSF allows 100 requests/hour unauthenticated versus 10,000/day with a token (see [osf_pat()]), and Dryad enforces a strict per-IP daily quota on repeated listing (see cache below). Dryad is a special case beyond rate limits: listing a Dryad dataset’s files works without a key, but actually downloading file CONTENTS from Dryad fails with HTTP 401 unless a key is set, even for a fully public dataset (see [dryad_pat()] or [dryad_auth()]). Since this module only lists files and never downloads their contents itself, that limitation applies to data_check and code_check (which do download content), not here – see their own documentation.

When a linked OSF page is a registration, its registered_from project (the one it was registered from, which the manuscript itself may never link directly) is also checked: if that project is public its files are listed under its own URL, and if it is closed or otherwise inaccessible the report flags it explicitly, since some registrations only describe their content rather than mirroring it.

Whose deposit is each link? The ownership table gives, for every repository linked in the text and (with references = TRUE) every deposit cited in the reference list, one of six labels: own_this (made by the authors for this paper), own_earlier_data or own_earlier_code (the authors’ own, from earlier work), others_data or others_code (made by other people, reused here), or not_data (a publication such as a preprint, not a deposit), with ownership_group (“own”, “others”, “not_data”) and the reason. It is decided without a language model, from the sentence or reference that cites the link, the paper’s authors (names, ORCID iDs, affiliations), and what the deposit’s own record and files say (who made it, what it names, when, whether it is a software package; for an OSF project cited only in the reference list, its record alone). On a round of labelled links the rules had not been written from, the three groups were right for 98% of links in the main text (93 of 95) and 90% in reference lists (74 of 82); the labels were made by Claude agents from each link’s evidence, and disputed ones checked against the deposits. Telling this paper’s own deposit from the authors’ earlier one is less certain.

Only the deposits that are this paper’s own are checked. A deposit linked in the text that holds other people’s work (others_data, others_code) or the authors’ earlier work (own_earlier_data, own_earlier_code) is listed in the report, with the reason, but its files are left out of table and kept in table_not_checked: they count toward nothing here (files that do not belong in a repository, naming, READMEs, studies, the traffic light), and code_check, data_check and reproducibility_check, which read table, do not check them either. reproducibility_check still puts the files of theirs that the paper’s code names in the folder the code runs in, as a re-analysis needs the data it re-analyses. When every linked deposit is set aside, the traffic light is “na”. A deposit that is the paper’s own is counted as such when its README, title or description names the paper (its title or DOI).

How Code Check works

This module retrieves information from repositories checked by repo_check about code files (R, Python, SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, MATLAB).

The Code Check module checks R (also R Markdown and Quarto), Python (also Jupyter notebooks), SAS, SPSS, Stata, Mplus, and MATLAB files, using regular expressions to check the code; JASP files are listed but not read. The language of a Quarto document or a notebook is read from the file itself (its engine or kernel), not from its extension. The regular expression search will detect the number of comments, the lines at which libraries/imports are loaded, attempts to detect absolute paths to files, setwd() and install.packages() calls, and lists files that are loaded, and checks if these files are in the repository; R code is also parsed. The module will return suggestions to improve the code if there are no comments, if libraries/imports are loaded in lines further than 4 lines apart, if files that are loaded are not in the repository, if absolute file paths, setwd() or install.packages() are found, and if R code cannot be parsed.

The regular expressions can miss information in code files, or falsely detect parts of the code as a fixed file path. Libraries/imports might be loaded in one block, even if there are more than 3 intermittent lines. The module was tried out internally on papers published in Psychological Science, but has not been formally validated. There might be valid reasons why some loaded files can’t be shared, but the module can’t evaluate these reasons, and always gives a warning.

The module also checks whether the repository pins the R/package versions the analysis actually depended on: an renv.lock file (parsed for the R version and every locked package + its version/source), a sessionInfo()/sessioninfo::session_info() text dump (matched by filename — sessionInfo.txt/session_info.txt and similar, or embedded in a README), or a groundhog::groundhog.library()/checkpoint::checkpoint() date-pin call in the code (a bare library(groundhog)/library(checkpoint) does not count — the pinning call itself must be present). When none of these is found, the report flags it as a reproducibility gap: package versions may drift between when the analysis was run and any later reproduction attempt.

If you want to extend the package to perform additional checks on code files, or make the checks work on other types of code files, reach out to the Metacheck development team.

Checking code requires downloading its file contents. For a paper whose repository is on Dryad, this never succeeds unless a Dryad API key is set: Dryad requires one to download file bytes, even from a fully public dataset (listing the repository’s files does not need one). Without a key, every Dryad-hosted code file is listed but unchecked, and a warning naming Dryad and ?dryad_pat/?dryad_auth is included among the download failures recorded in the manifest.

How Data Check works

This module classifies the files in a repository into semantic types (data, codebook, code, software, output, supplemental, readme, asset, other) and, for tabular data files, extracts each column’s type and summary statistics.

The Data Check module consumes the file list produced by repo_check (or any files added via local_path). Each file is classified with rule-based heuristics layered over metacheck’s file_category() and file_types: file names matching README / codebook patterns win first, then an extension crosswalk, then format-locked extension overrides. For every file classified as tabular data that is available locally, the module reads the file head, describes each column with data_col_facets() and computes summary statistics for numeric columns. How Reproducibility Check works

This module assesses whether a paper’s R and Python code can be run on its data. It works in two phases. The static phase (always run) collects the package dependencies the code declares, rewrites the data-file paths that do not work in the repository as the authors organised it, works out the order the scripts must run in, and diagnoses why any referenced input file is unavailable — reporting what a reproduction attempt would involve and where it is likely to break. The execution phase (opt-in, execute = TRUE) then actually runs the code: it copies the repository into a throwaway directory, optionally installs the declared dependencies into a throwaway library, runs each script in order in an isolated subprocess with a per-script timeout, and captures the outcome and full output of each run.

By default (execute = FALSE) the module does not run any code — it performs only static analysis: reading dependencies with the same extractors as code_check, finding the files the code references in the repository, and building a run-order dependency graph. It reports what would be installed and run rather than doing it.

When execute = TRUE, the module additionally runs the downloaded code on your machine — a deliberate, opt-in action gated behind that argument (callr is required for R code). Each script runs in an isolated subprocess so a crash cannot take down the R session; note this isolates crashes, not the filesystem or network. Installs (when install_missing = TRUE) and the throwaway run library never touch your main R library. Each script’s outcome is one of ran_ok, errored, timed_out, dependency_unavailable, not_parsed, run_by_source (an R script another script runs with source(), which is run only inside that script, since on its own it lacks the packages and objects the sourcing script sets up first), run_by_import (a Python file other files import, a module rather than a script, unless it has an if __name__ == "__main__": block), jags_model (a JAGS or BUGS model saved with an R extension, which another script reads) or not_r_content (a file that is not R at all, such as a JSON or HTML dump); these last two are not run and are not failures. python2 marks Python 2 code that was not run. With sandbox = "docker" such code runs with Python 2.7 (option metacheck.docker_image_py2; FALSE turns this off), which modifications records; it is never converted, since that can change results. defines_functions is a file that only defines functions, which is used, not run (table$used_by names the scripts that use it), and not_analysis is a file of software rather than of the analysis: a package’s code, tests, documentation or setup.py, a copy of a tool, a web application, a script a program wrote (table$not_analysis_why says which); neither is run or counted as a script. docker_failed means the container never started, which says nothing about the script, so it keeps the light from being green but does not make it red. needs_a_person is a script that stopped because it asks a person to choose a file or answer a question, or that starts an app, and needs_internet one that stopped because the network is off in a Docker run, or, with internet = TRUE, could not reach what it asked for; download_too_large is a script stopped for downloading more than the limits allow (see internet), and out_of_memory one that, or a program it started (such as a compiler), ran out of the memory each script may use. All four are treated the same way. not_downloaded is a script that stopped on a file the repository lists but that was not downloaded, such as one over max_file_size: it could not be tried, so it is not counted as a script that did not reproduce (summary_table$repro_not_downloaded_n counts them apart). not_read is a file metacheck could not read, so it was not run; the light is then not green, as the file may read and run on another computer, and table$error_message says why, naming a package that is installed but cannot be loaded on the computer that ran the check. empty is a file with nothing in it, which is not run or counted as a script. For a script that did not run through, table$error_message is its error, short (at most 300 characters): the error line of its output, which names a package that is not there, with the address it asked for when it needed the internet, or the time limit it reached; table$reason sorts it into a kind. A timeout means “still running at the cutoff”, not a failure, so raise timeout for legitimately long-running (e.g. Bayesian) scripts. dependency_unavailable means the script’s own package could not be installed from live CRAN, the CRAN Archive, or a named GitHub/URL source — an infrastructure limitation, not a defect in the paper’s code, so unlike errored/timed_out it does not force the traffic light red. The full stdout/stderr of every run is kept in the result’s run_results (the report shows it in per-script dropdowns, capped for readability). The traffic light reflects the STATIC readiness signals by default, but an execution error (errored/timed_out) does recolour it to red — a script that crashes on its own analysis is a genuine reproduction failure. A script that fails with could not find function is run once more with the library() calls the project’s other scripts make added at its top (or the one package that exports the function, or magrittr for %>%), and a script that then runs is reported as reproducible only after that repair.

Dependencies are reported name-only, with the source (CRAN, GitHub, or a base package already shipped with R) — static analysis of the code cannot know which version was used, and running against current package versions is itself informative: a break argues the authors should have pinned versions. When the repository does record versions, a run with recorded_versions = TRUE installs the packages the scripts load at those versions, but it does not change the R version, which remains a limitation of the check.

Run order is derived from read-after-write data dependencies (a script writing a file another reads must run first), source() edges, and numeric filename prefixes as a weak tie-breaker. Ambiguous orderings, dependency cycles, and inputs a script reads but that no earlier script produces (and that are not in the repository) are surfaced as warnings, since these are the situations most likely to make an otherwise-correct reproduction fail.

Python code (.py files, and notebooks and Quarto documents whose code is Python, from which the code is extracted first) goes through the same two phases, reported in its own “Python code” section. Each file is checked for syntax errors with Python’s own parser when a Python interpreter (python, or else python3) is available; dependencies are read from the import statements, with versions from a requirements.txt or pyproject.toml when there is one, and installed with pip; each script runs as its own python process (processx is required), so unlike R scripts, a name one script defines is not available to another. When no Python interpreter is available, the Python files are reported as not run and the R results are still returned. Statistics the Python scripts print are compared with the paper together with the R output and any self-reproducible output (JASP/jamovi, SPSS-Viewer, notebook outputs).

Abstract

Background: CKM syndrome was defined in 2023 as a five-stage continuum, with stage 3 assigned on predicted rather than established cardiovascular disease. Whether that stage identifies a population with mortality resembling clinical disease is unknown.

We pooled nine National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey cycles (2005-2023) and assigned CKM stages 0-4 from measured risk factors, kidney function, urine albumin, selfreported cardiovascular disease, and 10-year PREVENT risk. Years lived with disability used Global Burden of Disease weights; years of life lost combined national deaths, linked mortality files, and period life tables.

Results: In 2021-2023, 13.8%, 21.3%, 49.1%, 6.5%, and 9.3% of adults were in stages 0 to 4. Crude mortality was 6,709 per 100,000 person-years in stage 3 and 6,375 in stage 4. Adjusted hazard ratios increased from 1.39 in stage 2 to 3.22 in stage 4; stage 1 showed no significant excess (HR, 0.99). CKM accounted for 20.0 million DALYs annually, with 76% of attributable years of life lost in stages 3-4. Lipid-lowering therapy was absent in 53.7% of stage 3 and 33.6% of stage 4 adults, a gap that did not narrow over 16 years.

Conclusions: Risk-based stage 3 had a crude mortality rate similar to that of established cardiovascular disease, although its adjusted hazard ratio remained lower. The population nonetheless shifted toward stage 1, and stage 3 continued to receive less intensive lipid-lowering treatment than stage 4.

 Mortality-focused case finding may yield the greatest short-term benefit in stages 3-4.

Introduction

In 2023, the American Heart Association proposed cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome as a single construct linking adiposity, dysglycemia, hypertension, dyslipidemia, chronic kidney disease, and cardiovascular disease along a five-stage continuum from stage 0 (no risk factors) to stage 4 (clinical cardiovascular disease). 1 The framework has been rapidly adopted in epidemiologic research, and national estimates of stage distribution are now available. 2Stage 3 is the least tested part of the framework. Unlike the other stages, it is assigned on predicted ten-year risk rather than on measured disease, and it combines adults with a PREVENT score of 20% or more and adults with a high-risk kidney profile. Whether this group has mortality similar to that of adults with established cardiovascular disease has not been tested against national death data. If it does, the stage identifies a group that warrants intensive prevention; if it does not, the label has limited prognostic value.

National estimates describe how many people occupy each stage, but not how much health they lose. A syndrome whose burden sits in advanced stages argues for case finding and secondary prevention; one whose burden sits in early stages argues for primordial prevention. Prevalence alone cannot adjudicate between the two, because a common early stage and a rare late stage can carry identical prevalence-weighted risk.

Disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) resolve this by combining years lived with disability (YLD) and years of life lost (YLL), and by permitting decomposition across stages. DALYs are the standard metric of the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study, 3 but to our knowledge they have never been estimated by CKM stage. Existing CKM work has examined single biomarkers across stages, 4, 5 albuminuria prevalence, 6 and mortality, 7 but not health loss.

We therefore estimated years lived with disability, years of life lost, and DALYs for each CKM stage among US adults from 2005 to 2023, and tested the mortality gradient that the framework implies. We had three questions. First, does a PREVENT-defined stage 3 carry mortality comparable to stage 4? Second, how is health loss distributed across stages, and does it sit where the deaths are? Third, how has the stage distribution moved over two decades, and does that movement point upstream or downstream of where the burden sits?

Methods

Data sources and study population

We used nine National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) cycles: 2005-2006 through 2017-2018, the 2017-March 2020 pre-pandemic cycle, and 2021-2023. NHANES is a stratified, multistage probability sample of the noninstitutionalized US population.8 We restricted the prevalence and YLD analyses to adults aged 20 years or older who attended the mobile examination center and had a positive examination weight (Figure S1); variable definitions and source files for each cycle are listed in Table S2. The 2017-2018 cycle was retained for internal consistency checks but excluded from trend analyses because it overlaps the 2017-March 2020 cycle.

Because triglycerides are measured only in the fasting subsample, the prevalence and YLD analyses were restricted to fasting participants. The corresponding fasting subsample weight (WTSAF2YR; WTSAFPRP for 2017-March 2020) was applied to these weighted analyses. The analytic sample comprised 3,057 adults in 2021-2023 and 23,951 across all cycles. Mortality analyses used a separate pooled linked-mortality cohort of 27,012 participants, as described below.

CKM staging

Stages were assigned hierarchically, with the highest applicable stage taking precedence (Table S1). Stage 4 required self-reported congestive heart failure, coronary heart disease, angina, myocardial infarction, or stroke. Stage 3 required the absence of clinical cardiovascular disease plus either a 10-year PREVENT total cardiovascular risk of 20% or greater or high-risk chronic kidney disease (estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] <45 mL/min/1.73 m² or urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio [UACR] ≥300 mg/g). Stage 2 required any of the following: hypertension, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, hypertriglyceridemia, metabolic syndrome, or low-density lipoprotein cholesterol ≥160 mg/dL. Stage 1 required overweight, obesity, abdominal obesity, or prediabetes. Stage 0 required none of the above.

Hypertension was defined by measured systolic blood pressure >=130 mm Hg or diastolic >=80 mm Hg, or current antihypertensive medication; self-report of a prior diagnosis was deliberately excluded so that treated and controlled individuals were classified by current status. Diabetes was defined by self-report, glucose-lowering medication, or hemoglobin A1c >=6.5%. Prediabetes was defined as hemoglobin A1c 5.7%-6.4% without diabetes. Kidney function was estimated with the 2021 CKD-EPI creatinine equation. 9 Stage 0 was assigned only when all five core inputs (body mass index, blood pressure, hemoglobin A1c, eGFR, and UACR) were observed, so that missing data could not create spurious health.

Ten-year cardiovascular risk was computed with the PREVENT base equations, 10 implemented from the published coefficients and validated against the reference implementation. PREVENT was calculable for 75% of adults; participants younger than 30 years or with incomplete inputs could not meet the stage 3 risk criterion.

Disability weights and YLD

YLD were calculated as the product of stage-specific prevalence, disability weight, and population size. Disability weights were assigned at the level of GBD health states rather than conditions. Diabetes was assigned a weight of 0.049. For ischemic heart disease and chronic ischemic stroke, we applied GBD severity distributions11 to obtain severity-weighted averages of 0.0731 and 0.0845, respectively. Heart failure used the GBD 2023 severity distribution (0.0923).12 Chronic kidney disease weights were stratified by anemia severity using hemoglobin and the GBD CKD severity table: for stage 3, weights were 0 without anemia and 0.004, 0.052, and 0.149 with mild, moderate, and severe anemia, respectively; for stage 4, weights ranged from 0.104 to 0.237; and for stage 5 (dialysis), the weight was 0.569 (Table S5).13 Coexisting conditions were combined multiplicatively, DW = 1 -product(1 -DWi), as in the GBD approach to comorbid sequelae. Stages 0 and 1 were assigned zero disability because adiposity, prediabetes, and hypertension are treated by GBD as risk factors without an associated sequela.

Mortality, YLL, and attributable burden

Vital status through 31 December 2019 was obtained from the public-use NHANES linked mortality files (LMF) for cycles 2005-2006 through 2017-2018, linked by participant sequence number. 14 We pooled these cycles, together with the 1999-2000 and 2003-2004 cycles (2001-2002 was excluded because serum creatinine was not available in the public biochemistry file), to estimate the stage distribution of deaths within ten-year age bands and sex. Because the 1999-2000 and 2003-2004 cycles carry no survey weights, these shares were computed as unweighted counts of deaths within each age band and sex before being applied to national death counts. National death counts for 2021-2023 by year, sex, and ten-year age band were obtained from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention WONDER database. Death counts for the three years were summed to give cumulative YLL over 2021-2023, and annual YLL was obtained by dividing that cumulative total by three; annual attributable YLL was then estimated by applying the PAF to the annualized total. Stage-specific deaths were obtained by applying the LMFderived stage distribution of deaths to national counts; the 15-24 year band was halved to approximate ages 20-24. YLL were computed as deaths multiplied by remaining life expectancy from the 2024 US period life tables. 15 Attributable burden was estimated with the population attributable fraction. Stage-specific hazard ratios (HR) were obtained from a Cox proportional hazards model on the pooled LMF cohort (27,012 participants; 4,706 deaths) with stage, age, sex, and race as covariates. The PAF was calculated as sum(pi(HRi-1)) / (1 + sum(pi(HRi-1))) using 2021-2023 stage prevalences, and attributable YLL were obtained by applying the PAF to total YLL, with the stage decomposition following each stage's excess share. Cardiovascular deaths were identified from the underlying cause of death recode in the LMF (diseases of the heart or cerebrovascular disease), and causespecific hazard ratios were estimated with deaths from other causes censored. The model used the same 27,012 participants as the all-cause model, among whom 1,530 cardiovascular deaths occurred. Rates were directly standardized to the 2020 US population.

Attribution to upstream exposures

Smoking status and physical inactivity lie outside the CKM definition and were therefore treated as upstream exposures rather than components of the syndrome. Current smoking was defined by self-report. Physical inactivity was defined as reporting no leisure-time moderate or vigorous activity; the item set changed in 2007, and the two instruments were harmonized into a single binary indicator. Both exposures were available in every cycle from 2005-2006 onwards.

Hazard ratios were estimated in the same linked mortality cohort, with additional adjustment for the ratio of family income to poverty, educational attainment, and body mass index, so that the estimates would not merely reproduce socioeconomic position or adiposity. The population attributable fraction for the two exposures jointly was computed from their observed joint distribution in 2017-March 2020 and the adjusted hazard ratios for the four exposure combinations, using PAF = (sum p_s RR_s -1) / sum p_s RR_s, which accounts for the overlap between exposures. Attributable DALYs were obtained by applying this fraction to total CKM DALYs.

Results

Stage distribution

In 2021-2023, 13.8% (95% CI, 11.7-15.9) of US adults were stage 0, 21.3% (19.3-23.4) stage 1, 49.1% (46.6-51.6) stage 2, 6.5% (5.3-7.7) stage 3, and 9.3% (7.8-10.7) stage 4 (Table 1). Stage 2 was the largest category and was driven predominantly by hypertension (69.1%) and diabetes (16.7%). Stage 3 and stage 4 differed in composition: participants in stage 3 were older (mean 72.7 versus 66.0 years) and had worse kidney function (mean eGFR 70.0 versus 78.5 mL/min/1.73 m2) and more chronic kidney disease (55.5% versus 37.9%).

Disability and mortality burden

CKM accounted for 4,093,294 YLD and 15.9 million attributable YLL per year, or 20.0 million DALYs (8,537 per 100,000 adults crude; 7,881 age-standardized) (Table 2, Figure 2). YLL accounted for 80% of the total, indicating that CKM in the United States is principally a mortality burden, not a disability burden. Years of life lost per death fell from 29.8 in stage 0 to 10.1 in stage 3 and 13.1 in stage 4, the lowest value occurring in stage 3, whose members were the oldest.

Burden was concentrated in the advanced stages (Figure 2, Central Illustration). Stage 4 generated 65% of YLD and 49.0% of attributable YLL; stage 3 generated 11.4% and 27.1%; stage 2 generated 23.2% and 23.9%. Stages 3 and 4 together, 15.8% of adults, accounted for 76% of attributable YLL. The population attributable fraction for CKM was 32.2% (95% CI, 18.5%-44.2%), indicating that approximately one third of deaths were statistically attributable to the observed distribution of CKM stages under the assumptions of the PAF model.

The stage allocation of years of life lost was sensitive to age but not to sex. Using age-specific shares alone gave 4.1%, 9.4%, 34.4%, 21.6%, and 30.6% of years of life lost across stages 0 to 4, against 4.0%, 9.4%, 34.6%, 21.6%, and 30.4% when both age and sex were used. Ignoring age entirely shifted 5.5 percentage points of the total onto stage 4, confirming that age is the dominant driver of the stage distribution of death (Table S6).

YLD rates differed by race and ethnicity (Table S7): 2,433 per 100,000 among non-Hispanic Black adults, 35% above non-Hispanic White adults (1,796), with intermediate rates in Hispanic adults (1,443) and the lowest rates among Asian and other adults (1,307). Stage 2 accounted for 55.1% of non-Hispanic Black adults, compared with 47.2% of White adults.

Most adults in stage 3 entered through the risk criterion rather than the kidney criterion: 80.1% met the PREVENT threshold without high-risk kidney disease, 10.7% met both, and 9.2% entered through high-risk kidney disease alone. The kidney-only group was much younger (mean age 52.7 versus 75.5 years), so this small subset identifies adults more than two decades earlier in the life course than the risk-based majority.

Age-standardized to the 2020 US population, the overall DALY rate was 7,881 per 100,000 (crude 8,537), and the stage distribution was 13.9%, 21.3%, 48.3%, 5.5%, and 8.4% for stages 0 to 4, against crude values of 13.8%, 21.3%, 49.1%, 6.5%, and 9.3%. Standardization therefore attenuated the apparent burden of the advanced stages.

Burden was higher in men than in women (10,291 versus 6,851 DALYs per 100,000), and men accounted for 59.1% of total DALYs despite a slightly smaller population. DALYs per 100,000 rose from 938 at 20-24 years to 27,013 at 75-84 years (Table 5).

Adjusted mortality gradient

Unadjusted mortality rose steeply across stages (287, 390, 1,123, 6,709, and 6,375 per 100,000 person-years for stages 0-4), but most of that gradient was attributable to age. After adjustment, HRs relative to stage 0 were 0.99 (95% CI, 0.78-1.25) for stage 1, 1.39 (1.13-1.71) for stage 2, 2.21 (1.78-2.75) for stage 3, and 3.22 (2.60-3.99) for stage 4 (Table 3, Figure 3). Stages 2 through 4 were each significantly elevated; stage 1 was not.

Although crude mortality rates were similar in stages 3 and 4 (6,709 versus 6,375 per 100,000 person-years), the adjusted hazard ratio was lower in stage 3 than in stage 4 (2.21 versus 3.22). This finding suggests that the PREVENT-based criterion identifies a population with a high absolute short-term mortality burden, while its age-, sex-, and race-adjusted relative hazard remains lower than that of adults with established cardiovascular disease.

Cause-specific cardiovascular mortality

The pattern held for cardiovascular death specifically. Crude cardiovascular mortality was 35 per 100,000 person-years in stage 0, 72 in stage 1, 294 in stage 2, 2,230 in stage 3, and 2,632 in stage 4, so absolute cardiovascular mortality in stage 3 approached that in stage 4 while both were more than sixty-fold higher than in stage 0. After adjustment for age, sex, and race, cause-specific hazard ratios were 1.41 (95% CI, 0.74-2.68) for stage 1, 2.63 (1.48-4.69) for stage 2, 4.73 (2.62-8.54) for stage 3, and 9.00 (5.03-16.13) for stage 4 (Table S8). This pattern reflects the older age structure of stage 3: in absolute terms a stage 3 adult faced a cardiovascular death rate about 85% that of a stage 4 adult, while the adjusted relative hazard remained lower.

Treatment intensity despite similar crude mortality

Adults in stage 3 had mortality close to that of stage 4 but received less intensive preventive treatment (Table 6). Blood pressure was at or above 130/80 mm Hg in 65.6% of adults in stage 3 and 50.1% in stage 4; low-density lipoprotein cholesterol was at or above 70 mg/dL in 78.4% and 64.5%, respectively. Lipid-lowering therapy was absent in 53.7% of adults in stage 3 compared with 33.6% in stage 4, a difference of 20.1 percentage points, whereas antihypertensive use was similar in the two stages (65.4% versus 62.5%). The gap was therefore concentrated in lipid management, not in blood pressure treatment.

Treatment intensity improved in both stages over the study period, but the gap between them did not close (Figure 5). The proportion of adults in stage 3 taking no lipid-lowering drug fell from 73.4% in 2005-2006 to 53.7% in 2021-2023, and the corresponding proportion in stage 4 fell from 54.1% to 33.6%. The absolute difference was 19.3 percentage points at the start of the period and 20.1 percentage points at the end. The proportion with low-density lipoprotein cholesterol at or above 70 mg/dL moved in parallel, from 92.7% to 78.4% in stage 3 and from 79.6% to 64.5% in stage 4, a gap that remained between 13 and 14 percentage points throughout.

Two-decade trend

Between 2006 and 2016, with a constant blood pressure protocol, stage 0 prevalence declined by 2.28% per year (95% CI, -3.36 to -1.19) and stage 1 rose by 3.11% per year (1.89 to 4.35); stage 2 declined modestly (-0.85% per year) and stages 3 and 4 were unchanged (0.06% and 0.27% per year, both non-significant) (Figure 1; Table S3). Joinpoint regression placed breakpoints at 2016 for stages 0, 1, and the composite of stages 2 and above, coinciding with the change in blood pressure instrumentation; the apparent reversal after 2016 should not be interpreted epidemiologically.

Because the stage-specific mortality gradient did not change across the study period (likelihood ratio test for stage-by-period interaction, P=0.63), stage-specific HRs estimated from 2005-2018 were applied to 2021-2023 death counts.

Burden attributable to modifiable exposures

In the 16,220 participants with complete exposure and covariate data (1,767 deaths), current smoking carried an adjusted hazard ratio of 1.90 (95% CI, 1.68-2.15) and physical inactivity 1.65 (1.49-1.84) for all-cause mortality (Table 4). In 2017-March 2020, 16.2% of adults smoked, 44.8% were physically inactive, and 9.0% had both exposures.

Current smoking accounted for 12.7% of CKM DALYs and physical inactivity for 22.6%.

Because the two exposures co-occur, their joint attributable fraction was 30.5% (95% CI, 25.7-35.1), corresponding to 6.1 million DALYs per year (Figure 4). Physical inactivity therefore contributed roughly twice as much burden as smoking.

Sensitivity analyses

Raising the PREVENT threshold to 30% reduced stage 3 prevalence from 6.5% to 2.1% but changed the PAF by only about 2 percentage points. Omitting the low-density lipoprotein criterion moved 2.8 percentage points from stage 2 to stages 0-1 and changed the PAF to 33.0%.

Relaxing the stage 0 completeness rule had no effect, because all stage 0 participants in 2021-2023 already had complete core inputs. Assigning all conditions the mild or severe end of the GBD severity range moved YLD from 4.09 million to 2.97 million and 9.95 million, respectively, corresponding to DALYs of 19.5 to 26.5 million per year. The Monte Carlo 95% confidence interval for total DALYs was 8.9 to 29.8 million per year, dominated by uncertainty in the stagespecific hazard ratios, not in prevalence (Table S4).

Discussion

In this nationally representative analysis, a staging framework built on predicted risk identified a population whose crude mortality was close to that of established cardiovascular disease, and CKM syndrome as a whole accounted for approximately 20.0 million DALYs per year among US adults, or 7,881 age-standardized DALYs per 100,000. The burden is concentrated in stages 3 and 4, which comprise 15.8% of adults but generated 76% of attributable years of life lost. Over the same two decades, however, stage 1 expanded by 3.1% per year and stage 0 contracted by 2.3% per year, with no change in stages 3 and 4. This pattern has implications for how prevention is evaluated. Stage 1 was not associated with a statistically significant excess in adjusted mortality (HR, 0.99; 95% CI, 0.78-1.25), whereas stage 2 was associated with a moderate increase (HR, 1.39; 95% CI, 1.13-1.71). A prevention program evaluated solely by short-term deaths averted may show little immediate mortality benefit among the 21% of adults classified as stage 1. However, the absence of a statistically significant excess in short-term mortality does not imply an absence of preventive benefit. The principal value of intervention at this stage may be preventing progression to stages 3 or 4, an outcome that this cross-sectional analysis cannot observe. Burden metrics therefore tend to favor late-stage treatment, potentially more so for CKM than for conditions whose burden is concentrated earlier.

Crude mortality in stage 3 was close to that in stage 4, although the adjusted hazard ratio remained lower. Stage 3 is defined by predicted risk rather than observed disease, and its members were older and more likely to have chronic kidney disease. The finding that a risk equation identifies a group with crude short-term mortality similar to that of adults with established cardiovascular disease supports the construct validity of the staging framework and suggests that risk-based staging adds information beyond diagnosis counts.

Our estimates can be compared with existing CKM research. Hyun and colleagues reported stage distribution and risk-factor control gaps for 2021-2023 but did not estimate health loss.2 Our stage prevalences (13.8%, 21.3%, 49.1%, 6.5%, and 9.3%) differed from theirs by no more than 3.0 percentage points, despite differences in the operational definitions of hypertension and the PREVENT threshold. This concordance supports the robustness of the underlying construct.

Their analysis was confined to a single cycle and did not address mortality, time trends, or attributable burden. They also documented treatment gaps across stages 2 to 4; our treatment analysis differs by anchoring the gap to the mortality comparison between stages 3 and 4. Kibbi and colleagues examined albuminuria within CKM stages,6 and Zuin and Piazza examined pulmonary embolism mortality in CKM,7 but neither quantified overall burden.

The 80% share of YLL in total DALYs contrasts with many chronic conditions in which YLD dominates. This reflects the lethality of the constituent diseases rather than measurement artifact: cardiovascular disease and chronic kidney disease are leading causes of death, and their disability weights, while non-trivial, are modest relative to the years of life lost.

The analysis has several strengths. Risk factors were measured rather than self-reported, staging incorporated predicted risk rather than diagnosis alone, and disability weights were drawn from GBD health states with severity and anemia stratification rather than applied uniformly. The mortality model included 27,012 participants and 4,706 deaths followed for up to two decades, allowing the stage gradient to be estimated with reasonable precision and its stability over time to be assessed. The difference between the two exposures is relevant to prevention policy. Physical inactivity contributed roughly twice the burden of smoking (22.6% versus 12.7%), despite tobacco control having a much more developed policy architecture. Physical activity has been less institutionalized within cardiovascular prevention, and these estimates suggest that its share of the CKM agenda should reflect its contribution.

For clinicians, the implication is narrower than the policy one. Adults who meet the PREVENT threshold for stage 3 had crude short-term mortality similar to that of adults with established cardiovascular disease, yet the data suggest that they are treated more like a high-risk primaryprevention population than a secondary-prevention population. The treatment gap we observed was concentrated in lipid lowering: approximately half of adults in stage 3 were taking no lipidlowering medication, and four in five had low-density lipoprotein cholesterol at or above 70 mg/dL, compared with one in three and two in three, respectively, in stage 4. Closing this gap does not require a new risk tool, because the group is already identified by the existing one.

Our results should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, NHANES is cross-sectional; stage at a single examination is used as a proxy for a lifelong trajectory, and progression cannot be observed. Second, the PAF is imprecise (95% CI, 18.5%-44.2%) because the linked mortality cohort contains 4,706 deaths across five stages; the descriptive stage-specific burden estimates do not share this limitation. Third, stage-specific HRs from 2005-2018 were applied to 2021-2023 death counts. Although the absence of a stage-by-period interaction supports this approach, secular changes in treatment could still have altered absolute risks. Fourth, the transition from mercury to oscillometric blood pressure measurement in 2017 creates a discontinuity that precludes trend inference after 2016. Fifth, disability weights for ischemic heart disease and stroke were based on GBD 2013 severity distributions because updated distributions were not publicly accessible; the mild and severe bounds we report span the plausible range. Sixth, the stage-based PAF is a model-based attribution, not the causal effect of an intervention that would move every adult to stage 0, and it assumes that the stage-specific hazard ratios are unconfounded.

Seventh, the DALY estimates assume a steady state; a cross-sectional design cannot capture the lag between exposure and death, so the burden associated with today's stage 1 will become apparent only in future estimates. These limitations do not affect the central inference, which rests on the shape of the burden distribution rather than its absolute level; every sensitivity analysis preserved the concentration of burden in stages 3 and 4.
